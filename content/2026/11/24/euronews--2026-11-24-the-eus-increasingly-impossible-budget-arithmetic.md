## Good morning. Leo Cendrowicz here with your Tuesday newsletter. Today, ominous signs for the EU’s budget negotiations, a breakdown of the latest Russia sanctions package, and Chinese spies and hackers back in the news.

**Budget blues.** As if hammering out the EU’s next seven-year budget wasn’t already shaping up to be a Herculean task, the Visegrád Four have thrown a few more spanners into the works.

Meeting in Bratislava on Monday alongside Austrian Chancellor **Christian Stocker**, the prime ministers of Hungary, Poland, Czechia and Slovakia aired their grievances about the European Commission’s spending plans.

Hungary’s **Péter Magyar** doesn’t even consider the proposal an acceptable starting point. He has written to European Council President**António Costa** questioning the wisdom of holding a special summit later this week: “There is no point spending two days in Brussels not agreeing on anything.” Well, quite.

Czech Prime Minister **Andrej Babiš** wondered whether the Commission had given any thought to saving money, taking aim at that perennial bugbear, the European Parliament’s three seats.**The Ukrainian conundrum.** Perhaps the trickiest new fault line is how to finance Ukraine. Magyar insists support for Kyiv should stay outside the EU budget, lest contributions become mandatory. More intriguingly, Poland’s Donald Tusk, hardly a Ukraine-sceptic, also expressed reservations about joint EU borrowing to support Kyiv.

Slovak host **Robert Fico** stressed that the V4 had not adopted a common budget position, although the leaders broadly agreed on cohesion spending, agriculture and migration.

The arithmetic is getting ever more complicated. The EU needs to spend more on defence, competitiveness and Ukraine, repay its pandemic-era debt and placate governments fiercely protective of their agricultural and regional subsidies. And, lest we forget, the budget requires unanimity.

**Magyar’s European paradox.** Even as he complicates Brussels’ financial planning, the Hungarian prime minister continues to repair relations with Kyiv, announcing a visit to Transcarpathia and pledging solidarity with Ukrainians under Russian attack.

His efforts to restore the rule of law have also earned Hungary approval for €10 billion in EU recovery funding. German Green MEP **Daniel Freund** joins Europe Today this morning to discuss Budapest’s progress.

## **Sanction actionIt’s here.** As previewed in yesterday’s newsletter, the Commission has unveiled its 22nd sanctions package against Russia. Banks, crypto platforms, energy facilities, “shadow fleet” vessels and former soldiers are all targeted.**Jorge Liboreiro** breaks down what’s inside.**Taking Russia out of the game.** The Commission wants to exclude teams and athletes competing under the Russian flag, ban Russian symbols at sporting events across the EU and prevent Russians from financing or holding governing posts in European sports federations.

“Sports events can not be a forum for propaganda activities,” Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said.

The measures follow the International Olympic Committee’s decision in July to lift its suspension of Russian athletes, imposed after the full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Moscow has seized the opportunity to accelerate its sporting comeback, prompting pushback across Europe.

**What’s next?** Ambassadors discuss the measures on Wednesday, with a deal sought before the winter break. That would be the second package agreed under Ireland’s six-month presidency. Speaking of which, Dublin has dodged a bullet: alumina is conspicuously absent from the sanctions list.

Ukraine’s Commissioner for Sanctions Policy **Vladyslav Vlasyuk** joins Europe Today this morning to discuss the package.

Meanwhile, the war is increasingly spilling across Ukraine’s borders. NATO’s North Atlantic Council meets today to discuss Friday’s Russian incursion into Polish airspace, with Warsaw seeking additional allied support to protect its eastern border.

## **In other newsPanda in the machine.** The Commission suspects Chinese-backed hackers are behind the cyberattack plaguing its IT systems for a month, Euronews has learned. The first confirmed data leak involves names and email addresses of users of the EU Academy learning platform, now offline. Staff unions are furious about being kept in the dark, while officials insist secrecy is essential to thwart the hackers.**She said, he said. Ursula Von der Leyen**has clashed with US special envoy** Jared Kushner**over when and how European security architecture should feature in Ukraine peace talks. Find out why they are at odds.** Six ex-PMs and a Brexit hangover. Andy Burnham**told MPs on Monday it would be “unthinkable” for Britain to rejoin the EU without another referendum, but insisted last week’s Brussels deals were not a prelude to one. Rishi Sunak became the sixth former prime minister to warn against reopening the Brexit debate. Burnham accused his predecessors of being “stuck in the past”. After all, who got Britain into this mess?** Meagre relocations.**Only 1,283 asylum seekers will be relocated between EU countries in 2026, the first year of the bloc’s new migration rules, against the 30,000 initially proposed by the Commission. Just 562 have been transferred so far, all from Cyprus, as governments repeatedly watered down the relocation mechanism.** Vincenzo Genovese**has the exclusive story.** Between a rock and a hard place.**Croatia is turning up the heat on Montenegro over the Prevlaka peninsula, a strategic sliver of rock commanding access to the Bay of Kotor, Euronews has learned. Zagreb’s demands threaten to derail Podgorica’s ambition to wrap up EU accession talks by Christmas. The irony? Croatia joined the EU in 2013 despite an unresolved border dispute with Slovenia. Enlargement comes with a selective memory.** Carbon counterattack.**Eight EU countries are resisting efforts to water down ETS2, the bloc’s carbon market for road transport and heating. In a letter to Climate Commissioner** Wopke Hoekstra**, seen by Euronews, they argue that electrification is now as much about energy security as saving the planet. Their opponents, including the Visegrád Four, argue that imposing additional costs on households during an energy crisis is politically unpalatable.** Hindsight is 20/20.**German Chancellor** Friedrich Merz**says NATO made a historic mistake in 2008 by not admitting Ukraine, arguing membership would likely have prevented Russia’s invasion. NATO chief** Mark Rutte**was more circumspect about reviving Kyiv’s membership bid but categorically rejected Moscow’s demands to roll back NATO’s borders to 1997. His prescription for escalating Russian hybrid attacks? More military support for Ukraine.** Chips, spies and second chances.**Nvidia boss** Jensen Huang**is back, days after being ousted over extramarital affairs, including one with a suspected Chinese spy. The board unanimously reinstated him on Monday after reported intervention by** Donald Trump**. Nvidia insists national security was not compromised, but senior Democrats are threatening investigations. Investors seem less troubled by the corporate soap opera: Nvidia shares soared.**‘Race realist’ given platform.** The far-right Europe of Sovereign Nations (ESN) group hosts controversial academic**Nathan Cofnas** at the European Parliament in Strasbourg today. Cofnas, suspended by Ghent University in August, spearheaded plagiarism allegations against**Jason Arday**, a Black former Cambridge professor who later died by suicide. A self-described “race realist”, Cofnas argues that genetic differences contribute to disparities in intelligence between racial groups.** NGO ‘witchhunt’ report.**Conservative MEPs** Carlo Fidanza**(ECR) and** Dirk Gotink** (EPP) present the findings of Parliament’s Scrutiny Working Group on NGO financing in Strasbourg today. Tasked with examining potential misconduct in the Commission’s funding of NGOs, the group was boycotted by left-wing MEPs, who denounced it as a witchhunt.
