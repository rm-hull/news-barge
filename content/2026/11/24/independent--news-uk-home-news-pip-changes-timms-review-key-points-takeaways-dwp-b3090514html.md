Disability minister Sir Stephen Timms has released his report on the Personal Independence Payment (PIP), setting out how the government should improve the benefit for the future.

Co-produced with disabled leaders and informed by more than 40,000 people and organisations, the review was launched after a 100-strong Labour backbench rebellion forced ministers to shelve £5bn in planned welfare cuts last year.

In place for more than four million people, PIP is the UK’s most-claimed disability benefit, designed to help with extra costs incurred from a health condition or disability.

Sir Stephen, minister for social security and disability, said: “The Timms Review’s interim report was clear that while PIP is valued, the system is no longer fit for purpose.

“I am immensely grateful to my fellow co-chairs, the steering group and the tens of thousands of people who shared their experiences to shape this report. The government will consider the review’s wider recommendations carefully and respond in the new year.”

![Sir Stephen Timms said PIP had the right aims but needs a fundamental overhaul](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/22/23/acec88814f5f0339a19a8a5fb031df63Y29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzk1NDczMzk0-2.63148200.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The report divides its proposals into immediate fixes and a longer-term redesign. Ministers have already accepted two early changes: introducing fast-track routes for people with severe conditions alongside those nearing the end of life, and cutting unnecessary award reviews for lifelong or progressive illnesses.

Crucially, what the Timms Review leaves unchanged is just as significant as what it reforms – putting it on a collision course with Alan Milburn’s review into young people and work, which last week called for PIP to be scrapped entirely for under-25s.

Here are the key takeaways from the Timms Review:

## PIP to stay ‘cash first’ and unconditional for all

Despite recent debate over restricting support for younger adults or replacing payments with vouchers, the steering group insists PIP must remain a non-means-tested, non-taxable cash benefit available both in and out of work.

This brings it into direct contrast with the Milburn Review, which called for an entirely new benefits system for younger people, in most cases with payments conditional on them engaging with employment support.

Vouchers are also ruled out by the Timms Review. While future awards could include an optional, reversible scheme allowing claimants to trade part of their cash for vetted services – mirroring how Motability leases vehicles – financial autonomy remains central.

## Overhauling a ‘dehumanising’ claims process

More than 90 per cent of people who responded to the review’s call for evidence described the experience of claiming PIP negatively, with many saying they felt treated with suspicion. To rebuild trust, the report sets out over a dozen immediate fixes to the existing system.

![A white paper on welfare reform is expected early next year after the publication of Alan Milburn’s (pictured) youth unemployment report and Sir Stephen Timms’ review](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/22/23/4089c13bbfe74742c9c8e161ce470c4dY29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzk1NDczNjg4-2.86519496.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

These include rewriting the lengthy PIP application form in plain English, recording reasonable adjustments on a “tell us once” basis so claimants do not have to repeatedly ask for help, and funding independent local advisers to end a “postcode lottery” of support.

With 65 per cent of tribunal hearings currently overturning Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) decisions on PIP, the review calls for assessors to receive specialist training on how health conditions and trauma affect claimants. Assessments would also be recorded and sent to applicants automatically.

## Longer-term: A new assessment to replace the points test

PIP applicants are currently scored on whether they can carry out 12 specific everyday tasks, such as preparing a simple meal, washing, or walking a set distance.

The review says this narrow approach fails to capture mental health problems, neurodivergence, and conditions where symptoms flare up and recede.

It recommends replacing this system with a new ‘impact assessment’ that looks at how a condition affects someone’s daily life and increases their living costs over time – such as needing extra heating or accessible taxis.

Crucially, claimants would be judged on what support they need rather than having to hand over bills or receipts, ensuring low-income applicants who cannot currently afford extra heating or travel are not penalised.

## Longer-term: Reshaping who qualifies for PIP

The review makes no recommendations to cut welfare spending, with chairs spelling out from its inception that it would stay within existing Office for Budget Responsibility forecasts.

These currently project working-age disability benefit costs to rise from £14.5bn in 2020 to more than £34.6bn by 2032.

![Andy Burnham’s government will consider both the Timms and Milburn reports ahead of a welfare white paper in the new year](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/12/08/2304202086..?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&trim=0%2C0%2C0%2C0&auto=webp)

To provide more generous support to under-served groups without breaching that ceiling, the steering group admits future eligibility rules will need “rebalancing”.

While people with high care and fluctuating needs stand to gain, the report states PIP “is not appropriate for those who do not incur any additional costs associated with being disabled” – pointing to research from the Resolution Foundation that finds 16 per cent of current recipients report no extra disability-related expenses.

## What it means for the future

The government will consider both the Timms and Milburn reports ahead of a welfare white paper in the new year, which will require MPs’ backing.

Ministers are already working to bring backbenchers on side to avoid another damaging rebellion. While Mr Milburn insists youth employment support must go hand-in-hand with benefit cuts, some Labour MPs and campaigners are pushing for alternative tax-raising measures instead.

But the true test will come when the final plans are laid out.

One way the government could conceivably adopt both recommendations is to overhaul the benefit system for under-25s – completely removing PIP for these claimants – while adopting the improvements to the benefit put forward by Sir Stephen for all others.

But any “two-tier” system would undoubtedly face fierce pushback from campaigners and some backbenchers, opening the door to a showdown for prime minister Andy Burnham in the new year.

Experts at the Resolution Foundation have said such a plan would make “no sense”, especially given the cost of administering two benefit systems at once.

Louise Murphy, a senior economist at the think tank, commented: “When it comes to Pip, which is this extra-cost disability benefit, young people look a lot less distinctive than you might think.”

“The conditions and the needs that young people have aren’t radically different than slightly older adults, so to us there’s less of a clear case for doing a totally different system because that would involve a lot of administrative complexity across the age 25.”
