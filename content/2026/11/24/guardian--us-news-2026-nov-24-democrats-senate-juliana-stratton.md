Juliana Stratton, the incoming Democratic senator from Illinois, said she would support significantly reforming or abolishing the filibuster in order to allow Congress to deliver on the promises they made American voters.

The filibuster means that most successful legislation in the Senate must have 60 supporters instead of a simple majority. Eliminating that requirement is one of several reforms Democrats can act on after they won an unexpected 56-44 majority in the next US Senate, their largest majority in nearly two decades.

Stratton, currently the Illinois lieutenant governor, was a professional mediator before she entered politics. For two decades, she ran a dispute resolution firm, bringing people who did not agree together to find ways to solve problems.

The experience will serve her well in a divided government, she told the Guardian. She welcomes Republicans who want to stand up to Trump and “have a real spine” to work with and Democrats in the US Senate.

“We cannot go in kind of waffling on, ‘Well, we’ll see what we can do, it’s going to be hard.’ That’s the same kind of thing that really turned so many voters off this election cycle – people going in, telling them how hard everything is and that there’s so many hurdles,” Stratton said. “Sure, there are hurdles, but we were elected to do hard things.”

Stratton said she wants to see simple, clear bills moved quickly once the party is back in power, including a restoration of cuts to Medicaid and food assistance, returning subsidies to the Affordable Care Act marketplace, ending tariffs and ending the Iran war. If Trump then vetoes those, “that’s on Republicans”, she said.

“If he wants to run on how he’s doubling down on taking away people’s healthcare, if Republicans want to say it’s OK that children are going hungry – let them run on that, to their peril,” she said.

She said Chicago was “still feeling the effects” of Operation Midway Blitz, the Trump immigration onslaught that brought hundreds of federal agents into the metropolitan area and ended in thousands of arrests and detentions. She does not believe Immigration and Customs Enforcement can be reformed; it needs to be abolished. Senators should use “the power of the purse” to withhold funding for ICE, she said.

During her campaign, she pledged not to vote for Chuck Schumer to lead the Democrats in the Senate, a promise she is sticking by. But she also wouldn’t say whether she would support Senator Chris Van Hollen of Maryland, who is challenging Schumer for majority leader and has also called for eliminating the filibuster.

“I’m listening to everyone,” she said. “I want to see who steps up. We have one, Senator Van Hollen, who has stepped up. I want to see how this plays out and what vision people have for how we’re going to deliver, and how we’re going to move forward as a party.

“We need to meet the mandate that we’ve been given by the voters this election cycle,” she added.

Democrats can keep the momentum they saw in the midterms by delivering on their campaign promises, she said: “Show them what’s possible, and not just make it aspirational, but actually do it.”

Stratton, 61, started her political career within the past decade, running for a seat in the Illinois house of representatives in 2016 in a race in which she challenged the incumbent and received an endorsement from Barack Obama. After one term in the state house, she was tapped by JB Pritzker as a running mate for the gubernatorial ticket. She has served as the state’s lieutenant governor since 2019.

Running for the US Senate “was not my dream”, she said. She decided to run for a state representative seat because she was her mother’s caregiver – her mother had Alzheimer’s, and there was an effort to strip away healthcare access at the state level. She knew how hard it was to help her mother get what she needed, and the idea of taking healthcare away from vulnerable Illinoisans made her angry, she said.

Her mother died the day before Stratton launched her state house campaign. Ten years later, she ran on a Medicare for All platform, saying she believes healthcare is a human right.

She shared the story of caring for her mother often on the campaign trail, calling it “the hardest thing that I’ve ever done and the greatest honor of my life”. She said that every time she shared it, someone would walk up to her afterward to say they had similar stories.

Illinois will be the first state to be represented by two women of color in the US Senate when Stratton joins Democratic senator Tammy Duckworth. Stratton said she and Duckworth recognize the history-making element, and she thinks this representation does matter and creates a stronger democracy.

“I always say that we don’t want policy just to be made for us,” Stratton said. “We want policy to be made with us.”

Stratton is also a mom of four – three adult children, and one who is 10. She said being a mom has equipped her with stamina and problem-solving skills while giving her insights into what life is like for younger people. Her youngest daughter’s school was on lockdown twice because of ICE activity in the community during Operation Midway Blitz.

One of her adult daughters called to tell her about increases in the cost of her healthcare plan, purchased through the ACA marketplace, and how she’d have to choose a worse plan in order to afford care.

“We really need more people in Washington who are connected to the everyday struggles of the people that they represent,” Stratton said. “I want to build a better world for my children, and I want to build a better world for all children. And that’s what’s always going to keep me grounded as I continue to do this work.”
