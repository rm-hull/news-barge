## Free York events invite visitors to explore disability, myths and legends

Letter Press: Stephen Lee Hodgkins' letterpress printing press, which will be used during the free Disabled Myths & Legends workshops at York Explore. *(Image: Submitted)*

As York Disability Week returns from November 28 to December 5, Explore York Libraries and Archives is hosting a programme of free events celebrating disability culture, self-expression and creativity.

York Disability Week is a city-wide programme created by local organisations, artists and volunteers to celebrate disability culture, creativity and inclusion.

The week takes place in recognition of the United Nations International Day of Persons with Disabilities.

This year's programme at Explore includes the annual Eleanor Worthington Prize exhibition, featuring entries from the 12th year of the international competition for art students from Italy, the UK and Ireland, alongside two free, drop-in Disabled Myths & Legends printmaking workshops.

Led by artists Stephen Lee Hodgkins, Imogen Kate, Kerry Fox and Alfie Fox, the workshops will introduce participants to a range of printmaking techniques, encouraging them to create their own artwork inspired by disability culture and lived experience.

The workshops are inspired by York's remarkable printing archives, which are the focus of Explore's creative programme this year.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21735464.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Old Mother Hubbard: CBK/1/26, "Harrild's new instructive and pleasing tales for children" (19th century), originally printed by J. Kendrew and now available in Explore York Archive. *(Image: Submitted)*

Participants have been exploring collections including editions of the York Mercury, York's first newspaper printed by Grace White in 1724, 19th-century chapbooks filled with tales, poems and folklore, and materials relating to the Delittle printing business, one of York's best-known printing firms.

These collections reveal how people in York shared news, entertainment and local stories across generations, while also highlighting the work of pioneering printers such as Grace White and Ann Ward, whose contributions helped shape the city's publishing history.

Sadhbh Dunne, Access and Engagement Archivist at Explore York Archives, said: "Printing has always been a democratising medium.

"By sharing the written word, it allowed ideas, experiences and perspectives to reach audiences that traditional histories have often overlooked.  
  
"From chapbooks that educated and entertained children to pioneering women printers such as Grace White and Ann Ward, York's printing collections reveal the breadth and diversity of the communities they served.  
  
"It's exciting to see artists finding new inspiration in these stories today."

Artists involved in the York Disability Week workshops have drawn inspiration from these collections, using them as a starting point for new stories and creative responses.

Participants will have the opportunity to explore those ideas through hands-on printmaking activities led by disabled artists, experimenting with different techniques and creating original artwork inspired by the collections.

Activities will include collaborative storytelling through live letterpress printing, creating personal legends and exploring how myths, folklore and everyday experiences can be reimagined through a disability lens.

Participants' contributions will help create unique artworks inspired by both York's historic print collections and contemporary disability culture.

Stephen Lee Hodgkins said: "Myths and legends have typically represented disabled people in either negative or lacking ways.  
  
"From disfigured villains and deranged ranters to unfortunate degenerates, disabilities often became devices used to tell moral stories.  
  
"Yet disabled people are significant members of our communities and, through resistance, speaking up and simply being present, help make society more inclusive and human.  
  
"Our printmaking workshops will reimagine some everyday disabled stories through a myths and legends lens."

For many of the artists leading the workshops, York Disability Week is part of a much bigger story.

Over the past three years, Explore's Disability Arts programme has helped disabled artists work with York's archive collections in new ways.

Drawing inspiration from historical records, participants have created exhibitions, performances and artworks that have uncovered overlooked stories, challenged perceptions and helped make the archives more accessible to wider audiences.

Projects have included nationally recognised exhibitions and creative projects such as Shush, Snip, Rip and A Census of Disability Joy, alongside collaborations with organisations including York People First, Blueberry Academy, Pyramid Arts and disability-led theatre company Next Door But One.

Through exhibitions, workshops and performances, disabled artists have drawn on York's archives to tell new stories, challenge assumptions and bring overlooked voices to wider audiences.

One of the artists involved, Alfie Fox, reflected: "Art allows me to say what I can't say in words.  
It gives me access to communicate. I looked at York archives from the workhouse and how people with disabilities were sent there and the words used to describe disability. A lot has changed but a lot is still the same."

This year's programme offers a chance to discover York's archives from a different perspective, explore the work of disabled artists and get involved in creative activities inspired by the city's rich printing heritage.

Visitors are encouraged to drop in, take part and create something of their own.
