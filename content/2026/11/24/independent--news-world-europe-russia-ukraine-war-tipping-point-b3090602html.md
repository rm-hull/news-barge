Russia’s full-scale invasion against Ukraine may reach a “tipping point” next year that sees Moscow defeated, claims Latvian President Edgars Rinkevics.

While in New York to chair the United Nations Security Council, the politician argued that Russia is not genuinely interested in ending the war and likely viewed fresh negotiations on a ceasefire as a “decoy” to continue its aggression.

Meanwhile, Ukraine braces for a “perfect storm” of a brutal winter, increased attacks against energy infrastructure and escalating strikes against civilian targets.

However, Mr Rinkevics said Europe was standing by to help Kyiv with military, financial and infrastructure support.

Earlier this month, the EU proposed providing Ukraine with a loan of up to €45 billion (approximately £37 billion) ahead of an anticipated funding gap for 2027 – which would allow Ukraine to only pay it back when Russia pays reparations for its war.

![Latvian President Edgars Rinkevics says a 'tipping point' in the war is looming](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/12/2026-11-24T001900Z_251790931_RC2YTOARPWKB_RTRMADP_3_UKRAINE-CRISIS-LATVIA.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

”If we see Ukraine surviving from a military point of view, from an economic point of view – and I’m sure Ukraine is going to survive – then the tipping point could be next year,” Mr Rinkevics said in an interview on Monday.

On top of pressure on Russia's economy, ‌there is growing political consensus about the need to end the war, Mr Rinkevics claimed, citing attacks that had killed Indian sailors or struck ships from the Philippines and others.

"The number of countries that are putting pressure is going (up) so the tipping point may come in spring, summer, next year," he added.

The president also warned of increasing “hybrid activities” which have been directed against European countries and that are likely linked to Moscow, including airspace violations, cyberattacks and sabotage.

On Tuesday, Poland accused Russia of briefly entering its airspace during a drone attack on Ukraine.

“It’s hard to imagine this being a coincidence, so we assume it was an intentional act,” Deputy Defence Minister Pawel Zalewski told local news.

![Mr Zalewski assumes the move was an 'intentional act'](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/08/2251183492..?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&auto=webp)

Mr Zalewski added that Nato will deploy an “AWACS early-warning system aircraft” to Poland as part of its response to the missile incident.

Mr Rinkevics says Russia has been careful so far in not breaching an Article 5 declaration under Nato that would force members to come to each other's aid – but warned the country’s “appetite for risk” is growing.

He noted that the number of sabotage activities, arson attempts and attacks against Ukraine-friendly entities was up sharply from 2022, when Moscow launched its full-scale invasion.

Recent hybrid incidents in Germany, Spain and the Baltics - some of which are still under investigation - posed challenges in terms of determining a proportionate response, but Europe's intelligence agencies are improving their ability to cooperate and coordinate, preempting attacks in some cases, Mr Rinkevics said.

The Kremlin consistently ‌denies conducting sabotage operations in Nato countries or plotting ⁠any confrontation.

Latvia on Monday again summoned Russia's ambassador in Riga over recent Russian attacks, Mr Rinkevics said, underscoring the importance of such public rebuke at the same time that European countries were fighting back with "more cooperation, more intelligence sharing, more mutual support."

"That's ‌the result of very good cooperation ​across [the] intelligence and counterintelligence community," he said.

"The trouble always is ‌that you always have a very skilled enemy ​that is also implementing new ways of trying to test you and trying to attack you, and this is [an] ongoing fight."
