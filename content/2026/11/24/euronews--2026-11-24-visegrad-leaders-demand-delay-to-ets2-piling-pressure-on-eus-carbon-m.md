## Poland, Hungary, Slovakia and the Czech Republic want to soften the bloc's expanded carbon market ahead of the 26-27 November European Council

Poland, Hungary, Slovakia and the Czech Republic are urging Brussels to rethink the EU's carbon pricing scheme for buildings and road transport, warning that soaring energy prices make its planned 2028 launch untenable.

In a letter to European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen on November 23, seen by Euronews, the Visegrad Group leaders warn that the Emissions Trading System (ETS2) could cost households and businesses tens of billions of euros, hitting consumption, employment and investment.

“According to verifiable calculations, the Emission Trading Scheme 2 (ETS2) is set to impose an additional gross burden of tens of billions of euros from 2028 onwards,” reads the letter sent to Commission President Ursula von der Leyen on 23 November.

“If we do not address this issue in a timely and effective manner, there is a risk of a decline in household consumption (…) and a subsequent spillover effect on corporate revenues, employment and investment.”

EU leaders asked the Commission at their 15–16 October summit to update its assessment of ETS2. The four governments are stepping up pressure ahead of another summit on 26–27 November aimed at securing a deal on the EU's long-term budget.

Unlike the existing ETS, which covers power generation and energy-intensive industries, the new system will put a carbon price on fuels used in buildings and road transport, potentially driving up heating bills and prices at the pump.

The ETS2 has already been delayed until 2028. The four governments want the first auction of emissions allowances, scheduled for January 2027, postponed until the system's future is clarified.

"This first auction should be postponed in a way that ensures legal certainty," reads the letter.

By contrast, eight other EU countries urged the Commission on 23 November to keep the expanded carbon market on track, arguing that it is crucial to accelerating electrification and reducing dependence on imported fossil fuels.

The clash threatens to reopen one of the EU's most contentious climate policies, exposing deep divisions over who should bear the cost of the green transition and how to avoid a backlash from voters and businesses.

The Visegrad leaders are also putting the spotlight on who bears the cost of the EU's green transition, with governments questioning whether the EU can maintain its emissions targets without triggering a backlash from voters and businesses. Upcoming elections in France, Italy and Germany will be a key indicator.

The challenge risks reopening one of the EU's most contentious climate policies just as Brussels tries to defend its post-2030 emissions agenda.

## **Challenging Brussels' economic assumptions**

The four governments argue that the Commission's 2021 impact assessment no longer reflects economic conditions, particularly since the US-Israeli war against Iran disrupted global energy markets.

They want Brussels to reassess the scheme using updated energy prices, inflation forecasts and carbon price scenarios, examining the impact on household purchasing power, economic growth and businesses.

They argue that a uniform carbon price could have widely differing consequences across member states, depending on household incomes, heating systems, transport alternatives and national taxation.

According to Czech research cited in the letter, the poorest 10% of households could spend 1.58% of their income on ETS2-related costs, compared with 0.5% for the wealthiest 10%.

The leaders also question whether the Social Climate Fund, designed to help vulnerable households and micro-enterprises cope with higher fuel and heating costs, will provide sufficient and timely compensation.
