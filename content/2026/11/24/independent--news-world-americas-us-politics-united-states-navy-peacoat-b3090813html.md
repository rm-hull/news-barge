The U.S. Navy has unveiled plans to phase out the most iconic piece of its service members’ uniforms.

​The hip-length, double-breasted peacoat, worn by sailors for more than 100 years, is to be replaced by a black parka. ​The change, which will see the wool coat swapped out for the synthetic alternative, was unveiled in an administrative message released earlier this month and obtained by *The Independent.*

​The peacoat stopped being issued back in 2018 and has declined in popularity ever since. However, under the new plans, even optional wear of the garment will no longer be authorized from October 1, 2027.

Chief of Naval Operations Admiral Daryl Caudle explained in a statement given to *the Military Times* that the retirement of the peacoat represents an effort to cut costs for sailors.

![The US Navy is retiring one of its most famous pieces of clothing](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/16/2304485543..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

​“This decision reflects feedback from the fleet given the peacoat has not been issued for eight years, and our commitment to eliminating unnecessary costs while ensuring sailors have the cold-weather protection they need to accomplish their missions,” he said. “We can honor our traditions without being bound by them.

​“Putting sailors first means being willing to make common-sense changes that improve their quality of service.”

​The move marks the end of an era for a garment that has its roots in European menswear.

​The coat was originally worn as military wear by the British Royal Navy, whose traditions the U.S. Navy draws upon, military uniform historian Jennifer Daley told USNI News. The actual design of the coat, though, originated in the Netherlands.

![The peacoat (shown here) has been worn by service members for over 100 years](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/16/52/Untitled-4-copy.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&auto=webp)

​The garment was featured in U.S. Navy uniform regulations as far back as 1897, according to records reviewed by menswear publication *Heddels*. Some historians have argued that it was officially adopted as part of the naval uniform in 1913.

​For many, the coat remains the most recognizable part of a sailor’s uniform to this day despite a decline in its popularity at sea.

​A petition to allow the peacoat to be worn as an “optional uniform component” has been launched on Change.org, with campaigners arguing that the garment represents an integral part of naval history.

​“Unlike operational equipment, which must continually evolve to meet changing requirements, traditional uniform components also serve a historical and institutional purpose,” the campaign page reads. “They provide continuity between generations of Sailors and contribute to the Navy's distinctive identity.”

​Despite its retirement, Caudle says that one service member - the Lone Sailor statue installed in Washington, D.C. - will get to keep his peacoat.

​“The Navy is retiring the peacoat from the seabag, but we’re making one iconic exception,” Caudle said. “In recognition of nearly four decades of standing watch and representing generations of American Sailors, I’m granting the Lone Sailor a permanent waiver to keep wearing his peacoat.

![Critics have argued that the peacoat is an essential part of a sailor's visual identity](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/16/2304485465..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

​“After all, he’s stood his watch through bitter cold, biting winds, and humid heat without ever asking for relief,” the admiral continued. “Frankly, he’s earned it.”

​However, the peacoat is not the only part of sailors’ uniforms being retired under the Navy’s plans, the naval administrative message states.

​Also facing retirement in October 2027 is the dinner dress white jacket, a formal piece of clothing worn by service members at events where civilians would be wearing black tie. Instead, sailors will only be allowed to wear the blue version of the jacket.

​Similarly, the Navy’s acrylic V-neck sweaters will no longer be part of a sailor’s uniform, although the wool version will remain authorized.
