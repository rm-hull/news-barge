The motor industry has issued a significant challenge to the viability of the prime minister’s plan to “reindustrialise” Britain.

The Society of Motor Manufacturers and Traders (SMMT), the body representing manufacturers and the garage trade, has condemned what it calls a “triple lock” of policies which it says are now blocking crucial investment plans and job creation.

These, they claim, include the Zero Emission Mandate, which is badly misaligned with retail consumer demand for electric cars and forces companies to sell some EVs at a loss. It has cost the car companies £10bn to subsidise sales of electrics in 2024 and 2025, on top of incentives including low and zero interest finance deals.

The SMMT fears this is unsustainable, and the government already has it under another review. Reports suggest ministers may postpone or modify the current quotas once again by promoting more efficient petrol hybrid models and slowing the transition to pure electric models. As with other sectors such as steel, cement and ceramics, there is also concern about high energy bills.

But for domestic jobs. the biggest danger of all is if the UK remains outside the European Union’s new protectionist “Made in Europe” area.

Mike Phillpot, chairman of the SMMT and CEO of Kia UK, said: “Made in Europe could effectively lock us out of Europe and, together with tougher rules of origin and ongoing trade disruption, the competitiveness of UK production is under threat. We need to make the UK a more attractive place in which to invest. We need action now.”

The Made in Europe scheme is the EU’s response to intense competition from China, especially in the troubled European automotive sector which remains vital to the economies of Germany, France, Italy and some of the east European member states such as Slovakia where production has ramped up in recent years.

Makers such as Volkswagen and Mercedes-Benz are already making painful cuts to their EU operations. For British cars, vans and lorries, the EU is an essential export market, taking roughly half of all production. If further barriers were imposed, for example by banning UK made vehicles from official procurement programmes or British components no longer counting as “European”, it could have a devastating impact on what's left of the industry. This would inflict catastrophic plant closure, layers and jobs losses.

In the continuing reset talks, the EU is informally linking concessions to the UK on Made in Europe rules with the vexed issue of lower tuition fees for EU students attending British universities.

The widely accepted estimate of the loss of income to UK institutions from such a move would be around £500m, which would have to made up by the taxpayer. Such a sum is significant, but is dwarfed in comparison to the billions in social costs and impact on GDP of the loss of major manufacturing centres such as Ellesmere Port, Sunderland and Oxford.

The EU would most likely also ask the British to raise the tariff on imports of Chinese cars, to match the EU level, potentially raising retail prices for British drivers.

The EU-UK Joint Declaration issued after last week’s partial reset agreements pledged to “to limit negative effects on one another in measures impacting specific supply chains including EVs”.

The SMMT argue that government “must now work urgently with the EU to agree concrete solutions that safeguard the €80 billion a year trading relationship and support its future growth”.

A government spokesperson said: “We remain committed to the phase-out of all new petrol and diesel cars by 2030 and to all new car and van sales being zero emission by 2035, as well as to our legally binding net zero targets.

“Our consultation on the mandate will not change this and will ensure we are taking a pragmatic and balanced approach to the ZEV (zero emission vehicle) transition.”
