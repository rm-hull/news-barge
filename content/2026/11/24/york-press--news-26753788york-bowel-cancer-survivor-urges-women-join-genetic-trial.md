## York survivor urges people to take test that could have saved her sister's life

Katie McSweeney, from York, believes her sister Sara may still be here if genetic screening had been available *(Image: Jonathan Pow)*

A YORK woman who fought cancer alongside her sister is urging people to take part in a genetic screening which she believes could have saved her sister’s life.

Katie McSweeney - who is 39 and from Dringhouses, York - has encouraged women to take part in the PROTECT-C trial – a landmark screening programme to show women if they carry genetic information that can increase their risk of breast, ovarian, womb or bowel cancer.

It is the first time this kind of screening has been offered to the public and not just those with a family history of cancer – something Katie believes could have saved her sister’s life.

Katie – who works as a secondary school English teacher - was diagnosed with stage three bowel cancer at age 29 in 2017.

Her diagnosis came after she mentioned persistent symptoms to a nurse during an unrelated appointment for travel vaccinations – leading to Katie undergoing surgery to remove part of her bowel and six months of chemotherapy.

She said: “It was a big shock. Because I was so young, I just didn’t think it could be cancer. A part of me still feels totally infallible.

“I know I’ve had bowel cancer, and I know it was bad, but I have a positive mentality, and I always considered myself to be bulletproof.”

During her treatment, her sister, Sara, began experiencing bowel issues, which were originally attributed to pregnancy, until symptoms revealed that she too had stage 3 bowel cancer.

Sara was diagnosed with the same cancer as her sister, just as Katie finished chemotherapy.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21752239.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Katie wants to ensure her children have the knowledge that she and her sister Sara were robbed of *(Image: Jonathan Pow)*

Katie said: “One minute Sara was texting me wishing me good luck with my final chemo and the next thing I knew she had been rushed to hospital and immediately prepared for surgery.

“I rushed to be with her. She had the left side of her bowel removed, and we had a little joke that together we made a full bowel.

“It was funny because I’d copied Sara throughout my whole life. We had the same jobs at school, and we both moved to Scotland. She was my total idol and I adored her.

“I still really struggle to talk about her in the past tense because she’s so much a part of me and who I am.

“It was a huge shock and then it suddenly dawned on me - do my other siblings need to be worried?”

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21752235.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Katie is an ambassador for PROTECT-C as she believes the trial will help make sure other families don't experience the loss that her's did *(Image: Jonathan Pow)*

After being referred for genetic testing, Katie discovered she had Lynch syndrome, an inherited genetic condition that increases the risk of bowel, womb and ovarian cancer.

She added: “Finding out I had Lynch syndrome was really hard. I felt responsible for my siblings. I wished I’d known sooner so I could have told them sooner. I didn’t want any of them to have to go through what I had gone through.”

Katie and Sara underwent the same course of chemotherapy, however, while Katie’s post-chemotherapy scan came back clear, Sara’s showed her cancer had spread.

In 2018, just nine months after her initial diagnosis, Sara died – with her letter confirming a diagnosis of Lynch’s syndrome arriving two days later.

## “I genuinely believe there is a chance Sara might still be here if genetic testing had been available sooner.

Looking back, Katie said that she believes earlier genetic screening could have changed the course of her family’s lives – and potentially saved her sister’s life.

She said: “I can't help wondering what might have happened if something like this had existed for my family years ago.

“If we had known about Lynch syndrome earlier, we would have understood the risks we were facing and could have benefited from surveillance and support long before anyone became seriously ill.

“I genuinely believe there is a chance Sara might still be here if genetic testing had been available sooner.

“As cancer hadn’t previously affected our family, we would never had thought to seek genetic testing, and it is unlikely we would have fit the current criteria anyway.

“Of course, we will never know for certain, but having the information about Lynch syndrome earlier could have completely changed the decisions made about her care and the options available to her.”

In Yorkshire, an estimated 19,400 women carry inherited variants linked to cancer, yet around 97 per cent of these people remain unidentified, despite one in every 20 cancers in women being caused by genetic variants that are passed down in families.

The PROTECT-C trial hopes to detect this through an at-home saliva test – ensuring those at risk will be offered personalised NHS care, including regular screening and preventative medicine or surgery where needed.

The trial will also assess whether this approach to genetic testing is affordable and beneficial for the NHS and the psychological impact on those participating.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21752238.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Sara's daughter, Freya *(Image: Jonathan Pow)*

Researchers believe the model being tested could also pave the way for cancer genetic screening programmes for men or to identify genes responsible for other chronic diseases.

Katie added: “Tests later showed that my brother also carries the genetic variant, so I’m pleased he now has the knowledge to make informed decisions about his health and benefit from regular screening.

“But my niece, Freya, will spend most of her life without a mum, and our family have lost so many years with our wonderful, funny and supportive sister and daughter.

“Sara was a scientist and the fact that such an easy scientific test can have such a hugely positive impact is something she would find so exciting.

“If our experience encourages even one person to take part and helps prevent another family losing someone they love, then I hope that can become part of Sara’s legacy.”

Professor Ranjit Manchanda – who is leading the trial at Queen Mary University of London - said: “Identifying that someone has an increased genetic risk of cancer after they’ve already been diagnosed is a failure of cancer prevention.

“We now have a major opportunity to help change that by giving women greater opportunities to prevent cancers.

“If we want to reduce the impact of cancer across the country, we have to think bigger. Healthcare must shift from reacting to cancer to preventing it.”

Women, trans men and non-binary people with female reproductive organs can express an interest in participating at: www.protect-c.co.uk, with further information found at www.YorkshireCancerResearch.org.uk/protect-c
