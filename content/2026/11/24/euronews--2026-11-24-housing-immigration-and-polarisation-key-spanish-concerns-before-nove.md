## Housing and immigration top Spaniards' concerns ahead of the snap election, in a campaign also marked by corruption cases and political polarisation.

With Spain's 29 November general election just around the corner, the parties have fixed their attention on Spaniards' main concerns in order to adjust their programmes and campaign promises and woo voters with solutions to the problems that worry them most.

According to the latest barometer from the Centre for Sociological Research (CIS), published (source in Spanish) on 20 November, the main problem, according to 55.7% of those surveyed, is housing, while immigration (20.9%) and the country's economic situation (20.3%) complete the podium.

For its part, the GAD3 barometer, published (source in Spanish) by the daily ABC on 9 November, likewise identifies the housing crisis (55%) as Spaniards' main concern, followed by the economic situation (27%). Polarisation (22%), corruption (15%) and the government's performance (15%) round off the top five.

## Housing, a concern that comes to a head in an election

The difficulty of accessing housing has been one of the main talking points in Spain for years. According to figures (source in Spanish) from the National Statistics Institute (INE), house prices rose by 84.6% between 2015 and 2025, while average annual gross wages increased by 27.9% between 2015 and 2024, reaching €29,540.

"What you notice in a big city like this is the lack of housing, which I have experienced first-hand," Andrea Garriga, 61, told Euronews in Madrid. Originally from Chile, she will vote in Spain for the first time this year, after recently gaining Spanish citizenship.

Garriga also points to another issue that worsens the problem of access to housing: low wages. "There are jobs that do not match people's training or abilities," she laments.

In fact, a recent study (source in Spanish) by Oxfam Intermón, presented in October, warned that spending on housing has driven up the in-work poverty rate to 15.49%, that is, around 770,000 people across the country. According to the report, one in three working people who rent at market rates live in poverty after paying their rent. Added to this is a lack of supply: between 2021 and 2025, an estimated deficit of some 750,000 homes built up, according to the Bank of Spain.

"No one can own a home unless they earn a fortune; you have to rent. I have three children and I am renting," says Juan Carlos, 70, who spoke to Euronews in the city centre. "Spain is very different from many European countries when it comes to wages, pensions, everything."

Discontent intensified in September with the eviction of María del Carmen Abascal, known to everyone as Maricarmen, who at the age of 87 was removed from the flat in central Madrid where she had lived since 1956. Her situation sparked a wave of protests across the country, calling both for her return home and for a structural solution to the housing crisis.

The government of Pedro Sánchez then approved two decrees in an attempt to tackle the problem of access to housing. However, a lack of support in Congress meant that both measures were eventually voted down. The president's response was not long in coming and, just three days later, he announced that the general election would be brought forward to this November.

Finally, on 12 November, the Socialists managed to push through one of the two decrees in the Permanent Deputation, the body that maintains parliamentary activity while the Cortes are dissolved. The approved text, dubbed the 'Maricarmen decree', includes measures to protect vulnerable people from eviction, limit certain rent increases and expand the supply of affordable housing.

José Félix, a 36-year-old actor, acknowledges that housing worries him "quite a lot", but warns: "I am also worried about public debt in the future, and what that means for pensions (...) I do not know whether it will still be sustainable."

## Immigration, centre stage in the year of regularisation and the Ceuta crisis

The arrival of foreigners in the country is another major concern for Spaniards ahead of this election. According to data (source in Spanish) from the National Statistics Institute (INE), on 1 January 2024 the population born abroad accounted for 18.2% of the total in Spain, while foreign residents made up 13.4% of the population.

"There are a lot of foreigners here," laments Garriga. "Unfortunately, everything has a capacity and, if that capacity is exceeded, you start having problems (...) So that is a problem, and it is a self-inflicted problem. It is like suicide."

The issue has been particularly prominent over recent months because of two specific episodes: the regularisation process that began in April and the mass entry recorded in Ceuta at the end of July.

The first of these was one of the government's main initiatives on migration. On 14 April, the Council of Ministers approved a decree to launch an extraordinary regularisation that was initially estimated to benefit around 500,000 people living in Spain in an irregular situation.

The measure involved granting residence and work permits, as well as access to Social Security and the healthcare system, provided that applicants met certain requirements, including proving five months of uninterrupted residence in the country and having no criminal record.

The procedure, which was open until 30 June, far exceeded the government's initial forecasts. By the deadline, more than 1.2 million applications had been submitted. The initiative triggered a heated political debate, with the government arguing that it was useful for tackling the shadow economy and facilitating labour market integration, while critics voiced doubts about whether public services could cope with the impact of the measure.

However, the biggest migration crisis of the year took place in Ceuta. On 30 July, more than 70,000 people entered the autonomous city irregularly from Morocco, in an unprecedented episode that overwhelmed reception resources and forced the authorities to reorganise support arrangements.

The crisis revived the debate on the Immigration Act, return procedures and the territorial distribution of migrants. Pressure on reception facilities continued in the weeks that followed and led to further complaints from residents and social organisations.

In addition, the mass entry ended up before the courts. The public prosecutor's office backed the National Court's investigation into the events, considering that they had taken place on Spanish territory and that there might be indications of offences linked to irregular immigration, bodily harm and organised crime. Management of the crisis thus became another of the issues that have shaped political confrontation in the months leading up to the election.

## **Corruption and polarisation, two issues shaping the political debate**

Alongside housing and immigration, corruption and growing political division have gained prominence on the public agenda. As many as nine judicial investigations directly affect former Sánchez aides, Socialist leaders and people in his family circle, and have kept the government under pressure for much of the year.

One of the main focal points has been the so-called Koldo case, linked to alleged irregularities in the awarding of public contracts during the pandemic. The proceedings have affected former transport minister José Luis Ábalos, his former adviser Koldo García and the PSOE's former organisation secretary Santos Cerdán. In June, the Supreme Court sentenced Ábalos and García over their role in the face-mask contracts scheme, while other lines of inquiry have continued.

This has been compounded by court proceedings involving Begoña Gómez, the prime minister's wife, and Sánchez's brother, David Sánchez, as well as investigations into an alleged structure to obstruct judicial investigations affecting people close to the government. The accumulation of cases has fuelled accusations of corruption from the opposition, while the prime minister has rejected any involvement and denounced what he calls 'lawfare', that is, a deliberately instrumental use of the courts.

This context has developed in parallel with an increasingly confrontational climate between the main parties. Polarisation, which the GAD3 barometer places among the five main public concerns, has become visible in episodes such as the clashes recorded during Catalonia's Diada and in the mutual accusations between the government and the opposition over **the handling of the country's main crises**.

"Something that also worries me a lot, and it has worried me ahead of elections for some time, is **the political polarisation we are experiencing in this country**, where political speeches are almost meaningless and there is more name-calling and insulting each other," laments José Félix, who, in addition to acting, is preparing for a police entrance exam. "I think that is also creating a very tense atmosphere among the public."

However, Aída Rodríguez, a researcher at the Ministry of Economy, plays down the issue, arguing that, despite a "general sense of discouragement", there is simply "more noise now" than before.

The 29N election campaign thus arrives framed by two competing narratives: that of the government, which defends its economic and social record, and that of the opposition parties, which focus their criticism on judicial scandals, immigration and the institutional situation. The distance between the two blocs has turned much of the political debate into a confrontation over the government's performance and the responsibility of its leaders.

In short, the election campaign is unfolding around a set of problems that go beyond confrontation between parties. Access to housing, the economic situation, immigration, corruption and polarisation are creating a backdrop in which citizens are demanding answers to very different questions, but ones that directly affect their quality of life and their trust in institutions.

The question now is whether these specific problems as perceived by voters will affect the election result. On this, as on many other issues, divisions of opinion among Spaniards are once again clear.

"Obviously I am not going to vote for a person or a party that is against the values I feel are mine: feminism, the environment and even the sharing of wealth," says Rodríguez. In contrast, José Félix opts for a more pragmatic approach: "I believe that when you vote, you have to vote with your head, each person as they see fit, but with your head; this is not like football."
