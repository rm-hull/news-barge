Carried out of her home on a stretcher, the eviction of 87-year-old María del Carmen Abascal brought millions of Spaniards onto the streets to protest unaffordable housing.

Waves of nationwide demonstrations — including a general strike earlier this month that saw more than three million people march across the country — have since paralysed the government, forcing prime minister Pedro Sánchez to call a snap election for 29 November.

With the public now just days away from casting their votes, deadlocked opinion polling have so far failed to predict a clear winner.

However, betting markets paint a more decisive picture, spiking concerns that a coalition government could rely on far-right leader Santiago Abascal’s Vox party to secure power, propelling the hard right into government for the first time since the end of the Franco dictatorship.

![Huge protests over the housing crisis erupted after Ms Abascal was evicted prior to her death](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/20/16/2304659191..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

## Where the Race Stands: Polling vs. Prediction Markets

According to recent POLITICO polling, the popular vote is a virtual dead heat, with the centre-right People’s Party (PP) and Sánchez’s Spanish Socialist Workers’ Party (PSOE) locked neck-and-neck at roughly 30 per cent each, while the far-right Vox sits comfortably in third place on around 18 per cent.

The results reveal a deeply disillusioned electorate driven less by enthusiasm for any single leader than by a desire to remove the incumbent — or tactically block the far right from entering government as a junior coalition partner.

On crypto-based prediction platform Polymarket, where traders have wagered millions of dollars on the outcome, PP leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo is priced at around an 88 to 93 per cent probability of becoming Spain's next prime minister, compared with roughly 7 to 11 per cent for Sánchez and just 1 per cent for Vox leader Santiago Abascal.

That gulf between polls and the betting markets’ lopsided odds comes down to Spain's parliamentary arithmetic — and makes clear why a right-wing coalition is heavily favoured to win power even though Abascal himself is not expected to take the top job.

Under Spain’s electoral system, a combined vote share of around 48 per cent for the PP and Vox would likely be enough to secure a working majority in the 350-seat Congress of Deputies — a scenario Polymarket traders price at an 86 per cent likelihood.

Meanwhile, Spain’s progressive camp enters Sunday fractured, with infighting preventing the left from replicating the unified alliance that kept Sánchez in power in 2023.

## What is Driving Voters?

The POLITICO poll — conducted by PF Global between 15 and 17 November finds an electorate deeply divided over the country’s biggest crises.

Across the board, roughly one in three Spaniards cited the cost of living as a deciding factor in how they will vote, with immigration, housing, healthcare and corruption following in that order.

Nearly a third of all respondents said that removing Sánchez from power is a primary motivation for their ballot, a sentiment shared by nearly 60 per cent of prospective PP and Vox voters.

Almost a third of voters overall said they will vote tactically simply to keep a rival party out of government.

When asked which party they trusted most to clean up politics, the most common response was "none".

## The Main Candidates and Their Platforms

**Pedro Sánchez (Spanish Socialist Workers’ Party – PSOE)**

![Sánchez has faced major hurdles over the past years](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/05/20/2297654287..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

As leader of the European Union’s last major Socialist-led government, the centre-left, pro-European prime minister is campaigning on defending social welfare, public healthcare, and progressive reforms while warning voters against a historic return of the far right to national office.

But after eight years in power, his pitch has been heavily overshadowed by economic inflation pressures, corruption probes targeting his family, and the parliamentary collapse of his housing relief package in October.

**Alberto Núñez Feijóo (People’s Party – PP)**

![Popular Party candidate Alberto Núñez Feijóo has spiked concerns over potential collaboration with far-right party, Vox](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/15/download..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Positioning himself as a steady, pragmatic centre-right conservative, Feijóo has promised to restore institutional stability, cut taxes, tighten border controls, and end the political polarisation of the Sánchez era.

While the former Galician regional president insists he is campaigning to govern alone, he faces persistent scrutiny over whether he will hand cabinet seats to Vox in order to secure the keys to La Moncloa.

**Santiago Abascal (Vox)**

![Santiago Abascal has built his campaign around aggressive anti-immigration pledges](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/15/2305834002..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Leading Spain’s hardline, ultranationalist far right, Abascal has built his campaign around aggressive anti-immigration pledges, including mass deportations, “remigration,” and “national priority” policies for Spanish citizens.

Rather than vying to become prime minister himself, Vox is aiming to use a strong third-place finish to act as kingmaker and force its way into a coalition government with the PP.

The former mayor of Barcelona stepped in to lead the newly rebranded progressive alliance — bringing together Sumar, United Left, Más Madrid, and Comunes — after infighting fractured the wider left.

Running to Sánchez’s left, Colau is campaigning on a “rental revolution” to curb Spain's housing emergency, alongside stronger protections for women and LGBTQ+ rights against a potential right-wing administration.
