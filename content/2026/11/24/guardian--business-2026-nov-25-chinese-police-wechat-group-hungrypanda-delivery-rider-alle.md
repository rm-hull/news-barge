Chinese police have contacted several people who were part of a WeChat group that discussed attending a Melbourne vigil for Feng Jianming, the HungryPanda food delivery rider allegedly murdered earlier this month.

At least three members of the group set up after Feng’s death said they were contacted, raising concerns that Chinese authorities may have been alerted to discussions about the protest activity either automatically or manually by the Chinese-owned messaging and social app.

Feng was allegedly stabbed to death near Queen Victoria market on the evening of Friday 6 November after he and another rider confronted a man who was allegedly tampering with their bikes.

Angry members of the WeChat group set up in the immediate aftermath volunteered to host a vigil for the 49-year-old rider at the State Library of Victoria the following day, amid broader concerns about the treatment of riders by the company.

The group’s chat history, seen by the Guardian, shows that by Wednesday at least three members had told the group their parents or themselves had been called by police officers from their home towns in China asking about their involvement with the vigil. The group, which by then had more than 400 members, was disbanded the following day after participants expressed fears about the police calls.

Members of the chat group declined to speak on the record, citing safety concerns.

HungryPanda, founded in the UK, targets the Chinese expatriate market in 10 countries. In Australia, it operates in nine cities, including Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane and Adelaide.

The Melbourne incident mirrored an event in February when HungryPanda riders in Sydney were contacted by police from China after a WeChat discussion about a proposed strike over wages on lunar new year’s eve.

More than half the WeChat group’s 155 members left and the strike was abandoned after the calls from police, which were first reported by the ABC.

Joy Wang, a former HungryPanda rider who was in the February group chat, said she received four police calls from her home town in Henan province.

Wang said the police seemed unaware the protest was in Sydney. When she explained this, they threatened her with reprisals if she went back to China, she said.

“They said, ‘You better not attend the protest, or we could go after you when you return to China’,” Wang said.

Jack\*, who quit HungryPanda two years ago and now runs his own business in eastern China, said he joined the February WeChat group to show support to his former colleagues. He received three calls from police in his home town and was told not to take part in the strike.

Jack then quit the group chat. “I’m currently in China, there are things I need to consider,” he said.

Kitty Lu, global spokeswoman for HungryPanda, said the company had no responsibility for riders being contacted by Chinese police. The company was privately owned and independently operated, with funding from private and institutional investors in Europe and North America, she said.

Wang said the group members in Australia who were called by police had registered with WeChat under their Chinese mobile numbers.

She believed the use of keywords such as “strike” in the chat may have been the trigger for the messages to be transmitted to the Chinese authorities.

Jeffrey Knockel, a senior research fellow at the University of Toronto’s Citizen Lab, agreed. He and his colleagues studied a 2016 wage protest in Jiangsu province and found the phrase “industrial strike across the city” in the organisers’ group chat activated an automated mechanism on WeChat.

He suggested something similar may have happened in the Australian cases. Knockel also argued that WeChat may have flagged the group chat to a Chinese government organisation that would have then traced the riders’ details on China’s family registration system, known as *hukou*, and instructed local police to contact them.

WeChat has previously stated that the data of foreign users is stored in Singapore, beyond the reach of Chinese authorities, but Knockel said that if one member of a group registered their account with a Chinese number, the whole chat could be monitored by Chinese authorities.

WeChat did not respond to a request for comment.

Kevin Slaten, who leads the China Dissent Monitor at the US-based thinktank Freedom House, also thought the February calls might have been triggered by WeChat, given a rise in the number of labour disputes over wages in China during 2025 and early 2026.

“[The Chinese authorities may have] just assumed that these workers were in this wave of wage protests … not knowing initially that they weren’t physically located in China,” Slaten said.

But as the security apparatus was under direction to pay special attention to wage protests, the police may have persisted in warning riders to halt their action, even after learning they were overseas, he said.

The executive director of China Labor Watch, Qiang Li, has documented several cases of Chinese workers abroad being contacted by Chinese authorities after using WeChat to discuss industrial action. However, these workers were usually working for projects by China’s state companies.

Li said Chinese police had “overreacted” in the HungryPanda case but it was still alarming for Australia.

“Today we see these riders being called by Chinese police,” he said. “We could then see cases of anyone who uses WeChat, including Australian enforcement officials, their families, or anyone related to China, to be surveilled through this system.”

The home affairs department said it could not comment on operational matters but a spokesperson said it was “unacceptable for any foreign government to target members of our community to prevent individuals exercising their fundamental rights and freedoms in Australia”.

“Acts of harassment, assault, unwanted surveillance, intimidation, coercion or other threatening behaviour may constitute criminal offences.”

The opposition home affairs spokesman, Jonathon Duniam, called on the government to do more to address the potential use of WeChat for surveillance.

“The Coalition has raised serious concerns about WeChat’s links to the Chinese Communist party, its censorship practices and its potential use for surveillance, yet the Albanese government has not done enough to address these risks or ensure proper transparency and accountability from the platform,” he said.

In 2023, a Senate inquiry into foreign interference recommended banning WeChat on government devices and establishing a national security technology office to monitor “high-risk” social media groups, including WeChat.

The government said only that it “noted” these recommendations.

The Chinese embassy in Canberra declined to comment on the HungryPanda allegations.
