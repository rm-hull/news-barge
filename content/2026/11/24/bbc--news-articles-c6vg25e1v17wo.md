'It breaks my heart that I've lost this opportunity,' Shahira Sadat says

**When Shahira Sadat received an invitation for a Chevening Scholarship interview, she felt a rare glimmer of hope.**

The software engineer in Kabul had spent three years battling Taliban restrictions, jumping through hoops and working through power and internet outages to prepare her applications for UK universities and grants.

"I was so happy. I can't describe it because I allowed myself to dream about studying in the UK," she says.

But in March this year, the UK's Home Office announced an "emergency visa brake" on study visas for applicants from Cameroon, Myanmar, Sudan and Afghanistan. Skilled work visas for Afghans were also paused. It said the move was a response to widespread visa abuse and had been triggered by a sharp rise in asylum claims from these visa routes.

At the time, Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood said she was "taking the unprecedented decision to refuse visas for those nationals seeking to exploit our generosity". The US barred all visas for Afghan nationals earlier in the year.

Sadat had three acceptance letters to study a masters in AI and machine learning from Sussex, Bradford and Northeastern University London. Now with a shot at getting funding from the UK government-funded Chevening scheme, she felt the education she'd been denied in Afghanistan was finally within reach.

Then came an email saying she would no longer be considered to study in the UK because she was from Afghanistan.

"It was really heartbreaking. I lost all my motivation. I cried for hours and hours," says Sadat, her eyes welling up with tears. "It was not just about education for me; it was the opportunity to resume my career and to build a future for myself."

In Afghanistan, diktats from the Taliban's supreme leader have severely restricted the lives of women. They cannot attend secondary schools or universities, and are barred from most forms of work.

In the western city of Herat, Alia - whose name we have changed - received a similar rejection to Sadat. Her offers to do a digital media masters in the UK were withdrawn, which she says left her feeling trapped.

"Everything is banned just because I am an Afghan woman," she says, her voice cracking with emotion.

Shahira Sadat says she "cried for hours and hours" after she was told she would no longer be considered for study opportunities in the UK

In a highly competitive field of international students vying to get UK scholarships, Afghan women have additional challenges to navigate.

Alia had to attend a secret school to prepare for an English language test, in defiance of Taliban restrictions. Before long the institute was discovered and authorities shut it down - so she continued with an online teacher from Canada.

The next challenge was actually sitting the test. Most authorised test centres would not admit female students, Sadat says, in fear of falling foul of the regime's rules.

After that came the difficulty of finding a UK university willing to recognise a degree earned in Afghanistan.

Throughout the process, both Sadat and Alia say frequent power cuts disrupted their preparations. They also feared a repeat of the nationwide internet shutdown imposed by the Taliban's supreme leader in September 2025.

Many students also find it difficult to physically apply for the visas, as they often have to travel to neighbouring countries which are making it harder for Afghan nationals to enter.

Having overcome the obstacles, Alia finds the visa brake deeply disappointing: "I want to say to the UK government: don't treat us like our government. They have deprived us of education, so the global community should have more focus on us."

In response to the BBC's questions about the suspensions, the UK's Home Office pointed to the reopening of the UK Resettlement Scheme for refugees, "including Afghan women and girls". It also said it was introducing "named sponsorship and refugee study routes", which would give refugees "a safe pathway to come to the UK and rebuild their lives".

However, the Home Office did not answer specific questions about whether these routes would be open to women in Afghanistan who are not classified as refugees, such as Sadat and Alia, or how many places would be available to Afghans.

Data from the Higher Education Statistics Agency shows that fewer than 400 of the more than 680,000 international students in the UK in 2024-25 were from Afghanistan.

Since this article was published, the Home Office has contacted the BBC to share data which shows that in the three years before the visa brake was applied the number of asylum claims from Afghans linked to study visa routes have outstripped the number of visas issued. From January to September 2025, there were 360 study visas issued and 470 asylum claims linked to these visas, while in 2024, 550 asylum claims came from 470 study visas that were issued.

We asked Zainab Rezaie, an Afghan student who moved to Birmingham in 2024 and later applied for asylum, why she made that choice.

Zainab Rezaie says that in Afghanistan "every move women make has to be strategised"

Rezaie, who is pursuing a PhD in education at the University of Exeter, says she was "very anxious" about what would happen after she graduated. She feared that if her visa expired before she could secure a job or switch to another visa, she would have no choice but to return to Taliban-run Afghanistan where - especially given her field of studies - she would not be able to work, and the possibilities of her life would be severely limited.

Rezaie says she applied for asylum to "give myself some opportunity to think".

"The biggest contrast is that I'm not in prison in my room. I have the right to move around every day, to go out, to exercise, to buy something. Living in Western society, you may never think that the freedom to go out at any time is a big advantage. But in Afghanistan, every move women make has to be strategised."

She says it was "never my choice" to come and live in the UK as an asylum seeker. "It takes a lot of mental effort to live here on your own and to be separated from your family, to adjust to society… Nobody chooses this happily, willingly."

There are still opportunities for Afghans to study abroad, in Europe, Asia and elsewhere, but the women we spoke to fear other countries will follow in the US and UK's footsteps.

They feel the international community is slowly closing its doors on them.
