Health service staff are being cautioned that improperly viewing patient files could cost them their jobs under updated training being introduced across the NHS.

The initiative forms part of an NHS England crackdown following a series of high-profile snooping cases.

NHS chief executive Sir Jim Mackey urged health leaders to ensure staff complete the training to "finally stamp out this shocking behaviour".

New material added to national data security modules explicitly outlines that accessing records without a clear reason can trigger disciplinary action, including dismissal.

The training update follows guidance issued across the health service outlining the expectations of handling suspected unlawful access.

According to NHS England, the approach helped identify potential snooping by two ambulance staff following the death of William Chapman, who was mauled by a tiger at Yorkshire Wildlife Park.

It comes as figures obtained by the *Press Association* showed more than 3,000 instances of health workers inappropriately accessing records over the past five years.

Significant privacy breaches include dozens of employees looking at the medical files of victims involved in attacks across Southport and Nottingham.

Patient advocates warned that such breaches "erode trust" in affected organisations and the broader NHS, adding that a "lack of transparency" deepens the overall damage.

Freedom of Information requests submitted by *PA t*o 143 acute hospital and ambulance trusts in England showed the scale of staff snooping.

Some 79 trusts provided precise figures for incidents of staff inappropriately accessing patient records between 2021/22 and 2025/26 inclusive, which together totalled 3,029.

The actual number of incidents will be higher as other trusts declined to provide exact data and instead said there had been “fewer than five” in a particular year, to avoid a risk of individuals being identified.

The records of a toddler hurt after falling into a crocodile pit in Cambridgeshire in June were also viewed by around 40 hospital staff, while 10 staff at were suspended or removed from duty after a possible data breach of the medical records of three-year-old Noah Woods, who was found dead in a lake a day after going missing in Brantham, Suffolk.

Earlier this month, the Information Commissioner’s Office revealed it has received more than 300 reports this year of staff looking at records with no good reason, inspecting records of people they know or sharing information with others.

Sir Jim said: “We have zero-tolerance for any member of NHS staff who accesses private information in patient records that they have no business seeing – and we’re rolling out new content in our national training programme to make that crystal clear.

“The updated training is just one of the actions we’re putting in place to take a tougher stance on this issue, alongside the expectation that any member of staff being investigated for snooping should face immediate suspension.

“All NHS leaders should now ensure the training is completed so staff are in no doubt that they face dismissal if they inappropriately access patient records, and we can finally stamp out this shocking behaviour.”

![Three-year-old Noah Woods was found dead in a lake following a huge search](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/27/12/bf6ba4edc2bded159d394b0e3e9eec82Y29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzkwNTkzOTcw-2.85968960.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Paul Arnold, ICO chief executive, said: “Patients must be able to trust that their medical records will only ever be accessed by staff with a legitimate reason to see them.

“Recent incidents have shown that the culture and behaviours needed to protect that trust have not been good enough, and that needs to change.

“I know how much distress this type of behaviour causes patients and families.

“It is vital the health sector takes action and today’s announcement is a positive step.”

Matthew Hopkins, acute and ambulance network director at the NHS Alliance, said: “NHS staff work hard every day to protect confidential patient information and any misuse of records is a serious breach of trust.

“Strong data governance is essential as digital technology becomes increasingly central to patient care. Any requirements around access controls, audit trails and monitoring must be achievable within current systems.

“Maintaining public confidence in the use of patient data is crucial.”
