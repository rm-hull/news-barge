## The reform plans come as Brussels tries to preserve multilateral climate diplomacy while relying on smaller coalitions of countries willing to move ahead on key issues like the phasing out of fossil fuels.

The European Commission is drawing up plans to overhaul the UN climate summit process, warning that the current negotiating system risks becoming ineffective because a single country can block an agreement among nearly 200 parties.

The push follows the chaotic COP31 climate conference, which ended without consensus on a political declaration reaffirming the commitment to transition away from fossil fuels agreed at COP28 in Dubai.

Brussels says the failure has exposed deeper flaws in the process, from a lack of transparency and cumbersome decision-making to the inability of the annual climate summits to translate existing commitments into action.

“If we want to save the COP, it needs to reform itself,” a senior Commission official said on Tuesday during a briefing following the negotiations.

The Commission will draw up its reform proposals in consultation with EU member states.

Brussels wants to ensure that work outside the formal negotiations – through coalitions of willing countries – continues to advance the climate transition that governments have so far struggled to deliver through consensus.

Despite the COP31 fiasco, several EU countries, like Finland, Denmark, Portugal and Spain, have unveiled their own roadmaps to transition away from fossil fuels.

## Overhauling the COP system

The EU says there needs to be more transparency about how negotiations are conducted, when proposals are introduced and why positions change.

"The minister from Ghana objected to the very significant lack of transparency and democratic process in the COP. So we are far from alone in saying this. Preparing for the next COP means that we have to immediately draw the lessons from what happened," the senior Commission official added.

The lack of visibility over the decision-making process also became a source of frustration during COP31, with Brussels saying its plans will try to overcome negotiating deadlocks.

Under the current consensus-based system, a single country can prevent an agreement from being adopted, even when a large majority of parties support it.

Brussels wants to explore ways to address this problem while preserving the democratic legitimacy of the multilateral process.

The Commission is also concerned that countries are repeatedly reopening political agreements instead of delivering on them, particularly the COP28 commitment to transition away from fossil fuels, which was later endorsed by the UN summit in Santa Marta, Colombia.

The Commission says the EU and a coalition of more than 130 countries sought to reaffirm the Dubai agreement, including its commitments to triple renewable energy capacity, double energy efficiency improvements and transition away from fossil fuels.

But negotiations broke down over resistance from a group led by Saudi Arabia, which Brussels accused of seeking to replace the fossil fuel transition language with a greater emphasis on carbon capture and storage.

The proposed text included a target for carbon capture on a gigaton scale by 2035, which the EU considered "unacceptable" because it risked allowing continued fossil fuel use.

"That is simply impossible to achieve and impossible to accept, because this would have given a lifeline to the continuation of fossil energy in the energy system," the senior Commission official argued.

The Commission estimates that between 80 and 90 countries objected to the final proposed political declaration, including all 27 EU member states, the UK, Norway and Switzerland, as well as more than 40 least developed countries.

Japan, Canada and Egypt also supported the objections politically, according to the EU's assessment.

## Brussels rejects COP31's total failure

Brussels insisted the failure to agree on the declaration does not mean the entire summit was unsuccessful.

The senior Commission official cited decisions on other parts of the climate agenda, including carbon markets and the $103 billion pledged by development banks for climate finance, as positive steps forward.

However, the breakdown has intensified questions about whether the UN process can maintain political momentum on climate action when countries remain deeply divided over the future of fossil fuels.

The Commission also wants to streamline the expanding range of issues discussed at climate summits.

An increasing number of working groups and parallel initiatives are diverting attention from the core task of reducing emissions and implementing the Paris Agreement, the senior Commission official said.

Trade measures have consumed significant negotiating energy despite not being central to the original purpose of the COP process, the Commission noted.

Nonetheless, budget pressures are likely to fuel the debate over whether the COP still makes sense, with the United States' withdrawal expected to have financial implications for the organisation and its operations.
