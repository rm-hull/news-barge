Running a small business on the side of being employed can be a great way to increase your income and broaden your professional horizons.

UK law has nothing in it that specifically rules out having your own business while also being employed elsewhere.

Individual employment contracts are a very different matter though, and could mean you land in hot water if you start a business on the side.

While people may not always read the small print in their contracts, it is very important to do so if you have any plans to start a separate company. Your boss may well have an issue with you running a side business.

## Contractual obligations

Clearly, there are practical limitations to running a small business while a full-time employee.

First and foremost are the simple matters of time and energy. Both of these are finite and in most full-time jobs, you will need to dedicate a large proportion of both to the role.

Beyond a moral obligation to do your job well and not short-change people, there are likely to be contractual obligations that you have put your signature to before starting in the job.

Every employment contract is different, but a lot of them have some similar language about this matter.

The exact wording will vary but is likely to be along the lines of: “you must devote all your working time and attention to your duties within the role.”

This is more likely to appear in full-time employment contract than part time, as by definition a part-time job does not require all your effort for each week.

Other possible elements include wording on never competing with the company by offering similar goods or services.

![Avoiding conflicts of interest is likely a foundational issue with having a second job](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/12/57/iStock-1498980146-(2).jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The phrase “avoid any conflicts of interest” may feature, which means engaging in any action which could lead to you favouring your own side business to the detriment of the company employing you.

If these kind of statements are in your contract, it does not always rule out having a side business, as it may be possible to get permission, at your employers’ discretion.

If you can present a clear argument why the side business you have in mind does not diminish the quality and quantity of work you do in you main job, does not compete and will be handled entirely in you own time, you may get the answer you want.

## Intellectual property

This is where it gets very dangerous and perhaps unwise to proceed with.

Employment contracts will often specify that the commercially sensitive things you have knowledge of as a result of working somewhere, such as how a company makes its products for example, cannot be used by you in any other capacity.

Breaches in this area are taken very seriously, so it could be dangerous to try and go down this path.

## Running into trouble

Going ahead with a small business against the terms of your employment contract, without declaring it and receiving permission would be called a breach of contract.

There are a range of actions your boss can take in response which vary from company to company, but it is not likely to go well for you.

There are formal and informal disciplinary measures, which range from warnings to outright dismissal.

There is even a risk of being sued by an employer for breaching terms of employment contracts, in extreme cases where the company feels it has been harmed by your actions. Inappropriate use of intellectual property is the obvious example.

## What about tax?

There are no tax rules set by HMRC that rule out running a small business while also employed by a company or the public sector.

The crucial thing to be aware of is that you must register for HMRC self-assessment tax if you have a side business.

Being on PAYE tax with your employer does nothing for you in terms of tax obligations stemming from any income your side business generates.

Depending on the size and specific nature of your business you may also have the option to set up a private limited company and manage the tax through that, or have an accountant do it for you.
