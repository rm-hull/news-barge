A federal appeals court has granted permission for the U.S. Army to detonate tons of degraded explosive materials at an ammunition plant in Iowa.

The order from the 8th Circuit U.S. Court of Appeals was one of two rulings President Donald Trump indicated he was awaiting after directing the military on Monday to pause the chemical disposal pending judicial review.

The president noted that his determination remains contingent upon agreement from incoming Gov.-elect Rob Sand, warning that federal authorities would otherwise be forced to proceed with the planned detonation.

Initial plans to destroy 68 barrels of lead azide were set for last Friday, but the Army postponed the operation three times following significant public concern regarding potential health and environmental hazards.

The revised plan aimed for a Wednesday detonation ahead of Thanksgiving to take advantage of favorable weather.

President Donald Trump announced on Monday that his administration would defer to a federal appeals court prior to moving ahead with the disposal of the degraded bomb-making material at the Iowa facility.

The scheduled neutralization of the 68 lead azide drums has been delayed three times after sharp public apprehension over environmental and health risks.

![A sign at the US Army Iowa Army Ammunition Plant](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/19/2026-11-23T190558Z_2085947987_RC2TTOAIMFFJ_RTRMADP_3_IOWA-ARMY-DETONATION.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

While military personnel sought a Wednesday timeline due to weather conditions, the Army did not immediately comment on the president's statement.

"At the same time, have ordered the military not to do so until such time as the 8th Circuit agrees that we can go forward, and am further making this decision fully subject to the incoming Gov.-elect Rob Sand agreeing to the operation," Trump said.

He warned that if consent is withheld, the administration will halt the procedure, holding Sand responsible for "any and all death or destruction caused by this very unfortunate, and longterm, situation."

Sand responded in a social media post, writing that past administrative choices meant "No good options exist and the situation is more dangerous and unpredictable than it ever should have been."

He raised questions over why a contract intended to eliminate the hazard was canceled and "why we are rushing to demolition now in late November."

![Residents taped up windows in hopes of stopping them from shattering during the explosion](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/20/2306414930..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The delay follows a legal challenge from environmental groups, who secured a stay from the 8th Circuit Court of Appeals to review an emergency permit granted by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency.

Trump indicated a preference for quick disposal via a controlled explosion, describing it as the safer option.

However, he confirmed that the operation remains paused pending judicial and state clearance.

Sand stressed, "The safety of Iowans should be the top priority of everyone involved," adding that "All relevant federal, state and local government resources should be focused on protecting everyone that could be impacted."

Near the Middletown facility, some residents have taken preventive steps by sealing windows and doors against potential lead dust, while others expressed worry online about wind carrying potential plumes.

Officials acknowledged the toxicity of lead, advising residents to stay indoors during any explosion and to wash down outdoor surfaces with water.

In court documents, Army Col. Aaron Teller, a munitions emergency specialist, explained that reduced liquid inside the barrels leaves the substance susceptible to freezing, raising the sensitivity of crystals near the lids.

![Incoming Gov. Rob Sand](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/19/download..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

"Even a small amount of friction can break the crystal formation, causing an explosive chain reaction," he stated.

The barrels were initially discovered in March.

Although EPA records show chemical neutralization options were evaluated, officials determined that moving the drums posed a severe risk of an accidental blast. Environmentalists contend that alternative methods were overlooked, while officials warn that the danger of an explosion grows as temperatures fall below 30 degrees Fahrenheit (-1 Celsius).
