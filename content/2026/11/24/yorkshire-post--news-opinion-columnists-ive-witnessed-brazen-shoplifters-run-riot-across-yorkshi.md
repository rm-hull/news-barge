# I've witnessed brazen shoplifters run riot across Yorkshire in run-up to Christmas: Andrew Vine

## TIS the season to be jolly – if you happen to be a shoplifter in Yorkshire, that is. They appear to be having a field day as the shops become busier in the run-up to Christmas.

Out and about in Leeds, York and Harrogate over the past week, I’ve seen the menace of shoplifting up close, and felt desperately sorry for business owners and their staff whose livelihoods are undermined by it.

I’ve watched security guards chasing thieves out of shops, stopping them to recover stolen goods, getting a mouthful of abuse in return, and then seen these criminals simply walk away, no doubt to go into the next shop they like the look of and start stuffing their pockets and bags.

The chances are that most of them won’t face any criminal action. The shops don’t bother reporting the thefts to the police, because they know officers are under such pressure a response will be very slow in coming and detaining a thief for any length of time risks staff being assaulted.

The consequence is a crime wave unfolding in plain sight on the streets of virtually every Yorkshire town and city.

And it’s certain that for every thief the shop staff or guards spot, there will be a dozen more who get away with stealing whatever can be sold on, probably to buy drugs, taking advantage of the Christmas crowds to avoid detection.

Their sheer brazenness is breathtaking. In Leeds, a man stood outside the city centre branch of Boots stopping passers-by and trying to sell them cosmetics which he had almost certainly just stolen from the shop.

In York, there were two men standing outside M&S looking through a bagful of clothing which I’d bet they had grabbed from the displays inside.

Neither they, nor any of the people I saw being stopped showed the slightest trace of being bothered about being seen or apprehended. For them, it was just another day at work – the occupation in question being professional thieves.

Go into almost any supermarket and it’s the same – bags being filled by people who walk out without paying as if they haven’t a care in the world, some of them spotted and chased by guards, but others getting away with it.

It’s bad enough seeing the big stores plagued by this epidemic of theft, but what can it be like for the independent shops which are so important to Yorkshire’s retail scene?

They operate on much tighter profit margins than the big players, carry far less stock, may have only a handful of staff and can’t afford a permanent security presence on the door. Every theft from these shops must really hurt and damage their chances of making a living.

The scale of shoplifting is mind-boggling. According to the British Retail Consortium, there are 5.5m thefts from shops every year, which is costing them a staggering £2.2bn.

These losses are the last thing retailing needs. We all recognise, and regret, the decline our high streets have suffered in recent years as shops shut in the face of online competition.

At a time when they are also paying higher business rates, increased staff costs and rising energy prices, thefts are placing them under intolerable financial pressure.

Yet far too little effort has been put into tackling retail crime, even as the industry warned it was out of control. Hopefully, that is starting to change.

Last week, the Home Secretary announced that 500 of the country’s most prolific high-street thieves are being targeted by a crackdown that will see them tagged, banned from high streets and hauled before the courts for hearings to monitor their movements.

About time, too. Why has it taken so long for the government to act against what is probably the country’s most widespread crime? And is it going to provide the police with the resources to get officers out onto the streets to identify banned thieves and detain them?

It has been clear for several years that a hard-core of repeat offenders has been driving the shoplifting epidemic. Earlier this year, a survey of police forces found that just 270 criminals were responsible for 28,000 thefts.

Let’s not forget that behind those shocking statistics lie countless stories of shop staff being abused, threatened or even assaulted by thieves who are challenged.

Without arresting these people when they are identified by staff, and getting them off the streets, it is difficult to see how the crackdown will make much difference. Plainly, the scale of their repeat offending means they are taking no notice of sanctions short of being imprisoned.

Tough talk from ministers needs to backed up by greater support for the police, enabling officers to respond quickly and make arrests. Only that will enable shops and their staff to go about serving customers without being preyed on by criminals.
