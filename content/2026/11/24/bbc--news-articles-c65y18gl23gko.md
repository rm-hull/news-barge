**The first ever Children's Booker Prize has announced the eight books in the running for the new £50,000 award.**

Five novels, two graphic novels and an illustrated collection of short stories are on the shortlist, which is aimed at children aged eight to 12.

The authors in contention include former Children's Laureate of Ireland Eoin Colfer, who is best known for his Artemis Fowl series, and teacher and children's author Catherine Bruton.

Four children have also been announced as new judges after a UK-wide competition. Twelve-year-old Ibrahim from Hampshire was one of those picked from more than 4,000 entrants.

![Ibrahim smiling in a blue shirt with book shelves in the background](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/803f/live/34232a90-e7db-11f1-a094-5fdc692f3e33.jpg)

Ibrahim reads for an hour a day

He told me he was called back into school for a phone call with chair of judges Frank Cottrell-Boyce, who broke the news. Ibrahim said he couldn't be happier.

"First Children's Booker Prize judge! On my CV - that's going to take me a long way… I'm too overwhelmed to even be excited."

Cottrell-Boyce told the BBC "it's really, really important that this is a prize for children's books. Having judges who are children will be proof of that… If the children don't love a book, it won't win."

Ibrahim, his fellow child judges and Cottrell-Boyce will be joined on the judging panel by Ghosts and Horrible Histories actor Lolly Adefope and Sanchita Basu De Sarkar, who owns an award-winning children's book shop in north London.

Ibrahim said he loves reading "because it's just a way to unwind yourself from the outside world. If you get stuck in a book, you're not getting your face out of there until you finish it".

He's far too professional to share which books on the list he likes best, but he told me when he was reading one of them recently: "I had to tell my mum to confiscate it because I couldn't stop reading it and I was getting a headache."

Cottrell-Boyce said he isn't concerned over whether the adult judges might dominate the decision-making.

"If - like me - you've been visiting schools as a children's writer for 20 years, it's a rare thing to meet a child who is too over-awed to express an opinion. They're usually very forthright".

Ibrahim proves that point. He told me "the adults are not going to control that meeting… 100% not!"

He's looking for "something that has different perspectives, you can see it in a different way. New characters, not just the same old sloppy characters that authors make all the time. They just make these books that just have the same plot line".

## Which books made the grade?

The shortlisted books are by authors and illustrators from the UK, Ireland, Australia and Italy. Cottrell-Boyce described the list as "full of surprises." Here it is:

* The Worst Thing That Could Possibly Happen by Guy Bass, illustrated by David Roberts. Readers on Goodreads describe it as "super creepy" and "how horror for kids should be written."
* War by Eoin Colfer and Andrew Donkin, illustrated by Giovanni Rigano. It's the third book in their graphic novel series and tells the story of 14-year old Kat, living under siege in an unnamed European city.
* The Night I Borrowed Time by Iqbal Hussain - the British-Pakistani author and former journalist's debut Children's book weaves a story through the eyes of Zubair, a seventh son who has inherited the power of time travel and tries to fix the lives of his family.
* The Rambler's Curse by Mikey Please - the fantasy-comedy by the Aardman animator, who has also illustrated the book, has been described by The Guardian as a "laugh-out-loud, shaggy-dog spiral of purposes mistook".
* The Teller of Tall Tales by Liz Hyder, illustrated by Tom de Freston, was a critic's choice in The Daily Telegraph. It's about a grandmother living in a remote cabin who weaves mysterious stories for her estranged grandson.
* Tadpole Summer by Catherine Bruton is about a young girl who seeks solace in nature when her brother's serious illness worsens.
* Séamus Almost Famous by Jessica Scott-Whyte - this is the Irish author's second book and is illustrated by Becka Moor. Poor Séamus is on a mission to emerge from the shadows of his family - even his baby sister is an accidental internet sensation.
* Hometown by Australian Shaun Tan tells the story of a girl living on a distant planet who is one day told by a visitor in a spacesuit that she is actually from somewhere very far away. A review in The Observer called it "perfect fuel for a voracious imagination".

![Frank Cottrell-Boyce, wearing a white shirt, reads one of his books on stage](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/e72f/live/d8b350b0-e756-11f1-b407-33329b1ec67a.jpg)

Cottrell-Boyce told the BBC the range of subjects was "amazing".

"Children's books can be about anything, can deal with anything. But the tone has to convey hope. I can't think of a darker childhood than the one endured by Anne of Green Gables. I also can't think of a more joyous book than Anne of Green Gables."

He acknowledges the worrying statistics on reading. The National Literacy Trust's Annual Literacy Survey last year showed the percentage of children and young people who said they enjoyed reading was its lowest in 20 years. Just one in three aged eight to 18 said they enjoyed reading in their free time, down by more than a third since 2005.

"That's why we're here! To fight the fight. To make sure our children have as many opportunities as possible to discover how pleasurable, how bonding, how thrilling and enriching and fortifying it can be to slip out of the prison of the present into the infinity of the story."

Ibrahim told me "my friends don't read books at all".

He doesn't try to persuade them. "I let them live their life. I live my life".

But if he were to try to, what would he say?

"Put a timer on your phone, make sure it's blocked and then they'll decide to do something more productive. I might be able to convince them... because it can help you with English, your confidence, and help you build self esteem."

For him, though, the best thing about a book is when it takes him into another world. "If you try to take your head out of it, it makes you think 'oh my god what's going to happen next?'"

The winning book will be announced in February next year at the Young V&A in London. 30,000 copies of the shortlisted books will be given to children who need them most.
