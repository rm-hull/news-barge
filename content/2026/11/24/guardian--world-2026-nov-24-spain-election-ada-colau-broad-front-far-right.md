The leader of Spain’s leftwing Broad Front coalition has called for a drastic rethink of the country’s entire housing rental system, and described the eviction of an 87-year-old woman – the spark for nationwide protests that in turn triggered Sunday’s snap general election – as “the last straw” for desperate tenants.

“We’re not interested in small changes,” Ada Colau, the former Barcelona mayor, told the Guardian in an interview. “We want to reform the entire rental sector.”

Public outrage over the eviction of Maricarmen Abascal from her Madrid apartment in September prompted the government, led by Pedro Sánchez’s Spanish Socialist Workers’ party (PSOE), to propose a series of changes to Spain’s housing laws. After the proposals were voted down by a combination of rightwing parties and Catalan nationalists, Sánchez called an election.

“When Maricarmen, an 87-year-old woman who had paid rent all her life, was evicted out of pure greed, that lit the fuse,” said Colau, who stepped out of the political limelight three years ago after failing to win a third term as Barcelona mayor.

The Broad Front alliance was founded in February to unite parties to the left of the PSOE, and named Colau as its candidate for prime minister on 12 October. “When I saw how many people were taking to the streets that’s when I agreed to be a candidate,” she said. “I thought, if I can be useful now, I have to be involved 100%.”

Polls have indicated that the conservative People’s party (PP) will win the most seats in Sunday’s election, but will fall short of a parliamentary majority, meaning the far-right Vox party could become kingmakers. A coalition deal between the PP and Vox would signal the return of the far right to government in Spain for the first time in the country’s post-dictatorship democratic history.

Colau’s alliance does not include the leftist group Podemos or the Catalan Republican Left (ERC) party. “I said to both Podemos and ERC we could change the front’s name, I offered to stand aside in favour of a consensus candidate, but the parties decided on a different strategy and I have to respect their decision,” Colau said.

Colau said she was sceptical of polls pointing to a PP-Vox majority. “I don’t think the majority of people want to go back to pre-democratic times,” she said. “People in the street don’t share this fatalism that the political right is going to win.

“People are very motivated, they’re very conscious that this isn’t an election like any other. This is the first time since the dictatorship that the far right might be in government and we might see the return of censorship, that we might see something like ICE in Spain, as well as attacks on groups such as the LGBT community and women.”

Polls suggest that a broad leftwing coalition encompassing the PSOE and all the smaller parties to the left of it won’t get enough votes to secure a parliamentary majority, but Colau is undeterred. She pointed out that at the general election in 2023 it was predicted that the right and far right would win, only for voters to return a progressive government, “admittedly with a very mixed and rather fragile parliamentary majority”.

She added: “At the moment we’re at about 8% and our goal is 10%. We believe people want to vote not just to keep out the far right, they want to vote for social progress.”

Colau has been a housing activist for over 20 years, and says there is little to choose between the PP and PSOE on the issue, blaming both for the mortgage bubble that led to mass evictions after the 2008 financial crash and then allowing the current rental crisis to unfold.

“We’re not interested in small changes,” she said. “We want to reform the entire rental sector in line with something similar to Germany and, above all, get rid of the vulture funds that are destroying our neighbourhoods and driving us out of our cities. Vulture funds contribute nothing, everything they do is harmful.”

She said that 13 funds controlled about 100,000 properties, and that the Broad Front’s support for a Socialist-led government would depend on it agreeing to expropriate these properties “at a fair but not a market price”.

She said that when she was mayor she managed to increase the social housing stock by assuming control of more than 1,600 homes that were in the hands of big property owners. “If we could do that in Barcelona with fewer powers than the government, obviously it can be done at state level,” she said, adding that the constitution stated that housing was not only a right but that it must have a social function. “As for regulating rents, it isn’t such a radical proposal, it’s something that’s practised in various European countries.”

As mayor, Colau won international plaudits for her efforts to prioritise people over cars, but at home she was equally loved and loathed. During her time in office she came under constant attack from the pro-car lobby and faced over a dozen “lawfare” cases, all of which were rejected by the courts. Much of the hostility directed towards her was misogynist. “I’ve always said I’d only return to politics if I think I can be particularly useful, if not, I’ll do something else, because there are many ways to be political, not just in institutions,” she said. “If I can make a contribution now, I will do whatever I can to stop fascism returning to this country.”
