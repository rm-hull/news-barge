People who carry extra weight in midlife are significantly more likely to suffer accelerated memory loss and cognitive decline, according to major new research – but managing body weight can slow the damage within just two years.

In a study which tracked more than 8,200 participants over 24 years, scientists at the University of Georgia have found a link between rising body mass index (BMI) and faster deterioration in memory, concentration and decision-making.

While a gradual dip in mental sharpness is a normal part of growing older, gradual increases in BMI appear to speed up the ageing of the brain.

Unlike subtle age-related changes in healthy adults, the study found each unit rise in BMI accelerated a decline in executive functioning – the mental skills used to plan tasks, focus attention and regulate emotions.

![Researchers have found that higher BMI over time is linked to faster cognitive decline in older adults](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/07/09/09/06160845-d2ea4433-b304-4174-a014-1648b482d752.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The strongest correlation between weight gain and worsening brain health emerged eight years into the study, with adults aged 65 and older experiencing the most pronounced negative effects.

While previously understood as a risk factor for heart disease and type 2 diabetes, obesity – clinically defined as a BMI of 30 or above – is increasingly recognised as a key driver of neurological decline.

The findings, published in the *Journal of Neurology*, suggest controlling body weight could offer a practical way to protect the ageing brain, according to Dr Suhang Song, lead author of the study and an assistant professor at the University of Georgia’s College of Public Health.

![Managing body weight can slow the rate of cognitive decline in as little as two years](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/07/27/18/42/iStock-2228011085.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

During the study, researchers found people who managed their weight significantly lowered their rate of cognitive decline in just two years.

Together, the work concludes that the more a person’s BMI rises in mid-to-later life, the faster their cognitive function deteriorates – making weight one of the most accessible targets for intervention.

“We found that if people managed their weight, they could significantly lower their rate of cognitive decline in just two years,” explained Dr Song. “That makes BMI one of the most easily modifiable risk factors when it comes to ageing well.”

The biological mechanisms through which excess weight accelerates brain decline remain under investigation. Researchers note that carrying extra fat likely triggers chronic inflammation, insulin resistance and reduced blood flow to the brain – processes linked to mild cognitive impairment, Alzheimer’s disease and other forms of dementia.

Because the research was observational, the authors note that it establishes a strong association rather than direct proof that weight gain alone causes cognitive decline – though the findings add to growing evidence that metabolic health directly shapes brain ageing.

Addressing those risks remains a major public health challenge. Roughly one in three adults in the UK are classified as obese, according to the NHS.
