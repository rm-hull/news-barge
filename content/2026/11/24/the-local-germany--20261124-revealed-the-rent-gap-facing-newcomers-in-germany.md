Have you recently moved into a flat in Germany and wondered how much more you're paying than the people who've lived there for years?

A new study from Haus & Grund, the national association of property owners and landlords – with data crunched by the research firm empirica regio – gives a rough answer.

It can't tell you what your neighbour pays. But across all 400 districts and cities, it suggests Germany has two rental markets. One is stable and often affordable. The other is much tougher – and almost everyone who has just arrived is likely to end up in it.

**How big is the gap?**

The study measures rent as a share of take-home household income, excluding utilities.

Nationally, the study found that someone living alone in an existing tenancy spends 20.3 percent of their income on rent, almost exactly the same proportion they spent in 2016 (20.4 percent). A family spends 14.5 percent, up from 14.3 percent.

But move into a flat advertised online and the picture changes. Now, a single person looking for a new place would need to spend 27.6 percent of their income on rent, compared with 20.3 percent for someone on an existing lease.

For a family, it's 19.7 percent compared with 14.5 percent.

And while the existing-lease figures have barely moved since 2016, the new-contract figure for single people has crept up by about 2.2 percentage points over the same period.

The picture in Germany’s big cities is considerably starker.

In Berlin, a single person on an existing lease pays 23.3 percent of income, but 40.8 percent on a new one. In Munich it goes from 27.9 percent to 42.7 percent. Families in Berlin go from 18.5 percent to 31.3 percent.

Overall, the study ranks Offenbach am Main, next to Frankfurt, as the least affordable place in Germany, with single people paying 30.5 percent on existing leases and 42.2 percent on new ones.

**Where it's cheaper**

At the other end of the ranking, Höxter in North Rhine-Westphalia has existing-lease burdens of 13.6 percent for single people and 9.6 percent for families, with new contracts at 17.9 percent for single people.

Wunsiedel in Bavaria, Altmarkkreis Salzwedel in Saxony-Anhalt and the Erzgebirgskreis in Saxony are also near the most affordable end.

Haus & Grund is Germany's national association of property owners and landlords. In other words, it lobbies for the people on the other side of your rental contract. That doesn't make its figures wrong, but it does shape the message that average rents in Germany are affordable.

The percentages come from a simple sum. The research firm empirica regio worked out typical rents and typical take-home household incomes for each of Germany’s 400 districts and cities.

The rent is the basic rent without utilities (*Kaltmiete*), for a 50 m² flat for a single person or a 90 m² flat for a family.

"Existing contract" figures reflect what current tenants pay. "New contract" figures are based on flats advertised online.

Divide the rent by the typical income for that kind of household in the area and you get the percentage.

**The bill on top of the rent**

Rent isn't the whole story in Germany when it comes to living costs.

The study also suggests that consumer prices for net cold rent rose by 18.4 percent between 2015 and 2025, while housing-related services, such as the extras that appear on your annual bill, rose by 26.4 percent.
