Canada is a “sovereign nation” that does not “report” to the United States, the country’s defense minister said after the Pentagon released a scathing memo that criticized the supposedly “atrophied” defence capabilities of its northern neighbour.

The document, released on Tuesday, said Canada had “yet to make the hard decisions and tradeoffs” necessary to meet previous defence spending pledges and become a “credible partner in the defense of our shared continent”.

The Pentagon memo said it took Canada nearly two decades to finally reach spending promises made in 2006 “despite Canada’s role as a major G7 economy as well as one of the largest and wealthiest members of the NATO alliance”.

Historically, Nato members have fallen short of meeting the security organization’s funding goals. When Russia annexed Crimea in 2014, only three Nato members – the US, the UK and Greece – were meeting the alliance’s guideline of spending 2% of GDP on defence. By 2025, all 31 members with armed forces, including Canada, had reached the goal.

The document also suggested Canada faces significant operational hurdles in its air, land and sea operations, blaming political leaders for pursuing “parochial economic and commercial interests over military optimization”.

The Pentagon document said the US defense secretary, Pete Hegseth, was “laser focused” on modernizing Canada’s military, suggesting American taxpayers had been “footing the bill for its defense” for decades.

The defense minister, David McGuinty, told reporters he could not “divine” the thinking behind the Pentagon’s decision to criticise Canada’s defence spending now.

“The amount of cooperation we are pushing under Norad [North American Aerospace Defense Command] has not changed. What we’re doing together at Nato has not changed. Our military-to-military cooperation has not changed. Our work on integrated air missile defence has not changed,” he said.

McGuinty also bristled at the suggestion from a reporter that the memo implied Canada required approval from the US when it came to its spending plans.

“Canada is a sovereign nation. I’ll be very direct. We don’t report to the United States,” he said.

The memo came as a Canadian official travelled to Europe to pitch a new defence bank championed by the prime minister, Mark Carney.

Isabelle Hudon, Canada’s lead negotiator for establishing the Defence, Security and Resilience Bank, is looking to cajole more countries into joining a global initiative that would lend to governments and contractors at low cost to support defense projects.

Ten countries have pledged their support but no others from the G7 have done so yet, with both the UK and Germany so far declining to join. Hudon says the €4bn in commitments secured so far is enough to start the bank up but she is aiming for up to €100bn.

Carney has made significantly increased spending commitments in defence. The country spent $63.4bn on national defence in 2025, meeting its Nato pledge to spend 2% of gross domestic product for the first time.

Last year, Nato members agreed to raise the goal to the equivalent of 5% of GDP by 2035.

Canada’s federal budget this month laid out a plan to spend about 4% of Canada’s GDP on defence and security annually by the end of the decade, and 5% by 2035, including the purchase of submarines and early warning aircraft.

Canada’s air power is a key point of friction with Washington. Carney’s government has yet to make a final decision on its order of F-35 fighter jets from the US firm Lockheed Martin and is weighing the possibility of purchasing Swedish-made Gripen aircraft instead. The multibillion-dollar purchase has been the subject of a political review for more than a year. The Pentagon memo warned that operating a “mixed fleet” of aircraft would “compound existing personnel shortfalls”.
