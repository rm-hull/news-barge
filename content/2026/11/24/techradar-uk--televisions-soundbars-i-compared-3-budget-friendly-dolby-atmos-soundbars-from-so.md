Black Friday is coming up, and there’s no better time of year to upgrade your tech. And one thing I’d highly recommend checking out would be soundbar deals. A good soundbar can totally change the way you enjoy movies and TV, with clearer dialogue, genuinely powerful bass, and far more expansive audio than your set’s built-in speakers could ever dream of providing.

So, if you get a soundbar this Black Friday, which model should you buy? I think it’s always best to go for a soundbar with Dolby Atmos support — this tech is used across multiple streaming platforms, most 4K Blu-rays, and some video games too. Ideally, we don’t want to break the bank, either, so I’ve taken a look at some of the best soundbars I’ve tested with Dolby Atmos support that are still affordable.

The three I settled on were the WiiM Bar, Klipsch Flexus Core 200, and Sonos Beam Gen 2. Each of these is a single bar option, meaning you don’t need to lug a subwoofer into your living room or anything, and all of them have a very similar list price. I compared all of them across areas including features, performance, and design, ranking the soundbars in each category to find an overall winner.

Which model came out on top? You’ll have to read until the end to find out. But first, let’s take a look at the key specs for each of our contestants.

## Features

![WiiM Bar on TV unit with a TV](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/qLQPzYi4xaQ6uzXkorr2Z5-1200-80.jpg)

Let’s take a look at the spatial audio support, connectivity options, and customizability of these soundbars one by one, and determine which offers the most flexibility for users at home.

**WiiM BarThe good:** The WiiM Bar has excellent spatial audio capabilities, with support for both Dolby Atmos and DTS:X. It’s a 3.0.2 model with dedicated up-firing drivers, so you’ll get plenty of height to the soundfield when watching movies or listening to music in more expansive formats.

In terms of connectivity, it’s got you well and truly covered as well, with the usual HDMI eARC, optical, and Bluetooth, but also Wi-Fi for higher-res wireless listening, RCA for connecting a turntable, and an ethernet port too.

If you download the WiiM app you can also access a lot of brilliant features, including sound modes (like Voice or Night), parametric EQ, RoomFit calibration, or channel level adjustments. The app itself is also easy to use and well laid out.

**The bad:** As you can see, the WiiM Bar has just about everything you could want from a soundbar in this price-range… but ‘just about’ is the key phrase there.

One thing it omits is AirPlay 2 support, which may trouble iOS users — though Bluetooth could be a viable alternative in some cases, and Android users should be covered with Google Cast. Another thing I would’ve liked to have seen is HDMI passthrough. While it’s not commonplace for soundbars in this price-range, you already have to sacrifice and HDMI port for the soundbar itself, so getting an extra port is always nice.

**Klipsch Flexus Core 200The good:** The Flexus Core 200 supports Dolby Atmos and has dual 2.25-inch up-firing drivers for convincing height effects. It takes up a 3.1.2 configuration, and can be built out with additional surround speakers if you’d like amazing surround sound.

When it comes to connectivity, the Flexus Core 200 has most of what you need. There’s HDMI eARC, digital optical, Bluetooth, and USB-C as well. What’s more, there’s a sub out port for adding a wired bass unit and a Flexus Transport port for adding a wireless transmitter for compatible subwoofers and surround speakers.

Klipsch’s companion app also has preset and custom EQ options, Night Mode, a dialogue boost option, as well as volume controls.

**The bad:** The Klipsch Flexus Core 200’s biggest sin is its lack of Wi-Fi streaming support. That means that Spotify and Tidal Connect are off the table and you’ll have to stream music from an app on your TV or over Bluetooth (do note that the latter offers worse overall sound quality).

And unlike the WiiM Bar, it doesn’t support DTS:X, which may be a gripe if you prefer to use that format with some of your favorite 4K Blu-ray discs, say. It also doesn’t have a room calibration tool. You also won’t find HDMI passthrough here.

**Sonos Beam Gen 2The good:** Like the aforementioned options, the Sonos Beam Gen 2 has Dolby Atmos support, though it’s worth noting that this is virtual rather than ‘true’ Atmos. It uses a 5.0 driver configuration, enabling it to create solid width for a compact bar.

Something that makes the Beam Gen 2 stand out is its interplay with the Sonos ecosystem, there’s TV Audio Swap, which enables you to transfer media to your Sonos Ace or Sonos Ace Ultra headphones, multi-room connectivity, and the option to pair compatible speakers as a sub or surrounds.

The Sonos app also lets you switch up EQ, make channel level adjustments, or access a voice assistant. TruePlay is also on-board for room calibration.

**The bad:** While you get HDMI eARC and Wi-Fi connectivity, the Beam Gen 2 doesn’t have much else — there’s no Bluetooth, USB-C, RCA or anything fancy. It also lacks DTS:X, meaning it’s not quite got the versatility of the WiiM Bar. Like the other two models, this bar doesn’t feature an HDMI input slot.

## Performance

![Klipsch Flexus Core 200 soundbar on TV stand with colorful abstract image on screen](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/fjkMR6kkD7UMNzZtgM3XbM-1200-80.jpg)

I mainly focused on movies for my soundbar performance test, using 4K Blu-rays with the UHD Blu-ray player in our TV testing room at Future Labs. I used *Top Gun Maverick*to assess height effects and dialogue fidelity,* The Mask*to judge width and dynamism, and* The Boy and the Heron*to test bass and treble response among other things.**WiiM BarThe good:** I think the best thing about the WiiM Bar, performance-wise, is its expansiveness. In *The Mask*, there’s a scene where the titular character swirls around the screen, and this was replicated brilliantly — it was as if I could hear him spinning into the corners of our testing room. In* Top Gun Maverick*, scenes where Tom Cruise flew the jet upwards were also tracked nicely with relatively convincing verticality.

The WiiM Bar can also offer considerably better bass than your basic TV audio, something I noticed in a low-end heavy segment of *The Boy and the Heron*. Dialogue is generally clear too, and music in movie soundtracks is brilliantly balanced.**The bad:** Personally, I think that the WiiM Bar could sound a little more dynamic. When The Mask flung through the glass door, the almighty smash didn’t offer the sudden impact that I experienced from the Klipsch soundbar. More generally, I also felt that the bass on offer from the WiiM Bar was a bit softer than I would’ve liked.

While I found dialogue to be clear on this soundbar, it didn’t quite sound as natural or true-to-life as it did from rivals — there was a slightly artificial quality to voices. The Klipsch also arguably delivers marginally superior width to the WiiM Bar, though I was still very much impressed with its expansive sound overall.

**Klipsch Flexus Core 200The good:** I was mightily impressed by the Klipsch Flexus Core 200. Dialogue in *Top Gun Maverick* sounded spectacular, with the echoey quality of the large open room replicated very well. Said dialogue was also expertly separated from the movie’s dramatic score — something that I realized across all of the movies I tested.

For a single-box solution, the Flexus Core 200 also delivers seriously weighty bass. In *Maverick*, the roar of jet engines was deep and regimented, offering the most impact out of all three models. Atmos is great here too — the verticality of Cruise’s takeoff was captured to good effect, while the width of* The Mask’s*titular character felt exceptionally broad and spacious.**The bad:** There really isn’t much bad to report here whatsoever. In *The Boy and the Heron*, the ultra-low frequencies during one scene were perhaps not the most driven or full-sounding I’ve experienced, but they still came through decently. And height effects, while good, aren’t the most life-like when compared to some more premium rivals. Overall, though, the Flexus Core 200 put in a very strong performance.**Sonos Beam Gen 2The good:** For such a small soundbar, the Sonos Beam Gen 2 conjures up astonishingly authoritative and nuanced sound. In *Top Gun Maverick*, everything from the roar of engines to the accurate tracking of vehicular movements to the crystal clear dialogue instantly stuck out to me. I was even relatively impressed with the height effects on offer, despite the Beam Gen 2’s Atmos being virtualized.

Dialogue between mobsters in *The Mask*might just’ve been the clearest in this competition, and the dynamism on offer when the glass door smashed was more refined than on the WiiM Bar. The Mask’s spinning movements also had tremendous width, and sounds like gunshots being fired were placed nicely while coming through with plenty of bite and venom.**The bad:** While the Sonos Beam Gen 2 performed better than you may expect it to, there are a few limitations here. The most obvious is the lack of up-firing drivers, so sounds like jets flying overhead didn’t seem the *most* natural or believable (though still impressed me overall).

You also won’t quite get the same bass rumble here that the Flexus Core 200 can provide, which is totally understandable given this soundbar’s dimensions. And in some cases, I found that the WiiM Bar offered slightly better musicality than the Beam Gen 2, though overall, I surprisingly preferred the Sonos model.

## Design

![a closeup of the sonos beam gen 2 soundbar](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/2ovqAXGzvN5kjeSxBCrEJn-1200-80.jpg)

And finally, who will take the crown as the best-looking, and most brilliantly designed soundbar? Let's find out...

**WiiM BarThe good:** The WiiM Bar has a central display, which you can customize with an image saved in your phone’s gallery which I found pretty cool. The display can also display the time, album covers when listening to music, volume details, and more.

I also really like the remote that comes with the WiiM Bar. It feels really well made, has individual buttons for each sound mode, and even a built-in mic for voice controls. You can use the app for most of this instead, but given that some soundbars omit a remote control altogether, this is certainly nice to have.

**The bad:** As fun as the screen is, I think it gives the WiiM Bar a bit of a strange shape, with an awkwardly raised center. The plain black design is OK looking, but it lacks the texture or style of the Klipsch and Sonos bars, and fails to exude the sense of luxury its rivals offer.**Klipsch Flexus Core 200The good:** I quite like the aesthetic of the Klipsch Flexus Core 200. The top of the bar has wooden segments that just ooze class, only accentuated by metallic details. Sure, it’s a plain black model, but the variety in materials gives it a bit of an edge over the formulaic look that a lot of rivals possess.**The bad:** The remote that comes in the box doesn’t feel as premium as the WiiM Bar’s, with a plasticky look — though this is a fairly minor complaint. And while I like the materials used on the Flexus Core 200, the large Klipsch logo and fabric grille aren’t the prettiest.**Sonos Beam Gen 2The good:** I love the look of the Sonos Beam Gen 2. It comes in either Black or White to suit the rest of your system or vibe of your space and has a sleek, modern, and minimalist aesthetic which is versatile and understated. Even though the Beam Gen 2 is made from plastic, it’s finished really nicely, and the neat display on the top keeps things looking cohesive and organized.**The bad:** The Beam Gen 2 doesn’t come with a remote in the box. For most people, that won’t be an issue — you can adjust volume with your typical TV remote thanks to HDMI-CEC and make other adjustments in the Sonos app. But if you prefer a physical controller, then you may be a little disappointed.

## Final ranking

So, the results are in. And our winner is none other than the Sonos Beam Gen 2 — surprised?

Yes, the odds seemed to be stacked against the Beam Gen 2 here: it doesn’t have dedicated up-firing drivers, it’s more compact, and has the same list price as its rivals in markets like the UK. But even still, I think it’s the best all-rounder. It sounds spectacular, with perhaps the cleanest dialogue of all three models, plenty of low-end weight, and great virtual Atmos.

It also looks spectacular, and while it skips on some connectivity options, HDMI eARC and Wi-Fi are realistically going to be all you need in most cases. What’s more, it can often be found at a lower price than both of its rivals during sale periods.

The Klipsch Flexus Core 200 just misses out on top spot, ranking 2nd overall. Look, I’m very comfortable recommending this one too. While the lack of Wi-Fi streaming is a shame, this model performed really well, with excellent width and bass weight on offer. It’s not quite as stylish as the Sonos, but if you’re solely focused on performance for movies, you may want to grab this bar.

In third, we have the WiiM Bar. It’s a good soundbar for sure, with impressive Atmos effects, amazing connectivity options, and neat customization options in the WiiM app. But sound-wise, I think it falls just short of the competition, and as unique as the display is, I still think the overall design is a little awkward. If there’s a good sale on this one for Black Friday, I’d still say it’s an option worth considering, and it will definitely offer a considerable upgrade over plain TV audio, but ultimately, I’d go for Sonos or Klipsch over this.
