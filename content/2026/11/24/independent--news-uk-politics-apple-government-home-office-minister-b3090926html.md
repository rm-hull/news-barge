A minister says he is “disappointed” at a tribunal ruling demanding that the Government publicly confirms it sent a notice to Apple that would give it access to people’s communications.

Lord Collins of Highbury admitted ministers must accept the outcome as there was no mechanism for appeal to the tribunal.

Apple is challenging the Home Office over the use of technical capability notices (TCNs) at the Investigatory Powers Tribunal, a specialist tribunal that deals with allegations of unlawful intrusion and national security matters.

The notice, issued under the Investigatory Powers Act 2016, requires Apple to maintain access to its users’ data in decrypted form, allowing intelligence services to access it.

In a ruling last Thursday, Lady Carmichael, Mr Justice Chamberlain and Mr Justice Johnson said the Home Office had “not established it would be damaging to national security to reveal” that Apple had been given a TCN.

Addressing the upper chamber on Tuesday, Lord Collins, a Home Office minister, said: “We are disappointed with the tribunal’s judgment, we must accept it because the tribunal does not provide a route for appeal for these types of decisions.

“Successive governments have adopted neither confirm nor deny on the use of investigatory powers notices, because confirming whether or not access is available to specific services could enable terrorists and criminals to evade detection by avoiding those services, making us all less safe.”

The minister’s comments came in response to Liberal Democrat peer Lord Clement-Jones, who also alleged that the tribunal found that government briefings to news organisations “undermine the secrecy they were asking the court to protect”.

He called on the Government to publish the number of TCNs in force and for them to give the investigatory powers commissioner a “duty to report publicly on how they affect encryption”.

Lord Collins insisted the judgment only applies in the “narrow circumstances of this case” and there is “no wider implication for neither confirm nor deny as a policy”.

“The judgment only confirmed that a TCN had been given to Apple. We are not publishing the full details of the notice, and a judgment does not mean that every detail of the notice can be safely disclosed,” he said.

The judges also said that their decision means much of the full hearing of the claims, due to take place next year, can be held in open court.

Apple claims the Home Office has no power to issue warrants to intercept communications outside the UK, that the TCN amounts to a disproportionate interference with human rights and that it has been treated differently from other companies.
