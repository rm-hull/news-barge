Hundreds of thousands of people face losing their disability benefits under plans being considered by ministers as part of a shake-up of the welfare system.

Officials are drawing up plans to redesign the personal independence payment (Pip) system for adults in England and Wales after the work and pensions minister, Stephen Timms, deemed it “no longer fit for purpose”.

Timms recommended a new test for who should be allowed to claim Pip, which would exclude those who do not incur additional costs as a result of their disability.

According to a study by the Resolution Foundation, this would apply to as many as one in six of the 4 million people who claim the benefit – though officials stress they have not yet decided whether any cuts would apply to existing claimants or just new ones.

Timms told MPs on Tuesday: “The system is no longer fit for purpose and requires a fundamental reset.”

He added: “Support should correspond more closely to genuine additional costs and needs. Cumulative and fluctuating impacts should be recognised properly, and the highest support should reflect the most severe needs.”

Speaking on Tuesday morning, Timms’ co-author Sharon Brennan made clear some people were likely to see their payments cut.

“It’s likely there will be a rebalancing process in which some people will get more related to their needs, and some people therefore might get less,” she said. “But it’s for the government to make decisions based on our report about eligibility and what that cost should look like.”

Timms’ report is the second half of the government’s attempt to slow the speed at which the bill for disability benefits is climbing, with Pip forecast to rise from about £16bn in 2019-20 to just over £41bn by the end of the decade.

Last week a report by the former minister Alan Milburn recommended scrapping Pip entirely for under-25s and replacing it – as well as part of universal credit – with more concerted help to get back into work.

Timms rejected Milburn’s approach, saying Pip was still an important system for over-25s who claimed it, but he opened the door for the government to make cuts through a new assessment to see who should be allowed to claim and how much they should get.

He made two recommendations that have been immediately accepted by the government: to make it quicker for people with severe conditions to be able to claim Pip, and to reduce reviews for those whose conditions are unlikely to improve.

For the remaining 90% of claimants, the review recommends assessments that would be tailored to people’s individual circumstances. This would be a change from the current system which awards payments in four bands from £30.30 to £194.60 a week.

It said: “Those with the highest additional costs, and so the most significant needs, should receive the most support.” But it added: “This benefit is not appropriate for those who do not incur any additional costs associated with being disabled.”

The Resolution Foundation survey found one in six people – about 666,000 – say they currently claim Pip despite incurring no extra costs as a result of their disability. Ending payments for those people would save the government up to £4.4bn if applied in 2029/30, the thinktank said.

Officials say no final decisions have been made about how the assessments will work, or whether they will apply to existing claimants or only new ones.

If they are applied retrospectively, government sources say there are likely to be transitional arrangements to help people move on to the new system.

Andy Burnham has said he wants to bring the welfare bill down “as soon as possible”, though he has rejected what he says is the Conservative approach of imposing “crude cuts”.

Officials involved in drawing up the government’s response say the key question is how quickly to try to generate savings from the new system. One senior minister said: “The aim is still to bring the welfare bill down, the only question is how quickly.”

Even with Burnham’s more cautious approach, however, the prime minister risks a row with his backbenchers over the recommendations from both Milburn and Timms.

Debbie Abrahams, the Labour chair of the work and pensions committee, told the Commons on Tuesday: “We need to have extra support for disabled people to cover their extra costs, and that applies to everyone of all ages.”

The Labour MP Apsana Begum warned about “the deep anxiety that disabled people will have about reviews recommending changes to eligibility without having specifics provided”.

Campaigners and charities welcomed Timms’ review, which was drawn up in consultation with disabled people, but urged the government not to leave vulnerable people without a financial lifeline.

Sarah Hughes, the chief executive of Mind, said: “It’s promising to see the recognition that Pip should be a cash benefit unless someone chooses other support in kind, that it is available to those in and out of work, and isn’t conditional on undertaking certain activities. However, in the wake of the Milburn review we know that many people – particularly those under 25 – are really worried that their lifeline will be cut.”

Svetlana Kotova, the director of campaigns and justice at Inclusion London, said: “It’s evident that when disabled people are actively involved in designing policy, the outputs better reflect the reality of our lives. Therefore, the government should prioritise the Timms recommendations over the reforms suggested by Milburn. The extra costs of disability do not start at 25.”

The Department for Work and Pensions said the new system would “help disabled people achieve better health, higher living standards and greater independence”.
