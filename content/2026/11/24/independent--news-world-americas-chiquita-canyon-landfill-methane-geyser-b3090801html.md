A routine drilling operation at a California landfill struck an underground methane pocket, sending gas and debris erupting through the borehole.

The mishap occurred around 4 p.m. on Nov. 16 at Chiquita Canyon Landfill, in the 29200 block of Henry Mayo Drive. Los Angeles County Fire Department crews were sent to the scene after a report of a structure fire, but quickly determined there was no fire.

A department spokesman told City News Service that an auger used in a routine drilling operation to alleviate underground temperatures at the landfill struck the methane pocket about 150 feet underground. The release initially produced a dark-black geyser of material that rose hundreds of feet into the air.

![Nearby residents were offered hotel accommodations after the cloud of trash and methane was sent into the air](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/22/download.-2..jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The emissions later turned white, and the airborne plume diminished dramatically from the original burst. Members of the Fire Department's Health and Hazardous Materials team capped the leak just after 10 p.m. that night.

Six people, reported to be workers, were treated at the scene, and four were taken to a hospital as a precaution, the fire department said. None were seriously injured. Engineers and hazardous-materials personnel carried out air-quality testing at the site, and fire officials reported no health risk after monitoring the situation overnight.

Some nearby residents reported headaches, scratchy throats and dizziness, while others said their vehicles were coated with specks of dark material. Residents affected by the geyser can now receive free hotel stays through Dec. 3.

Last week, landfill operators initially offered three free hotel nights to residents of Val Verde and Hasley Hills at four hotels on a first-come, first-served basis. The stays were later extended by three more nights, with residents initially expected to check out Monday morning.

Following urging by Supervisor Kathryn Barger and a county Department of Public Health directive, the landfill agreed Monday to extend the hotel stays through Dec. 3, according to Barger's office.

"This is the responsible thing to do, especially while families are still waiting for answers about the potential health impacts of last week's incident," Barger said in a Monday statement announcing the extension. "Residents should not have to worry about losing access to a safe place to stay while testing continues."

Barger said she expected the landfill to follow through on its commitment and remain responsive to affected families' needs.

The county directive calls for Chiquita Canyon to provide short-term hotel accommodations through at least Dec. 3 or until all properties reporting debris from the geyser have been assessed and remediated.

![Pre-paid rooms were offered at the Hyatt Regency Valencia, Embassy Suites Valencia, Holiday Inn Express & Suites Valencia-Santa Clarita and Best Western Valencia](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/16/2026-11-19T201421Z_219553916_RC28ROAZIP2T_RTRMADP_3_IHG-NEW-YORK-HOTEL-LAWSUIT.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Pre-paid rooms were offered at the Hyatt Regency Valencia, Embassy Suites Valencia, Holiday Inn Express & Suites Valencia-Santa Clarita and Best Western Valencia. It was unclear how many residents used the accommodations.

Landfill operators have also been running a relief center at the Embassy Suites Valencia, 28508 Westinghouse Place, for Castaic and Val Verde residents. The center was originally scheduled to remain open through 7 p.m. Monday, but it will now operate from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. daily through Dec. 3.

A free hourly shuttle is being provided from Val Verde Park. Barger also called on the state Department of Toxic Substances Control to expedite testing of material released during the incident.

"A week has passed since last Monday's incident, and residents are still waiting to understand what was released and whether there are potential health or environmental impacts," Barger said. "I understand that getting accurate, scientifically sound results takes time. But residents should not be left in the dark."

The county Department of Public Health began door-to-door outreach over the weekend in communities near the landfill to document residents' health concerns, assess their needs and connect them with available resources.

The county directive requires landfill operators to collect and clean up landfill-associated debris and conduct additional testing to determine the extent of the debris field and identify components of the material released during the incident.

The landfill was also directed to establish an online system for residents to report possible debris on their property, provide cleanup services and regularly update the community about available resources and instructions for accessing them.

Landfill officials confirmed the geyser "deposited degraded municipal solid waste and other particulate matter across the top deck of the landfill," and crews spent nearly three days removing the material.

According to landfill operators, "several bins of debris" were removed from the drilling area, which was "covered with scrim to prevent it from commingling with stormwater."

Health officials also ordered Chiquita Canyon to operate local indoor refuge spaces with filtered air, provide transportation assistance and offer at-home air-quality support such as HEPA or carbon air purifiers.

County officials said residents who see unusual dust or debris believed to be associated with the incident should avoid dry sweeping, leaf blowers or other activities that could send particles into the air.

Residents were also advised to minimize contact with the material, use wet-cleaning methods if it must be removed, wear gloves and wash their hands afterward.

"Airborne particles from disturbed material may cause irritation of the eyes, nose, throat, or respiratory tract," according to the Department of Public Health. "Children, older adults, and people with asthma or other respiratory conditions may be more sensitive to exposure."

Despite residents' concerns about possible debris near schools and homes, Public Health said available ambient-air monitoring data collected during and after the incident did not indicate a need for broader communitywide emergency protective actions at this time.

"That network is taking hourly measurements for hydrogen sulfide, methane, benzene and other volatile organic compounds and sulfur species at 12 monitoring stations," Connie Villanueva, senior public affairs specialist for the South Coast Air Quality Management District, said last week.

Landfill officials also said Thursday that air monitoring had shown no elevated levels of harmful materials, including benzene.

Residents who observe possible landfill debris on their properties can report it to Chiquita Canyon Landfill by emailing [info@chiquitacanyon.com](mailto:info@chiquitacanyon.com) or calling 661-257-3655.

Barger called the methane plume "unacceptable" and said she would demand answers and accountability.

Under a motion by Barger, approved unanimously last week, county department heads are scheduled to report to the Board of Supervisors today on the incident and the government's response.

The motion also directed the Department of Mental Health to provide services in Val Verde and Castaic, including support groups, stress-management resources and individual and family counseling.

Barger openly criticized what she considered a slow response by the Department of Public Health when the geyser first erupted.

Assemblywoman Pilar Schiavo, D-Santa Clarita, has also criticized the county and county health officials over the past week, saying more should have been done over the years to regulate landfill operations and protect residents.

She has called for the firing of the leaders of the county Department of Public Health.
