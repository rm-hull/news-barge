A retired teacher and her brother have won a court fight to split their dead aunt’s £1million fortune after a legal “hiccup” involving two wills threatened to scatter her fortune among them and 16 relatives.

Dorothy Eirwen George died in December 2023, aged 83, leaving behind an estate valued at around £1million, wrapped up in UK assets and a holiday apartment in Cyprus.

During her final years, she made clear that she intended to leave her wealth to her niece and nephew - Helen Thyer and Peter Crawford - and in May 2017 made a will in the UK handing her English estate to the siblings.

But she unwittingly derailed those plans when she drew up a second will in Cyprus four months later - where she spent her holidays - bequeathing her apartment near Limassol to her niece, Mrs Thyer.

Mrs George, from Bexleyheath, south London, had decided to make the second Cypriot will in order that her niece should inherit the flat - while her UK will had left an equivalent cash legacy to Peter - so that each sibling should inherit equally.

Yet after her death, the two siblings learnt that their aunt drawing up the second will had the potential effect of cancelling out her earlier English will, meaning that by law she had died “intestate” - and her fortune stood to be carved up among all 18 of her known next of kin.

“I laughed when I was told this, because it seemed so ridiculous,” said Ms Thyer after a hearing at London’s High Court which ended with a judge accepting the validity of the English will, confirming the pair’s right to jointly inherit their aunt’s £1m fortune.

The siblings’ barrister, Michael O’Sullivan, argued that their aunt only drew up the Cypriot will leaving Ms Thyer her Limassol apartment because she wanted to “balance things out” as she had left her nephew more under her English will.

She never intended that making the second will could have the effect of nullifying the English will, he told London’s High Court.

But after Mrs George’s death, her nice and nephew found themselves struggling in a legal quagmire due to a paragraph in the Cyprus will which seemed to cancel out the earlier English document.

Scrapping the English will would have meant that she died intestate under UK law - with no heir nominated to inherit - meaning that by law her estate would be shared among all her known surviving kin.

Ms Thyer and her brother had to pay for a team of expert genealogists to track down some of her unknown relatives, who ended up being part of the case as potential beneficiaries.

“We had to pay for a genealogist company as the wider family was not fully known to us,” explained Ms Thyer after the hearing, adding: “it would be inappropriate to publish their details, they will be informed about the result. They were not close to her”.

Had the case gone against the two siblings, they would have had to share the inheritance between themselves and 16 of their relatives.

Ms Thyer, 73, and Mr Crawford, 71, were ultimately forced to go to court in a bid to get the English will recognised, arguing that their normally “careful” aunt had made a simple mistake in relation to her second will.

“The Cypriot will contained a general revocation clause which on a literal construction would have had the effect of revoking the earlier English will,” said their barrister in his written submissions.

“The claimants claim that the inclusion of such a general revocation clause was a mistake and did not accord with the deceased’s true intentions.

“The Cypriot will dealt only with property owned by the deceased in Cyprus. If the will had the effect of revoking the English will, then this would have triggered an intestacy in relation to the deceased’s English estate.”

But Ms George would never have wanted to deny her niece and nephew their full inheritance, he urged, claiming that all the available evidence backed this up.

“The claimants’ evidence is supported by the witness statement of the deceased’s friend, Rosemary Sanger. She states that Mrs George told her that she intended to leave her English estate to (both) claimants, but intended to leave her property in Cyprus to Helen Thyer.”

Master Katherine McQuail, judging the case, said none of the 16 potential co-heirs had come forward to challenge the Engiish will being confirmed, and said she had power to accept the 2017 will if the evidence suggested Mrs George never intended to scrap it through the cancellation clause in the Cyprus document.

“It’s clear that the court may admit an earlier will to probate despite a later will containing a revocation clause if all the evidence before the court shows that it wasn’t the intention of the testator to revoke that earlier will," she said.

“The deceased cannot have intended to revoke her English will when she made the Cyprus will.

“The Cyprus will was intended to deal with her Cyprus property in circumstances where the English will had been made very recently.

“I am satisfied that I should pronounce for the validity of the English will dated May 2017,” the judge concluded, also directing that over £35,000 the siblings spent pursuing their claim should be paid out by Mrs George’s estate.

After the court hearing, Helen Thyer said her experience in suing over the legal “hiccup” had taught her the importance of precision when writing a will.

"You have to be very very cautious about writing a will,” she said. “This has taken us nearly three years and cost a lot of money and some heartache."
