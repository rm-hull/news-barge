Anthony Albanese’s approval ratings have nosedived in the latest Guardian Essential poll, with the prime minister reaching his highest level of disapproval after his net popularity plummeted 10 points in just a month.

Australians have also turned strongly against AI datacentres, with nearly half of the country saying they don’t want one near their home. Women, older people and One Nation and Greens supporters are among the most likely to reject living near a datacentre.

This month’s Guardian Essential poll, of 1,087 people taken last week, found a tightening political horse race amid mounting political problems for the Labor government. Labor and One Nation remain deadlocked, both with a primary vote of 26%, each having registered a slight dip since both last month recorded 28%. The Coalition’s fortunes have improved slightly, moving to 25%, up from last month’s 23%, while the Greens are on 13%.

The biggest shift in November’s poll is a sudden collapse in the approval ratings for the prime minister. Just 32% of Australians approved of his job performance, down from 38% last month, while simultaneously 60% of people disapproved of Albanese, up from last month’s 56%.

That’s a net approval rating of –28, a 10-point fall since October.

Albanese’s approval rating has never been lower, and his disapproval rating has never been higher, since becoming prime minister. Even after ferocious criticism and scrutiny of the government after the Bondi terror shooting last December, Albanese recorded a 39% approval and 53% disapproval in January.

The collapse in Albanese’s personal ratings comes as cost-of-living pressures continue to bite the government, with scrutiny on Labor’s spending priorities and economic record amid high petrol prices and rising interest rates. Albanese has also come under personal attack from opponents over budget decisions, comments on podcasts and grants funding. The poll was taken last week, while Albanese was overseas at the Cop31 climate conference and international summits in Asia.

“No one wins popularity points on the global stage, particularly when living costs are high,” said Peter Lewis, the chair of Essential Media. “But worse for the PM was his proximity to the dysfunction that global affairs has become, with Cop collapsing and then playing second fiddle at Yalta 2.0.

“A chance for a break and a reset can’t come fast enough for him.”

The opposition leader, Angus Taylor, recorded a 35% approval and 42% disapproval, which has remained largely unchanged for several months.

Essential also probed Australians’ views on datacentres, finding a significant negative shift in sentiment around the AI factories.

After a major rise in opposition to datacentres at the US midterm elections, Essential found only 23% of Australians would support having one near their home, with 48% against.

That compares with 28% in favour and 37% opposed in July, the last time Essential asked this question. In just four months, Australians’ support for living near a datacentre has swung 16% to the negative.

“The deterioration in support for datacentres follows successful community campaigns against local developments, as well as a string of AI-related job cuts,” Lewis said. “The datacentre becomes an avatar for all the unresolved issues around the regulation and scale of this technology.”

Asked for their major concerns about datacentres, the most commonly cited worry was about impacts on energy demand and electricity prices (61% of people), local water supply (54%) and impact on the environment (39%). Some 38% of people voiced general worries about the growth of AI.

Broken down by demographic, women (55% opposed) were much less likely than men (40% opposed) to support living near a datacentre. Views get more negative as Australians get older, with 57% of over-55s against living near one, compared with 37% of 18-34s and 47% of 35-54s.

Greens (59% oppose) and One Nation (53% oppose) supporters were far more negative about datacentres than Labor and Liberal voters (both 42% oppose).
