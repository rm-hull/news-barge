Tributes have been paid to Holocaust survivor Lydia Tischler, who has died at the age of 97 after leading an "extraordinary" life of courage and achievement.

Ms Tischler arrived in the UK as a refugee in 1945, having survived the horrors of the Theresienstadt Ghetto, Auschwitz and forced labour at Oederan, a sub-camp of Flossenbürg concentration camp.

Alongside her sister, she was forced to dig trenches at Auschwitz, where their mother was killed.

The Association of Jewish Refugees (AJR) announced that Ms Tischler, originally from Ostrava in former Czechoslovakia, now the Czech Republic, died during the night between Thursday and Friday.

After moving to the UK, she trained under the famous psychoanalyst Anna Freud and spent over six decades working as a child psychologist. This included 23 years at the Cassel Hospital, a psychiatric facility located in Richmond, south-west London.

Ms Tischler also returned to her country of birth to establish a training programme for child psychotherapy, passing on her knowledge to younger therapists. The AJR said she considered this project one of the "proudest of her life".

![Lydia Tischler was honoured for her services to Holocaust remembrance and child psychotherapy](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/02/18/14/11aafc8ccc1047eba581329e4c971a4fY29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzcxNTA4MzY1-2.83521373.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Throughout her life, she also spoke of the importance of Holocaust education and attended many memorial events and ceremonies, including a gathering outside Parliament for the UK’s National Yom HaShoah, the Jewish day of remembrance for the Holocaust, earlier this year.

During the gathering at the site, which is the planned location for a new Holocaust memorial and learning centre, she told Jewish primary school children about her experience of the Holocaust.

Ms Tischler, who lived in Highgate, north London, was made a Member of the Order of the British Empire by the Princess Royal earlier this year for her services to Holocaust remembrance and child psychotherapy.

After the ceremony at Windsor Castle, she had told the *Press Association*: “My legacy, at least what I hope to pass on to people, is to help them to understand what makes people hate each other and feel that they can destroy them.”

AJR chief executive Michael Newman said: “Lydia Tischler’s life was one of extraordinary courage and achievement.

“Having endured unimaginable persecution and the loss of her mother during the Holocaust, she came to Britain as a teenage refugee and went on to transform her experiences into a lifetime of service to others.

“Her pioneering contribution to child psychotherapy, both in Britain and in her native Czechia, is a remarkable legacy.

“Lydia’s story reminds us not only of the devastating human consequences of the Holocaust, but also of the immense contribution that Jewish refugees have made to British society.”

Dr Bea Lewkowicz, director of the AJR Refugee Voices Testimony Archive, who interviewed Lydia earlier this year, said: “Interviewing Lydia was an immense privilege. I was struck by her remarkable memory, intellectual clarity, humour and her extraordinary capacity to reflect on a life shaped by both profound loss and exceptional achievement.

“She spoke with particular pride about returning to Czechia to train a new generation of child psychotherapists.”

She added: “When I asked what message she wished to leave for future generations, her response was characteristically profound: ‘Don’t project your hostility into the other. Take responsibility for it and don’t act on it’.”
