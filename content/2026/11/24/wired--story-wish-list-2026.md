![Image may contain: Blade, Razor, and Weapon](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00fe4b16288c93d13ccb54/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Metrolog-Ruler.jpg)

For the design freak in your life who obsesses over the most precisely considered objects, this is the ultimate desktop curio. The Brooklyn firm Craighill makes all sorts of fun everyday items—key rings, scissors, pens—with close attention paid to fit and finish. Place a thumb against the dimples of this telescoping ruler and extend the sliding mechanism with the most deliciously smooth push. The anodized aluminum body gives it a pleasant heft. It takes measurements, caliper-style, up to 8 inches or 20 centimeters, depending on your numerical affiliation.

![Image may contain: Bicycle, Mountain Bike, Transportation, Vehicle, Machine, and Wheel](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00fdd84846a986620a94dc/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Current-ADV-Electric-Mountain-Bike.jpg)

The direct-to-consumer ebike brand Aventon made its name on sturdy and reliable city commuter bikes. After branching out to cargo bikes and trail models, the company is going hard into mountain climbing. The full-suspension Current ADV is powered by a 750-watt motor between the cranks that delivers smooth pedal assistance over hill and dale, plus a boost mode for rocketing up the steep stuff. The battery is good for hours of riding; it can go over 100 miles on a charge in Eco mode, so your loved one can go even farther.

![Image may contain: Accessories, Sunglasses, and Glasses](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00fd937c78d96a15f90560/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Article-One-Accord-.jpg)

What’s a surefire gift for the person who is otherwise impossible to shop for? A good pair of sunglasses. The Minnesota company Article One has all of its glasses made in Japan to exacting standards. We especially like the Accord frames, with clean lines that give the specs an understated style that’s fashionable in nearly every situation. The lenses are made of TR-18 Tritan, a co-polyester material making waves in the eyewear industry that offers great visual clarity while being more durable than common tinted polycarbonate lenses.

![Image may contain: Clothing, Glove, Cutlery, Fork, Art, Aircraft, Airplane, Transportation, and Vehicle](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00fd423e447d10163e6777/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Cast-Hand-Stand.jpg)

Mostly known for his shiny and expensive pendants and lighting fixtures, Tom Dixon also designs fun—and affordable—items for around the house like this foot-tall cast-iron hand. Its powder-coated finish offers a little grip, making it the perfect perch for a phone, a notebook, or a wristwatch. A perfect gift for the person in your life with the funky taste who is otherwise *impossible* to shop for.

![Image may contain: Sphere](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00fcbd3c377becdbefb7f3/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Mini-Tangerine-Lamp--Terracotta.jpg)

Handmade from slip-cast terra cotta in Eugene, Oregon, this donut-shaped lamp puts out 600 lumens of soft, warm light. A 6-watt LED sits inside the frosted globe, and you can dim it via a rotary switch on the power cord. It’s a functional and tiny (5.5 x 4.5-inch) beacon to beautify your loved one’s love nest.

![Image may contain: Electronics, and Headphones](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00fc6ff081739979ed6a1c/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Headphone-(1)-Pro.jpg)

The mobile technology company Nothing has been focusing on its audio prowess lately, and its new Headphone (1) Pro are evidence, catering to audio professionals in the studio chasing the best sound quality. With a triple-driver architecture in each ear and studio-quality tuning, these cans are a surprise hit with what WIRED reviewer Harry Rabinowitz called “outstanding” sound quality.

![Image may contain: Advertisement, Ice, and Text](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00fbff2e2ee48d385d91df/4:3/w_640,c_limit/The-Frame-Pro-(55-Inch).jpg)

Once a niche category, Art TVs are now all the rage. These displays work like regular old boob tubes, but when you turn them off, their bezel-framed matte screens double as digital picture frames for fine art you can download. Samsung was early to the Art TV market, and its latest Frame Pro is its most gorgeous plug-and-play gallery yet. Art lovers will appreciate the bevy of options to show on the TV, with top-name painters and photographers available in Samsung’s store, as well as collections from The Met and MoMA. Sizes range from 55 to 85 inches, and wireless connections make setup a breeze.

![Image may contain: Camera, Electronics, and Digital Camera](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00fba54909d8ae8eb24fab/4:3/w_640,c_limit/GR-IV-HDF.jpg)

Your photo-taking friend already has a few cameras. They have the big one with the fancy lenses, and the smaller one with the even fancier lenses. They shoot tons of pictures with their phone, too, of course. But the camera they really want is the Ricoh GR IV. Fans of this pocketable, high-end digital point-and-shoot are like a cult, the type of people who find each other at parties and whisper in the corner about this camera and how much it’s totally changed their game. We could list the specs and tell you what it can do, but you can read our review for all of that. The most important thing to know is that if you want to splurge on something nice for your friend who’s obsessively taking photos all the time, this is the perfect camera for them. Be their hero. (Note: Our review is of the previous model. This newer version has a highlight-diffusion filter that improves the camera’s performance in extreme light and dark conditions, making it even more desirable.)

![Image may contain: Accessories, Bag, Handbag, Tote Bag, and Purse](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00fb3c295dddbac98c7a10/4:3/w_640,c_limit/DayTripper-Tote.jpg)

There’s no shortage of beautiful, functional bags in our midst, but this zipper tote is one of our favorites. Nomadix makes blankets, towels, and bags from recycled materials, and the DayTripper's nylon shell and polyester interior are made from 100 percent recycled fabric. The ripstop face adds toughness as well as some visual flair. Inside are a padded laptop sleeve, two bottle sleeves, and a zipper pocket. The 26-liter capacity lets it do double duty as a commuter and a weekender.

![Image may contain: Clothing, Footwear, Shoe, Sneaker, and Running Shoe](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00face209518b1b255d655/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Norda-002-Stealth-Black.jpg)

For the trail runner in your life who gets up at dawn to put in serious miles in the wilderness, only the best shoes will do. These kicks from Norda are tough, light, and responsive. The uppers and laces are made from ecofriendly Dyneema, a highly durable all-weather fabric made from by-products of the wood and pulp industries. The soles are made by Vibram, a pioneer in those thin-but-springy soles that let the runner really feel the earth beneath their feet.

![Image may contain: Julie Ferrier, Clothing, Long Sleeve, Sleeve, Spandex, Adult, and Person](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00fa5b86dbfc862f91c0c4/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Regulator-4-3-Full-Wetsuit.jpg)

For a long time, wetsuits were made with neoprene, a petroleum-based product with an eco footprint that didn’t really square with surfing’s ocean-loving, conservation-minded vibe. Patagonia was early to the market with neoprene-free suits, and now the company has completely overhauled its wetsuit designs. The new Regulator collection—made from natural rubber and recycled nylon—has thicker insulation and padding where it’s needed most. The rest of the suit stays flexible and comfy to wear for long stretches. A big U-shaped zipper runs around the shoulders, making it easier for surfers to get it on and off in the parking lot. Comes in various thicknesses, and in men’s and women’s cuts.

![Image may contain: Computer, Electronics, Page, Text, and Tablet Computer](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00f9e814eeaa83681a415e/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Kindle-(32-MB,-Aluminum).jpg)

Kindles have always been plasticky pieces of tech. However, now that Amazon’s hardware is under the eye of ex-Microsoft Surface hardware guru Panos Panay, the brand has been zhuzhing its devices. The basic Kindle now comes in an all-aluminum design. (You can still buy the plastic version for a little less cash.) It still lacks the warm light and auto-adjusting light of premium models, but you get double the storage, plus no ads. It's heavy in a good way, like a book, and the size is still just right for reading. It's a great upgrade for the book lover in your life—unless they've already converted to a non-Amazon e-reader.

![Image may contain: Device, Electronics, Appliance, and Electrical Device](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00f97deb9bffa81529a24b/4:3/w_640,c_limit/EP-35.jpg)

Musicians and audio nerds alike flip out over Teenage Engineering’s fun and inventive approach to creative noisemaking. This little multipurpose box is the latest must-have for TE fans. It’s technically a speaker—powered by a 12-hour battery, with connections for whatever you can think to pair or plug in—but it doubles as an amplifier for musical performance. Plug in a guitar, bass, keyboard, or microphone and enjoy 35 watts of volume from the dual speakers (a 4-inch woofer and a 1-inch tweeter). Dial up onboard effects like distortion, echo, and reverb. When rock time is over, your musical loved one can attach the optional antenna ($29) and use it as an FM radio, or plug in the optional mic ($29) for some karaoke.

![Image may contain: Electronics, Tape Player, and Hardware](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00f90907e33fe9255c9bf3/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Sharepower-Portable-Charger.jpg)

If you're always stealing your loved one's battery pack and leaving them with little charge left for their own devices, buy Nimble's SharePower for them (or yourself). This power bank splits into two, so you can always share power (get it) with someone else. Each half is 5,000 mAh, enough to recharge a smartphone. They combine magnetically for 10,000 mAh that can recharge your gadget twice. Nimble is also known for its sustainability chops, manufacturing the battery with 100 percent recycled plastic and shipping it in plastic-free packaging.

![Image may contain: Accessories, Strap, and Belt](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00f852590b61f354165bb1/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Magnetic-Camera-Strap-30MM.jpg)

Aren't magnets amazing? Wandrd's new Magnetic Camera Straps prove that yet again. These straps have a magnetic attachment system that makes removing the camera a breeze. The magnets guide the strap into the anchor point, while a locking mechanism ensures the camera can’t fall off. If your loved one already has a camera strap they love, like a fancy leather strap, Wandrd sells cheap DIY kits so they can add the ingenious magnetic system to their own strap.

![Image may contain: Analog Clock, and Clock](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00f7c2d0c9747518ede766/4:3/w_640,c_limit/PS-2026-Table-Clock.jpg)

This 15-inch clock, dreamed up by Polish designer Marta Krupińska for Ikea’s latest PS collection, is designed to resemble a marine periscope. Whether placing this curvy clock on the bedside table will result in nautical dreams, we can’t say. However, we can tell you that it won’t disturb your loved one’s sleep; the quartz movement is entirely silent, with no ticktock sounds. Buy it in their favorite color, as long as it’s red.

![Image may contain: Alcohol, Beverage, Liquor, and Absinthe](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00f75119e6c5e07d00ed17/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Tomato-Vermouth.jpg)

The San Francisco producer Veso makes zippy aperitifs as well as vermouths with unconventional flavorings like fig leaf, miso, olive, and anchovy. This bottling, a limited 2026 release, is vermouth flavored with tomatoes. Early Girls and Purple Cherokees take a seven-month bath in grape spirits, then the liquid is pressed and mixed into a pinot blanc with kombu, beets, and carrots. The result is a pinkish vermouth with a salty, vegetal, and umami character that’s wholly unique. Get this for the adventurous drinker in your life, and settle in to enjoy some tomato gibsons, tomato-mezcal negronis, and tomato-wine spritzers.

![Image may contain: Robot](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00f6c54909d8ae8eb24f9a/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Microduck-.jpg)

The season’s hottest toy is a programmable, robotic duck. That’s not a joke: The Microduck is a funky little automaton made by the robotics team at the AI company Hugging Face. The 10-inch-tall bipedal bot has a camera and a lidar system to interact with its surroundings, plus two inertia sensors that help it right itself when it inevitably tips over. The little guy can even pick things up with its beak. Owners train their physical ducks using a virtual duck simulator on their computers. Since the Microducks are on backorder—new purchases will ship in the new year—your loved one can prepare for their robot’s arrival by using AI models to train up the duck and imagining all the things it can do.

![Image may contain: Electronics, Hockey, Ice Hockey, Ice Hockey Puck, Rink, Skating, Sport, Ping Pong, and Ping Pong Paddle](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00f6210fef4fa9e52f026f/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Cambridge-Audio-Spira.jpg)

This plug-and-play turntable makes a great starter record player for the vinyl-curious audiophile or a solid upgrade for the obsessive record collector. A built-in preamp lets the Spira connect directly to an existing stereo without additional equipment. It comes outfitted with a very nice Audio-Technica cartridge so they can spin their most precious platters on day one. It even has Bluetooth to connect to a speaker or wireless headphones. Aside from those conveniences, the Spira comes from Cambridge Audio, a British hi-fi brand known for high-quality, sweet-sounding components that keep performing for decades.

![Image may contain: Wristwatch, Arm, Body Part, and Person](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00f5bff081739979ed6a16/4:3/w_640,c_limit/G-Shock-GAC2500-9A.jpg)

The horological aficionado on your list probably has a G-Shock—the iconic resin Casio was countless collectors’ first watch—but we’d doubt they have one of these. The new release ditches the digital readout for analog hands, handsomely encased in a shock-resistant, waterproof 44.5-millimeter case. A couple of complications round out the face: a sleepable second-hand dial and a date wheel in the eye at 3 o’clock. It comes in a variety of colors and materials, but this statement-worthy bright yellow version is our favorite.

![Image may contain: Chart, Business Card, Paper, Text, and Pie Chart](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00f57a696241a06b79ecd9/4:3/w_640,c_limit/%E2%80%98Charts-for-Babies%E2%80%99-by-Michelle-Rial.jpg)

Some folks are born with an eye for design, and some need solid training—preferably during the first couple years of life. Michelle Rial’s book presents familiar picture-book-for-kids fare (numbers, vocabulary, colors, feelings) as charts, diagrams, graphs, and infographics. While it does actually serve as a fine developmental learning tool for tots, we all know who the real audience for this book is. Get it for the person in your life at the center of the Venn diagram between “creative professional” and “new parent.”

![](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00c2e3eb9bffa81529a1a4/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Donkey-Kong-Arcade.jpg)

TIRED: Retro arcade games. WIRED: Retro arcade games made of Lego bricks that you assemble yourself. Yes, this 15.5-inch play set actually works, giving you a wholly analog representation of the 1981 classic, *Donkey Kong*. It’s made up of 1,367 pieces, and includes working controls, three characters, and a mechanism to keep launching barrels into the scaffolding on a loop. Move the joystick and make Mario (aka Jumpman) leap over the barrels, climb to the top, and save the kidnapped Pauline.

![Image may contain: Cup, Device, Appliance, Electrical Device, and Mixer](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00c2795cef65a03aedabfa/4:3/w_640,c_limit/the-Spiral-Luxe-Auto-Pour-Over.jpg)

If your loved one can’t start their day without a cup of pour-over coffee, this is the only brewing machine they’ll want. It automates the method of slowly pouring hot water over freshly ground beans. It even replicating the spiral pour pattern (hence the name) essential to pour-over brewing, as a swirl of the spout helps evenly distribute water to the grounds in the filter. The machine includes a bean grinder and a water tank, so one button push is all that’s needed for a cup or two of brightly flavored coffee.

![Image may contain: Cup, Chart, and Plot](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00c1d77020ceeb4716a1e6/4:3/w_640,c_limit/Stepped-Jigger.jpg)

The stepped shape of this stainless steel jigger is based on an antique measuring tool from the 1930s. The simple yet effective design makes eyeballing precise measurements exceptionally easy—especially helpful if the jigger has already gotten some use earlier in the evening. The steps are in half-ounce (15 milliliters) increments, with four indicators going up to a full 2 ounces (60 ml). Get one for the mixologist in your midst.

![Image may contain: Advertisement, Poster, Business Card, Paper, Text, Document, Id Cards, and Passport](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00c1508a18277ad68d7303/4:3/w_640,c_limit/HTTP-Code-Playing-Cards.jpg)

The Electronic Frontier Foundation is one of the premier nonprofits advocating for digital privacy and civil rights in the connected world. When they’re not busy taking on government, law enforcement, and big tech, they like to play cards. This deck, designed by EFF employees, has faces adorned with HTTP status codes. All the good ones are represented: 200 OK, 511 Network Authentication Required, 403 Forbidden, 408 Request Timeout, and of course, saucy old 404 Not Found.

![Image may contain: Gauge](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00c0138a897d974c2639f4/4:3/w_640,c_limit/IDX-Analogue-Data-Gauge-.jpg)

Here’s a fun idea for a connected device: The IDX is an analog gauge to display real-time data streams pulled from the internet. You can configure it out of the box to show the current temperature or air quality, the price of Bitcoin or $ANT, your home’s electricity use, or live download speeds. It can also be used as a focus timer. The digital face changes based on what data you’re displaying, but the big needle is completely mechanical, giving the data nerd in your home a way to monitor anything important to them without constantly pulling out their phone.

![](https://media.wired.com/photos/6b00bef9e6d43a3251b4bc90/4:3/w_640,c_limit/PlantWave.jpg)

The world is awash in tools to generate music with AI, and the results are often lacking. Far more interesting is this tool your musical loved one can use to generate music with PI—plant intelligence. PlantWave is a small box with a pair of electrical sensors that attach to the leaves of any plant or mushroom. The box measures the subtle electrical signals the plant gives off and converts that into musical data. Assign that data stream to one of many digital instruments in the companion app, and listen to the plants sing. We originally tested the PlantWave several years ago, but the app has since blossomed to include more sounds and even lets creators craft their own digital instruments.
