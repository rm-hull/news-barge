**The main disability benefit in England and Wales should be more closely linked to the additional costs claimants face, a government-commissioned review has recommended.**

The review on personal independence payment (Pip) led by Disability Minister Sir Stephen Timms said the current system was "not appropriate" for claimants who do not have any extra costs arising from their condition.

The government has committed to some of its recommendations, including fast-track payments for people with severe conditions, but will not respond fully to the plans until early next year.

The Conservatives said the year-long review was a "compete farce" and showed a "complete absence of substance".

Shadow work and pensions secretary Helen Whately added: "All we have is a report that tinkers around the edges of a failed system".

Pip is available to people with long-term physical or mental health conditions who have difficulty doing every day tasks, regardless of whether they work, and it is not linked to income or savings.

The number of people claiming Pip in England and Wales has roughly doubled since 2019 to around four million.

The Timms review was set up last year by Sir Keir Starmer's government after his aborted attempt to cut welfare spending, which was derailed by the threat of a rebellion by Labour MPs.

It was co-produced with groups representing disabled people, who had a role in drafting the report.

Giving a statement in the House of Commons, Sir Stephen said Pip "remains a vital source of support" but the system was "no longer fit for purpose".

He told MPs the current assessment process was "dehumanising" and focused on "what somebody cannot do", rather than "supporting independence".

The government has committed to a recommendation that people receiving end-of-life care or with "serious, well-evidenced conditions" would receive payments without a full assessment.

It has also agreed to reduce "unnecessary" reviews for people whose conditions are lifelong or unlikely to improve.

Under the report's proposals, cash payments would remain alongside better support to signpost people into education or employment, although claimants could choose to exchange part of their payment for practical services instead.

In the longer-term, the report recommends changes to the assessment process that look at someone's real-life additional costs and individual needs, focusing on "how people can live the life they want to live, rather than what they cannot do".

It proposes "improved" questions to understand the extra costs people face, although claimants would not be asked to calculate their costs or provide receipts.

## 'Rebalancing'

Currently eligibility for Pip is based on how difficult people find carrying out everyday tasks, with two levels of payment available.

But the review suggests future payment options could include bands more closely linked to extra costs.

The report says its approach is likely to lead to a "rebalancing" of payments, indicating that some claimants could lose out under a new system, either receiving less than they do currently or not qualifying for payments at all.

"Those with the highest additional costs, and so the most significant needs, should receive the most support," it says.

"Conversely, this benefit is not appropriate for those who do not incur any additional costs associated with being disabled."

The review also calls for an increase in face-to-face assessments, which has been show to reduce eligibility.

The remit of the review was not to produce savings, and it does not set out whether the reforms would reduce the overall welfare bill.

Spending on Pip is set to be about £32bn this financial year, a figure projected to rise to £41.5bn by the end of the decade.

It comes after a separate review by former minister Alan Milburn into young people not in education, employment or training (Neets).

The government welcomed Milburn's report, which included a recommendation to move under-25s off Pip and the health element of universal credit, and onto an entirely new benefits system.

This has lead to concerns about a two-tier system, where some people with the same conditions could get different levels of payments depending on their age.

The government has said it will consider the Timms review alongside Milburn's recommendations, with its final proposals set out early next year.

Sir Stephen said this would include detailing the overall costs of changes to the system.

## 'Months of uncertainty'

Eduin Latimer, senior research economist at the Institute for Fiscal Studies think tank, said the review's recommendations had "the potential to better tailor Pip to claimants' needs".

But he said this would also create challenges, "most importantly in defining what disability-related costs are included, and finding a fair way of measuring them".

Latimer said making it easier to apply for Pip in its current form would "likely increase the number of claimants - and hence spending".

He added: "If the government wants to make Pip easier to apply for while also keeping spending in line with current projections, they would likely have to tighten eligibility criteria or reduce award amounts."

Disability charities have largely welcomed the review's recommendations of fast-tracked support and fewer reassessments.

However, they have said any changes should not leave disabled people financially worse off.

The Liberal Democrats said younger disabled people would now face "months of uncertainty" whilst the government considers the separate reviews from Timms and Milburn, and urged ministers to say quickly what they intend to do.

Reform UK's Treasury spokesman Robert Jenrick said: "Timms' recommendations will not cut the ballooning benefits bill so taxes on working people will inevitably rise."

He said his party would "bring back rigorous in-person assessments and ensure benefits are only available for British people" in order to lower the welfare bill.

The disability benefit system is devolved in Scotland and will not change in line with England and Wales. However, the Timms Review could impact the funding the Scottish government receives.

Disability benefits are also devolved in Northern Ireland, but in practice it reflects any changes made to the system in England and Wales.
