## The new orbital economy is worth billions and increasingly involves private operators. Europe is seeking a strategy to avoid losing ground.

**Forty-five countries gathered in Rome** to discuss who will write the future rules for space. It is a race in which the United States and China are sprinting ahead, private players are gaining ground and Europe still has to close the gap in its capacity for autonomous access to orbit. This is the backdrop to the eighth edition of the *International Space Forum*, returning to Italy ten years after its debut in Trento.

The numbers show how crowded the race has become. In 2026 there are about 18,000 operational satellites around the Earth, almost twice as many as in 2020. And 88 percent of the satellites launched in the past year were linked to private operators, compared with 23 percent in 2010. Space is no longer just a matter for states and government agencies, it is a **rapidly expanding market** in which major private companies have taken on an increasingly important role.

However, the picture of launches also shows just how far **Europe** still**lags behind in terms of autonomy**. In 2025 there were 181 American orbital launch attempts and 92 Chinese ones, compared with only eight European launches. Yet the continent has an industrial value chain that is anything but marginal: according to *Eurospace*, it comprises more than 750 companies and institutions and in 2025 recorded almost 11 billion euro in sales.

It is precisely this **gap between industrial capacity and strategic autonomy** that makes the European question central. The Rome Forum, entitled "The Gate to Sky: Space for Peace", addresses the other side of the competition: who sets the rules, how to ensure the peaceful and sustainable use of space and how to prevent countries with fewer resources from being excluded from the new orbital economy.

Organised by the **Italian Space Agency (ASI) and the International Astronautical Federation (IAF)**, the forum forms part of Italy's presidency of the United Nations Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (COPUOS), which it took over in June for the 2026-2027 biennium. The role gives Rome a prominent position in international space diplomacy.

COPUOS, created in 1959 and made up of 110 member states, is the main multilateral United Nations body for cooperation and the peaceful use of space. Issues on the table include managing satellite traffic, dealing with debris in orbit, access to space technologies and the need to update international rules. These topics no longer concern only space agencies: satellites now underpin essential services for communications, navigation, environmental monitoring and security.

## Europe, first-class expertise but autonomy to be strengthened

This is also where Europe's position is measured. The continent has significant scientific and industrial assets, but still needs to turn them into a more autonomous strategic capability. In the new **race to the Moon**, Washington and Beijing have clearly defined national goals and programmes. Europe takes part in major international initiatives, but does not always manage to present itself as a single actor.

The **collaboration with the United States in the Artemis programme** is one example: the European Space Agency (ESA) is contributing to the mission with the service module for the Orion capsule, which is crucial for power, propulsion and life support. But the European contribution is not limited to acting as a technological partner.

The **Argonaut programme**, developed by ESA, aims to secure autonomous access to the lunar surface from the 2030s, with a** first lander planned by 2030**. The industrial consortium for the descent module will be led by Thales Alenia Space Italia, together with companies from other European countries.

The issue, therefore, is not just reaching the Moon, but **having the technologies, launchers and infrastructure needed in order not to be** entirely dependent on others. It is also a security challenge: in January 2026 the European External Action Service updated the EU's strategy for security and defence in space, highlighting as priorities the protection of orbital assets, reducing technological dependencies and strengthening Europe's ability to access space autonomously.

The European Union, in short, is not absent from the race. But its role remains divided between ESA's industrial and scientific dimension, EU policies and the initiatives of individual states. Coordination between these levels is precisely what makes the difference, in a field where national investment and decision making risk creating fragmentation rather than a shared strategy.

## Italy is banking on space diplomacy

For Italy, the Rome forum is an opportunity to showcase its role in this landscape. The presidency of **COPUOS**, entrusted to it for 2026-2027, allows the country to contribute directly to the debate on international rules, from the sustainability of activities in orbit to lunar exploration.** ASI president Teodoro Valente has been elected to chair the committee.**

During the forum, discussions focused on three broad strands: using space technologies to tackle climate change and prevent natural disasters; ensuring fairer access to satellite technologies for emerging countries; and building an international governance framework able to keep pace with the growth of the private sector.

Since the first edition in Trento in 2016, the initiative has involved around 130 countries, with stops in Nairobi, Buenos Aires, Reggio Calabria, Panama, Manama and Manila. In Rome the delegations worked on the "Rome Page", a final declaration intended to gather recommendations and priorities for international cooperation.
