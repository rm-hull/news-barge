Donald Trump’s agriculture secretary insists she did nothing wrong after a federal judge reprimanded her for sending “proselytizing” Christian messages to thousands of federal employees.

Now she wants to take the legal battle to the Supreme Court.

“Well, this is causing a massive conversation across the country. I’m so proud, obviously, of my faith,” USDA chief Brooke Rollins told Fox News personality Ainsley Earhardt on Tuesday.

“This is a country that’s based on faith. … I said ‘Jesus is risen’ on Easter. Now keep in mind, Joe Biden had ‘Transgender Visibility Day’ on Easter, which I guess was OK with the courts,” Rollins added. “We’re gonna fight it all the way to the Supreme Court.”

(Note: The International Transgender Day of Visibility falls on March 31 every year. The former president did not schedule the day to specifically coincide with Easter, which falls on a different day each year.)

![USDA Secretary Brooke Rollins wants to take a legal battle over her explicitly Christian emails to staff to the Supreme Court after a judge blocked her from ‘proselytizing’](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/18/2026-11-24T164008Z_353424949_RC2JCNANR8AK_RTRMADP_3_USA-FARM-RELIGION.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

A federal lawsuit accused Rollins of injecting explicitly Christian themes into work emails in an effort to pressure employees into conforming to her beliefs.

Last week, U.S. District Judge Trina L. Thompson said Rollins took her religious emails “too far” with “worrisome” messages that “portray her agency’s work as a mission to serve her particular Christian views” and blocked her from sending them while the legal battle plays out.

Rollins not only sent emails to mark Christian holidays but also injected Christian themes into messages marking secular holidays such as Independence Day and Thanksgiving, according to plaintiffs, which included a group of federal workers and a union that represents thousands of them.

After the lawsuit was filed earlier this year, she continued to send agency-wide emails on Memorial Day and Independence Day that quoted scripture and only “doubled down” on her “proselytizing messages,” according to court filings. She responded to the lawsuit on X with a message that it’s “just another opportunity to remind everyone: He is Risen.”

![Rollins sent an email to all USDA employees to commemorate Easter on April 5, telling workers ‘he is risen’ and indicating the resurrection of Jesus Christ is ‘the foundation of our faith’](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/07/23/14/43/Screenshot-2026-07-23-at-9-52-29-AM.png?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Federal workers “feel pressured to share in the Secretary’s religious beliefs, excluded and unwelcome in the workplace, and fearful of the consequences for expressing different beliefs,” according to court filings.

In her order, Judge Thompson said some of Rollins’s messages went “too far.”

“They suggest acquiescence that amounts to coercive pressure” in likely violation of the First Amendment’s Establishment clause, which prohibits the government from establishing a national religion or favoring one over another, Thompson wrote.

“By comparing federal employees to ‘the very first disciples to encounter our risen Lord in the Upper Room almost two thousand years ago’ and calling on those employees to carry out ‘the mission each of us has been called for’ with ‘Paschal joy,’ the Secretary is portraying the agency’s work as a mission to serve her particular Christian views,” Thompson wrote.

“Precisely because the tradition of worship and prayer is so personal and so cherished, protecting private devotion and spiritual reflection from government influence comes to the fore,” she added.

“The wisdom of the Founders was in recognizing that independence requires freedom of conscience. The command of the Establishment Clause, thus, is to prohibit the government from using its authority to try to sway an individual’s decision about whether and how to practice their faith.”

![The Trump administration has repeatedly been accused of violating First Amendment protections by sharing explicitly Christian messages with federal workers and members of the military](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/08/06/13/2259457401..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Thomas MaGee, one of the plaintiffs in the case, said in a statement to *The Independent* that the secretary “equates my professional calling to serve as a federal investigator for the USDA with her own personal calling to serve Jesus Christ.”

“She should not declare USDA's mission to be ‘our’ shared Christian mission. We are not government-sanctioned missionaries,” MaGee said. “We both took an oath to preserve, protect, and defend a Constitution that leaves religious faith in our secular republic as a matter of personal choice.”

Ashley Miller, another plaintiff, told *The Independent* earlier this year that there was “no escape from her proselytizing.”

“It was quite scary to come forward, but I am proud that we did so, knowing this will help everyone across USDA,” Miller said in the wake of the court’s ruling.

The Trump administration has repeatedly been accused of violating First Amendment protections by sharing explicitly Christian messages with federal workers and members of the military.

This summer, Trump’s Presidential Religious Liberty called for the dissolution of the separation of church and state, a fundamental principle that has been central to constitutional law for more than two centuries.

The commission, composed almost entirely of white Christians, has also recommended the repeal of the Johnson Amendment, which prevents tax-exempt organizations — including churches — from making political endorsements.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, an Evangelical Christian, has explicitly framed war with Iran through the lens of his faith and repeatedly invoked scripture and Christian prayer in his remarks.

Hegseth has also opened an “Office of Religious Affairs” that First Amendment advocates, religious freedom groups and active-duty service members fear could be used to elevate a Christian nationalist agenda informing U.S. military policy while ostracizing other faiths.

“Federal employees are public servants, not an audience for their bosses’ personal religious beliefs,” said Randy Erwin, president of the National Federation of Federal Employees, which represents more than 110,000 federal workers.

“Federal employees shouldn’t feel pressured to participate in religious expression or be made to feel that their faith, or lack thereof, affects their standing in the workplace,” Erwin said following last week’s ruling in the case against Rollins.

“Brooke Rollins is free to love Jesus, worship Jesus and talk about Jesus in her personal capacity,” the Freedom From Religion Foundation said in a statement in response to Rollins’s latest comments.

“But she was appointed to run the Department of Agriculture, not a church,” the group said. “Government employees shouldn’t have their boss’s religious beliefs preached to them.”
