## Twenty years after her husband's murder in London, one of the most notorious Russian intelligence operations on foreign soil, Marina Litvinenko warns Europe against negotiating with Putin from a position of weakness and says justice requires a trial of his regime in Russia.

The West's failure to respond decisively to the assassination of former Russian intelligence officer Alexander Litvinenko in London in 2006 emboldened Vladimir Putin, paving the way for Russia's war against Ukraine and its threats to wider European security, his widow Marina Litvinenko told Euronews.

Speaking on the 20th anniversary of her husband's death, following the publication of her memoir, *A Life in Fragments*, Litvinenko described his killing as "the first act of nuclear terrorism in London".

"And what was the reaction? It was not a very serious reaction at all. And what we have now? We have a war in Ukraine," she said.

She warned that Putin continued to test the West's resolve and that further hesitation risked an even wider conflict.

## **The images that shocked the world**

An outspoken Kremlin critic who had served in Russia's Federal Security Service (FSB), Alexander Litvinenko fled Russia with his family in 2000 and was granted political asylum in Britain the following year.

From exile, he continued to investigate alleged corruption within Russia's intelligence services and their links to organised crime. He became a British citizen in October 2006, just weeks before his death.

On 23 November, 2006, Litvinenko died at the age of 44 after being poisoned with radioactive polonium-210.

Photographs of Litvinenko lying in a London hospital bed, his head bald and his body ravaged by radiation poisoning, appeared on newspaper front pages around the world. They became an enduring symbol of the dangers faced by Putin's opponents, even in exile.

As his condition deteriorated, his wife refused to give up hope that he would survive.

"I still believe to last moment, Sasha will survive and he will continue to fight against this monster who is the president of Russia still now," Litvinenko recalled.

"And I believed he will survive and we will have a future life, but it did not happen. And I always remember how Sasha was strong to last minute of his life."

The assassination exposed the reach of Russia's intelligence services and foreshadowed further attacks on European soil attributed to Moscow, including the 2018 poisoning of former Russian intelligence officer Sergei Skripal and his daughter Yulia in Salisbury and the 2019 murder of former Chechen separatist commander Zelimkhan Khangoshvili in Berlin.

A British public inquiry concluded in 2016 that Litvinenko's murder was carried out by Andrei Lugovoi and Dmitry Kovtun and that the operation was probably approved by both then-FSB chief Nikolai Patrushev and Putin himself.

In 2021, the European Court of Human Rights ruled that Russia was responsible for Litvinenko's death and had failed to conduct an effective investigation. Moscow has denied responsibility.

## **Europe's pursuit of 'business as usual' with Putin**

"It was not a strong reaction in 2006 because United Kingdom was alone," Marina Litvinenko told Euronews.

Despite the EU's economic strength, she said European governments remained reluctant to jeopardise their relations with Moscow, believing they could continue doing business with Putin.

Now, amid US-led diplomatic efforts to end Russia's war against Ukraine, Western governments are once again debating the terms of engagement with Moscow.

Asked whether the West should negotiate with Putin, Litvinenko said: "Of course you have to negotiate, but not in a way what you allowed Putin to have what he wants. You need to be much stronger and believe in yourself."

She pointed to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy as an example for European leaders.

"You can see the Ukrainian president who is very strong in his small country and fighting against this huge country and what everybody believes like a second army in the world," Litvinenko said.

"European Union, it's much bigger and stronger. And you just trust yourself."

## **Justice must ultimately come from Russia**

Neither of the two men identified by the British public inquiry as responsible for Litvinenko's death faced trial in the UK.

While his widow regards the legal proceedings in London and Strasbourg as significant achievements, she believes ultimate accountability must come from within Russia itself.

"But justice only happened after Russia will have their own trial against this regime," she said.

Litvinenko sees the end of the war in Ukraine as a potential turning point, expressing confidence that her husband's case would form a central part of any future prosecution of Putin's government for crimes she attributes to it over the past two decades.

## **'He would be on the side of Ukraine'**

Reflecting on how her husband might view Russia and Europe today, Litvinenko recalled his opposition to Putin's second war in Chechnya and his support for the Chechen independence movement.

She described his support for exiled Chechen leader Akhmed Zakayev, a close friend of the family, as part of his efforts to bring about change in Russia.

It "was a part of his Russian duty to make Russia possible to change", she said.

Twenty years after his murder, Litvinenko has no doubt where her husband would stand today.

"And now I'm 100% sure he would be on the side of Ukraine."
