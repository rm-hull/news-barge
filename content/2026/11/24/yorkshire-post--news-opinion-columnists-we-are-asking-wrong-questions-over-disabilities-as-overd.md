# ​We are asking wrong questions over disabilities as 'overdiagnosis' debate spreads: Kay Kempers

## There is something deeply troubling about a society that becomes more concerned about the number of people being diagnosed with disabilities than about why so many have struggled for years without recognition or support.

The recently-published independent review by Professor Peter Fonagy into mental health conditions, ADHD and autism has reignited precisely this debate. Its findings have prompted familiar anxieties about rising diagnoses, over-medicalisation and whether too many people are being labelled as neurodivergent. However, this risks asking entirely the wrong questions.

The review acknowledges that population prevalence of ADHD and autism has remained broadly stable, although reliable contemporary data is limited. What has increased dramatically is the number of people being identified and recorded by services. These are fundamentally different measures.

An increase in recorded diagnoses does not automatically mean an increase in the underlying population with those conditions. It may also reflect growing awareness, improved recognition and generations of previously overlooked people finally finding explanations for their experiences.

Conflating these measures is not simply statistically careless, it risks fuelling prejudice against people who already face significant barriers to participation in education, employment and society.

In a political climate where disabled people are increasingly portrayed as ‘economic burdens’, such misunderstandings carry consequences.

The review itself recognises that there is insufficient evidence to conclude that overdiagnosis is occurring at scale. Yet its repeated emphasis on the possibility of overdiagnosis risks giving disproportionate prominence to a concern that remains unquantified. Meanwhile, the consequences of under-recognition are painfully familiar.

In higher education, we encounter students every year who are only beginning to understand that they may be neurodivergent. Some discover possible dyslexia through university screening, having navigated their entire school education without anyone recognising their needs.

Others arrive with ADHD or autism diagnoses, or are still seeking assessment, after years of being told they are lazy, difficult, disorganised or simply not trying hard enough. For these students, recognition is not an exercise in acquiring a fashionable label. It can transform how they understand themselves, their learning and their potential. Yet the prevailing public conversation too often frames neurodivergence through deficits: what people cannot do, where they fall short and how much support they supposedly cost.

Neurodivergence is neither inherently a deficit nor a superpower. It is a difference.

And difference should not automatically translate into disadvantage. This is where the review offers something genuinely valuable, but also where we must demand greater ambition.

Its recommendation to move towards needs-led support, rather than making assistance dependent upon diagnosis, is welcome. So too is its recognition that environments shape people’s opportunities to participate. However, these principles must become practical commitments, not convenient justifications for restricting access to assessment or specialist provision.

In education, inclusion should never depend upon a student producing clinical paperwork before their needs are taken seriously. Accessible teaching, flexible approaches to assessment, clear communication and inclusive learning environments benefit everyone.

They do not diminish standards or disadvantage neurotypical learners. They create more opportunities for students to demonstrate what they know and can achieve. Universities have a particular responsibility here, not least because we educate the teachers and other professionals who will shape future generations’ experiences of learning.

At Leeds Beckett University’s Carnegie School of Education, our commitment to social justice is inseparable from our responsibility to champion inclusive practice. If we genuinely believe in social justice, inclusion cannot remain an aspiration confined to institutional strategies. It must inform how we teach, assess, design curricula and prepare graduates for professional life.

We must also recognise that disability does not exist in isolation from race, class and other inequalities.

Those whose differences are less visible, or whose experiences do not fit prevailing assumptions, can face additional barriers to recognition, credibility and belonging.

Ultimately, the measure of a successful society should not be how effectively it limits diagnostic numbers, but how well it enables people to flourish.

The Fonagy Review presents an opportunity to rethink support. We should take that opportunity seriously. At the same time, we must refuse to let a conversation about improving systems become another public debate about whether disabled people are somehow asking for too much.

The problem is not that too many people are being recognised. It is that too many have spent too long being excluded.

*Kay Kempers is a Lecturer within Interdisciplinary Studies at the Carnegie School of Education*
