## Europe plans to triple data centre capacity within seven years, narrowing the gap with the US and China. This will boost AI competitiveness but also increase resource use and environmental harm. But how can the EU regulate this frantic surge?

While the US has about 5,044 to 5,427 data centres, the EU only has 2,269, which severely limits its cloud capacity. This means that Americans have roughly 44% of global IT power capacity, compared to Europe's 15%. So, the EU aims to implement a major legislative and financial strategy to accelerate construction without disregarding green standards.

Though data centres are essential to run everything from everyday apps, cloud services, and banking to artificial intelligence and critical public infrastructure, they also pose severe environmental issues.

They strain local power grids and ecosystems by consuming massive amounts of electricity, draining millions of gallons of water for cooling, generating significant carbon emissions and electronic waste, and competing for land.

So, can the EU expand cloud capacity while remaining sustainable? And how does it plan to regulate data centre expansion?

### What are data centres for?

Data centres are large, secure facilities that hold digital servers, storage systems, and networking equipment. Modern society cannot function without them, as they run the critical systems for daily life. For example, they process contactless card payments at shops, execute online bank transfers, and run ATM networks.

"Every element of our lives has a digital element, and every one of those digital elements lives in a data centre. A data centre is like a little brain, and we have all these nerve endings that connect to our phones and our computers", said Michael Winterson, Secretary General of the European Data Centre Association (EUDCA).

They are also highly necessary for hospitals and clinics (which rely on them to store and retrieve electronic health records, lab results, and medical imaging), and public transport. Commuters rely on data centres for digital tickets, real-time train and bus arrival boards, railway signalling, and smart traffic systems to manage road congestion.

Other everyday tools, like video conferencing, cloud documents, university portals, and digital IDs run continuously from these facilities. Because digital information travels through underground fibre-optic cables at nearly the speed of light, physical distance factors into response time. Keeping data centres close, in Europe, cuts delays and is overall more efficient.

Keeping the data in Europe ensures that sensitive citizen and business information remains strictly protected under EU privacy laws like the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). This protects vital records from foreign surveillance laws and extraterritorial government access requests. Storing information within EU borders also eliminates the costly legal hurdles and compliance risks of transferring personal data outside the European Economic Area.

### A power and environmental challenge

But this efficiency comes at a price. Data centres have been largely criticised for putting too much pressure on Europe’s local power grids because modern cloud and AI facilities require enormous amounts of electricity around the clock.

In major connectivity hubs like Dublin, Amsterdam, and Frankfurt, local electricity networks are already running out of capacity, forcing utility operators to place years-long waiting lists or outright bans on new grid connections. Critics point out that upgrading high-voltage transmission lines takes years, meaning data centres often compete directly with housing developments, public transport electrification, and local industries for limited power supplies.

Winterson acknowledges the concerns. “The water debate is real. Europe is suffering serious water scarcity. We, as an industry, are looking into ways to reduce our water consumption, and in fact, we are now measuring water consumption publicly, and we use water at a rate of 0.4 L per kilowatt of energy”, he explained.

High-density servers generate intense heat, and traditional cooling systems rely on evaporating millions of litres of fresh water every day to prevent equipment from overheating. In drought-prone regions across Southern and Western Europe, local communities and farmers worry that industrial cooling drains precious drinking water reserves during hot summer months.

Environmental groups also warn that running 24/7 keeps fossil-fuel power plants online when wind and solar generation dip, while banks of diesel backup generators, concrete construction, and rapid server replacement cycles add to carbon emissions and electronic waste.

Likewise, expanding data centre infrastructure in Europe is slowed down by land shortages. Developers struggle to find large plots of available land near major cities not only for the warehouses themselves, but also for the extensive underground corridors needed to lay high-voltage power lines and fibre-optic internet cables.

At the same time, Europe’s industrial electricity prices remain much higher than those in the US, driving up both construction and daily running costs.

### An economic boost

Still, the EU has strategic reasons to press ahead with building local data centres. At a global level, having domestic computing infrastructure is essential for developing homegrown AI, since training and running advanced AI models requires huge, specialized computing power. By building its own high-capacity facilities, Europe gives its researchers, universities, and startups the tools they need to create EU trained AI systems rather than falling behind the US and China.

Expanding local data centres is also critical to reducing Europe’s heavy reliance on foreign technology providers. Winterson argues that building more capacity at home helps to turn "Europe from a net importer of digital services today to a net exporter", as most cloud infrastructure and AI computing power used across the continent are owned and operated by non-European tech giants.

Without its own physical servers and cloud networks, Europe remains vulnerable to geopolitical tensions, sudden supply chain disruptions, or changes in foreign trade and export policies.

Then, from an economic perspective, data centre construction acts as a powerful magnet for international and domestic investment. Global technology firms and infrastructure funds are committing tens of billions of euros to build new campuses across Europe, bringing substantial capital into regional economies. And over time, clustering data centres in a region often attracts software companies, research labs, and digital businesses, creating thriving local tech ecosystems.

### The EU wants greener data centres

To limit environmental impacts, the EU proposed a common data centre sustainability rating scheme in September as part of its 2025 energy efficiency roadmap. It complements the Energy Efficiency Directive (2023), which asks facilities to report on sustainability indicators, including energy consumption, waste heat and water usage.

The scheme aims to make data centres greener. It requires facilities above 500 kW to disclose their energy and water usage, helping integrate them into Europe's energy system.

An automatically generated label on a scale from A to G will rate centres’ sustainability. It will show centres' power grade, the Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE), and water grade, the Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE). Both will be calculated by dividing facilities' total energy and water use by the energy used by their IT equipment. A PUE of 1.15 or below, or a WUE of 0.1 or below, earns an A. A PUE above 1.9 and a WUE above 1.0 earn a G.

Labels will also display other sustainability features, including the energy source, grid flexibility, whether the site is “waste heat reuse ready”, and cooling degree days (CDD). They will become operational by August 15, 2027.

On the digital side, the EU’s latest Cloud and AI Development Act strengthens Europe’s cloud and AI ecosystem through acceleration zones with streamlined permitting for data centres. The Data Act (2023) makes it easier for customers to switch cloud providers and reduces the risk of fines, while the NIS2 Directive (2022) protects critical infrastructure and essential services from cyber threats, including data centre service providers.

### Where are most data centres located?

Most data centre capacity remains concentrated in traditional hubs. Germany held 2,167 MW of IT power in 2026 (projected to grow to 4,228 MW in 2031), ahead of the Netherlands (1,086 MW) and France (874 MW), according to EUDCA’s “State of European Data Centres” 2026 report. Among single facilities, Frankfurt led with 1,273 MW in 2026 and is set to reach 2,546 MW in 2031, followed by Amsterdam (1,454 MW) and Paris (1,013 MW).

But power, renewables, and submarine cables are driving European expansion, with faster growth beyond traditional hubs, bringing colocation IT power in Europe to a projected 23.8 GW.

Southern Europe is on a strong growth trajectory, led by Portugal, Spain and Italy, whose combined capacity rises from 682 MW in 2024 to nearly 5.9 GW by 2031. Madrid and Milan attract significant investment thanks to new transatlantic and Mediterranean cable systems, expanding cloud-region commitments and strong renewable-energy potential.

The Nordics, the second-fastest-growing region, remain a leading destination for large-scale colocation and AI-capable facilities, thanks to abundant, renewable energy. Capacity is set to exceed 4.4 GW by 2031, supported by competitive electricity prices and a strong track record in developing large-scale campuses.

Growth in Central and Eastern Europe remains modest but continues to rise steadily, increasing from 690 MW in 2024 to 1.5 GW in 2031. Poland and Romania drive the growth, with demand increasingly tied to sovereign cloud, regional resilience strategies, and low-latency coverage in EU border regions. North Western Europe, including Belgium and Luxembourg, is also gaining importance, EUDCA found
