Meta launches AI assistant Muse - are you planning to use it?

Mark Zuckerberg’s Meta is launching its Muse AI assistant in the UK and is encouraging users to let it loose on their apps – and the high street.

Muse has been a hit in the US and is ranked first and second respectively on Google and Apple’s free app store, having been downloaded millions of times.

Muse can be used as an app or directly within Meta’s WhatsApp messaging platform. Meta invites users to give their assistant a name, a look and a personality, so they can talk to it as well as assign it tasks such as sending an email, booking travel or buying festive presents.

Meta says:

“From doing the Christmas shopping and searching for a cheaper energy deal, to sorting the school calendar … Muse can get it done for you.”

Meanwhile in the bond markets, the UK has paid a record high price to sell inflation-linked bonds.

Britain’s DebtManagementOffice sold £3.75bn of an index-linked gilt which matures in 2049, at a real yield (the return after inflation) of 2.5235%. That, Reuters has calculated, is the highest for a syndicated sale since records began in 2005.

Nvidia’s board is facing criticism after being bounced into taking Jensen Huang back as CEO.

New York City Comptroller MarkLevine, who helps manage retirement funds that hold some 5 million Nvidia shares, believes the whole situation undercuts the board’s standing.

Levine told Reuters:

“They told us flatly that he would not be coming back. ​They have done a total 180 [degree turn] without any explanation, or any indication that concerns about legal and other ‌liabilities have been resolved.

“They ‌have proven they’re not independent, and that is the defining quality that we need from any board.”

Plus, as my colleague Nils Pratley wrote last night, Nvidia’s board hasn’t even completed its investigation into reports that Huang was “the target of a foreign intelligence effort”…

In Taiwan, Jensen Huang’s birthplace, some people have been welcoming Nvidia’s surprise decision to bring him back as CEO, Associated Press report.

“He is quite important. All I can say is he is nice. He is a really good guy. Yeah. We all like him a lot,” said Hu Ke-hsin, 50, an employee at a preserved fruit store in Taipei that Huang used to visit.

Sophia Chou, a Taipei City resident, was also supportive:

“He is a leading figure (in the AI industry), right? Lots of industries in Taiwan still need AI to develop. So, from an ordinary person’s point of view, I am happy he is back.”

Jensen Huang is expected to be called to testify before the Senate Banking Committee next Thursday. In what could be a dramatic public interrogation of the now well-known tech CEO, the hearing will focus on oversight of US export controls amid a growing Congressional push to limit chip exports to China.

The US has sought to curb Beijing’s access to Nvidia’s advanced AI chips because it wants its own companies to stay ahead in the race for technological dominance.

However, Huang has reportedly lobbied the Trump administration to loosen restrictions on selling Nvidia chips to China, and has criticised what he frames as overly restrictive export controls that have encouraged Chinese rivals to accelerate the development of their domestic AI products.

The chairs of the House committees on China, intelligence and foreign affairs confirmed Huang will appear for separate transcribed interviews next Tuesday and Wednesday, as previously planned.

Huang will likely be asked whether US national security was compromised by his reported affair with suspected Chinese spy Alice Huang, who had been executive director of Megaspeed International. Alice Huang and Jensen Huang are not related, and Chinese officials have denied that she was a spy.

Since 2024, the Singapore-based company Megaspeed - the biggest buyer of Nvidia chips in South East Asia - has been under investigation by US authorities over whether it diverted AI chips to China. The US commerce department added Megaspeed to its trade blacklist on Monday, citing national security and foreign policy concerns.

Hong Kong’s stock market missed out on the Nvidia bounce today, with its Hang Seng index dipping by 0.73%.

Russ Mould, investment director at AJ Bell, suggests this may reflect fears of a clampdown on US AI chip exports:

“The big Wall Street rally on Monday was followed by gains in some parts of Asia on Tuesday, although trading was mixed and weakness in Hong Kong could reflect some fears that Huang’s return might be accompanied by stricter limits on the export of AI technology.

“Once the party poppers have been put away, and the chants of ‘Jensen, Jensen’ have faded within the walls of Nvidia’s HQ, investors may be mindful of a hangover as they start to weigh some of the potential political and governance implications of Huang’s return to the hot seat.

“The FTSE 100 was one step removed from all the drama given its limited tech exposure. Brent crude remained firmly below $90 per barrel on hopes of diplomatic progress in the Middle East. That is potentially good news for inflation but less positive for the UK market’s contingent of energy shares.”

German foreign minister 'shocked' by Trump's intervention

Germany’s foreign minister JohannWadephul has revealed he was “shocked” by US President Donald Trump’s intervention to have Jensen Huang reinstated as chief executive of Nvidia, the AFP newswire report.

Wadephul told a Berlin event, when asked about Trump’s move:

“It shocked me, to be honest, because as far as I know, if I’m personally looking back, I never saw and recognised such an intervention from the political side to a special company.”

Update: Wadephul added:

“I do have some doubts whether this is a very good model for the future,”

“In free, democratic and rules-based countries, political power should refrain from interfering [with companies]”.
