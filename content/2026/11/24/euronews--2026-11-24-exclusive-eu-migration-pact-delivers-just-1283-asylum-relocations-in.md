## EU member states have diluted the Migration Pact's "mandatory solidarity" despite an annual minimum of 30,000 relocations.

Only 1,283 asylum seekers will be relocated between EU countries in 2026, the first year of the bloc's new migration rules, Euronews has learned from EU and diplomatic sources.

The Pact on Migration and Asylum's principle of "mandatory solidarity" has met resistance from most member states, which have repeatedly watered down the relocation mechanism, using a range of tactics to minimise their commitments to frontline countries.

While the European Commission initially proposed relocating 30,000 asylum seekers from Italy, Spain, Greece and Cyprus, only 562 have been transferred so far, all from Cyprus to Lithuania, Romania, Bulgaria and France.

A diplomat said further flights from Cyprus are scheduled before the end of the year, bringing the total to 1,283.

That is a tiny fraction of the 669,000 people who applied for asylum in the EU in 2025.

## **How member states watered down solidarity**

Under the Asylum and Migration Management Regulation, a central pillar of the Pact, the Commission must propose an annual "solidarity pool" setting out how member states should support one another.

The pool has two components: a quota of relocations from countries deemed to be "under migratory pressure" and a quota of financial contributions from other member states. These can also take the form of "alternative solidarity measures", such as funding construction projects or purchasing equipment.

The minimum annual threshold is 30,000 relocations and €600 million in financial contributions. Each member state is assigned a "fair share" based on its population and GDP, but governments can choose how to fulfil their obligations.

For 2026, Spain, Italy, Greece and Cyprus were classified as being under migratory pressure, making them eligible for solidarity measures.

The Czech Republic, Croatia, Austria and Poland were granted exemptions from their quotas because they were deemed to be "facing a significant migratory situation". The remaining 18 countries were required to contribute, either financially or by accepting asylum seekers. Denmark is excluded from the scheme.

But member states argued that the first round of contributions should be reduced proportionately, since the new rules would apply only during the second half of 2026.

In December 2025, EU interior ministers agreed on 21,000 relocations "or other solidarity efforts" and €420 million in financial contributions, scaling back the Commission's original proposal.

Even then, the 21,000 relocations existed largely on paper.

Hungary and Slovakia refused to contribute anything, in an apparent breach of the mandatory solidarity principle that the Commission has yet to sanction.

Of the countries required to contribute, only seven agreed to accept relocated asylum seekers, while eight opted to provide financial support alone.

The total number of pledged relocations fell below 9,000, dramatically reducing the support available to Spain, Italy, Greece and Cyprus.

Member states also turned to another legal mechanism to reduce, or avoid altogether, the physical transfer of asylum seekers from frontline countries: the "responsibility offset."

Under this scheme, a country takes charge of a set number of "secondary movement" asylum seekers already on its territory, people who should have applied in their first EU country of arrival.

Nina Gregori, director of the EU Agency for Asylum (EUAA), told Euronews that contributing countries had struck bilateral agreements with frontline states to make use of the mechanism.

"The offset [has been] used for solidarity from Spain, Italy and Greece, so no physical transfers or relocation of asylum seekers [from these countries] is happening in this first policy cycle," she told Euronews in an interview.

Cyprus is therefore the only frontline country from which asylum seekers have actually been relocated under the scheme.

The EUAA helped transfer 253 asylum seekers from Cyprus to Romania and another 80 to Bulgaria.

"Others have been transferred to France and Lithuania, but without direct support of the agency," Gregori said.
