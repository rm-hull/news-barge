Court documents unsealed Monday show that a magistrate judge ruled Ilhan Omar could invoke privacy protections available to sitting members of Congress at the start of a recent search of her home by federal agents. U.S. Magistrate Judge Matthew Sharbaugh authorized the search of the Minnesota Democrat's residence.

The filings, released after media organizations sued for access, lay out how Justice Department attorneys and the judge worked through an initial disagreement over the constitutional questions raised by searching a member of Congress' private home as part of a criminal investigation.

Heavily redacted, the documents do not appear to shed new light on why the search occurred or what it produced. They also do not disclose new details of an immigration-fraud investigation that Omar says is politically motivated.

Ilhan Omar, D-MN, speaks at a forum held by the Democratic Women's Caucus titled "Trump's Report Card: Failing Working Women? on Capitol Hill](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/22/09/2026-10-16T001826Z_1713319104_RC27FJABUSZ6_RTRMADP_3_USA-ELECTION-MINNESOTA-OMAR.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

*The Associated Press*reported last month that federal authorities are looking into potential fraud in her family's immigration paperwork.* The AP,* citing a person briefed on the investigation who spoke on condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to discuss an ongoing matter, said grand jury subpoenas had been issued to some of her relatives.

Before authorizing the search, Sharbaugh raised concerns about the “Speech or Debate Clause” implications of searching a private residence belonging to a member of Congress, the unsealed filings show.

The clause is designed to protect legislators from executive branch interference or intimidation, and includes a privilege against compelled disclosures. The government argued the privilege extends only to official congressional offices and certain electronic devices. Sharbaugh said he disagreed with that position.

![Rep. Omar, who was born in Somalia, has long been under attack for her place of birth from President Trump and his MAGA allies](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/22/17/2306095700..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“That logic does not hold,” the magistrate wrote. He concluded that the government's law-enforcement interests had to be weighed against Omar's constitutional rights as a member of Congress.

The government ultimately agreed to a search protocol allowing Omar to invoke a privilege over any Speech or Debate Clause material before agents carried out the search, he said. Omar, a naturalized U.S. citizen born in Somalia, has been a target of anti-immigrant rhetoric from President Donald Trump and many Republican allies.

She has not been charged with any crimes and has denied any wrongdoing. Department of Homeland Security agents carried out the Oct. 12 search of Omar's Washington, D.C., home. Chief Judge James Boasberg ordered the partial unsealing of court filings related to that search.

He ruled after over a dozen news organizations, including *The Associated Press,* argued that the public has a First Amendment right to access the records. Boasberg agreed with the government that it would be premature to unseal a 67-page search warrant affidavit containing sensitive details of the investigation.

In a court filing last month, the government argued that releasing the court records before a charging decision is made would jeopardize the investigation. An attorney for Omar did not immediately respond to an email seeking comment on Monday about the unsealed documents.

The court-authorized search took place less than a month before the Nov. 3 midterm elections, in which Omar won her reelection bid. The Justice Department has historically avoided public investigative actions involving a candidate within 60 days of an election.

Omar has said DHS agents seized 17 items. They included phones, notebooks, a Somali-language children's book and a vintage camera her husband had received as a gift.
