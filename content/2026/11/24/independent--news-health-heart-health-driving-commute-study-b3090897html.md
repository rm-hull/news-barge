Spendings hours behind the wheel commuting to and from work could come with long-term negative impacts for your heart health, a new study cautions.

High anxiety levels when driving adds an extra heartbeat each minute for people driving eight hours a day, the University of Houston researchers found.

That could carry long-term implications for the health of millions of Americans. Some 140 million employees routinely commute to work and 69 percent drive alone, according to U.S. Census Bureau data.

“Driving requires continuous attention, decisions and readiness to react. The body may be sitting, but the mind is working - and the heart reflects those demands,” senior author and computer science professor Ioannis Pavlidis said.

“People need to get to work and go about their lives, but we want to understand how they can do that with less strain.”

![High anxiety while on the road is straining commuters’ health, new research shows](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/21/42/GettyImages-1208192662.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

## More beats, more problems?

Additional heartbeats - often felt as fluttering or pounding in the chest - is usually okay in people with healthy hearts and is common in people under stress. But if they happen more frequently, that’s when doctors start to worry, according to Penn State Health doctors.

More heartbeats per minute can potentially cause a condition called cardiomyopathy, or a weakened heart muscle. Cardiomyopathy can result in heart failures, blood clots, cardiac arrest, the need for a heart transplant and sudden death, according to the Mayo Clinic.

Our hearts naturally beat more during exercise, and that’s generally a good thing. That’s what helps supply oxygen-rich blood to the muscles, New England Baptist Hospital says.

“But when it works harder while we are sitting behind the wheel, we get none of the benefits of exercise,” said Pavlidis. “And when this happens for prolonged periods, day after day, we must wonder about the long-term health consequences.”

## 180,000 observations

Pavlidis and his team used Apple Watches and iPhones to track the heartbeats of 57 adults across major Texas cities, including Houston, Dallas and San Antonio.

They collected nearly 180,000 individual observations each minute over the course of a week, and compared heart rates with weather and traffic data.

![The researchers tracked the health of drivers using wearable tech](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/21/55/GettyImages-2206502456.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Analyzing the data showed that heart rates were above their resting baseline for 94 percent of driving time. That’s compared with just 81 percent during sedentary activities at home.

More research is needed to understand what makes driving a more demanding activity on the heart.

Next, the Houston lab will examine more diverse participants over longer periods of time, including long-distance commuters and people who drive full-time for work.

“With autonomous ride services from Waymo and Tesla creating a new kind of commuter - one who becomes a passenger rather than a driver - the team also plans to measure whether the heart gets a break when the vehicle takes over,” the university said.
