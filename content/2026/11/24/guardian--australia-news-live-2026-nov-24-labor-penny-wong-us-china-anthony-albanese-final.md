'Multibillion dollar fines' under digital duty of care bill, Greens say

Josh Butler

The Greens have gotten out ahead of Anika Wells’ press conference on the digital duty of care, releasing a statement trumpeting the amendments they say they’ve secured – including massive new fines for tech companies.

“The Greens have long campaigned for big tech to be held accountable for the harm they profit off online,” Sarah Hanson-Young said in a press release.

Sarah Hanson-Young. Photograph: Hilary Wardhaugh/Getty Images

The senator for South Australia said the minor party would back the bill in exchange for “significant wins”, including the private right of action, and a pathway to an independent ombudsman for big tech.

Hanson-Young also said there would be an “increase in fines to 5% of global revenue, with multi-billion dollar penalties”, as well as “dumping the doom-scroll with rules that force tech companies to prevent harm from their addictive design feature”.

Wells confirms deal with Greens on digital duty of care

The communications minister, Anika Wells, has heralded a deal with the Greens to pass the digital duty of care bill, labelling it “seismic reform”.

Speaking to journalists in Canberra, Wells says among the amendments, private citizens will now be able to sue tech platforms.

The legislation will also strengthen privacy protections, expand eSafety’s content removal powers, include addictive tools as a specific risk to be assessed and will increase fines to up to 5% of global revenue – something the Greens wanted.

Wells thanks Greens senator Sarah Hanson-Young for being so constructive.

Minns unsure if prime minister will be on NSW election campaign trail

Returning to the NSW premier, Chris Minns has said he is “not sure” if Anthony Albanese, will be on the campaign trail at the March state election.

The Sydney Morning Herald reported today that candidates had dropped some Labor elements from their campaign material, describing themselves as part of “Chris Minns’ NSW team”, as the NSW party seeks to differentiate itself from federal Labor at the vote.

Minns rejected the idea that Albanese was toxic to the NSW campaign, but said he was “not sure” if Albanese would be on the campaign trail before the March vote.

I’m not sure, I mean we’ll have to work that out as we get closer to polling day. But you know, we’ve got a big challenge in front of us in NSW, and the truth of the matter is, it’s a state election campaign based on state issues to do with the people of this state …

That’s a point of difference with the state treasurer, Daniel Mookhey, who told reporters on Friday “we will be campaigning with the prime minister in the next election ... of course as we did with the prime minister in the last election”.

NSW premier backs another minister’s preselection amid accusations of ‘captain’s calls’

The NSW premier, Chris Minns, says the candidacy of a second sitting minister facing a preselection battle will instead go to Labor’s national executive, as he faces accusations of making “captain’s calls” on candidates at the March election.

Appearing at a press conference this morning alongside the state’s sports minister, Steve Kamper, who has been facing a preselection battle in his south Sydney seat of Rockdale, Minns said he expected the national executive to finalise the candidate for the seat in the coming days.

Chris Minns and Steve Kamper in October last year. Photograph: Steve Markham/AAP

Kamper is a close political ally of Minns, whose seat Kogarah, neighbours Rockdale to the west. Minns said the recommendation of Kamper’s candidacy came from “not just me, but the party officials and the leadership of the parliamentary and the organisational wing”.

Minns has faced accusations of making captain’s calls on several marginal seats in recent weeks, after publicly endorsing candidates without rank-and-file preselections and before the powerful national executive had signed off on them. Last week, he said he fully expected the fair trading minister, Anoulack Chanthivong, would be the candidate for his seat of Macquarie Fields after the party decided to send the preselection to the national executive.

Chanthivong, who has reportedly lost the support of branch members, rejected the idea Minns’ endorsement was a quid pro quo after the premier overruled his department on underquoting laws. Minns did not say if Chanthivong would remain in cabinet if Labor is reelected.

‘That’s for America to decide’: Lawrence Wong insists it’s up to the US to investigate Nvidia CEO

Lawrence Wong’s timing to Australia could not be more impeccable (for the media), as he and Anthony Albanese face more questions on the potential ramifications of Jensen Huang’s conduct and his reinstatement as chief executive of Nvidia.

He is asked whether he’s concerned that Huang’s conduct has inadvertently assisted China with its development of AI.

Wong says Singapore isn’t involved in the US’s investigation, and repeats that they’re still conducting their own into Megaspeed.

With regards to Jensen Huang’s role, we are not involved in those investigations, it’s for the American government to decide, and we leave them to make those investigations and judgment.

Nvidia’s board will have to do what it needs to do … President Trump has already spoken, they may still be ongoing checks.

Where we are concerned, as I mentioned just now. We are partner with the US. We are continuing to do our own investigations where it comes to companies breaching our own laws. We are very clear about that.

Jumping out of the press conference for a moment. As we brought you earlier, a Labor-Greens deal on the digital duty of care bill looks locked in.

The communications minister, Anika Wells, has just announced a press conference at 1pm, and the Greens have one at 1.30pm, where we’re expecting things to be confirmed officially.

Wells brought several amendments to the Digital Duty of Care bill to Labor’s party room this morning, including a plan for a new power to set up an external ombudsman for big tech in future.

That was one of the Greens’ key requests. The Greens have also been pushing for higher fines, and moves to address “addictive” design features on social media; sources this morning indicated a deal had been close, and it seems it’s gotten over the line.

We’ll bring you more from those press conferences as they happen.

Singapore investigating if Megaspeed breached laws

Further to the last post, Lawrence Wong says Singapore is investigating Megaspeed, the company Alice Huang worked for (no relation to Jensen Huang).

We cannot investigate, you know, all that’s happening in these third countries. So we did require more information from the US side, and we are continuing to have discussions with American authorities … to confirm the verification of various documents, but these investigations into Megaspeed are ongoing.

We are focusing on whether Megaspeed might have breached any Singapore laws. And again, we are continuing with our investigations.

Anthony Albanese is also asked for his thoughts: he says Australia is concerned about the issue, but that the country is not a party to it.

Megaspeed International’s office in Singapore. Photograph: Taryn Ng/AFP/Getty Images

‘We do not want Singapore to be used and abused’ in chip fight, says Wong to question on Jensen Huang

The first question comes from SBS reporter Anna Henderson, who asks the Singaporean prime minister about the Jensen Huang debacle, and whether his nation is a “weak link” in chips being sent to China.

Lawrence Wong says the US has export controls on its chips while Singapore facilitates checks “by America to enforce its own laws”.

Wong says Singapore was investigating Alice Huang, who is suspected by the US of being a Chinese spy, but had released her.

Jensen Huang. Photograph: Carlos Barría/Reuters

On Megaspeed, a Singaporean company, Wong says they were being investigated but chips that were bought from Nvidia did not pass through his nation.

We do not want Singapore to be used and abused as a conduit by companies to circumvent America’s export controls. So what we do is to facilitate checks by America to enforce its own laws. At the same time, we are also very clear we do not want companies to breach Singapore laws.

So it’s on that basis that we were investigating Megaspeed, which is Singapore-based entity. And we also were investigating Alice Huang herself. But in the course of our investigations … We took her in, we investigated her, but we eventually released her because there was no basis for further prosecution. America understands what we have done.

We did ask for some information, not all of it was forthcoming, but we did what we did with her, and the investigations with Megaspeed are ongoing.

Albanese holds joint press conference with Singaporean counterpart

The prime minister, Anthony Albanese, and his Singaporean counterpart, Lawrence Wong, just held a joint press conference in Canberra. Albanese said the two countries had a “deep trust” centred on six decades of cooperation.

Albanese said Australia and Singapore had agreed on several efforts to “deepen this important relationship”. That includes a memorandum of understanding on essential supplies and a “first of its kind” treaty on “transboundary carbon capture and storage”.

Wong and Albanese at a press conference at Parliament House. Photograph: Saeed Khan/AFP/Getty Images

Albanese said:

The treaty will support regional decarbonisation and attract new investment in Australian industry.

Albanese said the pair had also agreed on a joint statement on artificial intelligence to work on regional and global approaches to AI “while ensuring this technology remains under human control”.

Federal and state budget spending could impact rate relief, ANZ says

There is another factor that could determine whether mortgage holders get some rate relief by mid-2027: how much new spending there is in the May federal budget and state budgets.

“If the discussion in Canberra were to shift from finding budget savings to further support for the economy, then the sequence of policy easing in 2027 might also be fiscal first, monetary second,” ANZ’s Adam Boyton says.

In other words, a rate cut in June next year depends in part on governments not ploughing too much extra money into the economy between now and then.

The treasurer said next month’s mid-year budget would show expected growth of “well below” 2% in each of the coming two years, and that unemployment would push above 5%.

Adam Boyton, ANZ’s head of Australian economics, writes that “a plausible path to a rate cut in the first half of 2027 requires three elements to come together over the next six months”.

This trio is: “A further deterioration in the labour market, evidence that excess demand is unwinding, and a step-down in underlying inflation.”

He went on:

There is already evidence that points to each of these being likely to occur over that time frame.

Price tag for new AFL stadium in Tasmania goes up – again

The estimated cost for Tasmania’s new AFL stadium has gone up again. Updated figures for the 23,000-seat roofed waterfront venue at Macquarie Point in Hobart were released on Tuesday.

It puts the price tag on the stadium at $1.23bn, above the previous $1.13bn mark set about 12 months ago, AAP reports.

The new estimate reflects an increase in the cost of construction, sustained high oil and materials prices, higher labour costs and its complexity, the state government said.

The state’s premier, Jeremy Rockliff,previously pledged to cap Tasmania’s contribution to the build at $875m but said that would probably rise via more borrowing to cover the $100m rise. He said:

Major projects are always challenging, but as a state we must be able to say yes to opportunity, new industry and economic growth.

Tasmanian premier Jeremy Rockliff. Photograph: Ethan James/AAP

Copyright rules already changing to adapt to AI, Wong says

The government is facing serious pressure to protect artists and creatives from having their work stolen by AI, as Labor works on a package to respond to and safeguard against the technology.

The foreign affairs minister will give a speech to the Senate later this afternoon, outlining how AI is changing the way countries interact and wars are fought.

On an issue closer to home, in an interview on News24, Penny Wong is asked if it is “inevitable” that copyright laws will have to change. The tech platforms have been asking for greater access to Australian material to train their AI models.

Penny Wong. Photograph: Mick Tsikas/AAP

Wong tells News24:

Australian artists are not protected in a number of jurisdictions already. So we already have this challenge with copyright.

The question is, how do we make sure that Australian artists, Australians creatives have control over what they produce and are appropriately remunerated or rewarded if their content is used. And that’s what the attorney general is working on.

I think it’s already changing. And I think the world is showing that, in terms of the jurisdictions have already moved. So, I think copyright, it’s a very good system, but it did operate in a world … without artificial intelligence.
