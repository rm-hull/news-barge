There were more than 5,000 linked to heat in England this year, the highest annual total since records began in 2016, the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) has announced.

An estimated 5,258 deaths were associated with heat after the country experienced the warmest summer on record. Parts of the country experienced multiple heatwaves over several months, with the first in late May and the last in mid-August.

The year saw the UK experience its warmest summer on record, with parts of the country enduring multiple heatwaves over several months, beginning in late May when temperatures peaked at 35.1C at Kew Gardens in London, an all-time high for the month.

The heatwave at the end of June prompted a rare red alert from the Met Office for extreme heat and saw temperatures reach 38.0C on June 26 in Lingwood in Norfolk, another new monthly record.

As the scorching summer continued, England saw its driest July since data began, with only 7mm of rain measured, or 10 per cent of the long-term average.

The heatwave in mid-August saw temperatures climb as high as 38.2C at Hartpury College in Gloucestershire, making it the hottest day of the year.

The latest UKHSA report, which covers the period from late May to late August, adds that the “analysis is based on provisional mortality data and the final number will be published in the annual official statistics report in early 2027”.

UKHSA observed summer 2026 saw “an unusually high number of heat episodes, several periods of sustained hot weather” and noted that “more people living with health conditions sensitive to heat, increasing the number of people potentially vulnerable to the health effects of extreme heat”.

The highest number of heat-associated deaths were recorded between 21 and 28 June, when an estimated 2,401 deaths were linked to this period of extreme heat.

This year’s total of 5,258 deaths follows a run of much lower annual totals, with 2,295 in 2023, 1,311 in 2024 and 1,504 in 2025.

The report said intense heat early in the summer season “can pose particular risks as unusually hot weather may occur before people, communities and services have fully prepared and are ready to respond to hot weather conditions”.

![Parts of the country experienced multiple heatwaves over several months, with the first in late May and the last in mid-August](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/10/2289658839..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

It added: “These findings therefore reinforce the importance of heat-health preparedness measures being in place from the start of the season, rather than focusing solely on the warmest periods of mid-summer.”

Dr Ross Thompson, principal environmental health scientist at the UKHSA, said: “Heat can affect anyone, but older adults, young children, pregnant women and people with underlying health conditions are at greater risk.

“Climate change is already affecting people’s health in England, and as the climate continues to warm we can expect periods of extreme heat to become longer, more frequent and more intense.

“That is why it is vital that individuals, communities, health and care services and local organisations are prepared, not just during a heatwave but in the longer term.

“The decisions we make now will shape the health impacts people experience in the decades ahead.”
