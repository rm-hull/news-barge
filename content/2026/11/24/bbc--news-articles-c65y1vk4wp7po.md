Sabrina Bahsoon now has more than 900,000 followers across social media

**Sabrina Bahsoon became known globally as Tube Girl after self-shot videos of her dancing and lip-syncing on the London Underground blew up online.**

The 26-year-old content creator had the world at her feet when she went viral in 2023, going on to build a social media following of more than 900,000 people.

She signed major brand endorsement deals and was pursuing a music career.

But then her "whole life turned upside down" when she had what appeared to be a psychotic episode.

Her symptoms made her violent and erratic, leading to her being sectioned in a mental health hospital - where doctors treated her with anti-psychotic medication.

But Bahsoon's family, helped by her father's neurologist friend, pushed for further tests.

And two months into her hospital treatment, doctors got to the truth.

She actually had a rare, but treatable, autoimmune disease which is known to sometimes present as psychiatric symptoms, as well as seizures, confusion and memory loss.

In her first interview since the experience, she tells BBC News she wants hospitals to offer a series of tests as standard practice if patients have these symptoms.

"In a way I do feel let down by the care I received. I feel like the urgency to discover what was actually wrong with me wasn't there," she says.

"And I also feel as a woman being written off completely as a psychotic patient without further investigation was a mistake."

A spokesperson for the hospital she attended has apologised for Bahsoon's experience and says an investigation has been launched into her care.

![Sabrina Bahsoon sitting in a hospital bed. There are pillows propped up behind her. She is holding a black coffee mug.  ](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/6128/live/418c3c00-e752-11f1-88dc-f746e0cde621.jpg)

The content creator was in hospital for about four months earlier this year

Bahsoon first had "psychotic symptoms" in March this year after filming a video on the Tube with girl group Flo.

She says that evening she began to scream, cry and her conversations became "very erratic".

The next day she broke up with her boyfriend, which prompted her sisters to check on her, as it was so unexpected.

Bahsoon says her condition got worse, becoming violent towards herself and posting videos which didn't make sense, before her sisters called 999 for an ambulance.

"I'm normally a very relaxed person, maybe anxious at times, but the way I was speaking about harming myself and the way I was screaming and being violent was super out of character for me," she says.

A&E doctors initially thought her symptoms could have been provoked by stress and a lack of sleep, discharging her with sleeping tablets.

She then began destroying things at home and spoke about having hallucinations, which led to police bringing her to hospital in handcuffs.

Bahsoon was sectioned and moved to a psychiatric ward where she was treated with antipsychotic medication, which she says made her "docile".

"I went from being a total firecracker to being completely catatonic," she says.

"I stopped eating, drinking. I could not speak properly."

Bahsoon has "very faint memories" of this period, she says, but her family has since shown her clips of her having breakdowns which she finds "heartbreaking".

![Sabrina Bahsoon wears a black halterneck dress and has silver sparkly eyeshadow. She is on the red carpet of a film premiere event. ](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/feff/live/8d81d760-e751-11f1-88dc-f746e0cde621.jpg)

Sabrina Bahsoon was pursuing a music career at the height of her fame

After two months on the psychiatric ward, Bahsoon was diagnosed with an autoimmune disease called anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis.

It was caused by tumours in her ovaries, known as teratomas, producing antibodies which attacked her body.

She had surgery to remove the tumours in June, which she says brought on a "drastic" change in her behaviour from "freaking out" to having more understanding of what was happening around her.

Bahsoon has since been recovering back in her home country of Malaysia and is having therapy to "deal with all the trauma".

But she says she feels "very lucky" as doctors have told her she is part of a small percentage of people who they expect to make a full recovery.

The content creator first revealed what happened in a TikTok video in October, and says she is sharing her story as she wants to raise awareness of the disease.

She also wants to "push people to advocate for themselves" if they feel they're not getting the "treatment they deserve".

"I do want reform in the way that hospitals deal with psychotic patients or patients that present with psychotic symptoms," she says.

"I hope I'm sharing my story so that nobody else has to go through this."

She plans to return to her content creation in the new year and is looking forward to "enjoying life with a whole new perspective and appreciation for what I do and what I now can help people with".

## 'Real risk' of going undiagnosed

Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis is more typical in women and mainly affects young people, with about 40% of cases aged under 18, according to the charity Encephalitis International (EI), external.

Dr Ava Easton MBE, chief executive of EI, says a "frightening" aspect is how quickly someone's behaviour and personality can appear to change.

"A person may suddenly seem confused, distressed or [to be] experiencing psychosis when the underlying problem is actually inflammation of their brain," she says.

She says there's a "real risk" of it going undiagnosed and encourages people to look out for the warning signs, external.

According to the NHS, external, cases of all types of encephalitis are rare, and EI says the condition affects about 6,000 people in the UK each year.

A spokesperson for Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust, which was the first to treat Bahsoon, said they were "very sorry" for what she experienced.

They said an internal review found some aspects of her initial assessment "were not followed up as they should have been" and an investigation had been started.

Dr Ed Beveridge, divisional medical director at Central and North West London NHS Foundation Trust, also confirmed they'd be part of that review.

He said anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis was a "rare autoimmune condition" that is not "routinely screened for nationally".

"We are pleased that her condition has now been identified and treated."
