Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth sent out a memo reportedly demanding that U.S. military officials seek permission before socializing with their Canadian counterparts, as relations between the longtime allies continue to deteriorate.

Hegseth’s memo, circulated last week, was described by one Canadian official as “a slap in the face,” *The Washington Post* first reported.

The newspaper did not review the memo, reportedly drafted months ago but only signed recently. It comes as ties between the longtime allies have been strained by U.S. President Donald Trump's trade war and his repeated threats to make Canada the 51st U.S. state.

At a gathering of defense officials in Halifax, Canada, over the weekend, Republican Sen. Mike Rounds of South Dakota said that the memo “is suggesting in a roundabout way that there be less informal activity or social activities without permission.”

“I think there were some social events where our officers and some of their officers were getting together,” Rounds added, according to *The New York Times.*

![Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth sent out a memo asking US military officials to seek permission before socializing with their Canadian counterparts, as relations between the longtime allies continue to deteriorate](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/11/13/2304464396..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“There’s a desire, I think, to put some emphasis on maybe getting some agreements on military purchases or cooperation on those types of things before we move forward with a normalization of social activities,” he added.

The U.S. and Canada are both members of the “Five Eyes” intelligence-sharing consortium that also includes the U.K., Australia and New Zealand.

Chief Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell said that the guidance “does not impact defense intelligence cooperation, our binational NORAD command, or our cooperation through NATO.”

Canadian defense officials only learned of the memo from the *Post’*s reporting, according to Canadian outlet the* Globe and Mail.* Canadian Defense Minister McGuinty said Saturday he had not seen the memo and had detected no change in military cooperation.

“I’ve seen no evidence of any kind of change. I’ve heard about the existence of a document. I’ve not seen the document,” McGuinty told reporters.

Democratic Sen. Tim Kaine called the directive's tone “bad” and “unnecessary,” while Sen. Chris Coons said it risked worsening relations with a close ally.

“The United States should be doing nothing to further aggravate some of the distance and division between the United States and Canada,” Coons said.

![Hegseth’s memo was circulated last week and was described by one Canadian official as ‘a slap in the face,’ The Washington Post first reported](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/15/58/GettyImages-2236317023.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Hegseth, like Trump, has made disparaging remarks about Canada. He was accused of bullying in September after he mocked a female Canadian youth cadet instructor on social media.

He posted the image of the instructor from Vernon Cadet Training Center in British Columbia presenting a cadet with a medal, captioning it with an emoji of a Canadian flag and the words: “(this is real).”

The post met with an immediate backlash, with one Canadian veteran writing in response: “Wow. The Secretary of Defense is body shaming cadet groups. A youth group. This is not a military unit. They are kids looking for adventure, test themselves and align themselves with the values of service. Don’t be a Pete.”

The secretary previously stated his opposition to women serving in combat roles in the military and revealed an apparent fixation with troops’ personal appearances, railing against “fat” soldiers and dressing down top brass in Virginia a year ago for allowing service members to cultivate long hair and beards.

Hegseth ignited tensions again when he downplayed the contributions of U.S. allies in Iraq and Afghanistan.
