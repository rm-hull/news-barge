**Dogyu Lee & Euronews Doha bureau**

## South Korea’s university entrance exam can shape a student’s future. Hidden cameras and smart glasses have fuelled fears of AI-assisted cheating, prompting last-minute security measures that drew criticism and led to the education minister’s resignation.

South Korea’s education minister has resigned after criticism of last-minute changes meant to stop students from cheating by using AI on the country’s university entrance exam.

President Lee Jae-myung accepted Choi Kyo-jin’s resignation on Sunday, three days after the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT), known locally as Suneung. Choi acknowledged that the government had failed to prepare adequately and that sudden changes had caused confusion.

On Monday, the Education Ministry said it was filing criminal complaints against five candidates caught using smart devices during the exam. Some complaints had already been filed, with the remaining cases due to follow by the end of the day, an official told Yonhap news agency.

Concern grew after the top scorer in this year’s certified tax accountant examination admitted on 15 November to cheating with a miniature camera, a smartphone and generative AI.

The confession came days before the university entrance exam, which plays a major role in students’ university choices and career prospects.

The Education Ministry had also reported four cases involving AI-enabled smart glasses in middle and high school exams this year.

Authorities deployed about 8,400 additional staff for the university entrance exam, including specialists checking for smart devices and monitors patrolling corridors. Supervisors were told to inspect students’ glasses and cover clothing buttons with stickers to block hidden cameras.

During the exam, the government sent three nationwide emergency alerts to mobile phones to detect devices that candidates had not handed over.

The ministry reported 111 suspected breaches involving electronic devices. Of those, 54 were detected through the alerts and 52 through other methods, while five involved candidates actually using smart devices. The figures do not establish that all 111 cases involved AI-assisted cheating.

## **Last-minute changes draw criticism**

The government announced the additional staff two days before the exam. Teachers’ organisations questioned whether supervisors could learn to identify sophisticated devices at such short notice.

The Korean Federation of Teachers’ Associations criticised a timetable that left staff being selected on Tuesday, trained on Wednesday and deployed on Thursday.

The day before the exam, the ministry also restricted students to black pencils. Exam centres were to provide other supplies, including erasers and correction tape, to prevent candidates from hiding cameras inside them.

Choi had already drawn criticism for telling a parliamentary hearing on 16 November that his age made him doubt his ability to detect all AI-assisted cheating.

“Now that the exam has concluded, I feel a deep sense of responsibility and am offering my resignation,” he said in his resignation statement.

Lee has put Prime Minister Han Seong-sook in charge of follow-up measures and ordered a review of how examinations are managed.

One proposal under consideration would add identification marks to question papers, allowing authorities to trace where photographed or leaked questions came from.
