ChatGPT has upgraded its vocal interactions to encourage more speech-based interactions. The upgraded Live Dictation feature turns speech into text as you talk, letting you watch your words appear on the screen in real time. You can also chatter as long as you want, as the 10-minute cap on recording is now gone.

The improved experience incorporates the enhanced speech-to-text model OpenAI introduced earlier this year that cut errors by 10% across the most used languages and made the AI better at understanding accents and what people are saying when it's noisy. Ultimately, talking to ChatGPT should feel like a more practical alternative to typing all the time.

I decided to put that promise to the test by spending an entire day dictating everything I wanted to tell ChatGPT. I wanted to see whether talking would make ChatGPT more useful or simply make me more aware of how often I ramble.

## Microphone over keyboard

Getting started with Live Dictation is simple, and it hasn't changed with the upgrade. Just tap the microphone icon in the ChatGPT message box and start talking, and your words appear as text while you're still speaking. You can tap the waveform to show or hide the transcription, and once you've finished recording, you can review and edit the message before sending it.

It's not the same thing as ChatGPT Voice, which is more of a straight vocal conversation. The dictation option simply lets you send ChatGPT a written message by saying it out loud.

My first experiment was deliberately ordinary. I asked ChatGPT for a few dinner ideas using chicken, rice, and whatever vegetables might be sitting in my refrigerator. Normally, I'd type something brief and hope the AI understood what I wanted, but speaking encouraged me to explain that I wanted something quick and preferably with minimal cleanup.

Watching the words appear as I spoke removed the low-grade anxiety of most voice-to-text tools where you're not sure it will get it right, sometimes in embarrassing ways. It also removed the familiar irritation of finishing a recording and waiting for a transcription that might contain that misunderstanding. I could see what ChatGPT was about to receive, giving me an opportunity to catch problems before they influenced its answer.

The dinner suggestions themselves weren't magically better because I'd used my voice, of course. But making it faster and easier to offer extra context made its response more useful than what I could type in the same time.

## Talking all day

I found the biggest advantage when I moved away from simple questions and started making more complicated requests. One involved planning a weekend outing, with the usual restrictions involving travel time and the possibility of unpleasant weather. Typing everything out would have felt like filling in a book report, but dictating it took little effort.

I described the kind of day I had in mind, tested changing my mind halfway through and adding another requirement without having to reorganize the entire prompt. It was nice to speak more naturally, letting it develop as I spoke, while still being able to read the answer as it came back.

The same went for organizing a series of errands. Instead of thinking it through and writing a carefully structured request, I spoke through what needed doing, which parts were more urgent and which could wait until later. ChatGPT turned the resulting stream of consciousness into something much easier to follow.

The catch is that speaking naturally means introducing hesitations and repetitions that I'd normally remove when typing. Even with improved transcription, a rambling prompt is still a rambling prompt, and ChatGPT occasionally had to make sense of thoughts that hadn't fully formed.

I also experimented with dictating a longer explanation of something I was trying to understand. This is where removing the 10-minute recording limit was useful. OpenAI hasn't specified a new maximum recording duration, but the previous cutoff is gone, making dictation more attractive for extended brainstorming or working through complicated situations. It also made it easy to have a friend add their own thoughts to the same prompt without having to deal with a back-and-forth with ChatGPT.

For most everyday questions, I doubt anyone needs 10 uninterrupted minutes of talking. Still, the freedom to keep going changes how you approach a task. You can explain your thinking without watching a clock, although the AI still benefits from having a reasonably clear destination.

There's also the issue of being too loose with wording in speaking. If I wanted to ask something involving a specific phrase or detailed instructions, typing still offered more control. Privacy is another concern. Though OpenAI says dictated audio is sent to its models for transcription and only retained while the associated chat remains in your history, some might not feel comfortable relaying that audio through the AI chatbot.

Still, I came away with a much greater appreciation for voice dictation than I'd expected. The live transcription makes the process feel immediate, and the improved accuracy is notable if you've used similar tools and encountered frequent errors.

I wouldn't abandon typing altogether, as it's still preferable when I need precision or simply don't want to speak aloud. But for casual questions and longer explanations, ChatGPT's upgraded dictation has made talking a more appealing option.
