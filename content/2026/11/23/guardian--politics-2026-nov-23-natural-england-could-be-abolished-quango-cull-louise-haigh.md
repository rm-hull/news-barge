The nature watchdog Natural England could be abolished as part of a cull of arm’s length government organisations, Louise Haigh has said.

Saying that the proliferation of quangos and other similar bodies had made decision-making by ministers more difficult, the first secretary of state said it could fall within a push to change the landscape of government by scrapping some organisations and bringing others into departments.

Abolishing Natural England could be justified on the basis that it could speed up planning decisions and thus boost housebuilding and growth, but removing or curbing its role would be likely to prompt concern from environmental groups.

Last month, the Conservatives announced that if they won power they would abolish both Natural England and the Environment Agency, saying this would help create more new homes.

Tony Juniper, the chair of Natural England, responded to the reports by saying he was “very proud of all the work we have done during recent years to unite our national ambitions for growth while achieving nature’s recovery”, adding: “It’s never been the case that healthy nature is in opposition to economic development, but actually enables it. It’s possible to do both.”

Haigh, the de facto deputy prime minister, was speaking before a speech to the CBI conference on Monday morning at which she was to make the case for greater government control of public utilities.

After the Times reported Natural England was in line to be abolished, Haigh was asked on Sky News about the proliferation of quangos. She said: “We’ve had successive governments complain about this, but unfortunately, successive governments have created more of these and pushed more decision-making out of their own departments, so that then when they make decisions they don’t agree with, they turn around and look in horror.

“That’s why we’re absolutely committed to devolving responsibilities where they can be devolved.”

Asked if she was considering getting rid of Natural England, she said: “We’re considering all arm’s length bodies, both devolving, dissolving, and bringing back into departments, because we cannot continue with this situation where politicians look round and can’t take responsibility for the actions that people have elected their government for.”

Asked again about the specific fate of Natural England in a later interview on BBC Radio 4’s Today programme, Haigh again did not push back against the idea it was being targeted for abolition along with other arm’s length bodies.

She said: “So all the ALBs are in my sight for either devolving responsibilities that can be pushed down to mayors and strategic authorities, whether they can be brought back into departments under the direct power of the secretary of state, or whether they can be dissolved entirely.”

In a section of her speech about utilities, according to extracts released in advance, Haigh was to say she would “put pragmatism and public interest ahead of failed political ideology”, adding: “Public control is not anti-business – it’s about getting the foundations right, so that private enterprise can do what it does best.”

While pointing out almost no other nation had so many essential services run by private companies, and often overseas ones at that, Haigh was to say: “The status quo is not the neutral, pragmatic centre ground. It’s the outlier. An exception that no other nation is trying to emulate.”
