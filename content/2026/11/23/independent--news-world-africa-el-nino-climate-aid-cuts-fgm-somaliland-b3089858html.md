Increasingly erratic weather is making it harder to eradicate female genital mutilation (FGM) in Somalia and Somaliland, as severe cuts to international aid funding also undermine efforts to challenge the practice.

The Horn of Africa is on the frontline of a changing global climate, and is now facing further potentially catastrophic extremes as a “super” El Niño threatens severe floods.

Climate shocks cause poverty and mass displacement, which can roll back community-based progress towards eradicating FGM, and the risk is compounded because it comes amid exceptionally severe cuts to humanitarian funding.

Organisations working to end the practice have lost staff and resources, while health facilities and services supporting survivors have closed.

“One of the biggest challenges we have is that Somaliland has been in a cycle of one disaster after another. There is drought, flooding or displacement caused by conflict,” said Joseph Madzvamuse, programme and business development manager at ActionAid Somaliland, breakaway territory to the north of Somalia.

“These conditions, which are linked to the climate crisis, exacerbate the already difficult situation for women and girls, particularly when it comes to existing social practices such as FGM,” he added.

According to Mr Madzvamuse, the more households are displaced and their economic pressures increase, the more vulnerable women and girls become – when families struggle to meet their basic needs, social protection becomes a lower priority.

Displaced families often become separated from the social networks and family systems that previously supported them, so families who may gave been previously receptive to efforts to combat FGM may then become integrated into communities where the practice still deeply entrenched.

“We have seen progress in raising awareness and working with women and girls to change attitudes,” said Mr Madzvamuse. “But when families move to IDP settlements, those practices can become prevalent again.”

Somalia has the highest prevalence of FGM globally, at around 99 per cent, according to the FGM research initiative. It is also among the most climate-vulnerable countries on earth.

For nurse Ubah, a 28-year-old nurse living in Ainabo, the consequences of FGM are personal. She underwent type three FGM as a child, recalling how an older woman held her down and used unclean equipment to carry out the procedure.

“I was afraid and frightened,” she said. “When I remember it, it still makes me sad.”

Ubah said she had grown up believing FGM was normal, but changed her views after receiving education about its effects.

“I will not allow my daughter or the girls in my family to become victims of what happened to me,” she said.

Somaliland is currently emerging from a severe and prolonged multi-season drought, which has destroyed livelihoods and left families struggling to access food and water.

The Somali government has also warned that El Niño-driven flooding could displace up to one million people by the end of the year.

The threat comes as the world leaders failed to agree an overarching deal at the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, as disagreements over fossil fuels and climate finance blocked consensus.

This failure is particularly significant for poorer countries like Somalia, that have contributed little to global warming but face some of its harshest consequences.

Education is particularly important in challenging the FGM, Mr Madzvamuse said, as girls learn about their rights and the physical and psychological consequences of cutting. Yet aid cuts have also disrupted education, with 477 learning spaces closed in Somalia last year and more than 78,000 students dropping out, according to UNICEF.

Climate-related displacement disrupts communities, while aid cuts undermine the organisations working to prevent health and social crises.

Organisations that previously worked to educate communities about FGM and support survivors are increasingly diverting resources towards immediate needs such as food and water.

“The work of grassroots organisations, women’s groups and community activists at the forefront of challenging this harmful practice was disrupted. So it's like you move 10 steps in front, and then you do 20 steps back again,” said Mr Madzvamuse.

More than 230 million women and girls worldwide have undergone FGM, according to the World Health Organisation, with a further four million girls at risk every year. The UN has set a target of eliminating the practice by 2030.
