Russia is planning to put nuclear weapons in space by the end of this year, according to a disturbing new report.

The German military and Federal Intelligence Service have warned other Nato states that a satellite carrying a nuclear warhead could be in orbit by December, a report by Germany’s NDR, WDR and Sueddeutsche Zeitung said.

Western services exchanged intelligence showing that the satellite, named Sputnik-S, was assembled several years ago and is stationed in the Moscow region, the report said.

Such a weapon detonated in low orbit would have devastating consequences for both military and civilian infrastructure on Earth, the head of US Space Command warned earlier this year, referencing similar intelligence.

![Russia's Soyuz rocket launches to the International Space Station from Kazakhstan in July (file)](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/08/2285477404..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Major General Armin Fleischmann of Germany’s Cyber and Information Space Command (CIR) said Berlin could hope to respond by developing “counterspace capabilities”, able to neutralise the threat posed by nuclear-armed satellites.

The Russian embassy rejected “all unfounded allegations” about its “space activities” as propaganda.

But similar concerns are shared by the US, where the Defense Intelligence Agency warned only last month that Russia was actively “developing a satellite designed to carry a nuclear weapon”.

“A space-based capability such as this could cause rapid and sudden loss or degradation of thousands of satellites operated by countries and companies around the world, as well as to the vital communications, navigation, scientific, meteorological, commercial, and national security services they provide,” it said in a report.

Gen. Stephen N. Whiting, the head of US Space Command, also testified to the Senate Armed Services Committee in March that Russia has capabilities designed to disrupt US space assets “including the potential placement of a nuclear weapon [in] orbit”.

“Armed conflict in space is not inevitable, but should deterrence fail, the integrated space power of our team will provide a decisive advantage,” he said.

![Russia's President Vladimir Putin and US President Donald Trump shake hands at the APEC summit in Shenzhen on November 19](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/22/17/2026-11-22T164503Z_1518491131_RC2TQOAP0QH9_RTRMADP_3_USA-TRUMP-PUTIN.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Gen. Whiting told *The Times* in April that placing a nuclear ASAT weapon in space would violate the Outer Space Treaty and “hold at risk everyone’s satellites in low Earth orbit”.

It would be an outcome “that we just couldn’t tolerate”, he said.

“All of low Earth orbit would be at risk, and you know, that's over 10,000 satellites today with these new proliferated low earth orbit constellations like Starlink,” he told the newspaper.

Ukraine has relied heavily upon Starlink - a network of satellites that beam broadband service directly to user terminals on the ground - in its frontline defence against the Russian invasion.

Despite repeated warnings about the potential Russian threat to US security, president Donald Trump met with his Russian counterpart Vladimir Putin in China last week and referred to him as a “friend” ahead of talks on Ukraine.

![Gen. Stephen Whiting, Commander of the U.S. Space Force Command (L), speaks during a Senate Armed Services Committee hearing on March 26](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/09/2267886052..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Returning to the White House, Trump was asked whether Putin was also a friend to the US.

“I can’t speak to America,” he replied, while emphasizing his “very good” personal relationship with the Russian president.

Democrats widely condemned Trump’s remarks, pointing to Putin’s autocratic rule and his ongoing war against Ukraine, which the US has backed.

Multiple reports have also emerged in recent months alleging that Russia has helped Iran in its war with the US, despite Putin’s supposedly cosy relationship with Trump.

An investigation by the FT earlier this year concluded that Moscow was helping Tehran to develop a new class of missile capable of threatening US aircraft carriers and warships stationed in the Middle East.

And a confidential document uncovered by The Economist earlier this year suggested that Russia had considered providing Iran with the tools and training needed to fight American soldiers in the Gulf.

The document, believed to have been drafted by Russia, was said to outline how Moscow could provide Tehran with 5,000 short-range fibre-optic drones, longer-range satellite guided drones, and guidance on use.
