## The agreement builds on a 1951 defence agreement and comes after months of heightened tensions between Washington and the island under Danish sovereignty.

A new deal sealed in September and ratified by Denmark last week is boosting Washington's military presence in Greenland, while also giving it the right to block strategic investments by non-NATO and non-EU countries.

At the same time, it also explicitly reaffirms the sovereignty and territorial integrity of the Kingdom of Denmark, as well as Greenlanders' right to self-determination.

## What does the US get out of this deal?

The US already operates Pituffik Space Base in northwestern Greenland, an important site for missile warning and space surveillance.

But the new agreement lets Washington expand activities there and establish two additional defence areas: Narsarsuaq in the south and Mestersvig in the east.

It's also meant to give US aircraft broad overflight and landing rights across the island and provide access for Washington’s vessels in its territorial waters.

On top of that, it includes the possible deployment of Trump's proposed Golden Dome missile-defence system.

Troop numbers, construction budgets or deployment dates haven't yet been defined.

For Greenland, the agreement brings potential economic benefits, but also tighter security restrictions.

The US commits to awarding contracts for building and maintaining military facilities to Greenlandic companies as far as possible. That could create jobs and business opportunities.

## What does the deal mean for the EU?

The EU is not a party to the agreement, and Greenland's status as an Overseas Country and Territory remains unchanged.

But the deal has important strategic and economic implications for Europe.

First, the investment restrictions could help limit Chinese and Russian influence over critical infrastructure and natural resources in the Arctic.

European investors are not excluded by the agreement. The EU already has a raw-materials partnership with Greenland, and the European Commission announced a separate 200-million-euro investment package in September covering areas including connectivity, energy and critical raw materials. But there is a strategic trade-off.

The agreement reinforces a US-centred security framework in the Arctic. And the EU has no formal role in deciding the island's new American military arrangements.

## Does the agreement give US ownership over minerals?

The deal also restricts certain foreign investments in sensitive sectors, including critical infrastructure and natural resources.

“Big investments, there shouldn't be a backdrop for China to get into having a part in big mining projects", Rasmus Leander Nielsen, Associate Professor at the University of Greenland, told Euronews.

"But that de facto has been the case for several years already. So it's not something completely new."

Greenland must implement these rules in cooperation with Denmark, whose authorities will consult Washington on investment decisions. The aim is to limit potentially hostile foreign influence, particularly from countries outside the Western alliance.

For Greenland, however, this could narrow the pool of investors available to develop its economy and natural resources.

The agreement does not give the US ownership of Greenland's minerals or ban all non-Western investment. But it gives Washington a more formal role over certain foreign investments on the island.

## Cost of living crisis: Is taxing the rich the way to go?

Also in today's episode, we look at growing calls across Europe to tax the wealthiest people more, as protests continue across the continent over the cost of living.

According to Eurobarometer, around two-thirds of Europeans back this approach. However, in most countries, popular support struggles to turn into concrete legislative action. Take Belgium, where fraught budget talks are ongoing to tackle the deficit.

As part of this, the coalition, made up of centre, left and right-wing parties, clashed after two parties demanded the rich help fill the gap.

This left-right divide also caused a political crisis in the Netherlands this month.

In Europe, countries like Switzerland, Norway and Spain already impose annual net wealth taxes. They generate between 0.2% and 1% of GDP, so the value is often difficult to quantify.

Economists warn that these measures can also discourage investments as well as increase the risk of tax avoidance.

"A net worth tax will probably not get you a lot of money and it will cost you growth. That's something that I see as very dangerous in Europe at the moment. In an economic environment that is very weak in general," told Euronews Dr Michael Christl, Research Fellow at Tax Foundation Europe.

## What are the alternatives?

Another avenue is focussing on housing wealth. Britain, for example, will add an annual surcharge on homes worth over £2 million from 2028.

VAT reforms are also part of the conversation, but some experts warn that higher VAT hurts lower- and middle-income households most, since they spend a bigger share of their income on consumption.

And under current EU VAT rules, countries cannot introduce a higher VAT rate exclusively for luxury goods.

No single tax ticks every box, so the answer may lie in a mix of smaller reforms.

But as the political crises in Belgium and the Netherlands over this debate show, agreeing on which ones is the hard part.
