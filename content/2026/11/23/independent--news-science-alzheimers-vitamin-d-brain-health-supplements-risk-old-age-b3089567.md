Having higher levels of vitamin D in middle age could protect the brain from a kind of protein that’s linked to Alzheimer’s, according to a study that may lead to new dietary guidelines for those at risk of dementia.

Tau proteins are found inside nerve cells and play a key role in stabilising the transport of molecules inside neurons and helping brain cells keep their structure.

However, in dementia patients, they have been found to become abnormal, with the tau proteins detaching from neurons and clumping into toxic tangles, ultimately killing nerve cells and driving memory loss.

Now, scientists have found that higher vitamin D levels in middle age are associated with lower levels of abnormal tau protein years later.

“This study demonstrates an association between higher vitamin D in mid-life and a lower burden of abnormal tau protein in the brain, a marker of Alzheimer’s disease,” said Emer McGrath, an author of the study published in the journal *Neurology*.

“Vitamin D in mid-life could be an important factor to protect future brain health,” said Dr McGrath, an associate professor in Medicine at the University of Galway.

Previous research has shown that low vitamin D in adults over 70 is associated with an increased risk of dementia.

But the latest study is one of the few to look at younger adults in mid-life, around the average age of 39, researchers say.

In the study, scientists assessed nearly 800 people with an average age of 39 who did not have dementia.

All the participants had their vitamin D levels measured at the start of the study and brain scans taken an average of 16 years later to estimate levels of tau and amyloid beta proteins in the brain, which are both biomarkers for Alzheimer’s disease.

A high level of vitamin D was defined as greater than 30 nanograms per millilitre, and a low level was less than that number, scientists said.

About a third of the participants had low levels of vitamin D and 5 per cent were taking vitamin D supplements.

Taking into account other factors like age, sex and symptoms of depression, scientists found that higher vitamin D levels are associated with lower levels of the Alzheimer’s biomarker tau protein years later.

The findings suggest that low vitamin D levels could potentially be a risk factor that could be modified and treated to reduce the risk of dementia.

“These results are promising as they suggest an association between higher vitamin D levels in early middle age and lower tau burden on average 16 years later. Mid-life is a time when risk factor modification can have a greater impact,” said Martin Mulligan, another author of the study from the University of Galway.

Since the nature of the research was observational, scientists said further studies were needed to test the practical implications of their results.

“The findings do support the need for clinical trials to determine if vitamin D supplementation in younger adults could delay or help prevent the onset of dementia,” Dr McGrath said.
