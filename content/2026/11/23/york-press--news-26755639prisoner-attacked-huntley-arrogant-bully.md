## Ian Huntley was 'arrogant' bully who 'got in my face', accused killer told police

Newcastle Crown Court heard that Ian Huntley was "arrogant" and "a bully" *(Image: Northern Echo/Archive)*

A whole-life prisoner said he launched a fatal attack on Soham killer Ian Huntley at a high security jail because he was arrogant and a bully, a court heard.

Anthony Russell swung a four-kilogram metal bar at least six times at the 52-year-old’s head, in what the prosecution said was a “cold-blooded murder” planned for weeks, Newcastle Crown Court was told.

Russell, 44, denies a single count of murder, with a defence of diminished responsibility, but has admitted manslaughter.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/20648203.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Court artist sketch by Elizabeth Cook of Anthony Russell appearing via video link at Newton Aycliffe Magistrates' Court, where he is charged with the murder of Ian Huntley. *(Image: Elizabeth Cook/PA Wire)*

Already convicted of three murders, Russell launched the attack when the two men were working in the recycling workshop at HMP Frankland, near Durham, on February 26.

Huntley died after the prison attack in hospital nine days later, having sustained a fractured skull and brain bleed, and never regained consciousness.

After the attack, Russell told a governor he was “sick” of Huntley, adding: “He put it on me about six months ago and I walked away, but when he sees weakness in people he tries to bully ‘em and he’s been getting in my face the last few weeks and my head just thought ‘f\*\*\* it’, I ain’t putting up with it anymore.

“I’m not getting out, I’m a natural lifer, so I thought ‘f\*\*\* it’.”

The phrase “natural lifer” referred to Russell being sentenced to a whole-life term, meaning he will never be released, jurors were told.

Russell later told police: “He’s been getting really arrogant, cocky, in my face, winding me up, just little smiles as if to say ‘yeah’ sort of thing, and I’d just had enough of it.”

Asked when he started to plan the attack, Russell said it had been over weeks, “because of the way he just talks down to you… his arrogance, it was just eating at me.”

Matthew Bean KC, prosecuting, told jurors: “The prosecution case is that this was a cold-blooded murder that had been planned weeks in advance of its execution.”

He added: “The prosecution say that this plan would seem to have been driven by nothing more than this defendant’s personal sense of grievance towards Ian Huntley.

“It arose out of what would seem to be wholly trivial incidents, including how he had looked at him and a belief that Ian Huntley had ‘got in his face’.

“The prosecution say that, against that backdrop, the defendant decided that he would kill Ian Huntley.”

He told jurors that Russell was previously convicted of murdering three people in October 2020.

Mr Bean said: “The name of the man he killed, Ian Huntley, will be known to many, if not all of you.

“He had been convicted of the murder of two girls, Holly Wells and Jessica Chapman, that took place in the village of Soham in Cambridgeshire in August 2002.

“These were offences that, for entirely understandable reasons, aroused feelings of disgust and anger.

“Due to the nature of his offences, you may feel little or no sympathy for what happened to him.

“More than that, the prosecution do not ask for sympathy.

“Ultimately, this case is not about the appalling actions of Ian Huntley during his life, but the manner of his death.”

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/20630975.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Soham killer Ian Huntley died in hospital after he was attacked in the workshop of a maximum security prison *(Image: PA)*

The two murderers were held on A wing at the high security prison and there had been “tension” between them in the months leading up to the killing, the court heard.

In March 2025, prison staff witnessed an argument between them in which they threatened one another and had to be separated.

About 10 months later, Russell was seen with a black eye and told staff he had fallen, although some members of staff believed Huntley may have been responsible.

Russell and Huntley were in a group of around 15 inmates working in the recycling workshop, overseen by staff in a small office, when guards heard a commotion during the attack in the prison’s recycling area.

Contractor Mark Staplin was first at the scene and saw Russell stand over Huntley and deliver at least six “brutal” blows to his head.

Mr Bean said: “His view of what he saw was that the defendant appeared ‘out to intentionally kill him’.”

Russell told a prison officer immediately after that Huntley had been “pushing my buttons for months” and “winding us up”, the court heard.

Huntley was bleeding from the head and unconscious, the court heard.

He was taken to the Royal Victoria Infirmary in Newcastle and found to have suffered multiple skull fractures and bleeding on the brain.

A section of his skull was removed in surgery and he was then taken to the intensive care unit but he could not be saved and he died on March 7.

A post-mortem examination revealed he died as a result of blunt force head injury.

Mr Bean said Russell told police how he “wrapped” the metal bar around Huntley’s head, and how he was not going to lose his enhanced prisoner status “for a little fight”.

The prosecution said the jury will hear evidence that Russell suffered from post traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) stemming from childhood incidents.

But Mr Bean said there was an issue over whether his PTSD was “active at the relevant time”.

He told the court: “Ultimately, this is trial by you, by jury, not by psychiatrist.”

He said: “The evidence clearly establishes that the killing of Ian Huntley was not done on the spur of the moment.

“This is not a case of a sudden loss of self-control triggered by PTSD.

“This was a carefully planned and executed act.”

Concluding his opening statement to the jury, the prosecutor said: “The prosecution say that this partial defence of diminished responsibility simply cannot be proven.”

The trial continues on Tuesday.
