## The warning comes as Americans face a growing run of failures affecting air travel, Microsoft email services, major banks and the National Security Agency's network.

The US's top intelligence official has cautioned that Americans should brace for more technology breakdowns, as a software fault at Delta Air Lines' Atlanta base left more than 1,000 flights running late on Sunday.

According to local media reports, Jay Clayton, the director of national intelligence, said the country's systems are in the middle of a major upgrade cycle and that disruption comes with it.

"We are going to have glitches, gaps, interruptions in service." Clayton told CNN.

Clayton has previously said investigators see no indication of a coordinated cyberattack or foreign state involvement. He attributes the failures to organisations rolling out security patches and software updates.

The warning comes as Americans face a growing run of failures affecting air travel, Microsoft email services, major banks and the National Security Agency's network.

## Weight-check software fails days before Thanksgiving rush

Sunday's fault struck the software Delta uses to check that each aircraft is loaded within safe weight limits. With the Thanksgiving rush only days away, staff fell back on manual procedures to dispatch planes while engineers worked on a fix.

Later in the day Delta said the tool was back online, but its schedule was still disrupted, and it hoped to clear the backlog before nightfall.

The Federal Aviation Administration briefly held some flights heading to Atlanta's Hartsfield-Jackson airport, one of the busiest in the world and Delta's main base, for roughly an hour.

Keeping a plane within its weight and balance limits is a core safety requirement, an FAA document says, because overloading can weaken the aircraft's structure.

The regulator stated on X that its own air traffic control technology was not involved.

FlightAware data from Sunday evening showed about 2,000 Delta flights, or 58% of its schedule, running late.

At Atlanta, around 58% of departures and half of arrivals were delayed, and Delta operates the large majority of flights there. About 3% of the airline's flights were cancelled.

Delta attributed roughly 70% of the delays and cancellations logged by FlightAware to the software fault and blamed the rest on bad weather.

The airline apologised to travelers, said it was rebooking those affected, arranging food and hotels where required, and offering refunds on cancelled services.

## Wider pattern of disruption on both sides of the Atlantic

The incident adds to a difficult month for US infrastructure. Earlier in November, a fault in the FAA's network halted departures across the country for more than four hours, the longest such stoppage in a quarter of a century, and airlines cancelled or delayed large numbers of flights.

United Airlines then reported a system outage of its own a few days later, which held up its departures.

Over recent weeks, similar glitches have also affected Microsoft email, large banks and the National Security Agency's network, but the US is not alone in seeing everyday systems come under strain.

Earlier this month, hackers remotely pushed the temperature inside supermarket refrigerators in Europe up to 50°C.

The fridge attacks were one of 16 incidents mapped between 18 October and 18 November by DigitalEurope, the Brussels-based trade association for Europe's digital technology industry.

However, the causes differ from those of the US outages. According to Clayton, the US incidents stem from maintenance and upgrades rather than an attack, whereas several of the European cases involved deliberate action.
