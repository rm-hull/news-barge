Historic England has challenged a landowner's claim that a proposed access track for a new campsite lies outside a protected ancient monument.

In a letter to the Planning Inspectorate, the heritage body says the track at Barn Close Field, in Richmond, would encroach on the scheduled monument known as Scots Dyke.

Nicholas Green, of the St Nicholas Estate, is challenging the council's refusal to allow the land to be used for Swalefolk Camping.

The appeals follow the council's decision to reject two applications for certificates of lawfulness, which would allow a temporary campsite to operate for up to 60 days each year without full planning permission.

Mr Green claims the council has overlooked important evidence and relied on incorrect advice from Historic England when stating the campsite would encroach on the protected ancient monument.

Historic England says the dispute centres on the precise boundary of the protected monument, which was originally mapped in 1965.

Although the landowner believes the proposed access track falls outside the designated area, Historic England has told the planning inspector that this is not the case.

In the letter, Jess Tipper, inspector of ancient monuments at Historic England, said: “We consider the access track is within the northern extent of the scheduled monument."

“We advise the Inspectorate that the proposed access track for this development therefore encroaches onto this protected site.”

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21457434.jpg?type=mds-article-620) *(Image: LDRS)*

The letter explains that the original monument boundary was drawn by hand on a paper map at a scale of 1:10,000, before modern digital mapping systems were introduced.

Historic England acknowledges that transferring historic maps into digital formats has introduced inaccuracies in the boundaries of some scheduled monuments.

However, it warns that the precise extent of a protected site cannot be determined simply by measuring distances on the modern map.

Instead, the map must be considered alongside the original listing description and an understanding of how the monument was designated.

The section of Scots Dyke involved consists of an ancient earthwork extending for around 150 metres, including a bank up to 15 metres wide and an adjoining ditch.

Historic England says the landowner's interpretation of the mapped boundary does not alter its conclusion that the proposed access track would enter the protected area.

The Planning Inspectorate will consider the letter as part of the appeal process.

Mr Green's submission cites an independent topographical survey, historic maps and Land Registry records, which he says demonstrate that vehicles can access the campsite without crossing the monument's protected boundary.

The submission also challenges the council's concerns about the campsite's relationship with the nearby Grade II-listed St Nicholas building.

Mr Green is seeking to have the refusal overturned and has requested an award of costs against the council.

The proposals have previously attracted objections from Richmond Town Council, Easby Parish Council and residents over highway safety, archaeology, noise and the impact on the countryside.

The council issued a temporary stop notice shortly after the campsite opened in May, with further enforcement action following in August.
