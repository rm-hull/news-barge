## Russian banks, crypto, "shadow fleet", oil refineries, soldiers and athletes are all targeted in the latest sanctions package proposed by Brussels.

The European Commission has proposed a new round of economic sanctions against Russia as the country disregards calls for an energy ceasefire and continues its relentless attacks aimed at making Ukraine's winter unbearable.

The proposal, unveiled on Monday afternoon, comes amid intensifying efforts to advance the peace negotiations into a multilateral format involving Ukrainian, Russian, European and US officials, although Moscow has yet to commit to taking part.

If approved by member states, it will mark the 22nd sanctions package since February 2022. Brussels aims for a deal in December before the holiday break.

"We will continue to propose new sanctions until Russia shows genuine intent for lasting peace in Ukraine," Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said in a video statement.

"Our position is clear: we are giving Ukraine the strength to secure peace – and Russia every reason to choose it."

The new sanctions seek to remove an additional 60 Russian banks from the SWIFT system, crack down on the crypto platforms that Moscow uses to bypass restrictions, and prohibit European companies from repairing Russian energy facilities, particularly oil refineries, which Ukraine's long-range strikes have significantly impaired.

The proposal also targets over 70 vessels from the "shadow fleet" that Moscow employs to bypass the oil price cap, currently set at $44 per barrel.

More than 670 of these vessels have already been denied access to EU ports and services. The decrepit ships, which lack standard insurance and often sail under false flags, are considered a security and environmental threat.

Von der Leyen has revived a measure from the previous package that was left pending: an entry ban on Russian soldiers who have fought in Ukraine.

The first attempt in July fell short after France and Italy **raised concerns** about the administrative burden and legal responsibility for consular services.

But a recent spike in hybrid attacks, many of them attributed to Russian services, has injected fresh energy into the issue. Denmark has asked for tighter visa rules after **foiling a Russian plot** to assassinate a US-Ukrainian rapper on Danish soil.

"Russian ex-combatants pose a security risk to Europe. They cannot be allowed to wander freely across our countries," von der Leyen said.

"Therefore, entry into the European Union will be off limits."

The package introduces a novel dimension: sports and culture.

The Commission wants to exclude Russian teams and athletes competing under the Russian flag and the display of official Russian symbols from all sports events inside the bloc. It also wants to prohibit Russians from providing financial donations to and holding posts in governing bodies of European-based sports federations.

In July, the International Olympic Committee (IOC) lifted its suspension on Russian athletes ahead of the 2028 LA Olympics, triggering an outcry across Europe.

"Sports events can not be a forum for propaganda activities," von der Leyen said.

Notably absent from the Commission’s proposal is alumina, a key raw material used in aluminium production.

The issue came to prominence earlier this year after a media consortium exposed the links between Aughinish Alumina, a sprawling refinery in western Ireland, and Russia's military-industrial complex. Dublin came under intense pressure from Kyiv to cease the exports and was forced to launch a government probe.

The investigation reached no definitive conclusion because **it did not find evidence** to either confirm or refute a direct link between Irish-made alumina and Russian weapons.

Though publicly Dublin has said it would not "cherry-pick" EU sanctions, Brussels did not sense enough Irish support to move ahead with restrictions.

Monday's proposal is the first since the Commission established a sanctions office within its Secretary General, bringing together officials from different departments

Lars Danielsson, a veteran Swedish diplomat, has been appointed to replace David O'Sullivan as EU sanctions envoy. O'Sullivan spent the last few years travelling around the world, with a focus on Central Asia, to convince other countries to put pressure on companies that help Moscow bypass sanctions and buy blacklisted items.

China, in particular, remains a headache for Commission officials, dismissing their pleas. The country has become the central passage for circumventing sanctions, providing Moscow an essential lifeline to sustain its resource-intensive war.

"Every time we have a summit with China, we raise this issue," a senior official said.

Ahead of the presentation, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy called on allies to tighten sanctions on the components that Russia uses to build the jet-powered drones that terrorise the civilian population.

"International sanctions, enforcement of sanctions regimes, and genuinely tougher export controls are what can curb Russia's weapons production. Every country and every leader who helps pressure the aggressor also helps protect lives," he said.

Zelenskyy is set to attend a special EU summit later this week in Brussels.
