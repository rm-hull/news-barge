A police force has refused to share the policing costs of Harry and Meghan’s first joint engagement on British soil since their return to the UK – after assessing that publication could lead to an attack.

The Duke and Duchess of Sussex arrived in Nottingham last month for a tour of community projects.

The visit gained noticeable public attention given it was the couple’s first public engagement since arriving back from the US to begin a new life with their children, Prince Archie and Princess Lilibet.

On arrival, the pair were greeted by well-wishers, similar to a traditional royal walkabout, before they went to an acting workshop, a poverty community action group and a young people’s sports initiative.

They were protected by their private security team and did not enjoy the benefit of the police convoys assigned to senior working royals. However, police officers were seen within the crowds.

In response to a Freedom of Information Act request for the list of policing costs by *The Independent*, Nottinghamshire Police refused to provide an answer due to concerns over national security and the protection of individuals.

![Harry and Meghan were pictured on their first public engagement since returning to the UK](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/03/08/2026-10-15T141121Z_465655316_RC2Q3OAPIWB6_RTRMADP_3_BRITAIN-ROYALS-HARRY.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Before its decision, it assessed the risk of releasing information, stating that if it got into the hands of terrorists or criminals, it could be detrimental to those in receipt of protection.

Those with criminal intent, the force said, could use the information to map money spent on protection across forces, thereby gaining an operational advantage. It added that this could lead to an attack.

The force also highlighted that the UK faced a serious threat from violent extremists.

It said: “The security of the country is of paramount importance and the police service will not divulge whether information is or is not held if to do so would place the safety of an individual in receipt of protection at risk or undermine national security.

![Police officers were seen with the crowds as the pair arrived in Nottingham](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/15/2300345349..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“Whilst there is a public interest in the transparency of policing operations and providing assurance that the police service is appropriately and effectively engaging with the threat posed by a terrorist attack, there is a very strong public interest in safeguarding both national security and the integrity of police investigations and operations in the highly sensitive area of terrorism prevention.”

In response to Nottinghamshire Police’s refusal to share cost information, Craig Moreton, chief executive at private security firm Pegasis Ops, said it was unlikely the cost figure could help someone plan an attack. He said the information would only provide value if it included detail such as where officers were deployed, routes taken, and police visibility.

He added: “The real concern is whether the underlying costings reveal officer numbers, deployment patterns, timings, routes or security vulnerabilities. Those details can support hostile reconnaissance, so a redacted summary is usually the right balance between public accountability and operational security.”

Two years ago, Harry lost a legal challenge against the Home Office which changed the level of his taxpayer-funded personal security. It was reported earlier this year that he was granted a review of the decision.

Last week, a government spokesperson told T*he Independent*: “The UK government’s protective security system is rigorous and proportionate. It is our long-standing policy not to provide detailed information on those arrangements, as doing so could compromise their integrity and affect individuals’ security.”
