Early in the U.S.-Iran war, as diesel topped $4 a gallon, Kevin Otto thought he could manage by asking his Minnesota trucking firm's 16 drivers to slow down.

He estimated driving 65 miles per hour would allow Otto Transfer's flatbeds to gain two-tenths of a mile per gallon, saving $7,000 per truck.

Instead, prices kept climbing. After diesel exceeded $6 a gallon in September, Otto added a fuel surcharge to every job for the first time. Tied to the weekly Midwest diesel average, the fee began at 5% and eventually rose to 12.5%.

"To me, it’s drastic, but it’s the only way to survive," said Otto, who took over the 80-year-old business from his parents in 2002.

High diesel prices this year have brought new pressure on small independent operators, which comprise most of the nation's motor carriers.

War-driven price spikes increased one of their highest and least avoidable daily costs just as the fragmented U.S. trucking industry showed subtle signs of recovery from a yearslong slump marked by weak demand and low rates.

Smaller trucking companies typically have fewer financial buffers against surging fuel prices than larger competitors.

Major fleets hold greater cash reserves, buy fuel in bulk discounts, and negotiate long-term contracts with adjustable fuel surcharges.

Many smaller operators negotiate rates one load at a time and absorb higher pump prices while waiting three to four weeks for spot rates to catch up, DAT Freight & Analytics said.

Even when a rate includes a separate fuel line item, carriers may wait 30 to 90 days to receive payment.

"Those lags in the rates catching up to fuel are even more challenging if you’ve got to put food on the table and you don’t have a large company that’s helping carry over that difference," said Alex Leslie, senior research associate at the American Transportation Research Institute.

On-highway diesel reached a record national average of $6.53 in September, according to the Energy Information Administration.

Prices stood at $5.94 a gallon for the week of Nov. 16, up roughly 56% from before the war, while gasoline averaged $3.81, about 30% higher.

Moving cargo across the U.S. and Canada costs significantly more than a year ago. Freight broker spot rates for individual loads were 44% to 48% higher in September, depending on trailer type, DAT reported, estimating diesel costs accounted for at least two-fifths of that increase.

The rest of the rate increases did not necessarily mean higher profit. Insurance, maintenance, equipment, tolls and other operational costs rose as the industry began emerging from a prolonged "freight recession," the American Transportation Research Institute said.

Operating a truck reached a record average cost of $2.34 per mile in 2025, up 3.4% from the prior year, while nonfuel expenses rose 4.2%, the research group reported in July.

Profit margins were also exceptionally thin last year. Standard truckload and refrigerated carriers reported average operating margins under 1%, while flatbed carriers averaged a 0.5% operating loss, ATRI reported.

The slump began in early 2022 when a pandemic-era surge in freight demand dissolved, leaving too many trucks competing for fewer loads. Industry data suggests a shrinking truck supply, rather than renewed shipment demand, is driving this year’s higher rates, masking ongoing financial strain across trucking firms.

At October diesel prices, a rig averaging six miles per gallon used $521 in fuel traveling 500 miles daily, up from $310 in February, DAT calculated. Yet monthly cargo volumes were lower across all three primary trailer types compared to last year.

"Demand actually hasn’t increased that much, and the market for the last few years has been quite depressed," said Adam Parish, director of trucking procurement at supply chain management company Flexport. "And so there’s been a lot of pricing pressure from customers."

Truckers are selecting loads carefully to offset fuel expenses. Wes Oberman’s firm in Huntingdon, Tennessee, works with 14 drivers who own their rigs but operate under Oberman Logistics’ federal motor-carrier authority moving steel and retail goods across the South.

The owner-operators take flat-rate jobs and purchase diesel using company discount cards. They receive 85% of each load's gross revenue, from which Oberman deducts fuel purchases and fixed expenses.

As diesel absorbed more load revenue, he became pickier about accepted jobs. That sometimes means waiting a day for a higher-paying load or one requiring fewer "deadhead" miles—distance driven empty between jobs.

"We just got to keep trucking, as they say, save every penny we can and just weather the storm until this diesel crisis goes away," Oberman said.

President Donald Trump attempted to ease pressure in October by directing the Internal Revenue Service to suspend federal penalties and reporting rules through Dec. 31 for using dyed diesel on highways. Federal excise taxes were deferred, though state taxes and restrictions still apply.

The restricted fuel is usually reserved for tax-exempt off-road uses like farming and is dyed red to make improper highway use easy for law enforcement to spot.

David McKinney, owner of McKinney & Sons Transfer in Lexington, Alabama, called the dyed diesel policy "absurd."

He doubted it would offer real relief to small trucking companies. After selling two trucks in September to retain cash, he worried residual dye in his remaining 15 trucks might trigger fines after the temporary rule expires.

The IRS is considering a grace period.

McKinney, a 30-year trucking veteran, maintains an in-house fueling station, paying less for diesel than drivers at public truck stops. Still, given industry conditions, his business is only breaking even.

"Our bottom line is: For the past four months, we have survived. We have not profited any money," McKinney said. "Rates need to be higher, but the trucking business is so competitive, it keeps it down."

In Delano, Minnesota, Otto worries his new surcharge could cost him clients. He previously altered freight rates only once a year, though long-term customers have accepted the charge.

Otto typically gives drivers hauling utility poles across the country a year-end bonus, but with costs surging, he is unsure if he can afford it this year.

The EIA expects diesel prices to ease next year as non-Iranian Middle East oil production recovers. However, the agency raised its 2027 average forecast by 54 cents in November to $5.03 a gallon, citing global refined-fuel constraints and warning that shipping disruptions could still cause short-term price spikes.

"It’s a five-alarm fire, absolutely," Otto said. "It is unsustainable, and I fear that it will only get worse."
