Families visiting the Adventure Playground in Berkeley, California, are pushing back against a new $5 entry fee for children ages 1 to 14, which ends decades of free admission at the waterfront park.

The fee took effect on Saturday following a resolution approved by the Berkeley City Council in May. The policy change was introduced as part of a package of fee increases designed to help cover program delivery, maintenance and facility costs.

As Parks Director Scott Ferris told *Berkeleyside*, the city implemented the charge largely because of a significant deficit in the Marina Fund.

Although city officials have long possessed authorization to charge $1 for admission, the amount was previously considered too low to justify creating a collection system.

Officials can backfill shortfalls using money from the general fund — a step the council took last year to delay the admission charge — but the city's structural deficit has made that option increasingly difficult. The Marina Fund has operated in the negative for years, with projected deficits exceeding $1 million for both the current and upcoming fiscal years.

![Children between the ages of 1 and 14 visiting Adventure Playground in Berkeley, California, must now pay a $5 entry fee](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/20/06/Ca-playground.png?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

To collect the new fee, visitors must use automated pay stations established at the entrance next to the South Cove West parking lot and present a receipt. Adults and older teens continue to enter for free, and payments are accepted via credit card or tap-to-pay rather than cash.

A message addressing the change on the City of Berkeley’s website stated, “We understand this is a change to our operations,” while noting that the revenue covers staffing and materials, “and allows us to continue offering this unique program to the community.”

Broader revenue-generation efforts across the waterfront have also involved implementing parking fees at popular marina lots. While collection has started at the South Cove West lot, initiatives for other lots experienced delays after the Bay Conservation and Development Commission mandated a more extensive permitting process.

Supporters of a newly approved ferry terminal at the Berkeley Pier view the project as an additional economic driver for the marina.

Established in 1979 and historically welcoming between 300 and 600 children daily, the park allows young visitors to engage in basic carpentry using tools, paint and recycled materials, according to *SFGate*.

“The magic of the place is that it’s forever changing,” Sammi Orth, assistant recreation director for the playground, said. “It never looks the same, within an hour, within a week.”

The concept itself traces back to the early 1930s, when Danish landscape architect Carl Theodor Sørensen envisioned children playing with debris, *SFGate* reported. This led to the opening of the Emdrup Junk Playground in Copenhagen in 1943.

Similar sites emerged across the United Kingdom following World War II, often using bombed-out areas, and gained popularity in the United States from the 1950s through the 1970s before safety lawsuits and the rise of traditional modular playgrounds led to widespread closures.
