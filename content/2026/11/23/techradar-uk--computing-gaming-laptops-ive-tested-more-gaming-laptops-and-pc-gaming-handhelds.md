I’m going to let you in on a secret: despite having access to some amazing gaming laptops, I rarely use them when I want to play a game.

At heart, I’m a desktop PC guy. I love building my own PC, and the customization options and upgradability make them my favorite way to play. Not to mention, desktop components can be much more powerful than mobile variants.

Gaming laptops always felt to me like a cut-down compromise, as you sacrificed all of that for a device that’s more portable, but because you’ll still need to play your games with the laptop plugged in (graphically demanding games will deplete your battery quickly), and most likely on a desk so you can use a gaming mouse.

Of course, gaming laptops do have their uses, and I can completely understand why someone would rather have one of those instead of a gaming desktop PC. They’re more convenient and easier to set up, can be taken with you when travelling, and don’t occupy as much space as a traditional gaming rig.

But as a desktop-first gamer, I’ve been increasingly drawn to handheld gaming PCs, starting with the Steam Deck and my beloved ROG Ally X, and I’ve been increasingly enamored with the latest generation of handhelds such as the MSI Claw 8 EX AI+ and the Acer Predator Atlas 8, which I’m currently reviewing.

“Matt! You hypocrite!” I can sense some of you thinking. Those gaming handhelds suffer from all the compromises I just reeled off when attacking gaming laptops. You can’t upgrade them, they aren’t as customizable, and less powerful than desktops. But with gaming handhelds, those compromises simply don’t matter as much to me, and it’s for one very simple reason: the form factor.

With traditional gaming laptops, I still play on them as I would with a desktop PC, which doesn’t just highlight the shortcomings of a gaming laptop compared to my powerful gaming PC, but it also limits where and when I play on it.

![Three-quarter view of MSI Cyborg 15 A13U open on desk on a pink background](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/24haCc3mztjk5dNwJHnht5-1200-80.jpg)

Handheld gaming PCs, however, have completely different designs. As their name suggests, they are designed to be held in your hand, with built-in gamepad controls and relatively small touchscreens.

This instantly makes them more convenient for playing while travelling, as they are smaller and lighter than gaming laptops, and you don’t need a surface to place them on. The smaller screens also mean some of the graphical settings you need to turn down aren’t as noticeable.

![Person playing with the Steam Deck with dog in photo](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/i93oeRRHWHGjN2fNMcqUxV-1200-80.jpg)

Handhelds also force me to play different games due to the built-in controller. For genres where mouse and keyboard are best, such as management sims, real-time strategy, and first-person shooters, I leave that to my gaming PC.

On a handheld, I stick to indie games, platformers, third-person RPGs, arcade games, and other genres I’ve often neglected. It lets me do some deep dives into my Steam, GOG, and Epic Games Store backlogs and mix up my playstyle.

Of course, if you have no interest in those latter genres, a gaming laptop will be a better buy.

## What about performance?

If you care about pure performance, as many PC gamers understandably do, then a gaming laptop with a dedicated graphics card is going to easily outperform a handheld, as its smaller size forces it to rely on less powerful integrated graphics.

However, mobile components have come a long way these past few years, and recent handhelds powered by the Intel Arc G3 Extreme are seriously impressive, allowing handhelds to run graphically intensive games such as *Cyberpunk 2077* at 60fps and over, with a bit of tweaking.

While you do need to lower some settings to keep frame rates playable, as I mentioned earlier, the smaller screens of handhelds are more forgiving if you drop certain settings and even resolutions to help smooth out performance.

![Close-up of WASD keys on the MSI Cyborg 15 A13U, with blue backlighting on](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/2LREufRQ9E9oFUcLjb8WzN-1200-80.jpg)

Sticking to older games, or less graphically demanding indie titles, is another good way to get the most out of a gaming handheld. For example, I’m trying to take my mind off the fact that it seems everyone else in the world is enjoying *GTA 6*by playing* GTA Vice City The Definitive Edition* on the Acer Predator Atlas 8, and while I know deep down it’s not going to compete with the latest game from Rockstar, it’s still a bit of a thrill playing an open-world game I first explored on the PlayStation 2 on a device I’m holding in my hands.

## What’s the cost?

This is where things get a bit tricky, and why Black Friday deals could really help. Back when the Steam Deck, arguably the first of this modern breed of handheld gaming PCs, launched in 2022 beginning at $399 / £349 (it launched later in Australia), it was a no-brainer for a lot of people.

You simply couldn’t get a gaming laptop for around that price (unless it was very old or underpowered), and even to people with a bit more cash to spend, I was suggesting they consider the Steam Deck over slightly more expensive gaming laptops.

![A woman&#039;s hands holding the MSI Claw 8 EX AI+ as she plays Clair Obscur: Expedition 33.](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/Zv42fyetVFJ3uMq5raAHwB-1200-80.jpg)

The current situation in 2026 is very different, sadly. The global memory shortage, sometimes called the ‘RAMpocalypse’ has seen prices for all kinds of devices, including both handhelds and gaming laptops, rise steeply.

Back in May, Valve raised the price of its Steam Deck OLED handheld by 50%, so it now starts at $789 / £649 / AU$1,199. Considering that apart from a nicer screen and more storage space to start, this handheld is still running on the same hardware as four years ago, I simply can’t recommend it anymore.

More recent handhelds have launched at wince-inducingly high prices, such as the MSI Claw 8 EX AI+, which began at $1,799.99 / £1,399.99 / AU$2,849.

When handhelds start costing as much as upper-midrange gaming laptops, they become a lot harder to justify. Unfortunately for us gamers, gaming laptops have also gone up in price as well.

Still, for around that same price, you could get something like the Acer Nitro V16 from Amazon, which comes with 32GB RAM, an AMD Ryzen 7 processor, and an Nvidia RTX 5070 GPU. This will absolutely destroy the MSI Claw 8 EX AI+ when it comes to performance, so it then boils down to the form factor, as you’re paying a premium for the smaller, more portable build of the Claw 8 EX AI+ compared to the Nitro V16, which, as a 16-inch gaming laptop that weighs 4.85lbs (2.2kg), won’t be the most comfortable device to lug around with you.

This is why this year’s Black Friday sales (many of which have already started) could be a real game changer. Not only am I expecting plenty of tempting gaming laptop Black Friday deals, but there should be some great handheld gaming PC deals as well.

Just this weekend, the MSI Claw 8 EX AI+ dropped by £300 in the UK for a limited time, and while that deal has ended, I expect similar deals to crop up this week in the run-up to Black Friday itself on Friday 27. That £300 discount suddenly made the Claw 8 *much* better value, especially when compared to gaming laptops around that same price.

That’s given me hope that Black Friday and Cyber Monday deals this year could make PC gaming great again, by giving us at least a small break in the price rise misery.
