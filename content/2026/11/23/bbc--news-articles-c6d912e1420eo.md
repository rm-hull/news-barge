Danny Quinn takes BBC correspondent Lorna Gordon inside Scotland’s largest data centre

**There is strict security in place as we enter what looks like a nondescript office building on the outskirts of Airdrie.**

We are stepping into a facility at the centre of a heated debate about the growth in data centres in Scotland.

Opponents argue that centres like this one are of no benefit to the local community and damage the environment.

DataVita claims its operations in North Lanarkshire are providing jobs and securing "data sovereignty" in Scotland.

It's rare to get access to a data centre.

They can process sensitive or valuable data; the companies who run them like to stay in the background.

![The outside of the data centre at Chapelhall, a large rectangular grey building with blue and white sections.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/5d23/live/94b53be0-e719-11f1-9063-0daa598de9d3.jpg)

There are perimeter fences and CCTV outside the data centre at Chapelhall

You have to pass through a series of security measures as you enter the facility at Chapelhall.

There are perimeter fences and CCTV outside the building, a large grey box with occasional flashes of blue and white.

Inside there are airlocked doors allowing one person in and out at a time, and timed entry to some of the rooms.

You need a pass to move around and visitors must have an escort at all times.

Data centres have a lot of processing power, located in what is known as a server hall.

As the door opens to this pristine white space the first thing that hits you is the noise - and then the heat.

"People are often surprised how hot it is in here," says DataVita's chief executive Danny Quinn.

"You can actually feel it coming out of the rack and going through. We then use the lovely cool climate of Scotland to cool that air and recirculate it through the data centre."

Not all the aisles are as hot. In others, colder air is sucked up through the floor and into the equipment to make sure it doesn't overheat.

Quinn says there is a misconception that freezing cold locations would be ideal for data centres.

"What you want is the kind of 'beige' - between 9 and 13 degrees - that Scotland is very good at all year round," he says.

He adds that the rack of servers we have been looking at consume roughly the same amount of electricity as a four-bedroom home every day.

An AI rack would use energy equivalent of 20 to 30 homes, he says.

![Servers inside the data centre at Chapelhall. There is a long, white corridor with cables behind a mesh screen on the left](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/6fb1/live/de669680-e4fd-11f1-a8e4-b1e12a49db15.jpg)

Servers inside the data centre at Chapelhall are inside metal cages

The processing power can be used for internet searches and to store bank and health records as well as data for multiple businesses and public bodies.

"Data centres are the most valuable commodity in business now," Quinn says.

"If a company loses its data, it predominantly loses its ability to operate.

"We have an obligation to keep their data secure, and to ensure that customers' most critical asset - data - always stays on."

DataVita is planning to scale up at Chapelhall.

The 16 megawatt centre is the largest in Scotland, while another 24 MW centre is currently under construction.

The company is also seeking planning permission for a 120 MW centre.

The sites would hold a mixture of conventional servers and those working with AI.

## 'I cannot find a benefit'

From the end of her street, Jennifer Thompson can see all the sites where DataVita hopes to build.

"Some of my neighbours have been here for years," she says.

"They want to move because of the centres."

Thompson is a member of the Say No to Chapelhall AI Growth Zone group.

She has concerns over the environmental impact, the number of jobs and energy use.

"There's no good environmental outcome. There's no good biodiversity outcome. There's no good jobs outcome. There's no good pollution control," she says.

"I cannot find a benefit."

![A crowd of people in winter clothing marching as part of a protest - many are holding signs and placards opposing AI data centres](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/0275/live/eb727b40-e02b-11f1-9ca9-b3b288fceb81.jpg)

Protests have taken place against data centres

Thompson says it's not just about her community.

"I'd be hard pressed to find a community anywhere on earth that says we're really glad we've got these," she says.

"I don't think data centres should be anywhere until they prove that they are safe."

Last weekend, members of the group were among thousands of people who took part in a demonstration in Edinburgh against the building of large-scale AI data centres.

DataVita say they have had four public consultations at Chapelhall and that only 15 of the 150 people who came to look around the site had negative comments.

Quinn says infrastructure is needed in Scotland for what he describes as an AI revolution, and that this will provide "data sovereignty".

"It's important not just for the economy but for cybersecurity and the physical security of the nation," he says.

"We need to make sure we have our own infrastructure that is independent of any other nation."

He says DataVita hires and pays taxes and rates in Scotland and that any benefits stay within the country.

There are more than 20 planning applications for AI data centres in Scotland.

Quinn says some of those are "speculative applications" in places that have caused people "genuine concern."

He refuses to be drawn on how many data centres might be spread across Scotland within the next 10 years.

"Anyone stargazing into the future about AI is a very brave person, and ultimately what we need to ensure is that Scotland has the right type of technology to support an evolving technology," he says.

The Scottish government is promising new planning guidelines for data centres before the end of the year.

Opponents say Scotland cannot cope, doesn't need, and won't benefit from large hyperscale sites.

Those who want to build them say this an opportunity the country cannot afford to miss.
