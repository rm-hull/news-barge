## Zagreb is demanding a settlement over a strategically important peninsula as Montenegro races to conclude its EU accession talks by the end of the year, raising fears that the dispute could derail its membership ambitions.

Croatia is stepping up diplomatic pressure on Montenegro to settle a territorial dispute as the candidate country races to conclude its EU accession talks, two diplomatic sources told Euronews on condition of anonymity.

Zagreb has a list of grievances dating back to the Yugoslav wars of the 1990s that it wants Podgorica to resolve before joining the EU. But in recent days, the Croatian government has intensified its demands over a long-running border dispute.

The diplomats say Croatia's real objective is to settle ownership of the Prevlaka peninsula and the surrounding territorial waters, with one dismissing its other demands as "smokescreens".

The other grievances, which Podgorica has already partly addressed, include locating the remains of prisoners of war, compensating Croatian families whose property was expropriated and returning a naval training ship that was in Montenegro when Yugoslavia dissolved in 1991.

"When it comes to the border issues, if we don't find any kind of solution that is for Montenegro and Croatia acceptable, then we can go to an international court or international arbitration. We are not against this kind of mechanism," European Affairs Minister Maida Gorčević told reporters last week.

Zagreb has so far resisted international arbitration. Instead, it wants Podgorica to recognise the findings of the Badinter Commission, established by the European Community in 1991 to provide legal opinions on the dissolution of Yugoslavia.

The commission established the principle that the boundaries between the former Yugoslav republics should become international borders upon independence. Croatia is now demanding that Montenegro formally recognise the existing land border.

On land, Prevlaka is little more than a virtually uninhabited strip of rock. But its strategic location makes it crucial to determining the maritime boundary between the two countries, with implications for navigation, access to the Bay of Kotor and potentially valuable offshore oil and gas reserves.

Since 2002, the two countries have coexisted peacefully under a temporary arrangement, with Croatia controlling the peninsula and Montenegro most of the surrounding waters.

Zagreb is now pressing Podgorica to reach an agreement by December, just as Montenegro is trying to conclude its EU accession negotiations before the end of the year.

## Croatia leveraging its membership

Montenegro still has eight of its 33 negotiating chapters left to provisionally close. At an intergovernmental conference on Thursday, Podgorica had hoped to close at least four, but managed only two because of Croatian opposition.

Time is running out for the Western Balkan country to complete negotiations by the end of the year. That would probably require another intergovernmental conference before the end of November, followed by a final meeting in December.

Montenegro is the frontrunner to join the bloc and hopes to become its 28th member by 2028. But the bilateral negotiations are deeply unequal, with Zagreb effectively holding the keys to Podgorica's European future.

"If Montenegrin citizens feel pressured to make territorial concessions, it is the whole EU integration process that would be damaged in terms of public perception. The risk is that Montenegro enters the EU with a growing anti-European sentiment," the second diplomat said.

Germany has also urged Croatia, through the so-called Berlin Process, not to allow bilateral disputes to derail Montenegro's accession, particularly as the EU seeks to use enlargement to strengthen its influence in its immediate neighbourhood.

But the border dispute has become politically sensitive in Zagreb, where the government is eager to demonstrate that it is defending Croatian interests amid reports that it is considering calling early parliamentary elections in 2027.

"Before Montenegro concludes its EU accession negotiations, we want to reach an agreement on the land border. It is crucial that we establish whether the current Montenegrin government understands and recognises this fundamental principle, which is key to the post-1991 settlement," Croatian Prime Minister Andrej Plenković told MPs earlier this month.

Croatia itself has an unresolved border dispute with Slovenia, which did not prevent it from becoming the EU's most recent member in 2013. Zagreb subsequently rejected an international arbitration ruling on the dispute in 2017.

The European Commission has said Montenegro has met the targets to provisionally close all 33 negotiating chapters, and it is now up to all 27 member states to unanimously green light their closure.

As so often in EU enlargement, political considerations have overtaken technical progress.
