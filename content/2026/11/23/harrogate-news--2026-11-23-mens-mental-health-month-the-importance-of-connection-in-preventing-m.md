**November brings renewed attention to men’s mental health through Movember and International Men’s Day. But beyond raising awareness and encouraging men to speak up, are we doing enough to build the connections that could help prevent suicide?**

Every November, campaigns encourage men to take their health seriously, talk about how they are feeling and seek help when they need it.

It is an important message. Men account for the majority of deaths by suicide in the UK, and the barriers that can prevent men from seeking help remain a significant concern.

> Latest 2025 figures indicate that approximately **15 men die by suicide every day across the UK** – more than 5,300 deaths over the year. That equates to around 103 men every week.
>
> Sources: ONS, National Records of Scotland and NISRA.

But there is a wider question that deserves attention: are we doing enough to reach men before they reach crisis point?

Much of the conversation around suicide prevention understandably focuses on crisis helplines, counselling, awareness campaigns and specialist support services. These can be vital resources for someone experiencing suicidal thoughts.

However, not every man who is struggling will recognise that he needs mental health support. Others may be reluctant to approach a charity, make a phone call or admit that they are finding life difficult.

For some, the difficulty is not simply knowing where to find help. It is having someone to talk to in the first place.

## Awareness is not the same as connection

There is a difference between telling people that support is available and creating opportunities for people to connect with others.

A poster, social media campaign or telephone number can raise awareness. Genuine connection requires something more: people meeting, talking, listening and developing relationships within their communities.

That could mean more informal community events, local discussion groups, opportunities to talk without the pressure of entering a formal support service, or partnerships with organisations that already bring people together.

Workplaces, sports clubs, community groups, pubs, libraries and local organisations can all provide opportunities to reach men who might never approach a mental health charity directly.

It is not about turning every social interaction into a mental health intervention. It is about recognising that isolation and disconnection can become serious problems, and that ordinary human contact can matter.

## Reaching men who do not ask for help

Simply telling men to talk may not be enough. The challenge is to create environments where conversations can happen naturally, without men feeling they first have to identify themselves as having a mental health problem.

For some, that might begin through a shared interest, a local club, a conversation over a coffee or simply having someone who notices when they are no longer around.

There are also the circumstances that can contribute to people becoming vulnerable: relationship breakdown, bereavement, financial pressure, unemployment, loneliness and the loss of a sense of purpose.

These are not problems that can necessarily be resolved by a helpline. They are also issues that communities, charities and support organisations should be willing to engage with.

## What are suicide prevention organisations doing beyond the helpline?

Suicide prevention charities have an important role in providing immediate support, raising awareness and helping people access professional services. But there is a legitimate question about how much emphasis is placed on connecting with people before they reach crisis point.

Are organisations actively going out into communities and meeting men where they are? Are they building relationships with local groups, workplaces and clubs? Are they reaching men who would not normally approach a support service?

There is also a question about how success is measured.

Calls answered, people supported and campaigns delivered are important measures of activity. But what about the number of people reached through community work, the relationships developed and the opportunities created for men to find support before they reach breaking point?

## Making prevention part of everyday life

No charity can be expected to solve loneliness, social isolation or every difficulty people face. Nor can community activity replace professional mental health care.

But suicide prevention should be broader than responding when someone is already in desperate need.

Men’s Mental Health Month provides an opportunity to look beyond awareness slogans and ask what practical action is being taken to bring people together.

Perhaps the most important question is not simply whether men know where to find help, but whether they have someone in their lives they feel able to turn to.

We talk about wanting to reduce suicide. The challenge is to build the connections that might help prevent someone reaching that point in the first place.
