More than 1 million Jetstar customers could be eligible to join a class action alleging the airline failed to refund them for flights it cancelled during the pandemic, the firm leading the lawsuit has said.

The federal court on Monday confirmed it had ordered notices be sent to customers informing them of their eligibility to participate in, or opt out of, the lawsuit, with a potential trial set for March 2027.

Echo Law, the firm leading the class action, said it wanted to alert the public to the notices – which were due to be distributed to about 1.1 million people by 4 December – to ensure they understood it was not a scam.

The class action alleges Jetstar issued customers flight credits instead of the cash refunds they were entitled to for flights cancelled between 2020 and late 2022, after cancelling thousands of scheduled domestic and international flights.

Echo Law ran a separate class action against Qantas over its handling of Covid-19 travel credits, which the airline in March agreed to settle for $105m with no admission of liability.

In its case against Jetstar, the firm alleges the airline breached its legal obligations to customers by failing to provide refunds for cancelled flights, instead offering customers credit vouchers.

It alleges Jetstar misled customers and engaged in unconscionable conduct in breach of Australian consumer law.

It also alleges that, by holding customers’ funds that ought to have been refunded, Jetstar unlawfully benefited from this conduct.

Jetstar has denied the allegations and is defending the class action

“During the Covid-19 pandemic, Jetstar took a range of steps to respond to rapidly changing travel restrictions, while continuing to support customers affected by disruptions,” a spokesperson for the airline said.

“We provided customers with a range of options during this period depending on the circumstances, including travel credits, fee-free flight changes and refunds.”

They said the Jetstar Covid-19 vouchers remained valid as the airline had removed the expiry date in 2023.

However, Andrew Paull, a partner at Echo Law, alleged Jetstar’s policy of “pushing” customers to accept vouchers was designed to preserve the airline’s cash position.

“It held onto ordinary Australian’s [sic] money and expected customers to just be happy with credits, or in some cases nothing, when we allege they were entitled to cash refunds,” he alleged.

Paull alleged many customers had ended up paying Jetstar more than their original booking to use their credits on new fares, as they were led to believe they had little choice but to do so in order to avoid losing the value of their flights.

The firm’s lawsuit against Jetstar is scheduled for mediation in December and is expected to go to trial if the parties fail to reach an agreement.

Echo Law said the litigation funder Court House Capital was backing its class action against Jetstar.

Court House Capital’s chief investment officer, Heather Collins, accused Jetstar of seeking to “delay resolution while continuing to hold onto money that belongs to Australian families”.
