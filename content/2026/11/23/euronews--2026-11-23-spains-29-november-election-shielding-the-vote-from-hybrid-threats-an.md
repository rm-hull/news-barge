## Disinformation, cyberattacks and the geopolitical use of migration put Spain in the sights of foreign powers. Experts urge shielding key sectors and speeding a joint European response to election interference.

In recent months, and especially throughout 2026, Europe has experienced an unprecedented escalation of hybrid attacks that have moved beyond the purely digital sphere to disrupt everyday services and critical infrastructure.

According to investigative journalists and European cybersecurity agencies such as the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA), in the last six months more than 50 physical incidents linked to foreign intelligence have been documented, in addition to thousands of cyberattacks.

Looking ahead to Spain's general elections on 29 November, which hybrid threats are causing most concern in these elections?

Carlos Galán Cordero, a professor at Nebrija University and an expert in hybrid threats, warns about disinformation.

"Spain is an attractive target for foreign powers. We know that Russia uses disinformation as its main tool, but there are other actors that resort to different tactics to influence elections or stir up the streets, provoking some kind of social unrest," he said.

## The battle to influence the Spanish vote

Dealing with hybrid threats requires a response on several fronts. The most urgent task is to shield critical infrastructure and modernise IT systems in key sectors, from healthcare and food supply to electoral networks, backed up by greater intelligence sharing and rapid reaction teams at European level.

"There are many actors with a strong interest in seeing one candidate or another win," Galán Cordero says. Asked which countries or agents are seeking to disrupt the electoral process in Spain, he points to multiple foreign powers.

Although effective contingency plans are in place, as seen recently with a disinformation campaign detected by Maldita.es that had very limited impact and barely any reach, experts warn that there is no room for complacency, since these actors can update their methods in search of greater interference.

## Europe's common strategy

The European Union has a common strategy that has been significantly tightened between 2024 and 2026 in response to the surge in sabotage, cyberattacks and electoral interference.

However, national security remains the responsibility of each member state, so the European model works more as a coordination network, a system of binding standards and information exchange than as a single agency.

Focusing only on Russia gives a skewed picture, since other countries operate in this arena through hybrid threats such as using the migration crisis to exert pressure.

"Migration can indeed be used as a means of creating instability," Galán Cordero continues.

Although the Spanish government maintains that there is no conclusive evidence to formally attribute the mass entry into Ceuta to Morocco, geopolitics experts point out that this is precisely how the so called "grey zone" works.

Pro-Moroccan networks coordinated during episodes such as the Ceuta crisis show how third powers exploit regional vulnerabilities to apply pressure without leaving an official trace.

"Not long ago someone sent me a meme showing Spain as a coveted and contested piece on the global chessboard, fought over by Washington, Beijing, Moscow, Rabat and Tel Aviv. The truth is that there is already a contingency plan under way that is detecting and neutralising these actions in time," Galán Cordero adds.

## Brussels' legal and diplomatic arsenal

The European Union's joint response combines political action, infrastructure protection and digital control. To react quickly to hostile campaigns, Brussels has the Hybrid Toolbox (EU Hybrid Toolbox), which allows sanctions and joint responses to be coordinated, and the new Emergency Security Protocol proposed by Ursula von der Leyen in September 2026, inspired by Article 4 of NATO.

To protect critical infrastructure, the European framework already imposes specific obligations. Internally, the NIS2 and CER directives require strict cybersecurity and early warning standards in vital sectors such as energy, healthcare and food.

In the digital and electoral sphere, the Digital Services Act (DSA) and the European Democracy Shield monitor algorithm transparency and advertising on platforms such as TikTok or X.

All this is reinforced through cooperation with NATO, based on joint exercises and the Helsinki Centre of Excellence for Countering Hybrid Threats.

## When hybrid threats hit everyday life

It is also crucial to protect the information space by forcing digital platforms to provide genuine transparency about their algorithms and political funding, while strengthening media literacy among citizens so that disinformation become less effective.

The hybrid offensive in Europe is no longer an abstract threat; it is hitting everyday life and essential services head on.

In recent weeks the continent has seen a chain of incidents ranging from fake bomb threats on commercial flights and in schools to sophisticated digital sabotage against critical infrastructure, such as the cyberattacks suffered by several blood banks, which put at risk the preservation of vital reserves for transfusions and clinical trials.

This gap between technological speed and institutional response capacity defines today's information ecosystem.

In his book "La guerra: teoría para comprender los conflictos del siglo XXI" (War: A Theory for Understanding 21st-Century Conflicts) Spanish military officer Federico Aznar, an expert in disinformation and geopolitics, warns that "old solutions no longer work," and the new ones do not quite match the problems they are meant to address. That questioning of the current model is what underlies the idea of post truth."

## Cybersecurity and monitoring to counter electoral falsehoods

With Spain's 29 November elections approaching, preventive measures combine the national security architecture with European cooperation, focusing on several key areas.

With the ballot in sight, Spain has activated its own preventive shield in line with Brussels. Under the coordination of the National Security Department and the Interior Ministry, bodies such as the National Cryptology Centre (CCN CERT) and INCIBE are working to secure the IT infrastructure of the electoral process, from data transmission networks to public information portals, against possible cyberattacks or impersonation attempts.

The digital investigation collective Antibot4Navalny, which tracks pro Russian disinformation campaigns, uncovered in November 2026 a coordinated offensive called Operation Matrioska, aimed at interfering in Spain's general elections.

The content pushed by this network seeks to discourage voting by alleging a supposed economic collapse caused by the housing crisis, calls for an end to sanctions on Russia and the withdrawal of funds from Ukraine, and promotes territorial fragmentation in Europe. The campaign circulates fake videos that mimic the look and style of media such as 'El País' and 'El Mundo' to lend credibility to disinformation narratives.

## Fact checkers and citizens against electoral disinformation

The Monitor Disinfo association is dedicated to investigating, analysing and educating about disinformation and hybrid warfare. Carlos Galán Cordero is part of this association: "We analyse messages and campaigns on social media and share these issues with platforms such as Maldita.es, Newtral and similar organisations so that they can verify them."

Newtral has launched the FIMI Radar (Information Manipulation and Foreign Interference) to monitor foreign interference during the election campaign, while Maldita.es has highlighted its "Buloteca" archive ahead of 29N with the slogan 'Estas elecciones, la desinformación la frenamos juntos' - 'In these elections, we stop disinformation together'.

"This community is really good at helping to verify whether something is true or not," Galán Cordero continues. To stop these campaigns, he places the first line of defence with citizens, urging them to spot falsehoods and report them to the platforms themselves. Although there is a European legal framework to support this, such as the Digital Services Act (DSA), he warns that it will be in force for the 29N elections without yet having been rolled out with all the necessary effectiveness.

At the same time, intelligence services are keeping the digital ecosystem under surveillance to detect coordinated bot campaigns or narratives designed to sow doubts about the integrity of postal voting and the vote count.

## Democratic resilience in the face of hybrid warfare and AI

This national deployment also draws on the European framework of the Digital Services Act (DSA), through which Spanish and EU authorities are demanding that platforms such as TikTok, X and Meta exercise greater control over their algorithms and political advertising during the final stretch of the campaign.

The aim is to prevent the scenario already seen elsewhere in Europe from being repeated in Spain: a combination of digital noise, falsehoods and sabotage designed to erode citizens' trust at the most sensitive moment for democracy.

## Technological foresight and alliances to shield democracy

In this constantly evolving context, defending democracies means combining technological foresight with international cooperation. Professor Galán Cordero sums up the scale of the challenge like this: "Hostile actors have a lot of imagination when it comes to cyberattacks and are prepared to do whatever it takes to undermine the democratic values of Spain and Europe."

Facing campaigns increasingly driven by artificial intelligence, the expert highlights the preventive work of the National Cryptology Centre, which anticipates risks through constant simulations and testing, and stresses the need to act as part of a network: "We live in an increasingly globalised world and we need help from third countries to fight these threats. Spain is also an intelligence provider for allies that draw on our knowledge to tackle their own challenges."

In the face of a disinformation machine operating on an industrial scale through bot networks, troll farms and algorithms designed to fragment society, the response must be proactive.

Federico Aznar argues in his book that "technology reaches geopolitics" and that, in the era of post truth, "the main value to protect against these attacks is encapsulated in the integrity of society."
