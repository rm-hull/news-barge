While lung cancer is the second most common cancer in American men and women, it is the leading cause of cancer deaths.

Now, researchers at Binghamton University in New York say a new breath test could detect the disease earlier than blood or urine tests.

The test works by analyzing DNA inside small sacks of proteins, fats and nucleic acids made by cells and released when we exhale. These are known as “extracellular vesicles.”

Detecting the cancer early is crucial, as it can help limit the severity of the disease and prevent fatal outcomes. Nearly 125,000 deaths are expected this year, according to the American Cancer Society.

“Early stage is tricky,” Associate Professor Yuan Wan said in a Monday statement. “The tumor is very small, and doesn’t release too many molecules to the surrounding environment.”

![A new test could exceed blood tests for early lung cancer detection, researchers say](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/21/37/GettyImages-656591086.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

To separate these extracellular vesicles from the breath, the researchers used an engineered nanobody, which is a tiny fragment of an antibody, a protein that protects the immune system.

The nanobody then attaches to the protein CD9 on the surface of the extracellular vesicles, making it easier to find and separate the sacks from a patient’s breath.

Looking at DNA within the sacks tells researchers how far the disease has progressed and any other cancer-related changes.

While looking at tissue samples during surgery can take up to a couple of hours, this process is faster.

![Lung cancer is the second-leading form of cancer in the U.S.](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/21/40/ct-scan-1200x675-1.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Though there is one hitch. Tumors closer to the respiratory tract are better candidates for the test because their sacs can more easily be exhaled.

So, doctors may need to consider the location of the tumor when choosing how to test someone.

A tissue biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosis, the researchers said.

“Surgeons are like detectives,” said Wan. “When they notice something, they also want to find other evidence to do cross-checking, to ensure this is cancer and not something else before proceeding with treatment. We want to provide an optimal solution for doctors and patients, to help both.”
