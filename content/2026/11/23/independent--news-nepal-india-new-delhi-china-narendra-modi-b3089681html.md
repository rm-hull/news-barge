India announced a $1.6bn (£1.2bn) package to help Nepal rebuild after deadly floods earlier this year killed 1,500 people, left more than 5,200 missing, and wiped out entire settlements.

Indian prime minister Narendra Modi announced the package on Monday after meeting his Nepalese counterpart Balendra Shah in Delhi.

The floods in August destroyed roads, bridges, power plants, farms, and homes, causing an estimated $4.7bn (£3.6bn) in losses, according to Nepalese authorities.

A study by World Weather Attribution in September found that human-caused climate change was a key factor in destabilising the mountainside whose collapse triggered the floods. Rising temperatures had thinned glaciers and thawed permafrost, weakening the ice and frozen ground that helped hold mountain rock in place.

![Balendra Shah and Narendra Modi remotely inaugurate a train service between India and Nepal after their meeting in Delhi on 23 November 2026](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/10/download.-3..jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Mr Modi said the assistance would help restore infrastructure, rebuild communities and support people who lost their homes and livelihoods.

Nepal earlier received an allocation of $20m (£1.5m) from the UN Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage, established to help vulnerable developing countries cope with the irreversible impacts of climate change.

The allocation, the fund’s first, was approved at an emergency board meeting in October. But it’s less than 1 per cent of Nepal’s recovery bill.

Nepalese officials and climate experts welcomed the decision but said the amount was a small fraction of what was needed.

Maheshwar Dhakal, a senior official in the climate and environment ministry, described the funding as small compared with the losses suffered by the country.

He argued, however, that releasing the money would send a message. “This shows that the global community can take the right decision at the right time to help those who are most vulnerable to climate change.”

![Puppets symbolising victims of flash floods in Nepal lie on the ground as activists take part in a demonstration titled ‘Climate Justice for Nepal’ during the UN Climate Change Conference](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/16/2026-11-17T163029Z_1521152421_RC2SPOA4MM7C_RTRMADP_3_CLIMATE-COP31.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Member states have pledged more than $820m (£626m) to the UN fund, which has received 176 requests for support from developing nations totalling about $2.8bn (£2.1bn).

The fund’s board is due to meet in December to consider further requests.

In September, the Nepalese PM said at the UN that his country was paying a devastating price for a crisis it had barely contributed to causing. “We did not create this crisis, yet we are paying a colossal price for it,” he said.

![People cross a newly built suspension bridge next to houses damaged by the deadly flash floods in Nuwakot, Nepal, on 23 November 2026](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/11/2026-11-23T061856Z_1113393729_RC2DTOASYVCC_RTRMADP_3_NEPAL-FLOODS.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Climate experts have said steep cuts to international aid budgets, including funding for the US Agency for International Development, have made it harder for nations like Nepal to prepare for and respond to climate-driven disasters.

In 2025, development assistance from major donor countries fell by 23 per cent, according to the OECD, with the US accounting for the largest reductions but Germany, France, Japan, and the UK cutting their aid budgets as well.

“The reality is that we need trillions of dollars every year, but developed countries have not met their obligation,” Harjeet Singh of the Fill the Fund campaign, a global group that pressures governments to contribute to the UN fund, said earlier.

The funding shortfall comes amid broader divisions over how the international community should support countries bearing the brunt of climate change. At the Cop31 climate summit in Antalya in Turkey, which concluded on Sunday, member states failed to reach an overarching political agreement, with disagreements over climate finance, fossil fuels and how to address the impacts of global warming exposing deep divisions between developed and developing nations.

![Nepalese lawmaker Sagar Dhakal speaks next to activist Greta Thunberg at Cop31 UNFCCC Climate Conference in Antalya, Turkey, on 12 November 2026](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/11/2304730434..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

India’s assistance also comes as Delhi seeks to strengthen its ties with Nepal amid China’s growing economic and infrastructure influence in the region.

India and Nepal have shared close historical, economic and cultural links, but relations have periodically been strained by political and territorial disputes.

The two prime ministers also launched several cross-border projects during Monday’s meeting, including a hydropower project, an oil storage facility, a pipeline, and a railway link.

Nepal remains heavily dependent on India for fuel and other essential supplies, but energy ties are becoming increasingly reciprocal, with India importing around 1,200 megawatts of electricity from across the border.
