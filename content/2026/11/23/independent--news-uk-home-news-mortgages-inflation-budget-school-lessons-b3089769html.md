Primary school pupils will be taught about inflation and mortgages for the first time under reforms to the national curriculum.

The Department for Education said they would involve “strengthened lessons covering everything from budgeting and saving to inflation, mortgages and spotting financial scams”.

It added that the changes were to “come alongside wider maths reforms in curriculum overhaul to strengthen core knowledge and put greater emphasis on applying it to everyday life”.

Unveiling the reforms on Sunday, the Department for Education said “rather than only learning how to perform calculations, we will support pupils to understand the reasoning behind them, articulate their thinking, and use that knowledge confidently”.

It added this could involve “comparing supermarket prices, working out whether a discount really offers good value, calculating earnings from a part-time job or understanding how savings grow over time”.

The department said only a third of children recall receiving useful financial education, “leaving too many young people without the practical money skills they need for adult life”.

It said as part of a rewiring of the curriculum to be set out in coming weeks, pupils will learn the ‘big three’ concepts of inflation, compound interest and diversification of risk, “deliberately connecting maths to real-life topics such as interest, mortgages and savings in citizenship”.

Education secretary Lucy Powell said too many young people are “leaving school without the practical skills and confidence they need to navigate adult life”.

She added: “Financial literacy will take on a much stronger role in the curriculum, giving young people the knowledge and confidence to navigate the financial decisions they will face throughout their lives.

“In primary school, pupils will build knowledge necessary to manage money, judge whether a deal is really good value and make sense of the figures they encounter every day.

“Then in secondary school, new lessons will cover why prices rise and how savings grow, to spotting financial scams and navigating mortgages.

“Alongside this, maths reforms will focus on deeper mastery of the fundamentals, cutting unnecessary content and better sequencing topics, giving pupils more opportunities to reason, solve problems and apply their knowledge.

“At GCSE, particularly at foundation tier, content will be rebalanced to strengthen the mathematical skills pupils need for life and work, including financial mathematics and statistics.”

![Education secretary Lucy Powell said too many young people are ‘leaving school without the practical skills and confidence they need to navigate adult life’](https://static.independent.co.uk/2025/12/15/16/01/classroom-stock-children.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The Richmond Project charity, founded by former prime minister Rishi Sunak and his wife Akshata Murty, worked with the Department for Education on developing the new financial literacy curriculum.

Mr Sunak said: “The new financial literacy curriculum will ensure that no child leaves school without being taught ‘the big three’, the foundational concepts of compounding, inflation and risk diversification.

“The Richmond Project is testing the new financial literacy curriculum in classrooms so that schools can teach it as effectively as possible from 2028.”

Sophie Pender, founder of The 93% Club, which advocates for state-educated people, said: “A life skill as essential as financial literacy shouldn’t be left to your family and network to teach you.

“Not every child benefits from knowing people who work in finance or who have lived experience of things like applying for a mortgage, investing, or making their money work for them, meaning many start adult life on the back foot.

“We also know that family attitudes to money can affect the choices you make.

“For example, when we surveyed state-educated people last year, 45% of working-class students said family or friends had questioned their choice to go to university because of concerns about debt, despite the likely increase in earning potential.”
