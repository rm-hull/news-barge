Australians could collectively cut climate pollution by more than 15m tonnes – more than grounding every domestic flight – simply by changing what they eat for lunch.

A nationwide shift to plant-based lunches could reduce food-related emissions by 29% – 15.4m tonnes per year – while saving the average household about $611 annually, according to a report by Bryant Research and not-for-profit ProVeg.

Nadia Schilling, the executive director of ProVeg Australia, said the findings highlighted the “huge difference” that relatively modest changes, such as a meat-free Monday or a plant-based lunch, could make.

That might mean opting for a black bean burrito, a poké bowl with tofu, or a lunchbox with hummus and fresh vegetables – instead of a cheese and ham wrap – she said.

“We realise that Australians aren’t going to change their diets overnight,” Schilling said. “This is a really practical change where everyone wins. Lunch is a huge missed opportunity.”

Australia has the highest per capita food-related emissions in the world, the report found. It modelled the environmental, health and economic impacts for a range of Australia-wide dietary changes, finding the shift to 100% plant-based meals would cut dietary emissions by two-thirds, while just switching lunches would cut nearly one-third.

Emissions from domestic aviation are about 10m tonnes a year, according to government data.

The organisations behind the report, which promote moving from meat to plant-rich foods and alternative protein sources, called for a national campaign to increase vegetable consumption along with policy and funding support for plant proteins.

Almost one-third of global greenhouse gas emissions are connected to the global food system through land-clearing, raising animals, growing crops, processing, packaging, transport and waste.

The National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) is reviewing the evidence about sustainability and diet as part of the first update of Australian dietary guidelines in more than 13 years. Draft guidelines are expected to be released for public consultation in December 2026.

An NHMRC spokesperson said the expert committee drafting the new guidelines had prioritised information on health impacts but “information about the economic, social, and environmental sustainability of diets was then considered as one of the many factors that influence people’s ability to follow a healthy diet”.

Dr Diana Bogueva, who researches food sustainability at Curtin University, said relatively small changes in everyday eating habits – when adopted by a large number of people – could have a major impact on the environmental footprint of food production.

However, Bogueva, who was not involved with the report, said the environmental, health and affordability benefits of eating less meat depended on what foods replaced it.

Whole foods such as legumes, grains, vegetables, nuts and seeds provided valuable fibre, vitamins, minerals and plant proteins, she said. “But not every plant-based product is automatically healthy,” Bogueva said. “Simply swapping meat for highly processed products does not necessarily improve the nutritional quality of the meal.”

Sustainable eating didn’t have to be expensive, she added, and some of the most nutritious sources of plant protein – such as beans, lentils and chickpeas – were also among the most affordable.

“We do know there are both health and environmental benefits if you reduce red and processed meat in your diet,” said Alexandra Jones, an associate professor at the George Institute for Global Health, who was not involved with the report.

But rather than focusing on swapping out one protein for another, the emphasis should be on “helping people eat more vegetables, legumes and minimally processed plant foods”, she said.

A good lunch could be as simple as an “old-school salad sandwich” on wholegrain bread, with lettuce, tomato, sprouts and beetroot.
