Measles is continuing to spread across the U.S. this fall, with nearly 6,000 cases confirmed this year - the highest level in over three decades.

In a major public health failure, the U.S. lost its measles elimination status last week, which it had held for 30 years. The status indicates there is no continuous spread of the virus for at least a full year.

This year alone, there have been 13 measles deaths reported in Wisconsin, New York, Virginia and Pennsylvania, including six children.

The vaccine that targets the measles virus provides lifelong protection against severe infection with two doses.

But people who received their vaccine before 1989 - when the two-dose regimen was introduced - might now need a booster, according to experts.

![Certain Americans may need a measles booster as the virus spreads throughout the U.S., experts say](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/20/00/GettyImages-2290372400.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Some people may have received one dose that wasn’t effective. Or, you may just not know if you were ever vaccinated against measles.

“A lot of people don’t know they’re not immune, or they assume they’ve gotten their childhood vaccines, but they didn’t,” Dr. Scott Roberts, a Yale Medicine infectious diseases specialist, explained in a statement.

“Now’s a really good time - it’s a good wake-up call - for people to just go back and check their vaccine records,” he said.

## **Early vaccinations**

If you were born before 1957, you likely don’t need a booster, because you were most certainly exposed to the virus back then.

“They got measles, and that immunity is very long-lasting,” Dr. Christopher Ohl, an infectious disease physician at Dartmouth Hitchcock Medical Center, said in a statement last week.

The first measles vaccine was released in the U.S. in 1963, according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. However, there was only one dose and between 1963-1967 some children received an ineffective vaccine, MIT Health notes.

That vaccine killed a version of the virus that was less effective than the current one.

“There is no harm in getting a booster if you were vaccinated in the 1960s and are not sure if you received an additional vaccination after 1967,” the healthcare system says.

![Two doses of the MMR vaccine are 97 percent effective against measles, according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/19/2305918563..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

## Power of two

If you were vaccinated between 1968-1969, you received a vaccine that has been proven effective, MIT Health points out. In 1971, the vaccine targeting the measles, mumps and rubella viruses - known as the “MMR vaccine” - was released. This is the vaccine America uses today.

One dose of the updated vaccine protects from infection at about a 93 percent level. But two doses provide 97 percent protection, according to the CDC.

“Two shots are what you need to really have a level of immunity that provides that herd immunity,” said Ohl.

Herd immunity refers to having 95 percent of a population vaccinated for measles - a rate that helps prevent transmission of the world’s most contagious infectious disease.

People who are unsure about how many doses they’ve received should check their vaccination records and consult with a physician, especially if they work in a high-risk setting or live where measles is spreading.

It’s okay to get another shot, even if you’re unsure about how many doses you have.

“It's not going to hurt you at all. All it will do is boost your immunity. And if you're not sure, then you'll know if you got another shot that you've had two,” Ohl added.
