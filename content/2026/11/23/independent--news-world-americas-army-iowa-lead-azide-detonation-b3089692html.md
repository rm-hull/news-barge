Environmental groups have accused the U.S. government of maneuvering around a court order as the Army pushes ahead with plans to detonate several tons of bomb-making chemicals in Iowa.

The groups said the move was an attempt to get around the order and avoid public demands for safety and transparency. The Army said Sunday evening that it plans to detonate the chemicals Wednesday “to allow for optimal weather conditions” and for all mitigation measures to be put in place.

The Army plans to invoke emergency provisions to avoid the court order, saying continued delays will make the operation more dangerous as the weather turns colder.

![A family arrives for an early Thanksgiving dinner at Augusta Community Center, as residents make plans for the US Army's detonation of a degraded stockpile of lead azide in Augusta, Iowa,](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/09/2026-11-22T201543Z_1641002763_RC26TOAYATOW_RTRMADP_3_IOWA-ARMY-DETONATION.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“To protect its personnel and the surrounding communities, the Army needs to take swift action,” government lawyers told the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals in a filing late Saturday. “Protection of human health and the environment demand action now, not delay.”

The chemicals came to light in March, when staff at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant found several dozen barrels of degraded lead azide, a material used in munitions production.

The material's crystallization created an unstable and dangerous situation, prompting the Army to assemble experts in munitions chemistry, engineering, and environmental safety to develop a plan. The detonation is planned for Middletown, a small community about 160 miles (257 kilometers) southeast of Des Moines.

The appeals court on Thursday granted environmentalists' request to put on hold a permit that cleared the way for the detonation. The operation was initially planned for Friday, but the Army has since changed the date three times following an outpouring of public concern about potential health and environmental effects.

![Environmentalists have called for a review of the emergency permit the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency approved earlier this month](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/00/IOWA-EJ%C3%89RCITO-DETONACI%C3%93N_13532.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Environmentalists have called for a review of the emergency permit the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency approved earlier this month. In a statement Sunday, the Iowa Environmental Council, Sierra Club, and Earthjustice questioned claims that all alternatives were given appropriate consideration.

“The Army seemed more concerned over the weekend with finding a way to sidestep the judicial process than on meeting the public’s demands for greater transparency, notice to affected communities, and far stronger protection of people, property, and the environment from serious harm,” said Earthjustice Attorney Thien Chau.

Government lawyers said the Army doesn't need a permit to take emergency action against an immediate threat from explosive material.

They said the standards of a federal law regulating hazardous waste don't apply in situations when there's an immediate threat to health, safety or the environment as determined by an explosive or munitions emergency response specialist.

Officials contend that the chemicals are unstable, and the risk of an uncontrolled explosion is growing, particularly as temperatures drop. They said the chemicals may explode as temperatures are expected to drop below 30 degrees Fahrenheit (-1 Celsius) in the coming days.

![Residential buildings stand near the US Army Iowa Army Ammunition Plant](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/23/09/2026-11-22T232245Z_764730682_RC2BTOA5521O_RTRMADP_3_IOWA-ARMY-DETONATION.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

According to EPA documents, alternatives such as neutralizing and decomposing the drums' contents with other chemicals were considered.

But officials said handling the drums would increase the probability of an accidental, fatal detonation. Environmental groups disagreed, arguing in their petition that officials did not fully consider other alternatives. They also criticized officials for not sharing more information with the public.

The Army had shared details during a public meeting in Middletown in October. But a public advisory a week ago was the first time other communities, some as far away as 100 miles (160 kilometers), learned about the potential impact.

Nearly 400 people live in Middletown, but thousands more in surrounding areas could be affected by detonated material that could disperse in the air and land on the ground.

Vibrations from the two planned blasts were expected to be quick, but they could break old windows. That led officials to recommend that some Middletown residents vacate the area.

Iowans in the region were told to stay inside; keep windows and doors closed; bring pets and livestock indoors; and clean cars, windowsills and outdoor surfaces afterward.

“The detonation will result in an air plume with some lead-containing particles which will disperse in the air and deposit on the ground,” an advisory states. “Lead is toxic to humans and animals.” The Army says it intends to carry out the detonation on Wednesday with all mitigation measures in place.
