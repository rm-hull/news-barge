Labor’s new defence bill is “lazy and irresponsible” lawmaking, one of Australia’s leading constitutional scholars has warned, claiming provisions for the government to secretly class any facility as a special security zone could raise serious legal issues.

The prime minister, Anthony Albanese, claimed on Monday that critics of the “accelerating capability and protecting bases” bill were “jump[ing] at shadows”, accusing them of “absurd” claims. But parliamentary crossbenchers opposed to the bill, which was rushed through parliament with little public scrutiny, were joined on Monday by Prof Anne Twomey, who expressed alarm about what she called an increasing trend of ministers exercising discretionary powers without parliamentary oversight.

“Yes, it’s important we have defence legislation that’s flexible to deal with uncertain times, but let’s remember we’re trying to defend our democratic system of government, and we don’t want to throw it out at the same time,” Twomey told Guardian Australia.

In a video on her YouTube channel, Twomey – professor emerita at the University of Sydney – claimed it was “flabbergasting that it was not the subject of proper debate or scrutiny before this law was enacted”.

“This notion of permitting secret alterations to commonwealth, state and territory laws is a really serious development.”

The wide-ranging bill criminalises entering or photographing a defence security zone or a person or thing within, entering or leaving it, with a maximum penalty of up to seven years’ jail time, a $152,880 fine, or both. Security zones can include regular defence bases but can also extend to emergency, temporary or mobile sites as decided by a minister, with wide-ranging powers to “effectively do anything”.

The minister can make a “capability declaration”, which allows for certain activity to be carried out “without contravening certain other laws, or changes the way that other laws apply” – including state and territory laws. Such a declaration can also be held back from being declared as a legislative instrument.

After passing the Senate last week, the final bill passed the lower house on Monday evening. Labor and the Coalition supported the bill, with only a handful of crossbenchers – including Kate Chaney, Monique Ryan and Andrew Wilkie – resisting.

The Greens, David Pocock, Digital Rights Watch and other civil society groups also strongly opposed the bill. But Albanese, in his first public comments on the legislation, said “people shouldn’t jump at shadows”, claiming the powers were necessary and “just practical”.

“It will be accountable obviously for things that are designated, but we live in unsafe times,” he said on Monday.

“I find it remarkable in 2026, there’s a question over whether we need to designate areas as being protected. And we live in times where we know there are more conflicts in the world today than at any time since world war two.”

The Greens leader, David Shoebridge, called the changes “extreme wartime powers” and “an assault on civil liberties”, claiming people may be charged for breaking laws they were not even aware of.

Pocock said Albanese’s response was “disingenuous”.

“We need to protect sensitive defence facilities from threats posed by drones and other technologies. But that doesn’t mean handing the defence minister extraordinary powers without proper safeguards,” he told Guardian Australia.

“At a bare minimum, far stronger safeguards are needed around the use of ministerial powers, the need to protect civil liberties and greater transparency.”

Twomey, in her video, branded the legislation “quite disturbing”, noting declarations could be made secretly – which she said raised “serious rule of law issues” – and called out what she said was a concerning movement of giving ministers more discretionary power.

“Instead of working out what laws need to change to accommodate defence needs and then balancing them against the needs and rights of the community, the parliament has effectively just given up and instead given the power to the minister or indeed senior public servants or defence officers to make decisions that exclude the application of existing laws to defence activities.

“This is a lazy and irresponsible abandonment by parliament of both its scrutiny and its lawmaking roles, raising the risk that these powers will be misused in the future.”
