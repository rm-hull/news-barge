# I thought I was tired because of my busy job - it turned out to be incurable cancer

## Mum of two left feeling scared by sudden shock diagnosis - but is "defying the odds"

When Katie Hartshorne started feeling exhausted, she brushed it off as part and parcel of having a demanding job in education. She’d also been experiencing rib and back pain but assumed she’d just pulled a muscle.

At the age of 55 she was rushed to hospital with heart palpitations. Within three weeks she was diagnosed with the incurable blood cancer myeloma.

Katie, from Garforth, near Leeds, was given the diagnosis in May 2024 which she says "came completely out of the blue" and left her feeling scared.

More than two years on, the mother-of-two continues to defy the odds. She has now thanked the team at St James’s University Hospital in Leeds for supporting her through the rollercoaster of treatment and nursing her back to health.

The hospital team has now scooped a national award for its commitment to patients living with incurable blood cancer myeloma.

Katie has thanked the team for its ongoing support.

"From the first appointment I had so much confidence in them," said Katie, now 57.

"They made me feel so at ease and I instantly knew that they'd done this before, that they were the experts, and that it was going to be okay.

"In the last two years I’ve never once felt rushed in clinic appointments.

"They’re so empathetic and they genuinely do understand and give you the time to ask questions. I’ve always felt listened to and valued.

"They consider your patient view and personal circumstances and make adjustments. For example, they revised my transplant date to accommodate my sister’s wedding during treatment.

"I feel very lucky to have such a knowledgeable, experienced and compassionate team looking after me. They thoroughly deserve this award.”

Myeloma affects over 35,000 people in the UK.

Around 1 in 2 people with myeloma have to wait more than five months before they get the right diagnosis.

In hindsight Katie says that she had been feeling unusually exhausted in the lead-up to her diagnosis, but had brushed the fatigue off as she had a busy job in education.

Things finally came to a head in April 2024, when her heart started racing one evening and she ended up in A&E. Within three weeks she was diagnosed with the incurable blood cancer myeloma.

"The diagnosis came completely out of the blue; I had no idea what it was," said Katie, who has two daughters, Emily, 25, and Ellie, 21.

"I had some tiredness, some fatigue and some rib pain that wasn't going away but I thought I'd pulled a muscle.

"But my heart started racing quite fast one evening and I needed to be taken into hospital because they couldn't slow it down. I knew there was something wrong with me, but I certainly didn't expect it to be cancer.

"They ruled out anything serious or cardiac related, so they then sent me home.

"From there I had some blood tests and, within three weeks, my GP called me and made an immediate referral to Leeds.

"She explained what myeloma was and said: ‘What I'd like you to do is not to Google anything. I'm going to send you a link to Myeloma UK, a really trusted charity that deals with myeloma’.

"I went to the unit really, really scared about what it was. It was a shock. I'd read it wasn’t curable but highly treatable, so I focused on that. Advances in medicine and treatment are significant now for patients. And I was young, fit and healthy before this."

Katie started chemotherapy straightaway followed by a stem cell transplant.

She initially received maintenance treatment to keep her cancer at bay for as long as possible but recently stopped after the debilitating side effects took too much of a toll on her health and energy levels.

Thankfully, more than two years on from her diagnosis, she is still in full remission.

She is now being monitored by the hospital for any sign of relapse.

"What this whole experience has taught me is that I can’t control the diagnosis and I can't change the fact that I've got this," said Katie, who ended up retiring early because of her diagnosis.

"But what I can do is control what I do with it moving forward. I knew that my life was never going to be the same again, but it was just going to be a different version of it.

"So, after my stem cell transplant, I got on a physical therapy programme because I wanted to make myself stronger and fitter and as healthy as I could be to be able to deal with living with myeloma."

Although she is still struggling with back and rib pain, Katie is determined to make the most out of time she’s been given.

She is as active as she can and does Pilates and Tai Chi. She recently travelled to Italy and Greece and is planning even more holidays with her husband and daughters.

"Italy was our first trip abroad since my diagnosis and it was really profound," she said.

"We did Pisa, walked around Florence, ate lots of pizza and finished in Verona, which was wonderful before coming home.

"Yes, I was in pain, and on paracetamol and ibuprofen on a regular basis but it didn't stop me doing anything. I was able to press that reset button and get up the next day and go again.

"I’d like to go to Japan or maybe Canada next year.

"I love to focus on the positives and I am now using my energy and skills to help support other patients through being an active member in a support group and being an advocate for stem cell transplant patients, fundraising for more fitness equipment for patients and raising awareness of myeloma whenever I can. I have received this diagnosis for a reason and I'm going to do something good with it."

She added: “When I was first diagnosed, I met a woman in the Bexley Wing who had had myeloma for 22 years. She said, ‘If I can do this for 22 years, you can do it. I go on cruises with my husband, I've now got three grandchildren. I just get on with my life’.

"I needed to hear that right at the beginning, to know that I'm going to be around. I am determined to live with it for a long period of time.

"And yes, I'm going to see my grandchildren. Yes, I'm going to grow old and grey with my husband."

Hospital staff were presented with the Myeloma UK Clinical Service Excellence Programme (CSEP) Award in recognition of their outstanding care and dedication to people with myeloma, an incurable blood cancer which claims the lives of 3,200 people in the UK each year.

They were praised for their efforts to improve patients’ quality of life and eagerness to listen to their needs.

The accolade, awarded by blood cancer charity Myeloma UK, recognises hospitals’ commitment to raising the bar for treatment and providing compassionate care.
