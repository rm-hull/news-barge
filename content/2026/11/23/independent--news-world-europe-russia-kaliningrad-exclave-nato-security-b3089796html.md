A high militarised Russian territory wedged between Poland and Lithuania could become a potential flashpoint with NATO, security analysts have warned.

Kaliningrad is a Russian exclave slightly bigger in area than the US state of Connecticut with a population of about one million. It is separate from the main body of Russia and comprises the northern half of what, until World War Two, was the German territory of East Prussia.

The Red Army captured it in April 1945 and, at the end of the war, the victorious allies divided it between the Soviet Union and Poland. Koenigsberg was renamed Kaliningrad, which also gave its name to the entire exclave.

Kaliningrad provides Russia with a platform for air defence, missile and naval forces that can project power deep ⁠into northern and central Europe while also restricting NATO forces' ability to reach the Baltic member states overland.

But at the ​same time, it’s ⁠boxed in between two NATO states, meaning it is difficult for Moscow to resupply or defend in the event of a war.

![Kaliningrad is wedged between Poland and Lithuania, which are both NATO members](https://static.independent.co.uk/2024/04/04/16/newFile-1.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

On Saturday, flights at the main airport of Kaliningrad were grounded for a few hours as authorities declared a drone alert after a Russian cargo ship with links to the military was hit by drones and caught fire in the Gulf of Finland.

The attack comes amid rising tensions in the region since Russia's 2022 full-scale invasion of Ukraine, which led NATO to move decisively to strengthen ‌its eastern flank.

The alliance has expanded in the region, with Finland and Sweden ‌joining in 2023 and 2024 respectively.

## Russia told to halt ‘increasingly reckless behaviour’ over Kaliningrad

Russia perceives a growing threat to Kaliningrad as its war with Ukraine nears its fifth year.

Back in May, it said Lithuania's foreign minister was "verging on insanity" for asserting that NATO must show Moscow it was capable of "penetrating the small fortress they've built in Kaliningrad" and razing its air defences and missile bases.

A Western military official ⁠told reporters that dozens of NATO aircraft had been involved in an unannounced operation around Kaliningrad in mid-August 2026 demonstrating the capacity to cut off the exclave's radio and radar links as well as its air defences in the event of a conflict with Russia.

In subsequent social media posts, Russian embassies in Europe have highlighted what Moscow sees as threatening NATO military movements in the Baltic region and hawkish rhetoric on Kaliningrad from policymakers and in the media.

![Moscow has vowed to defend Kaliningrad by whatever means necessary](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/21/14/2026-11-20T080014Z_652753456_RC20ROAKHMH7_RTRMADP_3_UKRAINE-CRISIS-RUSSIA-LOGISTICS.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

NATO says it has increased its presence in the Baltic Sea partly in response to concerns about threats to critical undersea energy and communications infrastructure.

Responding to the embassy warnings, it ‌urged Moscow to halt its "increasingly reckless behaviour" and "irresponsible nuclear rhetoric".

Russian President Vladimir Putin in October warned the West not to escalate a standoff over Ukraine by threatening Kaliningrad, which Moscow has vowed to defend, with nuclear weapons if necessary.

European ⁠states have been walking a fine line since 2022 — supporting Ukraine with weapons, intelligence and sanctions against Russia ⁠while stopping short of joining the conflict themselves — though Moscow has said they are already waging war against it.

Security analysts say any miscalculation could lead to a direct military confrontation - and Kaliningrad, by virtue of its location, would be a potential flashpoint.

Among the possible triggers ‌ could be drone incidents, disruption of critical infrastructure, major ​new missile deployments or anything perceived by Moscow as amounting to a NATO attempt to impose a blockade on the exclave.
