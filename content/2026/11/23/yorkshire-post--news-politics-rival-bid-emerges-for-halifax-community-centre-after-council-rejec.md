# Rival bid emerges for Halifax community centre after council rejects charity's takeover plans

## A rival bid has emerged for Halifax’s Mixenden Activity Centre (MAC) after Calderdale Reform threw out a proposal from a charity that says it was “unanimously recommended” to go ahead, the Local Democracy Reporting Service can reveal.

This month, Cabinet decided the Council would take over the running of the activity centre from North Halifax Partnership (NHP) and re-open the community asset transfer process, while reserving the option to sell on the open market if it stalls.

Following a call-in by opposition councillors this week, a scrutiny panel will now consider whether Cabinet should be asked to rethink the decision.

But one potential alternative has emerged in the form of Mixenden Community Partnership (MCP), led by chair Mandy O’Shea, who said they intend to bid for the centre.

O’Shea told the LDRS: “If the opportunity came, we are going to go for it. It would be silly for us not to.”

Unlike NHP, which reported annual income of £5.6m in its latest accounts, MCP is not registered as a separate legal entity – a requirement of the community asset transfer process under council policy.

O’Shea says MCP, which has 1,400 followers on its Facebook Group, is currently registering as a Charitable Incorporated Organisation.

Nikki Kelly, Reform Cabinet member and one of Mixenden’s councillors, lists membership of MCP on her register of interests.

NHP previously said it was told a “political decision” had been made to reject its bid, with no further reasoning given by the Council.

Cabinet papers show NHP’s full business case was deemed “compliant” by council officers, while an earlier assessment of the initial business case noted “NHP articulate a clear understanding of the need and demand for the MAC in the local community”.

According to NHP, the final assessment of its full business case was “very positive” recording green ratings across every area, and the Community Management of Assets Group (CMAG) “unanimously recommended” the Council agree to the transfer.

NHP also said CMAG described it as “a strong local community anchor organisation with a track record of delivery” and recognised “evidence of community benefit”.

**What went wrong?**

NHP is one of Calderdale Council’s largest third-sector partners, with spending records showing around £18 million in net payments to the charity between 2020 and 2026.

With a gross income of £5.6m in the year to March 2025, NHP spent £5.3m on community, health, wellbeing, childcare, and family support programmes and hubs across Calderdale in the same period.

Timetables posted by NHP show the activity centre had activities running four days a week, most days from morning until evening – including events run by O’Shea and a community kitchen that served free hot food every Thursday.

Before the centre closed last month, the LDRS visited to witness activities with local children, including those who were wheeling their bikes in to get them repaired.

Part of NHP’s business case detailed plans to fund more than half-a-million pounds worth of capital improvement works on MAC to make the best use of the building.

But the LDRS has been told community feedback via Illingworth and Mixenden ward councillors was a significant factor in rejecting NHP’s proposal.

Leader of the Council Dan Sutherland (also a ward councillor in Mixenden) said as much to Cabinet this month when he claimed the decision was made “after feedback from local residents and groups within the community”.

Aftera much-criticised move not to take oral questions from the public on the subject, Cllr Sutherland went on to tell Cabinet his vision was for the centre to be “truly community run” and for residents to “not feel that things are being done to them”.

Mixenden Reform went further with allegations on Facebook, claiming outsiders were “shipped in” and “local residents couldn’t even get a look in” under the old Labour council, arguing the centre “should be predominantly for use by local people”.

But Calderdale Lib Dems leader Cllr Ashley Evans has now said Reform “maligned” NHP with these claims, calling them “totally inaccurate” and “designed to denigrate” the charity to justify the Cabinet decision.

Asked if there had been a rift with the new administration, NHP’s chief executive Tina Wilkes said: “We have always been willing to work constructively with Reform, just as we are with other political representatives and organisations, where this supports the interests of local communities.

“That remains our position and will continue to be so. Our approach is to work with residents, community groups, elected representatives and public-sector partners in the interests of local people.”

NHP also disputes the idea that Mixenden locals missed out, stating it had:

* More than 16,000 attendances across over 900 different sessions.
* More than 2,100 free meals served through the Community Kitchen, with 80 percent of attendees from Mixenden and North Halifax.
* 1,544 attendances at MIXY Youth Bash (a free evening event for children), with 93 percent of registrations from the Mixenden HX2 postcode.
* 158 bike repairs for Mixenden residents through the Bike Co-op.

But O’Shea, a former NHP worker herself, has said grassroots organisations felt “dismissed” and “kept at arm’s length” by NHP while being asked to pay for space.

In a series of public Facebook comments, she also claimed bingo was removed, requests for activities or provision for SEND children were not adequately addressed, and that offers and ideas for increasing the centre’s use were not embraced.

Responding to O’Shea’s allegations, Wilkes said the concerns came from “a small number of individuals within one particular community organisation” and should not be seen as representative of the wider community.

She also said the charity had worked with a “diverse range of residents, groups and organisations”, and the consultation for the transfer included the views of local groups.

The charity rejected suggestions it was unsupportive of grassroots organisations, saying it had helped MCP establish its Open Arms SEND project and supported the group with funding applications.

NHP said room charges were lower than most comparable venues in the area, with discounts available, and that many groups received free equipment storage and access to additional space while NHP paid hire charges for NHP-led groups and events.

NHP also said restrictions imposed during its licence period prevented it from issuing keys to non-council groups or offering permanent tenancies.

But O’Shea insisted there were “barriers” when MCP made requests.

“There was no engagement from North Halifax Partnership with Mixenden Community Partnership, which was a shame because we are the voice of the people,” she claimed.

“Unfortunately, that breakdown in communication took place, and when the opportunity’s come, we are going to go for it and hopefully give Mixenden community what they wanted – which is their building.”

Asked how MCP would fund the centre, including the estimated half-a-million refurbishment, O’Shea said they would reach out to other organisations and write bids, while applying for National Lottery cash.

She also hopes to draw on Mixenden’s £20m ‘Pride in Place’ funding, allocated by Government to give the area a boost over the next decade.

“With the Pride in Place, being on the board myself, we are a grassroots programme and that’s what the Pride in Place money’s for,” she said.

“If there was an opportunity and it was going to benefit Mixenden, [I hope] the Pride in Place would help us a little bit.

“Without Pride in Place and everything else we are going to have to be accountable for the building, by running things from the building and getting other organisations in to work from the building.”

Along with Cllr Nikki Kelly and Labour MP Kate Dearden, O’Shea sits on the Pride in Place Neighbourhood Board that decides how the money is spent.

**What happens next?**

A community asset transfer usually involves handing over the long-term lease of a building to a not-for-profit group (usually for a peppercorn rent), on the basis that they cover the cost of repairs, maintenance, running costs, and insurance.

It can typically take up to two years to complete the transfer, and the group making the application must have a formal legal structure.

Reform’s opponents say running the centre under council management for two years would cost about £160,000 (based on historic figures), while vacancy costs are estimated at about £30,000 a year with the potential for unforeseen spending.

Should the process stall, the Council has said it has the option to sell the building (thought to be worth around £150,000) – though Cllr Sutherland previously toldthe Calderdale Inside Out podcast he would not let the building be lost to the community.

The Council leader also told the podcast “the finances are there” in reference to the Pride in Place funding that O’Shea hopes to use.

But the LDRS understands the Neighbourhood Board, which guides how the Pride in Place money is spent, was not consulted before the funding was publicly linked to the asset transfer by Cllr Sutherland and in Cabinet papers.

In a letter to Whitehall ministers earlier this month, Halifax MP and Neighbourhood Board member Kate Dearden accused Calderdale Reform of trying to “commandeer” the cash injection to “subsidise poor political decision-making”.

In his response, Minister for Local Government, Devolution, and Regional Growth Jim McMahon said the money should “add additional value to existing provision” and “not become a vehicle to fund currently provided local authority activity.”

This week, Calderdale Greens leader Cllr Martin Hey backed Ms Dearden’s concerns, arguing the Pride in Place funding “cannot be used to backstop Council cuts”.

Calderdale Labour leader Cllr Adam Wilkinson also said the potential sale of the building if the new process fails is “deeply concerning” as he called for the Council to share any evidence and risk assessments that were used to make the decision.

Responding to the criticism, Cllr Sutherland told the LDRS: “Last year Labour considered plans to sell the MAC, and if they block our proposal to transfer it to the community then that is what would happen.

“This Reform administration will fight every step of the way to ensure that the community of Mixenden are the ones running their community centre.”

The activity centre was closed under Calderdale Labour in 2020 to save £200,000 a year, but after a 500-strong petition the Council’s then director of public services committed to “securing its positive future as a community facility”.

After a fire at Mixenden Library, the centre became a temporary library until that service was moved to the Mixenden Community Hub.

NHP took over the activity centre’s licence in mid-2025, and after submitting an initial business case in December last year the old Labour Cabinet agreed to push ahead.

This week’s move to ‘call-in’ Reform’s rejection does not automatically overturn it – it just allows the Scrutiny Committee to question relevant Cabinet members and council officers before deciding whether to refer it back to Cabinet or full council.

As Reform have a majority on the Scrutiny Committee and full council, blocking the decision would require Reform councillors to break ranks.

In the meantime, ex councillor Joe Thompson (Labour, Town) told the LDRS he is still waiting (beyond the statutory 20-day limit) for a Freedom of Information request for the correspondence and documentation behind Reform’s decision.

We put our own questions to Calderdale Council, but these were not answered at the time of publication either.

The centre is due to open next week.
