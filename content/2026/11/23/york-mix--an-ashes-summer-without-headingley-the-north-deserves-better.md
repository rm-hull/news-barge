There is something deeply wrong with an Ashes summer in England when the north of the country is effectively told to watch from a distance.

When the Australian men’s cricket team come to defend the Ashes on English soil in 2027, they will play five tests – at Edgbaston, Lords, The Oval, Trent Bridge and Southampton’s Utilita Bowl.

Not Headingley. Not Old Trafford. Not Durham. Not a singles men’s test will be played north of Birmingham.

For a series that is meant to belong to the whole country it feels like a remarkable omission.

Nowhere is it more glaring than Headingley.

Headingley is not simply another cricket ground. It is one of the greatest stages of English test cricket.

A venue that has produced some of the most memorable Ashes moments imaginable, Ben Stokes’ extraordinary heroics in 2019 remain fresh in the memory.

An Ashes test at Headingley feels like an event. So why isn’t it coming to the North in 2027?

It’s complicated, as the English Cricket Board’s venue allocation has been caught up in a long-running dispute involving Hampshire and the 2023 Ashes. Hampshire said that they were promised to hold a test but ultimately, it was given to Headingley.

Recent reporting has revealed that the ECB ultimately paid Hampshire close to £1 million in an out-of-court settlement.

Whatever the legal history, the sporting result is difficult to defend with, Ben Stokes calling it ‘shambolic’ along with Harry Brook and Joe Root also expressing their disappointment.

Joe Root said: “It’s a shame we don’t get to play at Headingley next year, but I’m sure we’ll see many more brilliant test matches here.”

Harry Brook said: “I personally love playing at Headingley it’s one of if not my favourite ground to bat on. It is disappointing but we don’t make those decisions.”

England have won seven tests in a row at Headingley so form-wise the decision makes even less sense.

Headingley hosted the 2023 Ashes. England won an unforgettable Test there three wickets with the atmosphere being electric then and four years later, it has been completely left out.

There is nothing wrong with the five grounds selected, but five tests should not mean five opportunities concentrated almost entirely in the Midlands and South.

The North is not a peripheral part of English cricket. It is one of its foundations.

Yorkshire and Lancashire have produced some of the greatest cricketers in English history for generations, while Durham has established itself as a serious international venue.

The crowds are passionate and loyal and have proved it time and time again.

As I have experience just last week while watching the England v Sri Lanka one day match where the crowd is always chanting and adding to the atmosphere of the match.

So, as a fan from the North this decision just does not sit right with me.

Test cricket is already fighting for relevancy, and the ECB cannot afford to make international cricket feel geographically remote for huge sections of its own supporters.

Instead, they give the North nothing when the ashes is the biggest spectacle in English cricket history.

The ECB will point to commercial commitments and contractual agreements, but supporters do not experience cricket through legal documents.

The Ashes are bigger than any one ground, but they are also bigger than any one part of the country. If English cricket wants Test cricket to remain a national sport, the whole nation needs to feel part of the occasion.

In 2027, it won’t.

And Headingley, of all places, should have been there.
