Twenty students from Gaza are to travel to Ireland to take up scholarships after winning a battle for visas that undercut the Irish government’s reputation as a champion of Palestinians.

The group is due to leave the territory on Monday under strict Israeli controls and travel via Jordan to Dublin to take up places at Irish universities. Another group is expected to follow next month.

The evacuation comes after a four-month campaign to overturn a decision by the Irish Department of Justice to deny visas to more than 60 students in Gaza who had been offered scholarships in Ireland.

The decision dismayed opposition politicians and other critics who accused the department of betraying Ireland’s record of solidarity with Palestinians.

The government backed down last week after a Dublin high court ordered the department to reconsider the decision. The minister for justice, Jim O’Callaghan, approved 48 visas. The first group is expected to arrive on Tuesday, with the remainder coming in late December.

“It’s a mix of feelings I still can’t fully describe,” Amir Alareer, 26, who is to study cybersecurity and artificial intelligence at University College Dublin, said via email on the eve of his departure from Gaza.

“There is hope and relief after months of waiting and fighting for this moment. But it is hard to feel complete joy when I am leaving my family behind in Gaza, and when 28 of my fellow students, who hold the same visas and share the same dreams, are still here waiting.”

The students originally expected to come in summer, and had supporting paperwork from Irish universities, but in July the Department of Justice rejected the visa applications. “The refusals hit us hard,” said Alareer.

The department demanded bank statements, proof of intention to return and other documents and said any appeal would have to be made by post, saying screening and verification were integral to the immigration system.

The students said such documents had not previously been requested and that Gaza’s banking and postal service had been destroyed. Ivana Bacik, a legislator and leader of the Labour party, accused the department of using “spurious reasons” amid a hardening of immigration policy.

Alareer helped to lead a campaign from Gaza that was backed in Ireland by demonstrators, a petition and an open letter signed by actors, writers and artists including Hozier, Nicola Coughlan and the rap group Kneecap. “Running a campaign from Gaza, through blackouts and displacement, was not easy, but I knew that if we stayed silent, our case would be forgotten,” said Alareer.

In October, the high court ruled that there were “significant and demonstrable errors” in the refusal of a visa to Shaymaa Abudalu, a 20-year-old who had been offered an engineering scholarship at University College Cork, and who was the lead case in a batch of similar lawsuits. A high court judge welcomed the decision last week to grant 48 visas and said “huge progress” had been made.

Among the students are five members of a dance troupe who have been offered places at the Irish World Academy of Music and Dance at the University of Limerick.

Alareer, who studied engineering at Al-Azhar University – Gaza, said Israel’s attacks on Gaza had destroyed his home and displaced the family several times. “The hardest experience of all was the famine, when we couldn’t find even a single kilo of flour.” Floods have impeded recent relief efforts.

His family was proud of his scholarship, he said, but saying goodbye would be hard. “They are happy that I finally have this opportunity, but leaving them behind after everything we have been through together is incredibly difficult.”

After studying software and innovation in Dublin, Alareer plans to return to Al-Azhar University and establish a cybersecurity department. “I want to build a generation of Palestinian engineers and researchers who can protect our digital future. Gaza will be rebuilt, and its systems must be rebuilt securely, by its own people.”
