## Good morning. Leo Cendrowicz here, bringing you the Monday newsletter. This week, we’re dealing with more bombing of Ukraine, and the metaphorical bombing of the COP31 climate talks in Turkey.

A week of frantic diplomacy, high-level summits and promises of peace. Yet Russia's war in Ukraine shows no sign of slowing. If anything, the fighting is spreading beyond Ukraine's borders, raising awkward questions about what all that diplomacy has achieved.

**Tightening the screws.** If all goes to plan, the European Commission will today unveil its 22nd sanctions package against Russia,**Jorge Liboreiro** reports.

Having learnt from the previous package, which became mired in ugly vetoes, the Commission intends to keep this one neat and tidy.

The proposal is expected to target Russia's banks, cryptocurrency platforms and shadow fleet, along with companies in third countries helping Moscow obtain blacklisted goods. Nothing new under the sun, then. The aim is to strike a deal before EU ambassadors disappear for Christmas.

It will be the first package since the Commission established a dedicated sanctions office within its Secretariat-General to improve enforcement inside and outside the bloc. The office aims to bring together 50 to 60 staff from across the Commission.

The timing is hardly accidental. The proposal comes amid preparations for multilateral peace talks involving Russia, Ukraine, the US and Europe. Will Moscow seize on the sanctions as another excuse to reject the format? Don't rule it out.

The prevailing view in European capitals is that Russia has little interest in serious negotiations, and that more economic pressure is needed to change the equation.

**Diplomacy meets reality.** After last week's much-hyped summit between**Donald Trump, Vladimir Putin** and**Xi Jinping**, and the gathering of the Coalition of the Willing, there is precious little evidence of peace breaking out.

President **Volodymyr Zelenskyy** says Russia launched around 2,700 drones, nearly 1,460 aerial bombs and 165 missiles over the past week, striking 20 Ukrainian regions,**Sasha Vakulina** reports. He is demanding more Patriot interceptors from Washington while awaiting written details of Trump's proposed NATO-style security guarantees.

Ukraine, meanwhile, appears to be taking the fight further afield. A Russian warship was reportedly destroyed in a drone attack in Libya on Sunday, while another Russian cargo vessel was struck in the eastern Gulf of Finland. Kyiv has claimed neither attack.

And NATO is getting nervous. Three Russian cruise missiles violated Polish airspace on Friday during multinational military exercises involving British troops. Warsaw called it a deliberate provocation, while NATO chief **Mark Rutte** pledged to defend every inch of allied territory.

NATO ambassadors meet in Brussels today to discuss the incident, with Poland seeking additional support for its eastern border.

Meanwhile, Denmark has accused Russian intelligence of plotting to assassinate Ukrainian-American rapper **Kyivstoner** near Copenhagen, prompting the expulsion of Moscow's defence attaché. Russia denies the allegations.

So much for diplomatic momentum. European leaders are still discussing security guarantees, peacekeeping forces and negotiating formats. Moscow is still sending missiles. And Brussels is preparing to tighten the screws again.

We'll be asking this morning's Europe Today guest, General **Seán Clancy**, chairman of the EU Military Committee, about the latest escalations.** COP collapses, leaving climate politics in limbo.** A climate summit that couldn't even agree to mention fossil fuels. After two weeks of negotiations and a record 44 hours of overtime, COP31 ended in spectacular disarray on Sunday, with the EU leading a rebellion against a deal that threatened to take global climate policy backwards.

The showdown in Antalya, Turkey, came after oil-producing countries, led by Saudi Arabia, resisted even reaffirming the commitment made in Dubai three years ago to transition away from fossil fuels.

Ireland's Climate Minister **Darragh O'Brien**, speaking for the EU, refused to swallow the compromise. "We will not accept negotiating backwards in time," he declared, to applause. The UK, Norway and vulnerable island states joined the revolt.

The proposed pact also promoted controversial carbon capture technologies, while failing to guarantee that a new electrification target would be powered by clean energy.

**Climate flop.** The result? No overarching agreement, and the biggest failure of UN climate diplomacy since Copenhagen in 2009.

For Brussels, it was both a diplomatic disaster and a rare show of backbone. EU Climate Commissioner**Wopke Hoekstra** warned that multilateralism was in "dire straits", with a handful of countries effectively holding the world hostage.

The collapse leaves the EU seeking alliances beyond the cumbersome UN process while pursuing its own climate agenda: cutting emissions, expanding renewables and electrifying transport and heating. But Europe's targets will be harder to achieve without global cooperation, particularly as its industries complain about the costs of going green.

The wider picture is bleak. Scientists warn that the world is on course to overshoot the Paris Agreement's 1.5°C warming limit. Yet governments cannot even agree to name the fuels responsible.

The planet is heating up. Climate diplomacy, meanwhile, is running out of steam.

## **In other newsBacklash over Costa's budget cuts.** The battle over the EU's next seven-year budget is heating up. European Council President**António Costa** has proposed slashing €204 billion – more than 10% – from the Commission's original €2 trillion plan to win over Germany and other budget hawks.

But there's a catch. The cuts include €8 billion from cohesion funds for poorer regions, infuriating 17 countries that had demanded they be spared. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, meanwhile, says the cuts don't go far enough.

Costa has also floated a new Big Tech tax, potentially raising €5 billion annually, alongside other revenue measures.

And the European Parliament? Furious. Leading MEPs have rejected the cuts, even as they welcome the digital levy.

With EU leaders preparing for a crunch summit on Thursday, Costa faces a familiar Brussels dilemma: everyone wants Europe to do more, but nobody wants to pay for it. Bulgarian Deputy Prime Minister **Atanas Pekanov** joins Europe Today this morning to discuss the battle.**Brexit, what Brexit?** A decade of acrimony, recriminations and diplomatic sulking seemed to evaporate in Brussels on Friday as British Prime Minister**Andy Burnham** was welcomed back into the European fold. There were smiles, jokes and enough mutual admiration to make the ghosts of Brexit past choke on their croissants.

Ten years after Britain voted to leave the EU, Burnham stood alongside European Commission President **Ursula von der Leyen** and European Council President**António Costa** to celebrate a reset once considered unthinkable. Britain was back in favour, even if it wasn't quite back in the club.

"This is a good day for Britain, and this is a good day for Europe," Burnham declared. “Today, we draw a line on a decade of drift and division by reconnecting with old friends.”

But beneath the love-in lurks an awkward question: how far can this reset go? Burnham, who floated the possibility of rejoining the EU in September, promised more "brainstorming" with Brussels. Yet Friday's three deals may be as good as it gets without a bolder move towards the single market.

Not everyone was feeling the love. Reform UK leader **Nigel Farage was incandescent,** accusing Burnham of betraying Brexit voters in an interview with Europe Today.**MEPs challenge Rwanda return hubs.** Dozens of MEPs are questioning the legality of plans by five EU countries to deport irregular migrants to Rwanda from 2027.

In a letter seen by Euronews, to be sent on Monday to EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas and Migration Commissioner Magnus Brunner, the MEPs warn that Rwanda may not meet the human rights standards required under EU law for return hubs outside the bloc.

“Besides general human rights concerns, Rwanda has a particular reputation for serious maltreatment of refugees and asylum seekers in the recent past,” the letter says.

**Trading places.** The EU and Indonesia signed a landmark trade deal on Saturday, eliminating tariffs on 98% of European exports and creating a market of 730 million people. Negotiated over nearly a decade, the pact includes safeguards on palm oil and deforestation.
