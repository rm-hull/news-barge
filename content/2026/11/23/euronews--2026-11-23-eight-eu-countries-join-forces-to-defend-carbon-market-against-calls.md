## The eight countries warn that further delays to the EU's expanded carbon market would undermine energy security, prolong dependence on fossil fuel imports and jeopardise investment in clean technologies.

Eight European Union countries have joined forces to defend the bloc's expanded carbon market, warning against any further delays or changes to its implementation.

In a letter to Climate Action Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra, seen by Euronews, the climate ministers of Denmark, Finland, Germany, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Portugal, Spain and Sweden argue that the EU's dependence on imported fossil fuels is a major economic and security vulnerability.

They see the new Emissions Trading System (ETS2) as part of the solution, pushing governments and businesses to accelerate the shift to electrification.

"By providing a cost-effective means to accelerate the decarbonisation of the road transport and building sectors, ETS2 strengthens Europe’s energy security by reducing dependence on fossil fuel imports and promoting electrification," reads the letter dated 23 November.

The intervention comes amid mounting political pressure from the four-nation Visegrad Group, Italy and Greece to soften or delay the system following the 15-16 October EU summit in Brussels.

Resisting calls to scrap ETS2, EU leaders instead agreed to assess its impact ahead of its introduction in January 2028, after it was already delayed by a year.

ETS2 will put a carbon price on fuels used for road transport and heating, with energy suppliers expected to pass the additional costs on to consumers through higher bills.

The scheme has become increasingly politically sensitive as EU governments grapple with high energy costs and growing concerns about the impact of climate policies on households and businesses.

## **Energy security before climate goals**

In their letter, the eight ministers invoke the disruption of energy supplies through the Strait of Hormuz to argue that reducing fossil fuel consumption is no longer simply a climate objective, but a matter of strategic autonomy.

The EU imported €340 billion worth of fossil fuels in 2025, the ministers noted. Since the closure of the Strait of Hormuz earlier this year, the additional cost of imports resulting from higher prices has reached €127 billion.

The ministers warn that Europe's continued dependence on imported oil and gas leaves its economies exposed to geopolitical tensions, volatile prices and political pressure from suppliers.

They argue that the ETS2 can help reduce that vulnerability by accelerating electrification in buildings and road transport, reducing fossil fuel imports and creating demand for clean technologies manufactured in the EU.

Danish Climate, Energy and Utilities Minister Samira Nawa said ETS2 was essential to the EU's electrification ambitions.

“We need to accelerate the clean transition so the EU can reduce its dependence on expensive and unreliable fossil energy imports. That is crucial for lowering energy prices, strengthening our long-term competitiveness and stepping up our efforts to combat climate change," Nawa reacted.

## **A warning against reopening the ETS2**

The eight governments also warn against allowing the Commission's review of ETS2 to become an opportunity to reopen the legislation and water down its provisions.

They argue that the system is part of a carefully balanced climate framework, combining emissions reductions with financial support for those most affected by the transition.

Central to that balance is the Social Climate Fund, designed to cushion vulnerable households and businesses against higher heating and transport costs.

Changing ETS2 in isolation, the ministers warn, risks undermining that broader agreement.

They also insist that sticking to the agreed timetable would provide businesses with the regulatory certainty needed to invest in cleaner technologies, while generating revenues for national governments.

"A strong implementation of ETS2 without further delays provides regulatory certainty and investment signals that are crucial to ensure the necessary investments towards decarbonisation and electrification," reads the letter.

Czech Deputy Prime Minister Karel Havlíček said the energy crisis, Russia's war in Ukraine and continued geopolitical tensions have let to "exceptionally high energy costs" for households and businesses across the EU.

"Implementing ETS2 under these circumstances, when the system was designed and agreed upon under completely different geopolitical and economic conditions, simply does not seem reasonable to us," Havlíček told Euronews.

"It will also be increasingly difficult to explain to our citizens across the EU why additional costs should be imposed at a time when many households are already struggling with high energy prices and the cost of living."
