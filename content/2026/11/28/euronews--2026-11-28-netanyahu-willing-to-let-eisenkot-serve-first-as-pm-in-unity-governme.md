## The proposed power-sharing deal comes as Israel struggles to form a new government following October’s inconclusive election, with coalition negotiations approaching a crucial deadline.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu announced on Saturday that he was willing to let Yashar party leader Gadi Eisenkot serve first as prime minister under a proposed power-sharing agreement aimed at ending Israel’s political deadlock.

In a statement, Netanyahu said he believed that, as leader of the largest party, he should ordinarily head a national unity government but was prepared to compromise in the national interest.

“Of course, I should lead the unity government or, at the very least, be the first to serve as prime minister under a rotation agreement,” Netanyahu said.

“However, given the gravity of the existential threat and the possibility of forming a government incapable of addressing it, my responsibility as a leader requires me to set everything aside, including the principle that the largest party should lead, and agree to Gadi Eisenkot serving as prime minister first, while I serve as acting prime minister and deputy prime minister at the start of the parliamentary term.”

“In light of my agreement, I expect us to begin genuine, sincere and accelerated negotiations immediately, with the other side also prepared to make similar compromises and concessions, in order to form a national unity government capable of confronting these threats and safeguarding Israel’s security and future” , he added.

The post of alternate prime minister was created in 2020 through an amendment to Israel’s Basic Law governing the executive, as part of a power-sharing agreement between Netanyahu and then-Blue and White party leader Benny Gantz.

The position carries a special legal status. Under Israeli legal precedent, cabinet ministers are required to step down if indicted on serious criminal charges, but this requirement does not extend to the prime minister or alternate prime minister. Netanyahu could therefore assume the role despite the ongoing criminal proceedings against him.

The arrangement was designed to allow Netanyahu to remain in government after handing over the premiership to Gantz, although the planned rotation never took place.

## **Lapid and Golan reject Netanyahu’s proposal**

B’Yachad deputy leader Yair Lapid and Democrats leader Yair Golan dismissed Netanyahu’s proposal, questioning the sincerity of his offer.

“Netanyahu’s proposal is yet another transparent con by a serial fraudster who has cheated every partner and broken every agreement,” Lapid wrote.

He insisted that an Eisenkot-led government must first be sworn in before talks on expanding the coalition could begin.

Golan called on political leaders to respect the outcome of the election and press ahead with forming a government without Netanyahu.

“Enough with the stunts and tricks. Netanyahu lost,” Golan wrote. “It is our duty to respect the will of the voters and form a government of change and salvation.”

By contrast, far-right Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich welcomed Netanyahu’s proposal, saying he had been closely involved in discussions leading up to the announcement and was willing to act as a guarantor of any agreement with Eisenkot and his allies.

“Prime Minister Netanyahu’s offer of unity is genuine and sincere. I know this as someone who has spent many hours discussing the matter with him in recent days and was involved in the deliberations and the decision,” Smotrich wrote on X.

He urged Eisenkot and left-wing Zionist lawmakers to abandon efforts to form a minority government dependent on the support of those he described as “enemies of Israel”.

## **Eisenkot faces uphill battle to form government**

Neither Netanyahu’s bloc nor Eisenkot’s prospective alliance secured a parliamentary majority in Israel’s 27 October election.

President Isaac Herzog subsequently tasked Eisenkot with forming a government after 67 lawmakers recommended him for the premiership.

Eisenkot has until 6 December to form a government, although he can request an extension of up to 14 days.

On Thursday, Eisenkot reached a preliminary coalition agreement with the left-wing Democrats, following an earlier deal with the right-wing Yisrael Beiteinu party.

However, his prospective four-party alliance holds just 48 of the Knesset’s 120 seats, well short of the 61 needed for an outright majority. Netanyahu’s bloc, which includes far-right and ultra-Orthodox parties, holds 53 seats.

Without an agreement with Likud, Eisenkot would need to secure additional parliamentary support from Arab-led or ultra-Orthodox parties.
