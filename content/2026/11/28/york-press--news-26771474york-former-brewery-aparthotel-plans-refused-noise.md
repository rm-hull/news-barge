## 'Christmas has come early' - York music venue safe after hotel plans refused

A well-known York music venue is celebrating after plans to build a hotel and flats next door to it were rejected.

It had been feared that noise complaints from the proposed hotel could force the closure of The Crescent music venue, just west of the city centre off Blossom Street.

City of York Council posted the decision to refuse the scheme for the 29-bed aparthotel from Crescent Developments (York) Ltd yesterday (Friday, November 27).

It led to some 872 letters of objection, along with opposition from The Crescent.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21775723.jpg?type=mds-article-620)How the aparthotel would have looked *(Image: Pic supplied)*

The venue is owned by brothers Ed and Bob Leyland, who since taking over the site in 2015, have transformed it from a working men's club into one of York's leading independent live music venues.

Saying they had just received notification of the refusal, the brothers also posted online: "We would like to say a heartfelt thank you to everyone who sent a letter of objection, the people who shared our story and, of course, the people who made the decision.

"It has been over a year of waiting for an outcome and we can now return our focus to the venue! If you fancy a drink to celebrate, we will be celebrating too! "

The decision has been applauded with more than 100 comments already online supporting the refusal.

One said: "Such bloody good news!!! Well done one and all for making this happen. The loss of The Crescent would be detrimental in so many ways. Proud of you all for fighting and winning."

In their decision, City of York planners said the proposal to redevelop the site, which is within a conservation area, was turned down due to concerns over noise impact on future occupants.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21775724.jpg?type=mds-article-620)How the aparthotel would have looked *(Image: Pic supplied)*

The application, which included commercial units on the ground and lower ground floors, 20 aparthotel units on the upper levels, and nine residential apartments, was rejected by the council's planning department.

The site, which contains vacant former brewery buildings including a malthouse kiln, was deemed to have a neutral effect in the conservation area appraisal.

However, the proximity of the Crescent Community Venue raised concerns over the impact of noise and vibration on future occupants.

City of York Council's planning department stated: "The applicant has failed to provide sufficient evidence to demonstrate that the proposed noise-sensitive uses can be satisfactorily integrated alongside the existing operation of The Crescent Community Venue."

The department was not satisfied that future occupants of the proposed development would be protected from unacceptable levels of music noise and vibration, particularly in relation to low-frequency noise.

It was also noted that the department was not satisfied that the development would avoid placing unreasonable restrictions on the lawful operation of the neighbouring venue.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21775729.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The entrance to The Crescent today *(Image: Pic supplied)*

The proposal was found to be contrary to the agent of change principle set out in the National Planning Policy Framework, which requires new development to mitigate impacts arising from existing noise-generating uses.

This principle was also a key factor in the decision to refuse the application.

The planning department further emphasised that the nature of potential impacts had not been established and there was no suitable mitigation that could be secured.

The proposal, therefore, was deemed unacceptable on amenity grounds, contrary to several policies of the National Planning Policy Framework and the City of York Local Plan.

The proposed development followed a previously approved planning application for the site, which was to provide 3,830 sqm of commercial office space but was deemed unviable.

The new proposal included three main blocks, with the tallest western block incorporating five storeys to the front and a sixth storey added to the rear.

The block was designed to sit behind the retained malthouse facade and incorporate an elevated rear roof profile referencing the historic kiln roof.

The application was ultimately rejected on the grounds of noise impact and lack of suitable mitigation, demonstrating the importance of the agent of change principle in planning decisions.
