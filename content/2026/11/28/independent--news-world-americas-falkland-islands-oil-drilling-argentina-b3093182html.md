Argentina’s bid to block oil drilling near the Falklands Islands was thrown out by an international court on Saturday in another blow to Buenos Aires and a boon for the British Overseas Territory.

The Argentine government had asked the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea to order a suspension of the Sea Lion project north of the islands, arguing that there was an ongoing dispute over their sovereignty.

But the court in Hamburg today rejected the bid by 18 votes to 3, ruling that there appeared to be no basis for believing that Argentina had jurisdiction over the islands.

The remote South Atlantic archipelago is a British Overseas Territory but is claimed by Argentina and remains a hot button issue decades after a short war over the islands in the 1980s.

British foreign minister Ed Miliband and Attorney General Ellie Reeves welcomed the tribunal’s decision, saying in a joint statement that the outcome was a “vindication” of the UK’s legal defence.

But Argentine president Javier Milei said his country “will continue to defend its rights in the framework of international law” and that “this decision is not the final word” on the matter.

![The Falkland Islands are a British Overseas Territory](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/28/12/2305878352..?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&trim=1%2C0%2C1%2C0&auto=webp)

Milei, who has already sanctioned entities involved in oil exploration around the islands, had argued the project represents a “clear and present danger” to his country’s sovereignty claims.

The field, which sits some 140 miles north of the islands, is estimated to hold 500m barrels of recoverable resources and is projected to generate billions of pounds in revenue for the British overseas territory when it starts producing.

The UK has said drilling at the field will not commence until March 2027 with extraction expected to begin a year later.

Britain had argued that granting the request to suspend the project would prevent Falkland Islanders from developing their natural resources and would have "devastating consequences" for the territory's economy.

Argentina had recently presented legislation to crack down on companies involved in oil exploration activities off the islands after US president Donald Trump signalled that Washington could reconsider its traditionally neutral stance on the sovereignty dispute.

![Maurice Kamga, president of the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea (center), during the hearing in Hamburg, Germany](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/28/12/01M8GZVQ241BZD1X9WVRNP27P1.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Following the ruling, Rockhopper Exploration and Navitas Petroleum are expected to push ahead with the plan under licenses granted by the Falkland Islands government and backed by Britain.

They are looking to drill 11 subsea wells initially, followed by a further 12 in the next phase. Production is expected to last for more than 30 years, with the project run from Navitas offices in London, Aberdeen, and the Falklands’ Port Stanley.

The Falkland Islands, which Argentina calls the Malvinas, has been a British overseas territory since 1833.

Argentina invaded the archipelago in the South Atlantic in 1982 and fought a 10-week war that ended with Argentina's surrender to Britain, accelerating the fall of Argentina's military junta.

The largely self-governing British overseas territory has a population of about 3,660, most of whom are of British descent.
