**Shock! A prime minister wants to get noticed by voters who are not obsessed with politics. Horror! The resident of No 10 cares about their image.**

Except, hang on, neither of these things are remotely surprising. Margaret Thatcher used to phone in to chat to the Radio 2 DJ Jimmy Young - millions listening at home, doing the washing up, or with the chat humming in the background at work.

Winston Churchill didn't just like smoking big fat cigars, he posed deliberately with them - scowling in one of his most famous pictures, because the photographer had plucked the stogie out of his aristocratic mouth.

While Westminster has got itself in a spin over Andy Burnham's social media habits this week, the questions are not, should he care about who he is communicating with, or try to cultivate a certain image? The answers to both questions are no brainer yeses. The dilemma is whether Downing Street's efforts have gone too far?

![Prime Minister Andy Burnham stands behind a lectern inside Downing Street. He is raising his right hand and smiling. He wears a dark suit and tie.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/ed6c/live/535c9fb0-ea6d-11f1-a094-5fdc692f3e33.jpg)

Burnham has embraced social media, regularly recording his own content as well as collaborating with influencers

The whole fuss stemmed from a video posted by an online influencer who'd been at a Downing Street shindig. No 10 says the post by Max Balegde, a TV presenter and social media personality whose real name is Max Trobe, was unacceptable, and he's deleted it.

The PM's rivals attacked him for what happened. And it's sent parts of the press into a froth, with accusations that Burnham dodges scrutiny and tough, traditional questioning, preferring to DJ and hope that influencers' youthful cool rubs off on him.

From the first black T-shirt-wearing Instagram post when he started his by-election campaign (the second video was him unboxing campaign leaflets with an Oasis soundtrack, natch), it was obvious Burnham would try to play a strong social media game.

And since he's moved into No 10, he's devoted more time and political energy than any of his predecessors to social media platforms like Tiktok or Instagram, websites like Ladbible or popular podcasts like How to Fail.

Whatever you watch, read or listen to, and by reading this article you too are using a form of non traditional media because you are reading online, there is no point clutching your pearls about the prime minister and his team using everything available to try to reach voters.

"The comments outside No 10 were undeniably offensive and the whole thing is unedifying," one senior Westminster journalist told me. "But does it matter in the grand scheme of things? This isn't new - successive prime ministers have attempted to go direct to influencers with mixed success for many years now."

Before influencers there were plenty of attempts, some cringeworthy, some meaningful, by prime ministers to extend their influence beyond speaking in Parliament, to the established print press, or the main broadcasters.

![Influencer Max Balegde stands outside the front door of No 10 Downing Street. He is saluting with his right hand. He wears a dark jacket and a light-coloured top underneath.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/c648/live/12640ea0-ea70-11f1-99b6-a35a62fa4264.jpg)

Social media influencer Max Balegde deleted a video filmed outside No 10 in which he mimed along to explicit lyrics

Tony Blair popped up on Richard and Judy's sofa, although he might rather forget when he got dragged into the quiz, "You Say We Pay" and didn't do very well. David Cameron took questions from Gemma Collins and Joey Essex for Heat magazine, declining being featured in "Torso of the Week".

I'm reliably told one future prime minister's team asked someone doing work experience to suggest pop songs young people were listening to before going on the radio to discuss their favourite music so he'd look down with the kids.

Separately, Gordon Brown once claimed to be an Arctic Monkeys fan, although later couldn't name any of their songs, and could only say, "They are very loud". In time, he admitted it had all been a PR disaster.

What's different about Burnham is the scale of his 2020s-friendly comms. Arguably, that's a reflection of the sprawl of the modern media.

Perhaps some of the kickback has been because parts of the media fear their influence is being drowned out by the noise from a busier and busier online landscape. Although it's also worth saying many traditional outlets, including the BBC, also have huge presences on their own digital platforms and social media, with more podcasts, posts, and pieces than you could ever dream of.

No 10 might crack a smile of irony seeing established media voices thundering online about activity online. Despite this week's embarrassment, they have no intention of shifting their strategy.

Acknowledging it sounded almost too good to be true, one insider told me on a hospital visit in Watford at the start of the month, a member of the public passing the PM in the corridor grabbed his arm and said, "I follow you on TikTok, you give us hope".

But the row this week highlights what is a simple truth – all content is not created equally – there are important differences in who, and how.

When it comes to influencers, Burnham's team may now think more carefully about exactly who they invite through the door.

One experienced Westminster editor said: "Clearly Burnham's team didn't vet the influencers enough - no-one wants a row about offensive material ending up being the story, when really you were just trying to be a bit cool and appeal to the kids."

![A street sign reads: Downing Street, SW1, City of Westminster. It is placed on a grey wall. In the foreground of the image, dark railings are visible.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/bc59/live/cab3b380-ea6f-11f1-99a1-253766e7ad8b.jpg)

A No 10 spokesperson said Max Balegde's video was "clearly not acceptable"

A prominent content creator told me: "I think it's a great idea for No 10 to involve content creators in their comms strategy, I'm somewhat suspicious of the types of influencers on Burnham's list. There are plenty of journalistic content creators with large followings and engaged audiences who seem to be missing. Is it because they will be more inclined to hold him to account, perhaps?"

Influencers and content creators don't do the same job as journalists, and aren't pretending to either. They might agree to conditions journalists would not accept - sometimes they're not allowed to film footage themselves, and one told me they had to submit questions to a minister before an interview.

One senior political journalist told me: "The problem is when access starts being prioritised for people whose job is to make content, rather than ask questions. If influencers get the access without the scrutiny, it's the public who lose out."

And crucially, content that pops up online has also sometimes been paid for by the government – in other words, it's advertising. And if you see a clip of a politician when you're scrolling online, at first glance, it's not always easy to spot the difference.

When it comes to their own social media accounts? Politicians now can post their own material, even have their own YouTube channels or podcasts, and in the swirl of material swilling around online, you don't always notice straight away how something has been made.

And it matters if it's their press officer, or a paid influencer chatting to them to get their best line, or their most flattering side, or whether they are sitting down in a news studio taking questions they might not want to answer. The former is propaganda, the latter is scrutiny on all our behalf.

The prominent journalist continued: "Holding power to account is a serious responsibility. It requires independence, experience and a willingness to ask difficult and sometimes uncomfortable questions. The ability to reach an audience is no substitute for the ability to scrutinise those in power."

![Kemi Badenoch stands at the despatch box in the House of Commons. She is wearing a blue jacket and gesturing with her left hand.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/a0ce/live/98faa020-ea6e-11f1-99a1-253766e7ad8b.jpg)

Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch accused Burnham of turning No 10 into a "joke" after the influencer video row

But one former press secretary to a powerful politician suggests the traditional media could do with looking in the mirror, before complaining that ministers are looking elsewhere: "We have got into this dead end where politicians don't say what they think and triangulate their answers away to nothing, while our political discourse waits for them to say anything that isn't that. We need to get to a point where we are having the public debate on the hard choices that the country faces - with politicians sticking their neck out, and the rest of us not chopping it off when they do."

Downing Street is adamant the PM is not running from traditional scrutiny, highlighting five broadcast interviews, and two press conferences he has appeared at in the last fortnight, three, what are known as 'pool clips', short shared broadcast interviews this week. And 113 questions he has had to answer in Parliament just since Monday.

In the interests of transparency, Burnham's first TV interview as prime minister was with us for BBC One, and one of a tiny handful of live TV interviews he's done too was on our programme at the party conference back in September.

This week's saga shows there is a careful balance to strike. A senior media executive suggests the prime minister "risks looking like he is ducking scrutiny from proper journalists at a time of national crisis when the public are struggling with crippling bills".

Has it gone too far? Communicating in a deliberately different way is at the heart of what Burnham is doing. He is a politician who proudly displays empathy, who is as comfortable on camera, whether that's in a TV studio or on his own phone.

And it is part of a strategy that has transformed Labour's position in the polls, so it's not going to change for now.

But it has given his rivals material to tease him with, and his critics reasons to carp, and a reminder, in the words of the senior Westminster editor: "Unless he's scrutinised he will make mistakes and government will go wrong. It's as important as that. It's about making sure our democracy works and is checked. So go ahead, do the influencers, reach all of us, but by God make sure you're doing more than a dance around Downing Street."

This week's embarrassment does serve as a warning – post, and share all you want, but using social media doesn't always get you the likes.
