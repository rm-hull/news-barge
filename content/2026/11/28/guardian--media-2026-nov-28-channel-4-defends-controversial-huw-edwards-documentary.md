Channel 4 has defended its controversial Huw Edwards documentary, with the broadcaster saying the film does not diminish the “seriousness” of his actions nor “entitle him to sympathy, redemption or rehabilitation”.

The film, Huw Edwards – Guilty, which has followed the disgraced former BBC presenter for almost a year, has faced a fierce backlash before its airing on 1 December.

Edwards pleaded guilty to three charges of making indecent images of children after he was sent 41 illegal images by a convicted paedophile, Alex Williams. He was sentenced to six months in prison in 2024, suspended for two years.

A Downing Street spokesperson said the prime minister, Andy Burnham, had “no interest in hearing what” Edwards had to say, adding: “Broadcasters are expected to act responsibly and have appropriate regard to their audiences when making decisions.”

A government minister, Emma Hardy, said the planned documentary made her “feel sick”, telling LBC that Channel 4 was providing a platform to “someone who is on the sex offenders’ register”, while another minister, Anna Turley, told the radio station it made her “deeply uncomfortable”. The shadow culture secretary, Rebecca Paul, said: “This man shouldn’t get a minute of airtime.”

On Saturday, Louisa Compton, Channel 4’s director of news, digital and audience trust, responded to the brewing controversy, saying it was in the broadcaster’s remit to “stimulate debate” and that “interviewing someone is not an endorsement of them”.

She said: “Edwards was one of the most prominent and trusted broadcasters in Britain. He pleaded guilty to serious offences involving indecent images of children and has never publicly faced detailed questioning about his criminal behaviour or the complete collapse of his career and reputation.

“There is a clear public interest in confronting him with those questions rather than allowing them to remain unanswered.

“We are also acutely conscious that behind offences involving indecent images are real children and real victims – including those as young as seven – who have been abused.

“Nothing about making this film diminishes the seriousness of that harm, nor does Edwards’ participation entitle him to sympathy, redemption or rehabilitation. His account is there to be tested, not accepted.”

Compton also said that Edwards had not been paid for participating in the film or had any control over its content. “We should be capable of examining difficult issues rather than looking away because we don’t like what we see. We understand that some people will profoundly disagree with our decision to make this film,” she said.

“But discomfort is not, in itself, a reason for journalism not to ask difficult questions. We believe those questions are in the public interest.”

The film will follow Edwards at home in rural Wales, where he has been since his career ended after his criminal conviction. In a short trailer, the 65-year-old says he had “lost the plot” at the time of his crimes.

The film-makers said that after attempts to “ingratiate himself back into public life”, Edwards was shown being forced to “confront his loss of fame, declining health, fractured relationships and his crimes”.

The fall of Edwards, a veteran newsreader who announced the queen’s death on the BBC in 2022, has already been depicted on television in Channel 5’s drama Power: The Downfall of Huw Edwards.

The former presenter later said the programme was a “one-sided account” and has made a complaint to the media regulator Ofcom. It is investigating the programme under its “fairness and privacy” rules.

The drama became Channel 5’s highest-rated show of the year after its broadcast in March, attracting 3.8 million viewers in four weeks.

The two-part series focused on an earlier allegation against Edwards made by the Sun in July 2023 that claimed a “top BBC star” had paid a teenager more than £35,000 for sexual pictures.

A Channel 5 spokesperson said: “We stand by the programme and will be robustly defending this complaint.”

* Huw Edwards – Guilty airs on Channel 4 on Tuesday at 9pm.
