Among the nation’s many mass shootings, the massacre of December 14, 2012, at Sandy Hook Elementary School in Newtown, Connecticut, remains uniquely horrific.

Within under five minutes, a single gunman discharged 154 rounds, claiming the lives of 20 first-grade schoolchildren, aged 6 and 7, alongside 6 adults.

Following the deadliest elementary school shooting in US history, Connecticut legislators reacted swiftly by expanding the state’s prohibition on what it defines as "assault weapons" to incorporate the AR-15 and similar semiautomatic rifles like the one used by the Sandy Hook gunman.

Almost 14 years on, the U.S. Supreme Court is scheduled to hear oral arguments on Wednesday in a case that could dismantle that ban and declare a right under the US Constitution to possess firearms such as the AR-15.

Currently, 11 U.S. states and Washington, DC, prohibit the AR-15 and comparable weapons.

## A mother’s anguish

To Nicole Hockley, whose 6-year-old son Dylan was killed at Sandy Hook — shot five times in the torso and head — the prospect of the Supreme Court overturning the law born from the loss of children in her community evokes anger.

"This would basically be saying, 'We don't care what happened at Sandy Hook. We don't care that Connecticut wants to do something about it.' It would almost be saying, yet again, 'Dylan's life didn't matter,'" Hockley, 55, said in an interview.

"We have to value children's safety and people's safety as strongly as we value the right to have guns," Hockley added.

Holding a 6-3 conservative majority, the Supreme Court has adopted an expansive interpretation of the Constitution's Second Amendment, which protects the right to "keep and bear arms." The court steadily broadened gun rights through landmark rulings in 2008, 2010 and 2022.

The justices will evaluate Connecticut's legislation alongside a comparable restriction in Cook County, Illinois, which encompasses Chicago. Lower federal courts ruled against plaintiffs who brought legal challenges against the prohibitions, including the Second Amendment Foundation gun rights group and individuals seeking ownership of AR-15 pattern firearms.

President Donald Trump's administration has backed the challengers in the case. A ruling is expected by the end of June.

Challengers argue that established Supreme Court precedents clarify that because rifles like the AR-15 are owned by tens of millions of Americans and are thus in "common use," they cannot be restricted.

They point to the court's 2008 District of Columbia v. Heller decision, which invalidated a handgun ban in Washington, DC, concluding that "the sorts of weapons protected" by the Second Amendment are those "in common use."

"The stakes in this case couldn't be higher," Bill Sack, an official at the Second Amendment Foundation, told Reuters. "If the government has the power to categorically ban the most popular guns in the country, then the right to 'keep and bear arms' is a dead letter."

## ‘Unusually dangerous’

Connecticut, which expanded its prohibition further in 2023, maintains that under existing Supreme Court precedent, governments may restrict excessively dangerous weapons, and historically have done so precisely because their widespread presence posed immediate threats to society.

"From 14th-century England to 21st century America, governments have restricted weapons because of their unusually dangerous properties," the state wrote in a brief, citing the launcegay, Bowie knife and the "Tommy gun" as past examples.

"And the AR-15 is the signal example of an unusually dangerous weapon today given its unprecedented lethality and frequent use in mass-casualty events and law-enforcement killings," the state said.

Originally designed for military use, AR-15 rifles feature mechanisms capable of killing more people faster than any other commercial firearm available, state officials noted. State filings highlight that since 2012, AR-15s or similar rifles were used in 12 of the 17 deadliest US mass shootings, including Sandy Hook Elementary School.

For Dane Hassan, proprietor of Southbury Trading Post, a gun store roughly 6 miles (10 km) from the school, Connecticut's strict gun legislation leaves firearm enthusiasts like him feeling constantly defensive.

"Every year, more and more seems to be taken away," said Hassan, a former U.S. Marine and state police officer. "It's frustrating for the law-abiding citizen."

Hassan, 56, supports certain measures, including background checks for gun buyers, but contends the Supreme Court should strike down the AR-15 ban, despite not being a party to the suit.

"Unfortunately there have been some bad actors over the years that have used that platform to commit heinous crimes. And obviously I feel terrible for the victims and their families," Hassan said.

"But just to take that one platform and ban it doesn't stop anything," Hassan said, urging a stronger, national focus on people with mental health issues.

"Bad people with bad intentions will use whatever they can get to commit heinous crimes," Hassan said.

Attorneys representing the individual plaintiffs challenging Connecticut's statute noted that their clients declined to comment.

Outside a bustling Newtown preschool, a teaching assistant who cared for several children killed at Sandy Hook voiced concern over lifting the ban.

"What would you do to protect everybody? Children are so innocent. They don't have a voice," said Debi, 61, who asked that her last name not be used.

The Manhattan-based 2nd US Circuit Court of Appeals ruled in Connecticut’s favor, rejecting claims that AR-15s are protected due to "common use." Similarly, the Chicago-based 7th US Circuit Court of Appeals upheld Cook County's restriction.

## Hotly contested litigation

The legal battle touches on one of the most polarizing debates in the United States, which holds the world's highest gun ownership rate. Numerous outside groups have filed briefs backing both sides before the court.

Even the phrase "assault weapon" remains contested. Plaintiffs view it as a political term exploiting public confusion between semiautomatic rifles and fully automatic machineguns, while ban advocates observe that the gun industry historically referred to them as assault rifles or assault weapons.

Hockley, who co-founded Sandy Hook Promise to train educators and students on school shooting prevention, lamented the "politicized and divisive landscape" surrounding US gun regulations.

"Protecting children and protecting constitutional rights can coexist," Hockley said.

"I'm imagining that the Supreme Court justices have children in their lives. And I recognize their job is to interpret and uphold the law," Hockley said. "But can they look at their child and say, 'I'm going to override Connecticut's laws to protect its own people, and I'm going to value the AR-15 and semiautomatic rifles over your life?' It just makes no sense."
