# No one should mourn the abolition of the underachieving Environment Agency: David Behrens

## Process management in Whitehall is the dubious craft of devising a five-minute task, writing a 200-page report and then appointing a committee to tell you it’s not worth the bother.

There are at least 14,000 people in the corridors of power whose job involves ‘managing processes’ but no single representative body dedicated to looking after their interests. You’d have thought they’d have worked out a process for that.

But why would they? The job title is nothing more than a euphemism for watching your back, so that when everything collapses into chaos you can walk away from the wreckage and blame someone else.

“We followed the process – it’s not our fault.” It might as well be the motto they hang over the door.

It works in almost every situation. “We stuck to the process and there’s no alternative – we’re sending 1,200 asylum seekers to that old military base in your village.”

Risk aversion was the phrase used this week by Louise Haigh, the de facto deputy prime minister, to sum up the equation. In placing the heads of at least two quangos on the chopping block, she accused their organisations of behaving as if it were better not to let something happen than risk it going wrong.

What she wanted, she said, was a government machine that could “be bold, be fast and make things happen”. She’s got the wrong machine, then. She might as well plug in a Goblin Teasmade and tell it to pour her a coffee.

But she’s right to try. And in seeking to abolish the Environment Agency and Natural England she has identified a pair of underachievers even by Whitehall standards.

Natural England’s process is well known and widely ridiculed. “We know there’s a housing shortage but we followed the process and there’s a colony of newts in your pond so you can’t fill it in for new homes” – that’s its standard response.

Just this week it was accused of blocking houses in Kent because of some rare ‘jumping spiders’ in the vicinity. I’m no David Attenborough but I’d guess a jumping spider could leap out of the way of an encroaching JCB without any help from a committee of civil servants.

The Environment Agency is an altogether more insidious organisation because it’s not just its competence that is in question but also its probity. Earlier this year it was publicly accused of colluding with privatised water companies to deliberately downgrade pollution incidents, and of failing to secure a single successful prosecution despite nearly two million sewage discharges.

Now, you could argue that proper process management might have prevented failings like those because they were the work of rogue officials not doing things by the book. But let me remind you of the environmental harm the agency can do even when it does stick to its procedures.

Earlier this year, in the East Riding village of West Newton, it waved through a mining application for what you or I would call fracking, without having discovered that the company concerned was doing it to fuel the market for cryptocurrency.

You don’t mine that stuff from the ground; you mint it in data centres using vast amounts of electricity which is more economical if you generate it yourself – and that’s what speculators fooled the agency into letting them do, by pitching mini-fracking rigs that are just on the right side of the law.

The result is that valuable natural resources which the Environment Agency is supposed to protect have been sacrificed to the needs of investors and criminals who prefer the anonymity of crypto to real money.

This could have been avoided in West Newton had the agency asked common-sense questions about motive and benefits to the community. But its process allowed it only to judge whether the project fell within an arbitrary set of rules.

It is at the heart of the problem Louise Haigh identified: rigid workflows which put process before progress, impeding the right projects and enabling the wrong ones.

Look at it this way: if someone found newts in West Newton the operation there would be shut down faster than a government U-turn, because that’s what the ‘process’ demands. In fact it is exactly what has happened at the army asylum site at Piddington in Oxfordshire.

And the Environment Agency hasn’t learned from the West Newton debacle. This week it greenlit another frack-like excavation at Cloughton near Scarborough, despite the council’s opposition.

It could prove a pyrrhic judgement, for it demonstrates that process-bound organisations serve no-one’s interests but their own. The Environment Agency may soon realise that the dig it authorised at Cloughton is for its own grave.
