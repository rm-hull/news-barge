Nearly four in ten voters think Andy Burnham is reopening the Brexit debate in order to take the UK back into the European Union, according to a new poll.

The prime minister has insisted he has “no fixed destination” in mind when the government looks at the options for the UK's long-term relationship with the EU, including potentially rejoining the bloc. Other possibilities include becoming part of the customs union and/or the single market again, or maintaining the status quo.

Mr Burham insists the UK public will be the “boss” of any future UK-EU relationship, although he said in an interview last September: *“*I’m going to be honest. I’m going to say I want to rejoin. I hope, in my lifetime, I see this country rejoining the European Union”.

The More in Common poll found that when the public was asked, the biggest group, 39 per cent, said they think the PM is trying to rejoin the EU.

Another 34 per cent think that he wants to get closer to the bloc but stop short of actual membership, while 13 per cent believe he wants to keep things as they are.

![The prime minister has insisted he has “no fixed destination” in mind](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/18/2305443262..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Luke Tryl, the executive director of More in Common, said the EU “remains a political danger zone” for the PM, adding: “There is now a broader lack of certainty about what Burnham’s end goal is, with a plurality of Brits now saying that the prime minister is trying to use the negotiations to rejoin the EU. Telling a clear story about UK-EU relations will be increasingly important.”

Five former prime ministers, including Boris Johnson, Liz Truss, Lord Cameron, Theresa May, and Tony Blair have questioned whether the Brexit debate should be reopened.

But Mr Burnham hit back at his critics on Monday and accused his predecessors of being "stuck in the past" as he defended his decision to begin a conversion on the future of EU-UK relations.

He insisted that the move was “not a prelude to anything. It’s a pragmatic approach and it will continue to be a pragmatic approach”.

“If it’s in Britain’s national interest, I will recommend it ... If it is not, then I won’t. There is no fixed opinion here. There is no fixed destination,” he said.

“Europe could change again in the next year, 18 months, and we’ll have to obviously watch how life changes. But where Britain can make progress, surely we should make progress.”
