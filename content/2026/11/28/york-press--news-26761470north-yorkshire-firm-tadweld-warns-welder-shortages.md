The UK is facing a critical shortage of skilled welders, with industry experts warning of a looming crisis in manufacturing.

Tadcaster-based steel fabrication specialist Tadweld has raised the alarm over an estimated shortage of up to 35,000 welders.

This comes as a large proportion of the workforce nears retirement age and too few young people are entering the trade to replace them.

Chris Houston, managing director of Tadweld, said: "We’re approaching a point where the number of experienced welders leaving the industry could significantly outweigh the number of young people coming through to replace them.




"Welding is fundamental to everything from construction and infrastructure to defence and energy.

"Yet we’re facing the prospect of a major skills shortage at the very time we need these capabilities to support the future of British manufacturing."

The warning follows the publication of Alan Milburn’s final review into youth employment, released in November 2026.

The report highlights the decline in entry-level jobs and apprenticeships, calling for stronger collaboration between employers and government to rebuild these pathways.

Research from an MPL Fabrications industry report published in October 2026 found that the average UK welder is now 55 years old, intensifying concerns about a wave of retirements.

Department for Education figures show apprenticeship starts among under-19s in England fell by 5.4 per cent between August 2025 and April 2026, despite an overall rise of 8.7 per cent in apprenticeship starts.

Tadweld’s own research identified welding as one of the highest-paying skilled trades available through an apprenticeship.

Using data from the Office for National Statistics and employment platforms, the company found the average salary across 20 leading trade roles was £38,925, close to the UK median full-time salary of £39,039 recorded in April 2025.

Fully qualified welder apprentices can typically earn around £30,000 a year, with experienced welders able to make more than £50,000, not including overtime or bonuses.

Despite these earning opportunities, the drop in younger apprenticeship starters raises questions about how well technical careers are being promoted and supported.

Tadweld has pointed to rising apprentice wage costs, employer National Insurance contributions, and training expenses as barriers for manufacturers looking to offer new positions.

Mr Houston said: "Welding offers excellent earning potential, practical skills and long-term job security, but we need to make it easier for young people to access these opportunities and for businesses to afford to train them.

"We cannot rely on an ageing workforce without investing in the next generation."

Women currently make up less than four per cent of the UK welding workforce, with Mr Houston stressing the need to attract a more diverse and younger talent pool.
