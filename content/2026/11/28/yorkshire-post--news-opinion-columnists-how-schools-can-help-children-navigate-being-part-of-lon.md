# How schools can help children navigate being part of 'loneliest generation': Matt Bromley

## You don’t have to be alone to be lonely. For too many young people, feeling lonely in a crowded corridor is becoming an ordinary part of growing up. In The Loneliest Generation, the Institute for Public Policy Research (IPPR) describes young people facing weakening friendships, deteriorating mental health, and diminishing confidence in the future.

They have fewer close friends than a decade ago; one in 20 has none.

Secure work and independent living have become harder to reach. These are not simply problems of individual resilience; they point to the weakening of relationships, institutions, and shared spaces that help people feel they belong.

The Joseph Rowntree Foundation’s report Essential but Unequal argues that communities thrive when people can access places and activities that bring them together and nurture trust. Libraries, parks, and community centres are the foundations of a healthy society.

Whilst they cannot reverse housing insecurity or replace every closed youth club, they can help young people to contribute and belong. Belonging means more than being present or told everyone is welcome; it means knowing your presence is valued, your voice matters, and your absence would be noticed. And a young person who feels invisible, unsafe, or unwanted is unlikely to flourish.

As such, belonging must be deliberately cultivated and consistently protected. For some pupils, school provides reassurance through routine, encouragement, and adults who see their potential. For others, bullying, social hierarchies, or inflexible responses to difference deepen exclusion.

Relationships begin with small gestures: a greeting, a thoughtful question, a quiet conversation after a difficult lesson.

Knowing pupils as individuals offers something no algorithm can replicate: the experience of being genuinely known. Schools can also think beyond their gates. Working with councils, youth services, voluntary organisations, and sports clubs, they can connect provision – perhaps by hosting a community group or meeting for isolated parents.

To be clear, schools must not become unpaid youth services or compensate for every withdrawn public service.

Social infrastructure needs sustained investment; goodwill cannot replace proper provision. Schools can be part of the answer, but not the whole answer.

Young people need some control over what happens next. The IPPR identifies a loss of agency: ambitions may remain, but confidence in achieving them has weakened.

Schools cannot promise an easy adulthood, but they can offer opportunities to influence the environments in which pupils learn. School councils matter only when pupils see that their contributions change real decisions.

Consultation without consequence breeds cynicism; genuine participation teaches citizenship. Young people should help shape school spaces, anti-bullying approaches and activities, with responsibilities beyond symbolic roles.

Schools can broaden their definition of achievement. Grades matter, but so do the abilities to collaborate, create, and care. A pupil may find confidence in a workshop, theatre, sports field or community project. These are not consolation prizes for those who struggle with exams; they give young people identities richer than a predicted grade.

Friendship cannot be prescribed, but schools can create conditions in which it grows.

Who has somewhere to go at lunchtime? Which pupils never join a club? Activities should offer different routes into connection. Chess, gardening or music give pupils something to do together before they know what to say. Belonging must not depend on becoming like everyone else.

Neurodivergent pupils, those with special educational needs or disabilities, and young people facing poverty or discrimination may encounter additional barriers. Inclusion means making activities accessible, taking subtle exclusion seriously and listening when behaviour signals distress.

Fairness sometimes requires different support so everyone can participate.

Relationships with employers, colleges, universities and community organisations can make adulthood more tangible and show there is more than one route to a fulfilling life. Careers education should be an ongoing conversation, not a one-off assembly.

Curriculum learning can help pupils understand their surroundings. Local history can explore how industry shaped a town; geography can examine a changing neighbourhood; science can investigate biodiversity. Community projects can bring pupils together with older residents or improve a shared space, allowing young people to contribute to their place.

Schools should ask pupils whether they feel safe, have an adult they trust, can influence decisions and believe they have a future. Attendance and attainment data cannot tell the whole story. Questions must be asked sensitively and followed by action.

Schools have limits. Pastoral care cannot solve a housing crisis, and an after-school club cannot replace a properly funded youth service. Schools in poorer communities may face the greatest demands with the least capacity. Government needs to provide sustained funding, stable partnerships, and social infrastructure.

The question is not simply whether young people achieve at school, but whether they can imagine a future, form meaningful relationships, and participate in community life.

A good school gives pupils knowledge, qualifications, and skills. A great school also helps them discover what they can contribute, whom they can trust, and where they belong.

This may happen in quiet moments: a teacher noticing a pupil has gone silent, or a club where an overlooked child discovers a talent.

These moments do not abolish loneliness, and schools cannot repair the wider conditions that make belonging harder. But they can challenge the idea that everyone must navigate the world alone. Every child deserves to know there is a place for them, because they are part of something larger.

Matt Bromley is CEO of bee and chair of the Building Equity in Education campaign. He is the author of numerous books on education.
