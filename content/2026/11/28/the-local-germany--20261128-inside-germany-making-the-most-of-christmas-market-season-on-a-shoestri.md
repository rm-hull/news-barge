Living in Nuremberg, it sometimes feels like the whole month of December – and honestly a good bit of later November and early January as well – revolves entirely around trips to the Christmas and winter markets.

While the 'Christmas season' takes place throughout the Advent – which begins on Sunday, November 29th this year and extends to December 25th – I would argue that Germany also has a similar, but slightly extended 'Christmas market season'.

Importantly, the latter is not really specific to any particular faith or background. Virtually everyone who lives in Germany visits the seasonal markets around this time.

The catch, however, is that even a single trip to a Christmas market can be quite expensive if you're not careful. So if you, like me, are liable to end up at one roughly a dozen times in the coming month, it pays to have a strategy to keep your spending down.

With that in mind, here are my tried and tested tips for getting the maximum merriment for a minimal cost this Christmas market season.

**Check out the smaller, local markets**

The big markets – like Nuremberg's Christkindlesmarkt – are impressive, and for anyone visiting Germany during the holiday season for the first time, I would say a trip to a big, central market in whichever city you are in is a good place to start.

But personally I find trips to neighbourhood and village markets to be more rewarding. The smaller, less crowded markets feel more cosy, and they can offer a unique view of regional takes on the German holiday tradition.

They can be quite a bit cheaper too. Compared to the main markets in the bigger cities, the cost of a cup of *Glühwein*or a* Bratwurst*may be a couple of euros less at a small, village market not too far away.**READ ALSO:**

Sticking with the Nuremberg region for this example, if a friend were visiting from out of town, I'd make a point to start with an evening at the main Christkindlesmarkt (including its adjacent Sister Cities Market and Children's Market areas).

![View of a choir at the Nuremberg christmas market.](https://apiwp.thelocal.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/561016805.jpg)

But if I were meeting a friend for a quick drink and a chat after work, I'd instead head to the GoHoHoHo Market in the nearby Gostenhof neighbourhood. This is a much smaller market where locals gather under trees adorned with comically oversized Christmas bulbs to try new takes on the classic seasonal drinks – like pomegranate flavoured *Glühwein* or even a warm espresso martini.

For a fun mini-adventure on a free Saturday or Sunday I might catch a regional train to just about any town nearby; somewhere near the town's central square I will inevitably find a local Christmas market.

Just next to Nuremberg, there's the medieval market in Fürth, or Erlangen's three themed markets which each offer a slightly different flavour.

But to really get off the beaten track, I advise going further still.

One of my personal favourite Christmas market experiences happened in the small town of Altdorf bei Nuremberg, where I just happened upon the market on my walk back to the nearby train station after going for a small hike nearby.

Set in the courtyard of an old, Renaissance-style building, and with no tourists in sight, I felt as if I had suddenly fallen back to a simpler time where warm drink and food was served out of cauldrons, people wore animals skins over shirts with laces and buttons and everyone in the village seemed to know each other.

**Bring your own hot drink**

This is not really for the casual once-a-year Christmas market patron, but rather for anyone who will find themselves at the seasonal markets again and again in the coming weeks – and would rather not spend more on warm drinks than say health insurance in December.

The hack here is simply to bring your own drink of choice along with you when you head out to the market – better still if you have an insulated thermos that can hold a hot drink and keep it warm.

This is also a good tip for anyone who prefers a Hot Toddy, or another warm drink, to the variations on mulled wine and hot cocoa that are widely available at the market stalls.

Personally, I do like to try the drinks sold directly at the market, but then I find its nice to have a thermos full of something similar from home so that I can occasionally pour myself a 'free refill'.

Having your own thermos can also come in handy in those moments when you haven't finished your drink yet, but want to leave or explore further. In this case you could pour what's left into your own vessel and take it with you, and still return the mug to collect your *Pfand*.

![People enjoy Glühwein at a Christmas market in Hamburg.](https://apiwp.thelocal.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/559044755.jpg)

**Shop around before buying**

When it comes to gifts there are plenty to be found, and the Christmas markets are also useful for finding gifts for your family and friends.

The advice here is to try and resist buying something at first glance, and instead take a stroll around the market first to see if the same or similar goods are on offer elsewhere to compare prices and quality.

The same can be said to some extent for food and drinks. The main kiosks at a market usually have the same basic prices, but sometimes stands beyond the central area, or even to-go windows set up in nearby restaurants and cafes, can offer more competitive price points.

All of that said, the Christmas market experience is not only about the things you eat and drink and buy.

In my mind the magic of Germany's winter markets is found is the sense of warmth and light that they bring to a time of year that would otherwise consist of a series of cold, dark nights. And you really don't need to spend very much to tap into that feeling.
