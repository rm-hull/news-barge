# Town End Close Farm, Cawood: ​Generations game for new challenges

## One hundred and five years ago a man with one glass eye became the first of what has become a fourth generation of arable farmers and agricultural contractors. Stan Liddle had returned from WW1 with shrapnel from a bomb having taken half his sight.

Graham Liddle, his grandson, spent many happy times with him. Today he farms alongside his father Geoff, Stan’s son, and his own son Eddie at Town End Close Farm in Cawood. The Liddles can trace their ancestry in the village to 1803, but Stan started farming on his return from the trenches.

‘We farm just over 300 acres we own,’ says Graham. ‘And a further 1100-1200 on various arrangements from share farming, contract farming and farm business tenancies. We grow crops on around 1450 acres.

Graham has never wanted to do anything else. It’s the same for Eddie who has joined the family enterprise more recently.

![Geoff Liddle pictured with some of his vintage and classic tractors.](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOjM4MjNhNDRhLTljZWUtNDQ4ZC05YWJhLTU2YmMyZjg4NGE2MzoxZDE1ZmFjZC0xNmNmLTQzNDItYmVkZC0xM2I4NmVjMDRjOGM=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)


‘For next harvest we’re growing around 400 acres of sugar beet, 260 of winter and spring barley, 120 oilseed rape, 100 maize, 80 rye, 25 carrots and about 450-500 of winter wheat,’ says Graham. ‘We also rent out 40 acres for potatoes.

Graham’s changed his cropping to help the soil and find new markets.

‘This spring was the first time we’ve ever grown maize. It’s for the new AD plant at Sherburn in Elmet. We’re aiming to grow 1000 tonnes of forage maize and 1000 tonnes of forage rye for it and get the digestate back to reduce bills by using more organic fertiliser. It’s also the second year we’ve grown carrots.

‘Growing maize, rye and sugar beet means I don’t have any second wheats and will hopefully add further blackgrass control.

‘I’m quite a fan of the plough and not the plough. It depends on the crop that’s been in and is going in. I do play about with cover crops and we’ve done a lot of min-till cereal drilling for years. We’ve a 6-furrow Kverneland which is relatively new and a Lemken that dad bought because he wanted a real tidy reversible plough behind his Ford 8340 classic tractor to enjoy himself and go to ploughing matches.

‘Dad’s 87 still switched on and very active in the business and likes nothing better than taking his own classic outfit out and doing a bit.

Graham says the land type varies considerably and that the continuous flow dryer he had installed a handful of years ago has proved a successful purchase.

‘We have an absolute range of land from blow away sand, sandy loam, heavy clay, silt, warp and limestone. That’s why I’ve three different corn drills, combinations that suit each land type and weather.

‘We used to have a mobile batch dryer but the continuous flow means we deal with grain with a bit more moisture without a sweat. If I’ve around 1000 tonnes of damp corn I don’t lose sleep, I just press buttons. Years ago we would be working from 6 o’clock in the morning until 1 o’clock the following morning trying to keep our mobile dryer going.

‘Even this year, being as warm and sunny as it was, we still put everything through it to cool it. When I’ve combined, we put it on heap and let it stay there two to three days then blow some cold air through it. It’s storing really well.

‘Our crops have done a good average and far better than expected with the drought we had. I’d expected to put the combine in and get 3 tonnes per acre maximum for winter wheat, but we’ve done 3.75. I was also pleasantly surprised with our barley average at 3.3 tonnes per acre across spring and winter barley; and what we’ve achieved so far with the sugar beet.

‘Our winter wheat varieties harvested this year were Dawsum, Typhoon and Graham. We’d put some Scope in too and that’s done well. For 2027 we’ve put in Graham, Scope, Typhoon, and also some Arnie, Defiance and tried a bit of Solitaire. I stick to feed wheats because of combine situations when we have been contracting, which we also do across 900-plus acres. Flying home at night and banging it in the shed quickly meant we were just shoving it all up together.

Graham says getting crops established this year was a case of how long to leave it.

‘It was so dry, and we didn’t know when rain was going to come. We started drilling the oilseed rape on 28-29 August and the winter wheat on September 8. We completed establishment of wheat and winter barley by October.

‘Because of the weather we didn’t seem to have as much pressure as usual, whereas it’s normally a case of ‘get on as fast as you can’, but like a typical farmer

in the back of my mind it’s always ‘when’s the rain going to come?’

Graham is now on with beet harvesting, or at least Eddie is more so, as he now drives the harvester most.

‘We’re about halfway through. We have two sugar beet harvesters and the chaser that I made recently. Eddie’s driving the Vervaet Q Series that was new last season.

‘The lads that work with me are great. John and Dave have been here quite a few years. Eddie is a younger version of me. From being about five years old he was always riding around with me and dad looking at the land. We’d be talking about soils being too wet and too dry and he could hold his own in adult conversations on all of it, in fact any subject, he always used to pick it up.

Graham says that although he’s been pleasantly surprised with yields it is input costs constantly on the rise that are the problem.

‘They are killing us all. The fertiliser price and now fertiliser tax, the price of red diesel is horrendous, the price of agricultural machinery is horrendous, as is the price of parts. Any input is proving too much for the outputs we have.

But none of this, at present, means that either Graham, Geoff or Eddie are in any way thinking of moving out of a life they love.

‘We’re always interested in taking on more land either as an agricultural contractor, or on a share or contract farming arrangement.

‘My mum, Doreen, always said to me ‘You know you don’t have to become a farmer’ but it’s always what I wanted.

Graham studied at Askham Bryan College and Bishop Burton College and was a member and held many official positions at Selby YFC, as well as being part of a very successful Tug o’ War team and is in his second term as president. He also spent 12 years representing his fellow NFU members on the National Sugar Board representing the North East until 2024.

Graham also mentions the part his wife plays on the farm and two other young ladies.

‘Georgina currently works as an audiologist and in the office here, helping out with the administration side. We met through Young Farmers. Our daughter Jess recently graduated from York College where she studied special effects and makeup including prosthetics. Eddie’s girlfriend Abi is also from a farming background.
