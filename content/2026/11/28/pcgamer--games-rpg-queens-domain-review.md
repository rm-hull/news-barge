### Our Verdict

It's prickly and keeps you at arms' length, but Queen's Domain is a new all-timer.

PC Gamer's got your back

Metroidvania is an annoying genre name, but it's useful. A Metroid-type game, with a little 'vania for the fellas: Platform around an open-ended, vertically-oriented world. When you hit a wall, go somewhere else until you unlock a movement-centric powerup to open new pathways. They call 'em "search-action" games in Japan, and that's a way better name.

They're mostly 2D. Metroid Prime did it 3D-style and it's an all-time classic. Dark Souls 1 had the dense, vertical, interconnected world, but not the movement stuff, so let's say it doesn't count as a genre-mate. Queen's Domain is the best game of this style I have ever played, 2D or 3D. Hollow Knight, Symphony of the Night, Metroid Prime⁠—I love 'em, but there's a new queen of the genre. After around 50 hours, four endings, all 52 achievements, and a lot of collectible hunting, I remain in awe of this game.

It's mysterious, prickly, strange, and wonderful. It has writing and story⁠—and what's there is good⁠—but it is a peak "show, don't tell" sort of game. Its more obscure endings demand completion of exacting, Nintendo 64-style collectathons, but almost one in 10 players on Steam have gone to the effort⁠—just over 10% for ending number three, approaching 8% for ending four at the time of writing. That's *nuts* when you consider the amount of people who buy a game on Steam, noodle around for half an hour, and decide it's not for them or that they'll come back later. But this game has inspired that level of obsession from its players⁠, myself included.

Queen's Domain's lack of guidance and retro style of first person melee combat have not been to all tastes on our team, and its first 30 minutes to an hour of gameplay, while enticing and atmospheric, are its most challenging and frustrating given the absence of a game-defining movement mechanic. I see this as less a flaw, more a question of taste—the contrast between that first half hour and what comes after is crucial. I urge all-comers to dig deep for some good old-fashioned stick-toitiveness and/or use the cheat code for a semi-automatic Soviet battle rifle to get through this section⁠—it weirdly does not kill the immersion.

It feels deranged and impossible that this game was made by two people (not including QA and marketing), and retails for twenty bucks.

You play as a prince searching for his father on a distant archipelago, the elder royal having set out to slay an ancient evil. Knowing the survival rate of protagonists' dads in this line of work, you'll also likely have to slay said evil yourself.

There are tons of fun lore books and notes scattered throughout Queen's Domain, while its supporting NPC cast is lightly-sketched but endearing. This game's best storytelling, though, is environmental: The shared enemy types and statuary in two different zones implying a shared history between them, for example. Or two, seemingly-unrelated areas revealed to be connected by an end-game shortcut you'll only find after dozens of hours, implying an exchange or connection between the two. There is an X-factor of partially-explained fictional history and implied storytelling in Queen's Domain that stands proudly with the likes of Dark Souls or Metroid Prime. Sure, there's "lore," but what's more important is what you interpret and take away from this world.

And the interconnected world of the Isle of Neasied is a mechanical marvel, one that fits together and makes sense with almost no fudging⁠—co-developer Eric Berger indicated to me in a recent interview that there is a *single* instance of two zones that would clip into each other slightly if loaded at the same time. That's it.

How many bad or just mediocre 2D metroidvanias are there at this point? Ones with truly excellent worlds, like last year's Hollow Knight: Silksong, are rare triumphs. Managing this magic in full 3D is generationally rare.

Progressing through Queen's Domain for the first time, "Where the hell am I?" "Wait, it keeps going?" and "What even *is* this game?" were the sorts of things I frequently muttered to myself to my wife's growing concern. There are one-way shortcuts to terrifying, endgame, ball-buster areas hidden in plain sight in the starting zones, a hilarious way to haze careless players reminiscent of the infamous Caelid teleporter chest in Elden Ring. But I love that there was no teleportation required in Queen's Domain, just exploiting the natural way this game's levels fit together like puzzle pieces.

Queen's Domain isn't concerned if players see every cool thing it has to offer, but it has so many moments of serendipitous discovery, this potential for surprise and wonder, you have strong odds of stumbling across at least a few organically. For me, these moments number among my very favorite in the hobby. And this is my favorite part of Queen's Domain, its sense of pure, unadulterated adventure. 49 out of 50 hours in, it still had one more breathtaking thing to show me, a reward for its most gruelling collectathon quest. But if you only have the time or patience to see the first two of four endings on a 25 hour or so critical path, I don't think you'll leave disappointed.

I always remember one critique from the reviews of Metroid Prime I read over the years, and then I felt it, viscerally, when I finally played the game myself: The backtracking. Revisiting old areas with new powers is a foundational part of the metroidvania genre, but it can often make for frustrating busywork. I habitually battle a sense of "what the hell am I supposed to be doing" in these games, or else find it a chore to truck back through areas, looking for the one thing I missed.

Queen's Domain pulls a magic trick in this regard, and meaningfully moves the ball forward for the whole genre. My first time through every area was tense, challenging, and meaningful, but backtracking was easy and fun. The closest it ever got to being a chore was when I had to redo a challenging room in the final dungeon to look for a hidden chest, but that's about it.

Queen's Domain's teleporter economy is a big part of this. There are checkpoint save crystals, as well as teleporter pads connecting the main areas together, with consumable items to warp back to your most recent save crystal and access the teleporter pad network. Early on, these items are rare and highly-prized, useful in an emergency or on completing a zone's critical path. They grow more plentiful as the game goes on, and eventually, you find infinitely reusable versions of them.

It reminds me of Morrowind: You start out trudging everywhere, but eventually Almsivi and Divine Intervention spells, plus the Silt Strider network, let you zoom around like you have access to the best public transit system in the world. The warp pads and save crystals in Queen's Domain are so centrally, strategically located as to make endgame backtracking a breeze.

The other key is Queen's Domain's movement tools, mostly the central airdash mechanic granted in the first level, and slowly expanded upon throughout the game. Those early game areas feel so threatening and vast with access to only one to three airdashes, but at the end of the game, with a full 10 and the [REDACTED] spoilery endgame power? I felt like I was using cheat codes, zooming around the ceilings of levels that gave me so much trouble just hours previously.

It feels like the culmination of the metroidvania exploration and movement promise, the most extreme contrast of early game struggle to endgame mastery I've ever seen in one of these games, and it contributes to a world that's a pleasure to backtrack through, not a chore.

I found the combat in Queen's Domain to be serviceable, but enjoyable in a similar way to Metroid Prime or fellow indie King's Field homage, Lunacid. The melee combat relies on a basic rhythm that can be found everywhere from King's Field, to Ultima Underworld, to The Elder Scrolls: Bait out an enemy's attack while backstepping away, then move in to bop them on the nose. This style of first person melee feels like an old friend to me, but it's admittedly played out.

Queen's Domain spices things up with a particularly satisfying system of throwing weapons, as well as weapon-by-weapon special attacks reminiscent of the ashes of war from Elden Ring. Like in Elden Ring, generic, common weapons have pretty basic⁠—but often reliable⁠—special attacks, while the Legendary Badass Godsword always has some fantasy bullshit that put a smile on my face. The weapon variety picked up a lot of slack for the otherwise "just ok" combat.

Coming back to a point I made at the beginning, this combat, in its most basic form at the very beginning of the game, has been the biggest stumbling block I've seen other players encounter, the filter keeping them from the coolest parts of Queen's Domain. I don't think I would change how Queen's Domain reveals itself, and I don't know how to get more people past that barrier aside from recommending they just grab the damn gun.

I found Queen's Domain to be a polished experience on desktop PC and Steam Deck. I had one crash using photo mode, then another while using a weapon's special attack⁠—I was unable to replicate either. Thankfully, Queen's Domain does keep backup autosaves in addition to checkpoint saves for just such an occasion. Performance and stability have otherwise been rock solid for me, but I will note that Queen's Domain is a bit of a battery hog on Steam Deck. I found a 10-watt TDP limit and 45fps cap to be a sweet spot for two-and-a-half to three hours of uptime on an original LCD model.

These are the largely prosaic concerns nibbling at the edges of a new cult masterpiece. I found Queen's Domain invigorating, a reminder of how surprising and wonderful games can be. I'm going to be thinking about it for years to come, and I will absolutely replay it once I've forgotten enough of it. I cannot wait to see what development duo Bradley Boothe and Eric Berger do next. You owe it to yourself to play this game.
