An Australian university has "unreservedly" apologised to Palestinian-Australian academic and author Dr Randa Abdel-Fattah for attempting to restrict what she could say at a writers' festival last year.

La Trobe University and the City of Greater Bendigo, a regional council in the state of Victoria, said in a joint statement on Friday that her legal claim against them had been resolved.

The council's chief executive, Andrew Cooney, "unreservedly apologises to Dr Abdel-Fattah for the harm the council caused to her", the statement said.

The council respected the importance of intellectual and artistic freedom and acknowledged the need to support them as human rights, it added.

"While it did not intend to infringe such rights, it acknowledges and sincerely regrets that we infringed Dr Abdel-Fattah's intellectual and artistic freedom.”

The statement also offered the unreserved apology of La Trobe vice chancellor Theo Farrell for any harm caused over "how it attempted to manage its concerns" about the festival. "The university has learned lessons from the 2025 Bendigo Writers Festival and will do better in the future," the statement said.

In a comment included in the statement, Dr Abdel-Fattah said that she accepted the apologies “as acknowledgment of the harm inflicted on me, my right to intellectual and artistic freedom and my human rights”.

In an Instagram video posted on Friday, she hoped the resolution would send a "clear message to other institutions about their accountability and responsibilities".

Dr Abdel-Fattah launched proceedings in Victoria's Civil and Administrative Tribunal in July, alleging the council and the university had unlawfully discriminated against her because of political beliefs.

She withdrew from the Bendigo writers' festival in August 2025 over a requirement that all speakers sign a code of conduct, which she and others argued required "complete self-censorship" over Israel's war in Gaza.

She had been invited to the festival as part of the “La Trobe University presents” panels.

In solidarity with Dr Abdel-Fattah, more than 50 participants withdrew from the festival.

The code of conduct directed speakers to "avoid language or topics that could be considered inflammatory, divisive, or disrespectful".

It also required them to comply with the La Trobe anti-racism plan, including its definitions of antisemitism and Islamophobia.

The university had adopted the definition of antisemitism used by Universities Australia, which many academics warned could have a "chilling" effect and limit the scope of what could be taught about the Middle East.

In January this year, organisers of the Adelaide Writers' Week, a festival in South Australia, removed Dr Abdel-Fattah from its line-up. More than 180 writers then boycotted the festival, which was eventually cancelled.

The organisers cited "cultural sensitivity" in the wake of the Bondi beach attack, which targeted the Jewish community in Sydney and left 15 people dead. It said that while it didn’t "in any way" suggest that Dr Abdel-Fattah or her writings had any connection to the attack, the decision was made "given her past statements".

She had previously faced criticism for comments on Israel, including alleging that Zionists had "no claim or rights to cultural safety". The Adelaide decision led to the resignation of the festival's director, Louise Adler.
