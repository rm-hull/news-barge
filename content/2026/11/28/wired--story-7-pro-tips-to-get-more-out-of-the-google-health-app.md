If you've been using the Fitbit app in recent years, you'll know that it's now been replaced by Google Health. (Google bought Fitbit back in 2019.) The app will eventually replace Google Fit too, though we don't know exactly when that will be. Google has previously said it will happen before the end of the year.

While many features have been carried over into Google Health from its predecessors, some haven't. There have also been new additions, and a lot of rearranging has gone on too, so it can take a while to figure out exactly where everything is.

Whether you're a veteran from the Google Fit or Fitbit days, or you've just installed Google Health for the first time to use with a new device, these tips will help you explore everything the app has to offer and get the most out of it.

## 1. You Don’t Need a Wearable

If you've been waiting to buy a device to track your steps before installing Google Health, you don't have to. You can still get the step-tracking features from the app, as long as you carry your phone with you on all your walks and runs.

Tap the **Connections** icon (top left), then**Phone** to start pulling in data from your Android or iOS smartphone. If you later add a wearable, you can switch to this for steps—or keep them both enabled (handy if you forget your tracker at home one day, for example).

## 2. Organize Your Today Page

The **Today** page is the first one you see when you open the Google Health app, so it makes sense to fill it with the stats that actually matter to you, rather than the metrics the app picks. Tap the pen icon just underneath the top bank of widgets to make changes.

You're able to switch between **Standard view** and**Expanded view**, depending on how much health and fitness information you want to see at once. In both views, you can swipe right or left to see more widgets.

Tap the **+**(plus) icon next to any widget you want to add to the Today page, and the**-**(minus) icon next to any widget you want to remove. Hiding widgets doesn't do anything to your fitness data, it just means that data isn't shown on the** Today** view.

## 3. Add Workouts Manually

You don't have to rely on your fitness tracker, smartwatch, or phone to capture all of your movement. To add something manually, tap **Log** on the**Today** view, then**Activity**, and pick the activity type—like** Walk**,** Basketball**,** Indoor climbing**, or whatever it is.

The app asks you to fill out key details, like the start and end points of the activity, before saving the record. You can manually add lots of other information from the same screens, from the hours you’ve slept to the meals you've had.

## 4. Set Your Goals

There are various parts of the Google Health interface, including the widgets on the **Today** page, where you'll see references to your health and fitness goals. These aren't going to be all that useful to you if you just leave the default values in place.

To make these metrics more meaningful, tap your profile picture (top right), then choose **Google Health settings > Activity**. You'll find numerous goals listed here, together with a few other settings, like whether or not to detect activities automatically.

Tap on any goal to set it or edit it. These targets affect the layout of screens throughout the app, and you might get notifications about them too, from time to time—if you don't want this, choose **Manage notifications** from the**Activity** screen.

## 5. Ensure Fitness Tracking Accuracy

You'll of course want your fitness tracker or smartwatch to be logging everything as accurately as possible. Even if the step counts are only off by a little each day, that can add up to a big difference over the weeks and months.

There are two settings to check here: If you tap your profile picture (top right), then choose **Google Health settings > Activity**, you can set your** Stride length**. This lets you give Google Health more precise measurements for your gait, whether walking or running.

Second, tap the **Connections** icon (top left), then your wearable. Choose**Device preferences,** and you can tell the app which wrist your wearable is usually on, which should mean more accurate step and movement tracking.

## 6. Upgrade for More Features

You may have seen mentions of Google Health Premium in the app or seen pop-ups about it, but what exactly is it? And what do you get for your money? The subscription replaces the old Fitbit Premium and works along similar lines.

For $10 a month, you get personalized and adaptable health plans that give you specific instructions for reaching your health and fitness goals. You also get a Gemini-powered AI Coach that gives you workout recaps and fitness advice based on the metrics you're recording.

The sleep summaries are more detailed in terms of sleep stages and the sleep scoring system. You also get access to a library of guided workouts and mindfulness sessions in the app.

One extra note: If you're signed up for one of Google's AI plans at the $20-per-month tier or higher, you get Google Health Premium thrown in as part of it (as well as Google Home Premium and YouTube Premium Lite).

## 7. Turn the AI Coach Off (or On)

It's possible that you want all the Google Health Premium extras but find the AI Coach a bit intrusive. It does take a very proactive stance when it comes to giving you tips and pointers, but it can be a bit too eager to help.

To hide it from view, tap your profile picture (top right), then **Google Health settings > Coach**. From here you can opt out of the Coach feature entirely or just hide the main insights from view. (You'll still be able to ask questions about your data.) When you need the coach’s encouragement and you want to turn it back on, you can do so in the same place.
