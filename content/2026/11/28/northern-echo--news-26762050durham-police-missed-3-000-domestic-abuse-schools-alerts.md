Children living with parents subject to domestic abuse are missing out on the help they need due to police failings, watchdogs have said.

Durham Police are supposed to alert schools when pupils are living in homes with domestic abuse, but failed to do so more than 3,000 times in the first six months of this year.

Officers also failed to turn up to 47 meetings in the same spell between January and July, Inspectors from His Majesty's Inspectorate of Constabulary and Fire & Rescue Services (HMICFRS) said.

"Some children aren’t receiving the help they need because of these gaps," HMICFRS said.

"The constabulary isn’t meeting its responsibilities in these cases."

The scheme, Operation Encompass, is designed to ensure schools are informed when children are affected by domestic abuse so they can provide support.

However, Durham police have now said "the picture is improving and we have processes in place to ensure it continues to deliver that improvement".

The findings raise further questions about Durham Constabulary's ability to protect vulnerable children, following a separate inspection in May which uncovered serious failings in its response to child abuse and exploitation.

In one case, a 12-year-old girl told police a 60-year-old man had sexually abused her for several months and may have targeted other girls.

However, the investigation stalled after the suspect was arrested and evidence seized. CCTV was not obtained for more than a month, by which time it had been deleted, while the girl's video interview was delayed by four months.

Another case involved an eight-year-old girl who told teachers her father had slapped her. Although police identified her mother as a victim of coercive control, officers failed to record or investigate the alleged assault on the child.

Inspectors said the force "wasn’t recognising and protecting children as victims".

The latest PEEL inspection also found officers were not properly assessing the risks facing missing children, with potential criminal exploitation and recent risks not always taken into account.

Inspectors said: "These cases were assessed as medium risk when they should have been high risk.

"We also found that when managing missing children, risks weren’t always understood or responded to appropriately."

The latest report also exposed problems with crime recording, after inspectors examined 50 records involving vulnerable adults and child protection cases.

They found 25 crimes should have been recorded, but only 19 were logged correctly.

Backlogs in multi-agency safeguarding referrals involving child protection, vulnerable adults and domestic abuse were also identified.

Chief Constable Rachel Bacon accepted the findings were a fair assessment of the force.

She said: "Overall, I think the HMICFRS inspection report is a fair assessment of where we are at."

Ms Bacon said the force had prioritised issues identified by the public despite rising demand and historic funding pressures.

She added: "HMICFRS acknowledge we are delivering real results, but with a challenging backdrop of ever-growing demand, there is clearly more to do."

Durham Police and Crime Commissioner Joy Allen also pointed to funding pressures.

She said: "The findings from HMICFRS come as no surprise. They highlight the shortcomings of the current funding formula, which continues to leave Durham Constabulary being asked to do more with less."

She said she would continue to hold the chief constable to account for improvements.

Addressing the May inspection, Ms Bacon said: "No area of policing is more important than keeping children safe from abuse and exploitation, and this was, as it should be, a very testing inspection."

She acknowledged that complex investigations needed to improve, adding: "We have identified the need for more resources and better data analytics, and we are putting those resources in place, but protection of children, especially online, is an ever-growing challenge."

While the May inspection praised the force's response to children in immediate danger and its partnership working, it called for significant improvements to investigations into abuse, neglect and exploitation.

Durham Constabulary said work was under way to address the latest findings and its strategic plan was delivering improvements.

A spokesman for Durham Constabulary said: “No area of policing is more important than keeping children safe.

“Since the inspection was carried out in the summer, Durham Constabulary has worked very closely with our partners, particularly our local authority colleagues, and have made significant progress in addressing this issue over recent months: the picture is improving and we have processes in place to ensure it continues to deliver that improvement”.
