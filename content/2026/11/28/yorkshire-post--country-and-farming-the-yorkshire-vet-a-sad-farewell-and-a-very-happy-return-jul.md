# The Yorkshire Vet: ​A sad farewell and a very happy return - Julian Norton

## This week I made two visits, both of which were long overdue. The first was a sad one. I’d been treating Lorna, a very elderly Golden Retriever for many years. Her legs were weak and arthritic, but she stubbornly refused to yield to the ravages of time and stoically barked like a champion every time she saw me.

I had become accustomed to her raucous tones, which could be heard in all parts of the practice whenever she attended. Eventually though, her mobility failed and the time came for that final visit. I’d promised I’d make a house call at the end, although re-arranging the jam-packed list of consults was not easy. Lorna’s owners were very grateful, and Lorna did manage to bark loudly at my arrival, true to form, albeit very briefly. The fact that she couldn’t raise herself from the sofa confirmed everything we knew. It was sad but peaceful, the timing was right, and it was the right thing to do. It’s a special sort of privilege to be there at the end and, for patients we’ve been looking after for most of their life, so significant.

Our next visit was much happier, and Anne and I couldn’t believe we had left it so long to return. For Anne, her last visit was many years ago when the kids were primary school age. For me, it was even further in the past, when I was the same age as Jack. It’s a mere hour’s drive away and is full of masterpieces. Our destination was the Yorkshire Sculpture Park.

It could not have been a more perfect autumnal day for exploring this haven. The weather was completely still and a haze of mist hung low over the lake. The majestic Emley Moor mast towered eerily nearby, surely the most impressive of all the sculptures. Dogs were allowed, provided they were kept on the lead, so Emmy came too. She was as enthusiastic as ever but, of course, had no interest whatsoever in any of the sculptures. The collection is impressive. As expected, several of Henry Moore’s works took pride of place. Surely, he’s the most renowned person to hail from Castleford, just a few miles up the motorway. In his early years, he lived just around the corner from my Gran. One of my favourite installations was from Henry’s contemporary Barbara Hepworth, but there were acclaimed masterpieces everywhere, including work by the famous Damian Hirst. It was amusing to see these works of art amongst grazing sheep and squabbling swans.

Our little map showed us the route around the sculpture park, up some steps, which were also, apparently, an installation themselves. They just looked like steps to us, but the oak pieces had been embedded in thirty tonnes of coal to reflect the mining heritage of the area. The fact that the implanted coal would erode and eventually disappear from the path also carried some symbolism. We found more works of art at the top. There were some brass tree roots which were very good and highly realistic, some fallen trees suspended in, and enclosed by, dry stone walls (also very appealing and interesting) and then a huge sculpture of a pensive-looking bloke sitting at the top of the hill, staring directly at the mast. It, too, was hard to take one’s eye away from. The smorgasbord of sculptures kept coming, by the lake, on the hillsides and in the trees (some even were trees.)

We’d had a truly fantastic day, which provided a great dog walk, some amazing views and a cultural experience that was beyond my expectations. It might have been a visit that was long overdue, but we won’t be leaving it as long before our next visit to The Yorkshire Sculpture Park!
