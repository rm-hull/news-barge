At first sight, it makes no sense that Kemi Badenoch should seek to restrict the appeal of the Conservative Party to opponents of net zero and the European Convention on Human Rights. These are minority views among the electorate when the party needs to broaden its support.

In particular, the Tories are fighting the Liberal Democrats across much of the south of England, trying to appeal to better-off graduates who are rather attached to recent One Nation Tory policies on green issues and human rights.

Many centrist Tories have had their noses put out of joint. David Gauke, who was blocked as a Tory candidate in 2019 by Boris Johnson for crimes against Brexit, said this morning: “The UK signed up to the ECHR in 1950. Since then, we’ve had 20 general elections in which the Tories have supported continued membership. Now the party leader has changed her mind, everyone else must follow or they’re ‘not a Conservative’ and can’t be a parliamentary candidate.”

Tobias Ellwood, the former defence minister, said that he had been told he couldn’t be a Conservative candidate at the next election because “did not pass” the “purity tests”.

Others have bowed to the new party line. Mark Spencer, the former chief whip, sat next to Badenoch as she announced on Friday that all Tory candidates at the next election would be required to sign a “covenant” opposing net zero and the ECHR. As the Tory candidate in Newark, Robert Jenrick’s seat, he proudly brandished his copy of the document, not exactly signed in blood but we got the idea.

It sounds puritanical, dogmatic and exclusionary, but imagine the alternatives. Either Badenoch could have stood by net zero and the ECHR and lost support to Reform, or she could have opposed them – which is what she actually believes – and allowed Tory candidates to take a different view. This would cause chaos at the next election, with many candidates, including sitting MPs, standing on personal manifestos at odds with party policy on two important issues.

Not only would the Tories then lose support to Reform, as voters motivated by opposition to net zero or the ECHR would prefer a party with a united position, but they would also lose support from voters who don’t care about those issues but who are put off by disunity.

Badenoch is not in a position where she can afford to take a “big tent” approach. The Conservative Party is fighting for its life. It has to establish dominance over Nigel Farage before it can turn its attention to an all-out assault on Labour.

In any case, there are strong arguments for a less absolute policy on both net zero and the ECHR. Rishi Sunak delivered a speech in September 2023 in which he set out a “more pragmatic, proportionate, and realistic approach” to net zero. Tony Blair’s recent push to switch the emphasis from clean power to “cheaper power” (while keeping the 2050 net zero target) has set out a strong centrist position.

Sunak also said, while he was prime minister, that if he were forced to choose between the ECHR and our national security, he would pick national security.

Helpfully for Badenoch, he set out his view in more detail in his *Sunday Times* column last month, saying that, “with only two years left in the parliament and with no mandate to do so”, trying to leave the ECHR would have been “a recipe for constitutional chaos”. But he backed her approach, saying: “With a mandate, a majority and a five-year term, it would be very different, and worth doing.”

More surprising, perhaps, was support from David Cameron. He said in his recent Bloomberg interview that he and Badenoch “have some different outlooks” on net zero and foreign aid, but he acknowledged that the net zero target did not take sufficient account of the cost of living, and he supported her on the ECHR.

Both Cameron and Sunak qualify their opposition to the ECHR by saying that Britain should try to change it, and should leave only if that fails – which is a position that might leave some scope for Tories to sign the covenant with their fingers crossed behind their backs.

The logic of Badenoch’s covenant, then, is inescapable. She has to push Farage aside in order to establish her right to fight the next election as the alternative prime minister to Andy Burnham – and she thinks she can win the arguments on net zero and the ECHR with centre-ground voters.

But even if she succeeds in both those aims, she still faces an uphill struggle at the next election. Waiting for Burnham to fail is one thing, but expecting the voters to turn to the Conservative Party if he does is quite another.

The government of which she was a member made such a terrible mess of the basics, losing control of immigration, raising the cost of living and trashing the public finances, that it may take longer than four or five years for the voters to forget.

One telling clause in Badenoch’s covenant says: “The Conservative Party is committed to learning from past mistakes, including failures of discipline, unity, fiscal restraint and border control...”

How Badenoch distances herself from the Tory record is more important, in the end, than a loyalty oath on net zero and the ECHR.
