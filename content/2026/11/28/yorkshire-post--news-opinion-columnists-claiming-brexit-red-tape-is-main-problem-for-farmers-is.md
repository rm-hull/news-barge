# Claiming Brexit red tape is main problem for farmers is thick as mince: Sarah Todd

## FOOTAGE of a social media influencer flouncing through the corridors of 10 Downing Street started this week off. Never mind pussyfooting around, it’s time to stop mincing our words and demand our Prime Minister stops making a mockery of the role he has somehow ended up with.

Forget trying to be chummy with the cool cats of TikTok or those gawping at Gogglebox, our country’s highest office is debased at our nation’s peril.

We’ll be able to negotiate nowt - never mind frighten foes - if the rest of the world thinks we’re wet lettuces.

Like a dog with a bone now when it comes to using the word mince, this columnist reported the other week that all the minced beef in a local supermarket hailed from across the Irish Sea.

As an aside, the lamb was from New Zealand - a journey of around 11,000 miles - and the pork was from Germany, a country which keeps 90 per cent of its farrowing sows in crates and over that same percentage of those reared for meat in intensive pens, mostly on bare slatted concrete floors without straw bedding.

To add insult to injury the cooked chicken, for sandwiches and suchlike, was from Thailand.

This, dear reader, wasn’t in a cosmopolitan corner of London - or even Burnham’s backyard of Manchester - but in a North Yorkshire market town where (with the wind in the right direction) the auctioneer’s patter can be heard from the local livestock market.

What on earth is going on, never mind over the Channel but right here in what was once Great Britain?

But back to mince. It was a mince-based product - a Marks & Spencer spaghetti bolognaise to be precise - that our esteemed leader chose as an example to curry favour for his new European Union food trade deal.

Yes, it’s a rum job that moving the ready meal from a factory in Lincolnshire to a shelf in Dublin requires 300 pages of paperwork.

But a betting person would give good odds that the farmers of Ireland would rather people ate home-grown mince in their ready meals. Like Britain’s sons and daughters of the soil would prefer it was meat they had reared on our country’s supermarket shelves.

There must surely be no doubting that the consumer from the Emerald Isle would rather the prick-and-ping micro meal was put together locally rather than having been brought over 500 miles by road, plus the ferry crossing?

Just one other little detail. In the first half of its 2026/27 financial year Marks & Spencer’s Food division posted an operating profit of £288.6 million. Reading a spreadsheet isn’t a specialist subject, but surely proves this retailer is doing just fine. It hasn’t, for example, had to drop making an expensive Christmas television advert because of those blessed bolognaise export problems.

Now if rather than sucking up to the big boys our Andy had stood in a field next to some real clarty-wellied farmers who had given their blessing to going (flat) cap in hand to European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen then that would be another story.

What the Farmers Weekly is full of is saving small abattoirs and trying to produce food rather than become solar statistics. Rural crime, fly tipping, dogs causing carnage every lambing season, fuel, energy and other soaring costs, planning and payment problems compounded by ten Secretaries of State in as many years and even Internet connectivity hit the headlines more than anyone banging on about Brexit.

Salt is rubbed in these wounds by lefty local authorities, hell-bent on feeding schoolchildren, prisoners, pensioners and anyone who attends a civic reception anything but homegrown food. Then, oh the irony presuming those farmers they forever fail will always arrive on tractors to save the day when wildfires, snow or storms come.

Try to understand and, as a saying just found goes, it’s as thick as mince.

Fear not, your correspondent always attempts to finish on a positive.

The morning television news bulletins can no longer be stomached. If it’s not more illegal migrants its billions blown on benefits. However, solace has been found in soothing shows.

Flicking through the channels - Portillo’s train travels and the Yorkshire car auction house are also cathartic - it was a joy this week to stumble across the late steeplejack Fred Dibnah and readers in need of a wholesome distraction are urged to follow suit.

In fact, tuning in should be prescribed on the National Health Service. Striking doctors can stew in their own juice; we’d all be as fit as fleas following Fred’s pearls of wisdom.

In one episode he visited an old weaving mill and - does this ring any bells? - spoke sadly about how it was cheap imports that did for this particular industry. Thinking aloud, what would the boilersuited one make - going back to where we began - of the lad larking around on the landing behind the most famous front door in the country, or that other one mouthing some rubbish or other outside?

Learning this month marks 22 years since Dibnah’s death made this viewer ponder motoring to Lancashire to eat one of his beloved Holland’s “only British meat” pies in his honour. Mince and onion of course ...

Sarah Todd is a journalist specialising in farming and country life. Read her weekly column in Wednesday’s edition of the Yorkshire Post.
