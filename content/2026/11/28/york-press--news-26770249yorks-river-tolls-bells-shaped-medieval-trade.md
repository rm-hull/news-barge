## Why York once chained the River Ouse and rang bells across the city

This image shows the North Street Postern and Barker Tower, and was probably taken in the 1860s. Between the medieval Barker Tower and Lendal Tower, once stretched an iron chain across the River Ouse *(Image: UCG)*

*How York's river tolls and bells shaped medieval trade by DUNCAN MARKS*

THE latest agreements between Britain and the European Union, aimed at reducing barriers to trade, might seem a particularly modern preoccupation. Yet, walking around York, you can find physical reminders of centuries of negotiations over who could trade, where, when and at what price.

The oldest document held in York’s city archives concerns international trade. Dating from around 1155, a charter issued by Henry II and witnessed by Thomas Becket confirmed the trading privileges of York’s merchants in England and Normandy.

Royal patronage could be commercially lucrative, but came at a price. Richard the Lionheart granted York’s merchants exemption from tolls across his territories in 1189, but only in return for £133 as he sought to finance his campaign in the Crusades.

From 1579, buildings on Ouse Bridge were used by the Eastland Company, which promoted trade between England and Scandinavia.

But controlling access to the city’s own markets was just as important.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21774180.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Barker Tower in York. Image: Dylan Connell

Between the medieval Barker Tower and Lendal Tower, alongside Lendal Bridge, once stretched an iron chain across the River Ouse, preventing boats from proceeding without paying a toll. For merchants travelling downstream, this was not necessarily their first encounter with a toll collector. St Mary’s Abbey operated its own system further upriver.

The competing economic interests of the abbey and the civic authorities were a source of considerable friction. Both enjoyed royal privileges, but their respective rights were frequently contested.

In 1262, tensions erupted into violence along Bootham. Tenants of the abbey were killed, their merchandise plundered and properties burnt. The abbey walls that survive along Bootham and Marygate owe something of their existence to these commercial and jurisdictional disputes – the need to protect wealth as well as help do God’s work!

York’s geographical position gave considerable economic advantages. For nearly a millennium, Ouse Bridge provided the only bridge crossing from Boroughbridge and downstream to the Humber estuary. From as early as 1280, horses and carts were charged tolls to help maintain the city’s bridges. York was understandably protective of its position, opposing proposals for a bridge at Selby, which eventually opened in 1792.

🌉 **Bridge tolls** – From the 13th century, horses and carts paid towards maintaining York’s bridges.

📅 **13-month year** – York railway worker Moses Cotsworth proposed a radical calendar featuring an extra month called Sol.

🏛️ **Hidden history** – Many places connected with York’s centuries of battles over trade and commerce can still be seen today.

Even time itself was regulated in the interests of commerce. A clock is recorded on Ouse Bridge from around 1390, its bells regulating the working day and helping to prevent ‘forestalling’ – trading ahead of the official opening of markets. On nearby Foss Bridge, a bell known as the Scaytbell sounded at eight in the morning to signify the opening of the fish market. Only freemen could purchase fish – no doubt choosing the freshest! – until ten o’clock, when others were permitted to buy.

The freedom to trade was not enjoyed by everyone. York’s Merchant Adventurers interpreted their royal charter of 1580–81 as awarding a trading monopoly within the city. When Mary Tuke opened a grocery business in Walmgate in 1725, she faced years of legal challenges from the Company. It was not until 1827, when a court ruled in favour of John Harwood, a Petergate druggist, that the Company’s trading monopoly finally came to an end.

The arrival of the railways brought fresh opportunities to negotiate York’s economic future. George Hudson, working with George Stephenson, helped secure York’s position on the emerging national railway network.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21774181.jpg?type=mds-article-620)George Hudson, English railway chairman, c 1850..

The railways encouraged another important commercial development – the replacement of local time with a common national time. But one York railway employee wanted to go considerably further.

Moses Cotsworth, a former pupil of York’s Blue Coat School at St Anthony’s Hall, Peasholme Green, worked for the North Eastern Railway in what’s today’s The Grand hotel. Unequal months and differing numbers of working days made comparing monthly business performance unnecessarily complicated.

In 1905, he published in York his proposals for a new calendar. There would be 13 months, each of exactly 28 days, including an additional month called Sol.

His proposals attracted international attention, gaining the support of businesses including General Motors and Woolworths. George Eastman, founder of Kodak, financially supported Cotsworth, and his company adopted the calendar for internal accounting. In 1931, international calendar reform was debated at a League of Nations conference in Geneva.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/19557747.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Moses B Cotsworth c 1922. Photo from Anna Cook

Ultimately, religious objections and the lack of international agreement thwarted Cotsworth’s ambitions.

The challenge of reconciling local interests with the advantages of common rules is far from unique to York. But it is welcome that many of the places connected with those earlier negotiations are still around us, hiding in plain sight.

*Duncan Marks is the planning and heritage manager at York Civic Trust*
