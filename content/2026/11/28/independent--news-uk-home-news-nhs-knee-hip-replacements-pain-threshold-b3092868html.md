Regional health organisations are imposing limits on hip and knee replacement procedures, with several requiring individuals to cross a specific threshold of pain before qualifying for surgery, according to research.

Leading surgeons have cautioned that patients "should not be left in pain because of where they live".

Data indicates that around 5.4 million people across the UK suffer from knee osteoarthritis, while a further 3.2 million live with hip osteoarthritis.

Initially, individuals are provided with exercise regimens, guidance on weight management, and mobility assistance.

Medication may also be prescribed to help manage pain and inflammation.

Official advice from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (Nice) outlines that patients ought to receive hip or knee replacements when their "joint symptoms are substantially impacting their quality of life" and where "non-surgical management is ineffective".

These national standards state that clinicians must rely on "clinical assessment when deciding to refer someone instead of systems that numerically score severity of disease", rather than turning away patients based on "age, sex, smoking, comorbidities or weight measures such as body mass index (BMI)".

![Data indicates that around 5.4 million people across the UK suffer from knee osteoarthritis, while a further 3.2 million live with hip osteoarthritis](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/27/15/01/iStock-2252201589.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Nevertheless, the study reveals that regional health authorities controlling local budgets are putting restrictions in place that curtail the number of individuals eligible to receive these operations.

The study, which has been shared with the Press Association, highlights how “restrictive commissioning policies have attracted sustained criticism”.

The review, led by researchers from Nottingham University Hospitals NHS Trust and the University of Nottingham, saw experts examine policies from all 42 local health bodies in England, also known as integrated care systems.

Published in the Annals of the Royal College of Surgeons of England, it concludes that half (50%) use a “restrictive pain criterion”, meaning they impose restrictions on access to hip and knee replacement surgery based on patients’ levels of pain before they can receive life-altering surgery.

Some 55% of these organisations say patients must have been experiencing symptoms – which can include as pain, stiffness or reduced function – for a minimum period before patients can have the operation on the NHS.

For eight regions, commissioners say patients must have had these symptoms for at least six months before they can be offered surgery.

Almost one in five (19%) regional health authorities say patients must lose weight or have a certain BMI before being eligible for the operation – a policy which has been criticised in the past as patients may struggle to exercise to lose weight before they have had surgery.

Meanwhile, 7% say patients must quit or try to stop smoking before being offered the operation.

![Guidance says patients ought to receive hip or knee replacements when their "joint symptoms are substantially impacting their quality of life" and where "non-surgical management is ineffective"](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/27/15/20/iStock-1897614233.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The authors warn restrictive criteria can delay treatment, leaving patients in pain for longer, worsening disability and potentially contributing to poorer surgical outcomes and greater health inequalities.

They said patients who do not meet NHS criteria may also turn to the private sector, potentially widening the gap between those who can afford care and those who cannot.

Dr Haseeb Khawar, lead author of the study and a trainee orthopaedic surgeon who has an NJR/RCS England research fellowship, said: “Health inequalities may be widened by restrictive policies and may impact certain groups of patients more than others.

“The next steps are to understand which groups of patients suffer most from poor access, and how this can be improved.”

Tim Lane, president of the Royal College of Surgeons of England, said: “The decision to have surgery should take account of a patient’s full clinical picture and not be dictated by arbitrary thresholds that vary depending on where they live.

“While it is encouraging that restrictions based on weight and smoking have become much less common, patients who could benefit from surgery should not be left in pain because of where they live.”

A Department of Health and Social Care spokesperson said: “Your postcode should not determine how long you wait for treatment.

“We know too many patients are still being left waiting in pain, with their lives put on hold, and we are determined to tackle disparities in access to care.

“Clinical decisions should be based on patients’ needs, and we expect NHS trusts to follow Nice guidance.”

An NHS spokesperson said: “Hip and knee surgery should be based on a clinical assessment to ensure the best possible treatment and outcome for each individual patient, and pain threshold alone should not be used to determine access to surgery.”
