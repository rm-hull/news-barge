# Christmas at Harewood House: 'It's magic, from fairies in jars to talking chairs and a new Elf Village’

## Continuing our series of stately homes’ Christmas experiences, Stephanie Smith finds out what lies behind Magic at the Mansion at Harewood House, near Leeds. Pictures by James Hardisty.

There is more than a touch of magic at Harewood House this Christmas, as if the Yorkshire stately home is enchanted, rather like the fairytale castle in Disney’s *Beauty and the Beast,* with teapots, chairs and candles coming to life to talk and sigh and sing for the delight of guests young and old.

“We have got a singing candle tree,” says Zoë Hughes, head of Public Programme at Harewood. This bewitching chorus greets visitors in the Music Room at the start of their festive journey through the house. Later, in the Old Library, two Chippendale chairs reminisce about those who have sat on them over the years (children are not favourite sitters). “It's a really lovely mix of humour, beauty, nostalgia,” Zoë says.

Created by award-winning Studio McGuire, Magic at the Mansion uses light and sound projections to bring to life a cast of household items - pillows, pictures, even talking gifts beneath the tree - to tell stories inspired by Harewood’s own history.

After the rambunctious extravagance of last year’s festivities when the house was dressed by flamboyant interior designer Siobhan Murphy, the 2026 Harewood Christmas experience is a very different and, at first sight, relatively restrained experience - but detail is everything.

It tells the stories of Harewood Christmases past, with a script created by Yorkshire-based writer Christina Lewis, working with Harewood’s curator and archivist Rebecca Burton.

Mary, Princess Royal and Countess of Harewood, for instance, was an expert gift buyer whose meticulous note-taking should serve as an example to us all.

Born in 1897, the only daughter of King George V and Queen Mary, she was the sister of kings Edward VIII and George VI. In 1922, she married Henry Lascelles, later the 6th Earl of Harewood, and lived at Goldsborough Hall before moving into Harewood House in 1930, living there until her death in 1965.

“Christmas was a time of real industry for Princess Mary, and she obviously had hundreds of people - friends, family, but also her contacts with the organisations that she worked with - to give to. She kept really meticulous lists of everything that she gave,” Rebecca says.

“Her ladies-in-waiting helped her to keep track of all these things. They actually created binders, little Filofax-type things, and each person would be included within the binder in alphabetical order.”

These notes tell of a thoughtful woman who bestowed gifts such as antiques and collectibles to close family and friends, through to more domestic items. “The head gardener received a Thermos mug, for instance, a really practical gift,” says Rebecca. “Other people, spectacle cases or knitting needles or bed sheets or tea cloths, soap.

“She's tapping into people's interests as well, she knows what they would like. There was one particular lady and she must have had a Pekingese dog because all of the gifts somehow relate to a Pekingese dog, so there might be some handkerchiefs with a Pekingese on or something else decorated with little dog motifs.

“Some of the Lascelles family's dogs feature in the storyline as well. One of them ends up sneaking one of Princess Mary's gifts from the present pile, which was a little Cartier owl.” This and other objects are on display in the Steward’s Room.

Rebecca, from Leeds, has worked at Harewood for about 12 years, and has a degree from Durham University in Ancient History and Archaeology. “When you work with the collection so deeply, you build up knowledge,” she says. “You learn something new every day, and every day is different.

“We try to tie and root the Christmas narrative in Harewood's history and use it as an opportunity to delve into the history and tell it in a more creative and accessible way.

“For instance, we have some cakes and pies and plates talking about the Servants’ Ball that was held every year at Harewood. We have that documented in our archives.”

On the Dining Room ceiling there is an astonishing Sistine Chapel style projection featuring gods and cherubs playing a game, inspired by Harewood House’s architect Robert Adam, who designed the interiors in his signature neoclassical style, and painted gods and goddesses into his ceiling designs.

Built between 1759 and 1771 for West Indian plantation owner Edwin Lascelles, 1st Baron Harewood, and set within 1,000 acres of gardens and landscapes designed by Capability Brown, Harewood is still home to the Lascelles family.

Rebecca says: “We have got references to things like grand banquets and balls. Studio McGuire has created these talking pillows on the State Bed that remember Princess Victoria's visit in 1835, and the second Countess talks about all the preparations that went into this visit, and a menu that would have been a yard long.”

Zoë says: “There's some real bits of humour in the script, but there's also those elements of more reflective beauty that Studio McGuire also does so well.”

A stunning example of this can be found in the Drawing Room where framed pictures of beautiful flowers open and close as if enchanted by frost.

Zoë says: “The magic of what Studio McGuire does has such a multi-generational appeal. You've got things that appeal to kids and then you have got jokes for adults that maybe the kids won't quite understand, but it engages all ages and all sorts of demographics, and I think that's what works really well in bringing together lots of different audiences.”

The Spanish Library is probably her favourite, she says. “There's 11 different fairy jars which have got these beautiful, intricate little fairy scenes. There's like a rose fairy, there's a woodland fairy, there's all sorts.

“We've installed them among the books, all around the bookshelves. And then we've taken that theme of woodland that a lot of the fairies are within the setting of, so they've got little moss on the floor in their little bell jars.”

This is the tenth year of Harewood Christmas visitor experiences, which have grown year on year, says Zoë. “We couldn't do it without the volunteers. They have such a huge contribution. There's probably around 60 volunteers that help across the install period and some of them have been working on this for months.”

For example, one volunteer Pauline Stone, a former GP, made 77 huge paper flowers to decorate a Christmas tree in the Yellow Drawing Room, and she joined other volunteers in making the cakes for the Servants’ Hall.

“They have had craft clubs over the last few months making different bits, wrapping presents. So it's a real team effort and it's really lovely to see everybody come together,” Zoë says.

This year sees Harewood’s first-ever Elf Village, partnering with Harrogate-based Enchantica and YurtBar to transform the North Lawn with twinkling lights, beautifully dressed yurts, cheerful elves and Santa.

“Alongside Magic at the Mansion, our Elf Village brings a completely different kind of Christmas magic to Harewood, with families able to step into an immersive festive world and meet Father Christmas,” Zoë says.

“What makes Christmas at Harewood so special is that it belongs to this House. It isn’t something that could simply be lifted and placed somewhere else; every story, every character and every moment comes from Harewood’s own history.

“We’re bringing stories out of the collections and giving visitors the chance to discover little details they may never have noticed before. There are layers of meaning throughout the experience, whether you’re visiting for the first time or returning year after year.”
