It's been a tough old year for competitive multiplayer shooters. We've seen Highguard fail, Marathon struggle, 1047 Games flail futilely around with Splitgate: Arena Reloaded and Empulse. Even Arc Raiders has bitten the bullet and admitted that its players simply do not want to shoot one another. In fact, were it not for Wardogs bucking this grim trend, you could build a strong case to say that 2026 is the year competitive multiplayer shooting died.

Among the pile of casualties awaiting revival from a medic is Last Flag, the competitive, capture-the-flag shooter developed by Night Street Games, a studio founded by Imagine Dragons' lead singer Dan Reynolds, and his brother and band manager Mac Reynolds. Released back in April, Last Flag ran into the cold shoulder of the market almost immediately, and within two months of launch, Night Street ceased development and laid off half its staff.

![Last Flag](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/fESTsD86vajpbD5Ay6SJcR-1200-80.jpg)

Rather than pull Last Flag offline, however, Night Street decided to put the game's future in the hands of players by adding the ability to host custom lobbies alongside the already-existing bot support, and slashing the price. I decided to take this life-support version of Last Flag for a spin, and it's a damn shame this game didn't grab an audience, because it's a ton of fun.

Now, I won't pretend to be baffled as to why Last Flag *didn't*grab people's attention. At a glance it resembles that infamous Randy Pitchford tweet about Battleborn. It mixes capture-the-flag with hide-and-seek, hero shooters, control-point domination, all with character art direction that screams: "My voice lines are going to annoy the* shit* out of you!"

But just as a list of ingredients does not make a meal, a list of features does not make a game. The way Last Flag puts these elements together is fairly ingenious—producing a style of multiplayer that doesn't really feel like any other game.

![Last Flag](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/GW56DuARuu7ybM7vCxDhcR-1200-80.jpg)

A round of Last Flag starts with a minute-long hide phase, where one player from each team is selected to hide their team's flag somewhere in their half of the map. Last Flag's maps are holier than a Swiss cheese blessed by the Pope, their undulating landscapes riddled with apertures, cutouts and tunnels perfectly suited for squirrelling away a flag in.

Once both flags are hidden, the match begins in earnest, and both teams set out to capture the other team's flag. But of course, unlike traditional CTF, neither team knows where their opponents' flag is.

There are a couple of ways to track down the flag. But if you want, you can just roam around enemy territory looking for it. This is not ideal. Apart from the fact that you're likely to get gunned down by the opposing team, maps are pretty roomy, so you're unlikely to stumble upon the flag by chance.

![Last Flag](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/sqfhWK2qyXbYPVsLWbNCcR-1200-80.jpg)

This is where the control points come in. There are three control points that each team can conquer, and holding a control point does three things. It lets you respawn there, which is handy for getting back into the fight. It can help heal players, as each control point generates a healing radius. But most importantly, it will narrow down the search area for the flag.

See, Last Flag features a grid-based minimap showing both team's territory. If you hold a control point, some of those squares will be coloured red or blue to show where the Flag *isn't*. The more control points you hold, the faster the squares get eliminated, and the quicker you can track down the flag.

This mechanic is Last Flag's main spark of inspiration, as it transforms each round from a relatively aimless game of hide-and-seek into a much more structured affair where the tension escalates constantly. Each minute that ticks by, the chance that your flag gets discovered increases. The combination of CTF and domination also introduces an element of tactics. Do you prioritise the control points to narrow down the flag's location as quickly as possible? Or do you scour enemy territory and try to get ahead of those invisible timers?

![Last Flag](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/R5rH9RqzPTepWbcahAAfcR-1200-80.jpg)

Even once the flags are found, you still essentially have the actual capture-the-flag part to play. Your team must capture *and hold* the enemy flag for a set amount of time before you'll be declared victorious, so each round ends in an exciting base defence as you protect your stolen goods from a desperate enemy assault.

It's a really well thought-out ruleset, and much like Wardogs, takes a basic, familiar multiplayer shooter concept and reworks it into something that feels fresh and new. There are other fun touches too. Alongside the fact that each character has their own abilities, they can also upgrade those abilities during a match by shooting cashbots to earn some moola. As well as giving players something to do while searching for the flag, players can also hunt these unfortunate automatons during the hide phase, keeping them occupied while the flag is concealed.

I do have one gripe with Last Flag though: as a shooter, it's a bit unsatisfying. Bullet impacts sometimes feel vague and hard to read, and I do wonder whether the cartoonish art-style robs the game of some of its lethality.

![Last Flag](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/DmDhcPvYhCqZqJc8wvCJcR-1200-80.jpg)

Night Street Games may have been better off drawing more inspiration from Last Flag's arena-shooter roots, building a game that looked and felt a bit less Overwatch and a bit more Unreal Tournament. Or to split the difference and shoot for Team Fortress 2. Then again, many multiplayer shooters which have appealed to an older crew have also fallen flat on their flak-cannons, so you can understand why Night Street Games made the creative decisions it did.

Yet while Last Flag is flawed, it is most certainly not a cynical celebrity cash grab like, say, the Mr Beast x James Patterson novel that nobody apparently read. It's a game made by people with a clear understanding of and appreciation for the genre, looking to take it somewhere a little bit different. Night Street's experiment may not have been as successful as Wardogs, but that doesn't invalidate the attempt. Even without a community to support it, I would still say Last Flag is worth investigating for shooter aficionados.
