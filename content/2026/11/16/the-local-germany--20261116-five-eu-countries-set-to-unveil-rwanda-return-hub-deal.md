The group, also comprising Greece and the Netherlands, has been gunning to open a "return-hub" despite rights groups' concerns, after the European Union cleared the way with a recent toughening of migration rules.

The five have kept the location under wraps as they negotiated with potential hosts, but European diplomats and other people briefed on the talks have indicated Rwanda as the chosen country.

The African nation had confirmed in August it had begun "preliminary discussions" with several European states.

It is sending a delegation to a migration conference in Vienna, where several sources said the agreement would be unveiled at a press conference on Monday.

Ministers from the five EU countries gathered in the interior ministry in Vienna from 2:30 pm local time (1330 GMT) for a "working session".

After that, they were scheduled to join Austrian Chancellor Christian Stocker and visiting Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen at a press conference at 4:00 pm, according to the Austrian government.

Ahead of the meeting, European Commission spokesman Markus Lammert welcomed the plans, noting that the EU had been "closely following the preparatory work of the five member states for such a return hub".

**'Stop EU Rwanda-deal!'**

Around 100 protesters gathered in Vienna on Monday, holding placards with slogans such as "Stop EU Rwanda-deal!"

The announcement will mark the culmination of an EU push to boost deportations launched in 2024, amid a souring of public opinion on migration.

Migrant arrivals have steadily declined over the past three years, as the bloc struck deals to stem departures with a host of countries in Africa and the Middle East.

But migration has remained a hot-button issue, fuelling far-right electoral gains across the bloc.

Policymakers' focus has now turned to improving the repatriation system, which currently sees less than 30 percent of people who are ordered to leave the bloc returned to their country of origin.

An EU reform granting authorities broader detention powers and allowing for the creation of return hubs was approved earlier this year.

Proponents say such centres – which would serve either as the final destination or as transfer centres for those expelled – could facilitate repatriations and act as a deterrent for would-be irregular migrants.

But critics have questioned their effectiveness, pointing to the hurdles faced by similar projects and likening them to "legal black holes" that could see migrants stranded in limbo with little oversight.

The choice of Rwanda has also caused concern, with Human Rights Watch pointing to its "well-established record of repression of dissent, arbitrary detention, ill-treatment in detention" and support for an armed anti-government group in the neighbouring Democratic Republic of Congo.

**'Welcome centre'**

In 2022, Rwanda reached a similar deal with Britain to take irregular migrants before their asylum claims were heard but the accord was later scrapped by the UK.

It is among a number of African countries to have received migrants from the United States under a similar, contested US immigration scheme championed by President Donald Trump.

Unlike the discarded British plan, the EU's scheme would see rejected asylum-seekers sent to the African nation.

The five EU countries have expressed hope to have a centre up and running in 2027.

Diplomatic sources say the hub in Rwanda will be initially run as a pilot, hosting about 100 people, with each of the five nations possibly sending around 20 migrants.

It would not be a detention facility, with Rwanda inclined to name it a "welcome centre" to underscore the possibility for some migrants to stay and work in the country, a source said.

The United Nations' International Organization for Migration (IOM) could play an ancillary role.

The EU Agency for Fundamental Rights (FRA) warned that any potential arrangement "must fully respect EU law".
