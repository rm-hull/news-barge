## “The blood of these brothers cries out to God and demands justice,” Pope Leo said, to applause from a crowd many of whom wore the feathered headdresses and face markings of the region.

Pope Leo XIV on Sunday demanded justice for environmental defenders killed while protecting the Amazon and backed Indigenous communities fighting to stop their lands from being ravaged by logging, illegal mining and what he called an “insatiable thirst for domination”.

On the penultimate day of his South American tour, Leo travelled into the Peruvian rainforest and echoed a central teaching of Pope Francis: that care for the most vulnerable and care for creation cannot be separated.

“The cry of the earth and the cry of the poor are inseparable,” he told Indigenous representatives and missionaries gathered on the Ucayali River in Pucallpa, in Peru’s northeast.

He said he had come to support them in their search for justice and peace and referred to the 57 murders of environmental defenders that campaign groups estimate took place between 2010 and 2024.

The visit to Cusco, 3,400 metres (more than 11,000 feet) above sea level, tested Leo’s stamina as he continues his recovering from lung cancer surgery last month.

## A call for a different model of development

Drawing on his years as a bishop in Peru and on Francis’ constant preaching on the environment and Indigenous peoples, Leo used the encounter and a Mass that followed to call for an economic model that respects the Earth and those who protect it, rather than the “irresponsible exploitation” practised by outsiders.

“Very little has been done — and very slowly at that — while the means to exploit and pollute creation have accelerated,” he said at Mass. “In the face of these evils, let us not bury the Lord’s gifts, for he gives us the strength to repair and heal the suffering caused by an insatiable thirst for domination.”

Illegal mining and coca production are also driving deforestation and encroaching on Indigenous territories, putting ancestral lands at risk and exposing communities to violence and organised crime.

On Saturday, Peruvian police reported three operations against illegal mining in Madre de Dios, one of the country’s most biodiverse regions, destroying equipment, rafts and engines valued at €3.8 million ($4.1 million).

In Peru, most killings of environmental defenders go unpunished, and convictions are rare. In 2025, the courts upheld 28-year prison sentences for four timber merchants over the murder of four Indigenous people who opposed logging, after a trial lasting a decade.

The most recent conviction came in the case of Kichwa leader Quinto Inuma Alvarado, ambushed and killed by hitmen on a river in the San Martín region in 2023.

In August, a court sentenced four people to up to 35 years in prison. It found Inuma had filed a complaint against the defendants to stop deforestation and other crimes, including drug trafficking, and that they plotted his death because he had spoken out.

## **Sexual violence against Indigenous schoolgirls**

Leo also addressed a sharp rise in sexual violence against Awajún and Wampis schoolgirls in Condorcanqui province, in the northern Amazon, and condemned the “complicity”, “impunity” and “corruption” surrounding it.

The Awajún and Wampis Women’s Council, Comuawuy, has recorded 370 cases of sexual violence in the province between January and July alone.

After persistent demands from women’s groups, specialised prosecutors’ offices were created and the government launched a prevention plan. In January, the Ministry of Women and Vulnerable Populations announced a 32-year prison sentence for a man accused of sexually abusing a 14-year-old girl.

In Peru, most murders of environmental defenders go unpunished; only a few have resulted in convictions.

In 2025, the Peruvian courts upheld a 28-year prison sentence for four timber merchants for the murder of four Indigenous people who opposed logging, following a trial that lasted a decade.

The most recent conviction came in the case of Kichwa leader Quinto Inuma Alvarado, who was murdered in 2023 when he was ambushed by hitmen on a river in the San Martín region, the Amazonian territory he was defending.

Overall, the 71-year-old has appeared unfazed by the three-nation trip, the longest of his pontificate, which began on 6 November.

While others aboard the papal plane have fallen ill, Leo has shown no sign of fatigue and has seemed energised, particularly by the days spent seeing old friends in his former diocese of Chiclayo.

The Pope mostly sat in the chair of his popemobile during part of the ride around Cusco, but it wasn’t clear if that was to preserve his energy or to steady himself on the uneven streets.
