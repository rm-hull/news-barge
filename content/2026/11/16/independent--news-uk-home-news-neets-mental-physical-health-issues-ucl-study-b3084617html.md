Poor mental and physical health is a significant factor in the rising number of young people out of work, education or training in the UK, a study has suggested.

Researchers from UCL looked at data from more than 15,000 people aged 16 to 24 in the UK who answered survey questions as part of the UK Household Longitudinal Study between 2009 and 2023.

More young people were identified as having a health problem in the years 2019-23 compared to 2014-18, which was deemed a significant factor in them not being in education, employment or training (Neet).

It comes as nearly a million 16-24-year-olds in the UK were declared Neets between April and June this year.

Ahead of his report on youth unemployment former Labour health secretary Alan Milburn has acknowledged the changes he will recommend will not be “pain-free”, with some young people likely to lose out on financial support under his plans.

Plans to bar under-25s from claiming the health element of universal credit (UC) or personal independence payments (Pip) have been reported, which campaigners have warned could push people further into poverty without creating jobs.

Lead author Dr Jacques Wels (Unit for Lifelong Health and Ageing at UCL) said the study disputes claims that young people with health issues are more sensitive than previous generations.

“Our study shows that rising ill health has had a big impact on the number of young people out of work, education or training,” he said. “A view you hear a lot is that young people with health issues today are more sensitive – that in the past they would have worked, whereas now they don’t. Our findings suggest this is absolutely not the case. Young people with a health problem in our study were no more likely to be out of work, education or training than a decade earlier. Young people after 2019 were not more fragile – they were just more sick.”

Senior author professor Praveetha Patalay (Unit for Lifelong Health & Ageing and Centre for Longitudinal Studies, UCL) said the research suggested a need to reverse the decline in young people’s health through preventative treatment and better access to care.

“Our research suggests that interventions to the labour market are unlikely to fully tackle the problem unless at the same time we try to reverse the decline in young people’s health, with more investment in mental health prevention, earlier intervention and better access to treatment.”

Levels of clinical diagnosed depression rose and fell with NEET rates, the study found.

The researchers found that the proportion of 16-24-year-old NEETs was at 12-15 per cent after the financial crash (2009-2013) and around 15-16 per cent between 2019 to 2023, which covers the Covid-19 pandemic. Between 2014 and 2018, this was at a lower rate of 10.5 to 11.5 per cent.

A higher proportion of young people reported a health problem, disability or psychological distress in 2019-23 compared with the 2014-2018 period, which accounted for a greater proportion of young people not in work, education or training.

The proportion of young people reporting a health condition or disability and psychological distress increased steadily between 2017 and 2023, researchers found. As levels of clinical depression rose between 2022 and 2023, this contributed to higher levels of young people not in work, education or training during those years.
