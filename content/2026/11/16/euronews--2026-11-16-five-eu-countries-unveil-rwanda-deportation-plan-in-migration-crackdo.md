## Denmark, Austria, Germany, the Netherlands and Greece aim to begin deporting rejected asylum seekers to Rwanda in 2027, despite concerns over human rights and potential legal challenges.

Five EU countries announced a partnership with Rwanda on Monday to establish the first EU return hub outside the bloc, marking a radical hardening of European migration policy.

Although the details of the agreement are still being negotiated, Denmark, Austria, Germany, the Netherlands and Greece say they are confident of reaching a deal and beginning deportations of irregular migrants in 2027.

The first facility is expected to accommodate around 100 people at a time. Those sent there will be free to move around Rwanda and are expected to integrate into local society and the labour market, according to people familiar with the negotiations.

“I think this [agreement] is a game changer. We will take back control and remove the incentive for illegal migration,” said Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen, during the announcement in Vienna, along with Austrian Chancellor Christian Stocker and the interior ministers of other countries involved, including Rwanda’s Vincent Biruta.

The initial hub will be a small-scale pilot project, with plans to expand the scheme once it is operational and the risk of deportations being blocked by legal challenges has diminished.

## **Who will be sent to the return hubs?**

The five countries will deport only rejected asylum seekers who are subject to enforceable return decisions.

Minors will be excluded, while each country will apply its own criteria for selecting those to be deported.

The Netherlands will focus on adult men from countries in Rwanda’s region, while Germany will target migrants who refuse to cooperate with efforts to establish their identity and come from countries reluctant to readmit their own nationals, according to people familiar with the negotiations.

After a period at the facility, the length of which has yet to be determined, migrants will be expected to rebuild their lives in Rwanda or voluntarily return to their countries of origin or another country where they have the right to reside.

The EU border agency Frontex will not have a role in deportations in the initial stages, despite a reform of its mandate recently proposed by the European Commission, which would allow it to do so.

## **Costs remain unclear**

The five EU countries will bear the full cost of deporting migrants and accommodating them in Rwanda. However, no estimate for the initial facility was provided at the press conference, as negotiations are still ongoing.

According to people familiar with the negotiations, services in Rwanda will be considerably cheaper than equivalent services in Europe.

What Rwanda will get in return is also unclear at this stage. Kigali is, however, set to sign a partnership agreement with Denmark next week, related to cooperation on migration.

## **“The agreement is a shame”**

Return hubs have raised concerns over their compatibility with EU law and the protection of human rights.

Critics warn they could lead to human rights violations, particularly when established in countries with limited democratic standards.

“The agreement is a shame,” said French lawmaker Mélissa Camara, of the Greens/EFA group, considering the countries involved are “crossing another disgraceful moral line by reaching an agreement with an authoritarian regime.”

The group of five countries said that all procedures will be carried out in line with international human rights standards.

However, legal complications may arise when deportations to Rwanda begin next year, as the European Court of Human Rights prohibits countries from transferring individuals to states or situations where they may be subjected to violations of fundamental rights.

The United Nations has raised concerns about Rwanda's human rights record.

A similar agreement between Rwanda and the United Kingdom, under which Britain planned to send asylum seekers to the African country, was ruled unlawful by the UK Supreme Court in 2023 because of the risk that those transferred could subsequently be sent to countries where they faced persecution.

According to the law enabling EU states to set up return hubs outside the bloc, only non-EU countries — “where international human rights standards and principles in accordance with international law are respected” — are eligible.
