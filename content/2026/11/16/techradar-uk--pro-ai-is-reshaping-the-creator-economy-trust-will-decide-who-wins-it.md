There’s always been a balancing act between creators and creator platforms over profit, value, and ownership. Now, AI tools are upsetting that balance and reshaping commercial dynamics, while raising new concerns about transparency and control.

As creators compete for engagement against low-effort AI “slop,” they must also navigate opaque monetization policies and inconsistent enforcement. These pressures are rapidly increasing the friction between creators and the platforms they rely on.

At the same time, as more platforms and revenue streams emerge, creators have greater choice about where to build an audience.

As the terms creators operate under keep changing, creators are pushing for more control. They want their voices to be heard, their work protected, and the value they generate recognized.

For platforms, this raises the stakes for attracting and retaining top creators. Trust is becoming a competitive differentiator, and the platforms that recognize this are protecting their creators and paying them reliably in the age of AI.

## The creator economy’s ongoing transparency issue

Today, high-volume, low-effort AI-generated content has become an inescapable part of the online experience. But for creators, lax moderation of that content can directly impact earnings by creating an uneven playing field and increasing competition for engagement and views.

Not only must creators compete with AI-generated content, but concerns are also mounting that their work is being used to train the models producing it, without their consent or compensation. Trust is eroding as a result, and inaction risks alienating the very people whose work underpins the success of digital platforms.

These challenges are becoming increasingly evident. Recently, a US court approved a $1.5 billion settlement relating to Anthropic’s use of pirated books to train its AI models. While the case represents a clarification for rights holders, smaller creators and artists often lack the resources required to pursue litigation in the first place.

Concerns have also been raised about YouTube reportedly using AI to alter creators’ videos without their consent. Even where there is no evidence of financial loss, the incident highlights a potential broader imbalance in the relationship if platforms can make unilateral changes to creators’ work without meaningful consultation.

Creators rely on platforms to distribute, monetize, and govern their work fairly. But platforms need creators too. Their business models depend on compelling content that attracts and retains audiences. It’s a mutual dependency that makes listening to and responding to creators’ concerns a strategic imperative.

## Creator trust is intrinsically linked with income reliability

Questions about trust extend beyond how content is used to how creators earn a living. In just a few decades, the creator economy has evolved from a relatively informal ecosystem into a multibillion-dollar global industry. The decisions made by the platforms shaping this industry now directly affect how millions of creators build their careers and earn income.

Even before AI, changes to recommendation algorithms, monetization models, and advertising guidelines meant creators had to become accustomed to their earnings changing overnight. These realities have encouraged them to move away from relying on individual platforms for predictable income and monetizing their content and brands across multiple platforms and revenue streams instead.

Whether they earn through commission-based selling on ecommerce platforms, livestreaming, brand sponsorships, or content views, creators’ incomes are becoming increasingly fragmented. While this diversification reduces their dependence on any single platform, it also creates greater complexity for the businesses responsible for managing and paying them.

Across the industry, poor payout experiences are already damaging creator relationships. Tipalti’s recent global payments research revealed that 26% of businesses lost contributors because of payment-related issues in the past 12 months, with creators accounting for 36% of those losses. Payment friction can directly affect a platform’s ability to retain the people generating its content.

Failing to address these pain points creates a commercial risk, particularly when creators have more options than ever to build and monetize their audiences. If a platform is frustrating to work with or fails to provide reliable payouts and transparent breakdowns of earnings, creators can move to a competitor that offers a better customer experience.

## The foundations of creator trust

To keep creators engaged and earn their trust, platforms must address two priorities: protecting creators' work and paying them reliably and transparently.

First, creators need confidence that their interests are protected and that they remain in control of their work. Platforms must acknowledge the issues posed by AI, introduce safeguards for creators’ content, and ensure that any resulting benefits are shared fairly.

Second, platforms must offer a transparent and reliable payout experience. At the end of each payment cycle, creators should receive a clear breakdown of how their payout was calculated, including the factors responsible for any monthly variations. They should feel confident that the economic value they create is recognized and that they will be paid accurately and on time.

Both priorities carry more weight as creator economies grow worldwide, presenting new opportunities for global partnerships. New markets often bring inconsistent standards for content protection and payments, and incompatible financial infrastructure can bottleneck growth.

To compete for creators in these regions, platforms need protections that hold up regardless of where a creator is based, and payout infrastructure capable of supporting an expanding range of currencies, payment methods, and regional requirements.

## The next stage of the creator economy

AI is changing what creators make, how platforms distribute it, and how value gets created. But the fundamental relationship hasn’t changed: creators need platforms to reach audiences, and platforms need creators to keep those audiences coming back.

As that relationship becomes more complex, trust will matter more, not less. Creators need to know their work is protected, their earnings are transparent, and they’ll be paid accurately and on time, no matter where they are. These aren't minor priorities. They show whether a platform actually values its creators, or just profits from them.

In the age of AI, trust won’t just shape the creator economy. It will shape who succeeds in it.
