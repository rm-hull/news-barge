When Sam Grimshaw was at grammar school, going to university to earn a degree wasn’t even a question.

Aware economic graduates were ranked among the best-paid, and with a keen interest in the subject, he applied and won a place on a three-year psychology, politics and economics course at the University of Warwick.

But more than 18 months after graduating with a 2:1 degree, the 23-year-old is living back in his boyhood bedroom at his parents’ home in Totnes, Devon, while working 20 hours a week on a zero-hours contract at a local cafe.

“I have to make a real effort to keep my sanity,” he tells *The Independent*.

As the number of people going to university hit a record 262,820 in September, Mr Grimshaw is one of the tens of thousands coming out of the other end, faced with a challenging graduate job market.

Figures published by Adzuna showed a 45 per cent fall in jobs for graduates advertised in August compared to last year.

It means greater competition for young people finishing university, with many resorting to life unemployed or non-graduate jobs. Tellingly, of graduates in work, more than a third are in jobs unrelated to their degree subjects, according to The National Institute of Economic and Social Research.

On Tuesday, Alan Milburn will publish his review into tackling youth unemployment, with a focus on so-called Neets – 16 to 24-year-olds not in education, employment or training – after the number hit a million earlier this year.

But for Mr Grimshaw, expected recommendations like overhauling the welfare system and reducing national insurance paid by businesses on workers in retail and hospitality are unlikely to help him, or many other graduates chasing skilled roles.

Since graduating from the University of Warwick in the summer of last year, he has applied for around 20 graduate scheme or entry-level jobs in economics, receiving just one job interview. Many, he says, resulted in a letter with no feedback due to the number of applicants.

“It’s incredibly hard,” he says. “I’ve gone back to living in my parents’ home, working part-time in a local cafe. I’m grateful for the work but when you are set up with the expectation that if you take the reasonable steps, like going to university, and work hard, it feels like the rug has been pulled up underneath you.”

Mr Grimshaw realises he is one of the lucky ones.

He is able to live at his parents’ without paying rent and, alongside his part-time job, he is able to volunteer at a local cinema and regularly goes to the gym. But he also has a near-£50,000 student loan debt hanging over him. The debt will only continue to rise while he does not pay it off.

“It’s like living in unreality,” he says. “It’s almost as if the degree didn’t pay, it is strange being back in your childhood bedroom. It’s like being left behind. My grandfather was 22 when he had my father. I feel infantilised. The life markers feel so out of reach when there’s lack of opportunity.”

On his friends, Mr Grimshaw says some have been more flexible on jobs they apply for, with one applying for 100 roles in three months before securing a place on a graduate scheme in London. However, the scale of pay compared to rent means they could be worse off financially than he is.

“All the incentives are stacked in conflicting directions,” he says.

He wants the Milburn review to offer more for graduates, encouraging retailers like John Lewis to not just open up more shopfloor jobs, but also to expand the size of graduate schemes.

Ahead of the Tuesday’s publication, Mr Milburn, a former Labour health secretary, told the BBC that young people should have a legal guarantee of a job, training or employment support. He also claimed that previous employment schemes have been temporary, and do not go far enough to help young people.

Mr Milburn is expected to also call for under-25s to no longer be able to claim the health element of universal credit (UC) or personal independence payments (Pip), moving them instead to a “youth allowance”, paid at the same level as the standard rate of universal credit.

Back in May, the National Union of Student said the government must deal with the threat of AI to graduate-level work, warning Mr Milburn of a “generation left behind” if he is unable to face up to the challenge.
