Adam Abbou stars as Freddie in the hit BBC series This City Is Ours

**With millions of viewers and a major role in a Bafta-nominated series, you might not expect Adam Abbou to know much about feeling self-conscious.**

"When we were kids it wasn't so cool to be from Liverpool," the This City Is Ours actor says.

"I'd be in these rooms with people who were really posh and I always felt out of place."

The Liverpool-based BBC series has become a hit, with Abbou, who plays Freddie, stepping into a prominent role for its second season.

But it's not just the storylines on drugs, gangs and murders hooking audiences - it's the setting too, and the Scouse actors bringing it to life.

Created by Liverpudlian writer Stephen Butchard, This City Is Ours recruited heavily from Merseyside.

Lead actors James Nelson-Joyce, Jack McMullen and Mike Noble were all born in Liverpool, as was Abbou, who grew up in Anfield.

But like many young and aspiring actors, for a long time he thought where he came from could hold him back.

"I didn't see a lot of [Liverpudlian accents] on TV when I was a kid," the 23-year-old says.

As a result, acting "always felt really out of reach".

Abbou played the title role in a touring production of Billy Elliot the Musical in 2015, and has since appeared in dramas like Casualty, Time and Waterloo Road.

He says he wasn't expecting his latest gig, where he plays Freddie, gangster Banksey's son, to become so significant.

"If you would have asked me 10 years ago, I would have said you probably need to move to London," he says.

"So for me to be able to do a show like this in Liverpool, it just further proves that you can do it from anywhere."

![Adam Abbou as Freddie. He's pictured outside on a scooter wearing a dark green puffer jacket and blue beanie hat.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/0fb4/live/b160e000-df80-11f1-b844-3146c4983677.jpg)

The young actor says more needs to be done to improve working-class representation in TV

Abbou is passionate about promoting working-class stories and actors from underprivileged backgrounds, who he says are "some of the hardest working kids you'll ever come across".

While This City Is Ours provided him with his big break, he says more needs to be done across the industry to open doors.

New research from the Creative Industries Policy and Evidence Centre, external published last week found representation of people from working-class backgrounds has fallen over the past five years, from 25% in 2021 to 21% now.

"I think it's a trust issue," Abbou says, with producers less willing to take risks on new talent.

"But if you give a working-class kid a chance they will run with it and they won't let you down."

## 'Making waves for young Scouse talent'

In January, the BBC committed to working harder to make sure people from different backgrounds were portrayed accurately and authentically in its output.

It followed a report that recommended the broadcaster could improve how it represented working-class communities, highlighting how their stories often focus on "themes of poverty, crime and addiction".

That's true for This City Is Ours, which follows the lives of drugs gangs, with Freddie becoming more involved in the business throughout the second series.

That portrayal of the city is something Liverpool's mayor said he wasn't happy about.

But Abbou insists he was confident about the series' ability to represent his hometown authentically and dismisses accusations it glamourises crime.

"I was never worried because Stephen [Butchard] roots all of his writing in Liverpool," he says.

"He is a Scouser, he knows what it's like to grow up a Scouser. The environment and gorgeous scenery of the city is always embedded in the script.

"It's a show that paints the city in a wonderful light. Of course the characters are flawed, but that's the nature of the show that we're creating."

![Adam Abbou as Freddie. Looking frightened, he has his hand on a door and looks over his shoulder.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/dee6/live/93ed0a80-df71-11f1-8112-6b9154c79b48.jpg)

Abbou was thrilled to be trusted with a more prominent role in the second series

From the reaction he's had locally, Abbou thinks the series has been a boost for Liverpool overall.

The reaction has been "quite crazy" in the city, he says, "but of course it would be because people love to see themselves and their accent reflected on TV and when it is, you almost feel like you know the person".

"It has truly been incredible to see the rise of the Scouse accent," he adds, crediting the Netflix series Adolescence, starring and co-created by Stephen Graham, with accelerating the change.

"Stephen Graham really was the one who started the wave for Scouse actors," he says.

"And then the likes of Jodie Comer and now you've got your Mike Nobles, your James Nelson-Joyces... and now your Adam Abbous.

"We're making waves for young Scouse talent."

As for what's next, Abbou says the series has helped push his career forward and he has some "exciting projects lined up" which he can't speak about just yet.

"I just want to do things that make me feel creatively fulfilled and mean something and have a voice."
