Over 60 rare and brightly colored snakes have been released into the wild in California - but don’t fret, scientists say it’s for a good reason.

The San Francisco Zoo & Gardens announced last week that 62 young San Francisco garter snakes have been released this fall at undisclosed locations throughout San Mateo County, just south of San Francisco.

The species, known for its bold red, blue and black stripes, was included on the federal government’s first endangered species list in 1967, and only several thousand still exist in the wild. Despite its name, the San Francisco garter snake is only found in San Mateo County and Santa Cruz County, where they live.

The release represents a major milestone following a years-long effort from wildlife biologists and conservationists to help rebuild the species’ population.

“This is an extraordinary accomplishment for everyone who has worked for generations to protect this remarkable species,” biologist Karine Tokatlian said. “Seeing these young snakes returning to the wild represents years of research, collaboration, habitat restoration and careful conservation work.”

![Dozens of San Francisco garter snakes that were raised in captivity as part of a conservation effort have been released in California](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/16/40/Zoo-born-San-Francisco-garter-snake-edited.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“This first release is an important step toward building healthy, resilient populations that can sustain themselves over the long term,” Tokatlian added.

The snakes were raised using a conservation technique called “head-starting,” which raises young animals in captivity during their most vulnerable developmental stages.

Zoo officials said providing basic needs and a safe space can increase their chances of reaching breeding age.

Last year, U.S. Geological Survey biologists collected pregnant San Francisco garter snakes and brought them to the San Francisco Zoo. After the snakes gave birth, zoo staff returned them to the wild while caring for their offspring.

The offspring remained at the zoo until they were large enough to better withstand challenges they will encounter in the wild, including predators and competition for food.

![Wildlife officials plan to release more of the snakes into the wild in 2027](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/16/12/Zoo-born-San-Francisco-garter-snake-in-wetland-habitat.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

About one-third of the snakes were returned to the areas where their mothers first lived, while the remaining two-thirds were distributed in several other areas that needed greater genetic diversity.

For the San Francisco garter snake to be removed from the endangered species list, there have to be at least 10 self-sustaining breeding populations, each containing at least 200 individual snakes.

Zoo officials did not share the specific release locations in order to protect the vulnerable population from poachers, vehicles and potential disease exposure.

“This species is special in many ways, but unfortunately its critical status is not unique,” said wildlife ecologist Ariel Starr. “The success of this project and the associated research will not only benefit the San Francisco garter snake but will also hopefully inform the recovery of many imperiled reptile species.”

Wildlife officials are already preparing for another release. More than 130 additional baby San Francisco garter snakes were born at the facility this year and are being raised to be released into the wild in 2027.
