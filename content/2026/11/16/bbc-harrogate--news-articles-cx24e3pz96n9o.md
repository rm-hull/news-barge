North Yorkshire Police could be merged into a regional force if the so-called "mega force" proposals are approved

**Senior politicians across North Yorkshire have spoken of their opposition to proposals for so-called police "mega-forces".**

Sir Mark Rowley, Britain's most senior police officer, recently said policing was "heading for failure" without change and has proposed between eight and 10 regional forces should be established.

But Jo Coles, York and North Yorkshire's Labour deputy mayor, warned bigger forces would jeopardise links between officers and their communities, adding that merging forces would prove a "distraction" and would "take resources from the front line".

A Home Office spokesperson said the current model of policing "has not changed since the 1960s and is no longer fit for purpose".

Coles, whose role is to oversee both North Yorkshire Police and North Yorkshire Fire and Rescue Service, said she and York and North Yorkshire's elected mayor David Skaith were, however, not opposed to the creation of a National Police Service to tackle serious cross-border crime.

Coles said the national service, plans for which were set out last January in the government's policing white paper, would save money by pulling together areas of regional expertise.

Under that government proposal, the service would bring together various organisations, including the National Crime Agency, Counter Terror Policing and the National Police Air Service.

Coles said: "It would drive through a lot of efficiency and save a lot of money. That is the money we think should be filtered back down into neighbourhood policing."

But Coles and Skaith have argued for the existing mayoral combined authorities, set up to deliver and scrutinise the elected mayor's policy programme, to be the "default structure" for any policing reforms.

Jo Coles, York and North Yorkshire's Labour deputy mayor for policing, fire and crime, says merging forces would be a "distraction"

North Yorkshire Council's Conservative leader Carl Les, who serves on the combined authority, said there was "a logic to joining up some services to make a stronger whole".

But he warned that "losing more identifiable local forces will run the very real risk of losing a local presence, which is essential to maintaining community confidence".

"Time and again, resources will be sucked away to meet needs elsewhere, especially in cities, at the expense of rural areas like North Yorkshire," he said.

Coles added: "At the moment, we have some really good oversight of policing and have the ability to bring partners together who can reduce harm in communities and build public safety. That's what is at risk from a mega-force.

"Criminal justice, health, housing and transport - all of those people need to be pulling together to make our communities safer.

"The Home Office is looking at this through a lens of policing and policing alone, whereas actually we build public safety in partnerships with other organisations."

Nigel Ayre, who leads City of York Council's opposition Liberal Democrat group, said it was "not very often" he agreed with Coles, who is also a city councillor.

But he said: "On this one, she is absolutely right. Policing needs to be close to communities. That is when it works best.

"While there needs to be cross-border collaboration on certain things like county lines drug dealing, policing needs to be in the community where it is most effective."

Chris Steward, who leads the council's Conservative group, said: "Across the country, fewer forces might be a good thing, but York and North Yorkshire would undoubtedly lose out.

"Any merger would see us get less attention, and merging with West or South Yorkshire would be an absolute nightmare.

"Even the likes of Cleveland would be extremely bad, so North Yorkshire Police should be kept as it is."

Meanwhile, Tom Seston, leader of North Yorkshire Council's Reform UK group, said the "vast" rural area which North Yorkshire Police covered meant a merger with West Yorkshire or South Yorkshire Police would be "wholly unsuitable".

"Having competing systems between rural and urban policing would make the public perception appear the police are far more detached from local communities," he explained.

York and North Yorkshire's taxpayers help fund the county's police force through a sum known as the precept, which is set by Skaith and which is added to council tax bills each year.

One of those taxpayers, Fred Smith, waiting for a bus in York city centre, said he feared any force mergers would see cuts to officer numbers in North Yorkshire.

"Leave it as it is," he urged.

"When I was younger, there was a police officer on each housing estate. Everyone knew him, they respected him and could go to him."

"We need more coppers," Smith added.

Eric Major, preparing for a day trip to Whitby, said mergers were "a bad idea".

"The police would end up concentrated in the bigger cities and not in the countryside, which needs as much protection.

"There could be more isolation and less control of rural crime."

The Home Office spokesperson said: "A structural overhaul is overdue, and we will deliver the most radical reform of policing in 200 years."

They added that the reforms would deliver "strong local policing, focused on neighbourhood crime".

"There will be more resilient forces providing specialist capabilities to tackle serious and complex crime threats, and a National Police Service tackling national and cross-border threats."

North Yorkshire Police has been contacted for a response.
