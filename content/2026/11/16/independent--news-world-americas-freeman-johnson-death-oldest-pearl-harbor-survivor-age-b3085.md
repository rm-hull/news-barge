Freeman Johnson, the oldest of the few remaining survivors of the Pearl Harbor attack, has died.

He passed away Saturday at his home in Centerville, Massachusetts, at the age of 106, according to one of his daughters, Diane Johnson.

"It’s going to be a hell of a Thanksgiving," Diane Johnson said. "He’s going to be up there with all of his friends and family and my mom."

Johnson was among an estimated 87,000 military personnel stationed on Oahu on Dec. 7, 1941. When Japanese forces launched their surprise attack, he was positioned below deck working on a boiler aboard the USS St. Louis.

During a May 2026 interview with *The Associated Press*, Johnson explained that he neither saw nor heard the bombardment. He never even heard his fellow crewmates firing anti-aircraft guns at attacking planes, despite them shooting down a torpedo plane.

"While all the rigamarole was going on topside, I was inside a steam drum. Couldn’t see anything, absolutely nothing," said Johnson, whose duty on the ship had been as a firefighter.

![Freeman Johnson holds up his dog tag on May 6, 2026, in Centerville, Mass.](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/14/US_Freeman_Johnson_71158.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Even as the St. Louis sailed out into the Pacific Ocean, Johnson knew very little about what had transpired. By the time he finally went topside, the light cruiser had evaded midget submarines and reached open water safely.

"We were way out to sea, way out. You couldn’t see any land at all. All you saw was ocean," he said. "I was just a sailor, just a swabbie, I was not an officer. They don’t tell you anything if you don’t need to know. And I didn’t need to know it. So they tell you nothing."

The roster of surviving veterans from the attack had stood at around a dozen following the December 2025 death of 105-year-old Ira "Ike" Schab.

Although Johnson did not witness the Pearl Harbor attack, which killed more than 2,400 Americans and propelled the United States into World War II, he was a firsthand witness to other historic events during the war.

He helped commission the USS Iowa and recalled the battleship's preparations in November 1943 ahead of transporting President Franklin D. Roosevelt to the Tehran Conference with British Prime Minister Winston Churchill and Soviet leader Josef Stalin. The vessel was equipped with two elevators and a bathtub. All the ammunition and much of the oil had to be removed to lighten the ship for its journey down the Potomac River to pick up Roosevelt, before being reloaded ahead of setting out to sea.

Johnson was also present aboard the Iowa when the war came to an end. Standing on the Iowa’s mast on Sept. 2, 1945, he watched the surrender ceremonies happening about a mile away in Tokyo Bay aboard the USS Missouri.

"I could see the boats coming up with the Marines escorting the Japanese onto the ship and sitting around a table," he said. "It was all over. That was the end of the war. A bunch of us got together — the war is over. Let’s go home."

Despite all the historical interest in Pearl Harbor, the gruff Johnson, remembered for his mischievous smile and cackling laugh, did not consider it one of the defining moments of his life.

For him, those moments were marrying his late wife after the war and raising three daughters. He spent years working in a machinist shop, later worked in a convenience store, and finally delivered meals to senior citizens, retiring from his last job at age 90.

"Pearl Harbor just happened. I can’t put it any other way," he said.
