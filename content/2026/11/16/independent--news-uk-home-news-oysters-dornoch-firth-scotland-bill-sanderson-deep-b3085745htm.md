Native oysters are breeding in the Dornoch Firth for the first time in more than 100 years, scientists have established.

The shellfish are thought to have been fished to extinction in the firth, in the Scottish Highlands, in the late 19th century.

Their return is the work of the Dornoch Environmental Enhancement Project (DEEP), a 12-year effort to restore the extinct oyster reef, which reintroduced oysters to the area in 2017.

That year, 300 oysters from Loch Ryan were placed on the floor of the Dornoch Firth.

By October 2026, more than 140,000 juvenile oysters had been released there.

Researchers from Heriot-Watt University first noticed baby oysters, known as spat, last summer.

This summer they returned to find the spat had grown as large as a 50p piece.

![Professor Bill Sanderson from Heriot-Watt University preparing to release oysters into the Dornoch Firth](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/14/01M7J4M3QDQ0VV72N5XN1VENGV.jpg?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&auto=webp)

The findings were verified by independent scientists, and the researchers said they confirm the population is reproducing successfully in the Dornoch Firth.

Professor Bill Sanderson from Heriot-Watt University said: “This is a moment over 12 years and 140,000 oysters in the making.

“We knew we had about 3000 oysters reaching maturity across the two beds we’ve established, which meant the possibility of spat was higher.”

He added: “When we began DEEP 12 years ago, it was among the first Native European oyster restoration projects, so we have been trailblazing all the way.

“The oysters here are now reproducing, but we’ll redouble our efforts to add juveniles to reach critical mass – we’re aiming for around four million oysters by 2031 and additional funding to get it done.

“Baby oysters are released by the mother oysters after brooding and drift in the plankton for a few days before starting to look for a place to settle and call ‘home’.

“We have found that there is nothing like the presence of other oysters and shell reef material to draw them in.”

Gurjameer Ramday, the DEEP research associate who discovered the spat, said: “Sea conditions were warm in 2025, and this year in particular.

“So we have been extra vigilant and hopeful that this could be the year when we saw them.

“It’s brilliant. Wild oyster populations have collapsed throughout Europe, so finding baby oysters now is proof that targeted restoration can work.”

![As of October 2026, more than 140,000 juvenile oysters have been released into the Dornoch Firth](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/12/16111052-faab2ac5-23db-4f90-8e0b-c41d1f761a7c.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The DEEP project partners are The Glenmorangie Company, Heriot-Watt University and the Marine Conservation Society.

Sandy Luk, chief executive of the Marine Conservation Society, said: “We are so excited that oysters in the Dornoch Firth have successfully reproduced.

“This historic milestone shows what is possible and will help inform our oyster regeneration projects across the UK.

“The breeding success wouldn’t have been possible without the help of hundreds of volunteers who supported the DEEP scientists.

“Their dedication has been vital to restoring these long-lost oyster beds, which not only provide habitat for other species but also help capture carbon and improve water quality.”

Caspar MacRae, Glenmorangie’s president and chief executive, said it is a “very proud moment for all of us.”

He said: “At Glenmorangie, we aim not just to protect our beautiful Highland world, but to enhance it for future generations.

“Rather like whisky, restoration takes time.

“Nature has started to take over, and this fledgling reef is on its way to becoming a fully biodiverse, self-sustaining ecosystem.”

The team will continue to monitor the site to keep track of the oyster population and any improvements to biodiversity.

Last week another 5,000 oysters were placed in the firth.
