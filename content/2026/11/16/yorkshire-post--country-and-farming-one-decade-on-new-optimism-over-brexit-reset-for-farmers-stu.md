# One decade on, new optimism over Brexit reset for farmers - Stuart Minting

## It’s just over a decade since I visited a North Yorkshire farm to interview our then Prime Minister about the potential impact of Brexit on the region’s agriculture.

It was a gloriously sunny June day, shortly before the referendum that polls and studies have concluded saw a majority of farmers vote to leave the single market.

Mike and Lorna Wilkinson had spent days preparing their arable and egg farm for the visit which saw farmers sat around a farmhouse table telling David Cameron how tough farming was with beef, lamb, egg and milk prices down.

A long-term food strategy was needed to aid investments.

![A Swaledale ewe and lambs in fields near Swaledale Woollens in Muker](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOjEyYTgwMGI3LTg2NjUtNGVhMi1iZDhhLTk3YzQzNWUwYzA5YjozMDcxY2FlMS1kMTI5LTQwODgtYmQzZS1iODhhZjFkNTU1NjM=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)


Mr Cameron warned a UK exit from the EU would heap further misery on Yorkshire farmers, hampering access to a huge market and ending farming support payments that "keep Britain farming".

"We would be putting our farmers at risk”, he told me. “Today we sell no beef, not one single piece of beef to America."

As the prime ministerial entourage drove off down the lane, the local MP and a Pocklington dairy farmer looked on, with the latter telling me he was being paid just 17p a pint for milk which cost him 23p to produce.

Roll on ten years and six prime ministers and little appears to have changed for farmers.

The EU still accounts for the vast majority of UK beef exports, and it has taken until this year for the UK to send is first shipment of beef to the US.

The dairy farmer, Paul Tompkins, is still feeling the pressure from milk prices, but is now deputy president of the National Farmers Union.

So it was that I had in mind when NFU President Tom Bradshaw told the Northern Farming Conference that the government was now “desperate” to discuss resuming closer relations wih the EU.

Just as farmers who voted Brexit insisted they were sick of European red tape, the government now has an ambition of clearing damaging red tape introduced as a result of Brexit.

The government has claimed the UK could harvest £5.1bn in benefits a year from the reset deal, which appears one of the most positive news stories for agriculture this year, and, possibly the last decade.

This mood of optimism was captured by Country Land and Business Association president at the conference with a vision that would see agriculture firmly in the driving seat in ten years’ time.

By 2036, Gavin Lane predicted, the debate over emissions would have moved on, water shortage and pollution issues tackled and farming enterprises based on a number of income streams.

In a decade’s time it would be good to look back and see more positive changes for farmers than the last one has witnessed.
