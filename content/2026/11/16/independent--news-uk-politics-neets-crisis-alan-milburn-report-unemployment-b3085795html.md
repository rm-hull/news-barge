A benefits shake-up, new apprenticeships and a £3,000 incentive for employers are among the measures proposed by Alan Milburn to help nearly half a million young people into jobs over the next decade.

The former Labour cabinet minister will publish a major report on Tuesday on the one million under-25s trapped across the UK not in education, employment or training – known as Neets – with a raft of measures aimed at making “work, not welfare, the future for the next generation”.

Campaigners have described the report as “a once in a generation opportunity” to resolve the issue which is costing British taxpayers around £125 billion a year.

However, the recommendations will represent a challenge to Andy Burnham who could face backlash from some Labour MPs who will not want to cut welfare – a rebellion which ultimately saw the downfall of his predecessor Sir Keir Starmer.

Mr Milburn has already acknowledged his radical proposals – including early years and schools, apprenticeships and health and welfare support – would not be “pain-free”, giving a candid warning that they could lead to some people getting less financial support.

His report will include an overhaul of the benefits system, including a proposal for a new “youth allowance” for under-25s, which would stop young people receiving the disability benefit personal independence payment (Pip) and the health element of universal credit (UC).

![Former health secretary Alan Milburn is due to publish his report on Tuesday.](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/11/16102721-8f9d2d23-7538-48f5-8b3e-9c6de795d320.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Mr Milburn will say: “Britain has had a youth Neet problem for 25 years and never a plan to end it. We are at a fork in the road and half measures will not do. A complete system reset is needed.”

Among his proposals are:

* A new benefits system for under-25s, with the Universal Credit health element replaced
* A doubling of the number of young people achieving an apprenticeship over the next decade
* Instant access to needs-based mental health support without waiting for a diagnosis, including fast-track help for young people who are NEET
* More support for parents to ensure 90 per cent of children are ‘school-ready’ by age five
* A payment of £3,000 to an employer who hires young people who have been NEET for six months, with higher payments for small businesses

Mr Milburn’s recommendations for reform would result in 600,000 more young people in education, employment or training each year – around 440,000 of whom would be in sustained work – by 2036, according to modelling for the report. It would also reduce relative poverty, after housing costs, by 50,000 people.

The report will also set out a national ambition to reduce the Neet rate by more than half from 13 per cent to around 5 per cent by 2036, which he will say would make the UK one of Europe’s best performers on youth participation. The UK rate has never been lower than 9.7 per cent since data began in 2001.

The report argues that the Neets crisis is a choice and that other countries have made a different one. The Netherlands has a rate of 4.8 per cent.

Other reported recommendations include gradually cutting national insurance paid by businesses on workers aged under 25, starting in retail and hospitality, as well as a “legal guarantee” of work, training or employment support for young people, with those who refuse to take up the offer potentially losing benefits.

But while the reforms will produce savings, it will cost more in the immediate future. Mr Milburn estimates that an £5.8bn annual investment by 2036 will be offset by £3.6bn of welfare savings and £2.4 billion of extra tax revenue and reduced benefit payments.

At a press conference on Tuesday, Mr Milburn is expected to say that the current youth unemployment rate is not just an economic crisis but a moral one too and that “youth participation has to run through public policy like a stick of rock”.

He will say: “A lost generation is not a fact of life. It is a choice. And I believe it is the wrong one.

“Not just because it is a fiscal crisis or an economic crisis. But because it is a moral one.

“We are failing as a nation to gift the next generation the opportunities they need to succeed.

“It is time for a new settlement that makes work not welfare the future for the next generation.

"Demographic change makes getting young people into work a necessity. Britain’s future relies on us backing young people.”

The report has been welcomed by business leaders and mental health charities.

Leading economist Andy Haldane, formerly on the Bank of England now chief executive of the Royal Society of Arts, said: “There is no more pressing challenge - economically, societally, morally - than reducing the young person’s NEET problem in the UK. Alan Milburn’s report provides the blueprint for doing so. It is now for government to summon the resources and political will to deliver on that blueprint."

Shevaun Haviland, director general of the British Chambers of Commerce said: “The challenge of getting hundreds of thousands more young people into work is massive and the Milburn Review does not shirk from that. Having almost a million young adults outside education, employment or training is a huge waste of talent that we simply cannot afford. They are the workforce of the future.”

Allen Simpson, chief executive of UK Hospitality said: "This is a rightly ambitious plan to solve the NEETs crisis. Hospitality, as the largest employer of young people in the economy, stands ready to provide young people with the job opportunities they need to be part of the work generation. I've been delighted to have worked closely with the review team, and I look forward to working with the Government on taking these plans forward."

Jon Sparkes, chief executive of Mencap said: "This is a once-in-a-generation opportunity to back young people and invest in them so they can realise their potential. We know young people with a learning disability want to work, but the system fails to provide them with the opportunity and tailored support to do so.”

However, there was a warning from James Taylor, executive director of strategy at disability equality charity Scope.

Statistics out earlier this month showed just under half (45.3 per cent) of Neets were classed as disabled in the year to March – a proportion which has more than doubled in just over a decade.

He said: "We are deeply concerned by proposals that could leave some young disabled people with less financial support. PIP helps with the extra costs of disability, whether someone is in work or not. Taking that support away could make it harder, not easier, for young disabled people to get into work and live independently.

“The government must be ambitious about creating jobs and opportunities for young disabled people. But that ambition must not come at the cost of the vital financial support they need.”

The report comes after*The Independent* revealed last week that another 250,000 young people are heading for a life trapped on benefits unless major changes are made.
