## Serbia’s Student List will speed up rule-of-law reforms needed for EU accession, MP Aleksandar Baucal said in the movement’s first international appearance.

The Student List will speed up work on meeting the EU’s interim accession benchmarks on the rule of law, professor and newly elected MP Aleksandar Baucal said in the much-awaited first international public appearance of Serbia's new main political force.

“The next question is whether Serbia will remain on the European path. For us, the European path is one of reforms: strengthening European values, building functional institutions and a genuine rule-of-law system, developing a sustainable economy integrated in the European market,” Baucal said on Monday.

Baucal spoke at the EU Enlargement Forum in Brussels, the first time a Student List representative has addressed an international audience. Most of the room gave him a standing ovation after the speech.

For the professor, the EU accession path is not only key for good neighbourly relations, but, more broadly, for building a more democratic society.

“One of Serbia’s strategic objectives will be to accelerate the fulfilment of the interim benchmarks under chapters 23 and 24, as well. We want Serbia to be a reliable partner to the European Union and to its members,” he continued.

The party’s historic electoral victory last month raised hopes of a reset in Serbia’s troubled relations with the EU. The movement, born of nearly two years of mass protests, ousted Aleksandar Vučić’s party after 14 years in power.

Two weeks ago, Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos met a Student List delegation in Brussels, their first formal exchange with a senior EU official. The delegates said the incoming government would focus on restoring the rule of law, judicial independence and media freedom, not only for the sake of accession but above all for the Serbian people.

Baucal returned to those themes, pledging to advance on the “fundamentals” of EU accession. The professor-turned-MP said the new government would stay open to dialogue and seek to solve concrete problems rather than create new ones.

“This is not only about the European Union helping Serbia protect democracy. It is also about a democratic Serbia contributing to a stronger and more resilient Europe,” he said.

Yet prominent figures in the Student List remain cautious about aligning with EU foreign policy, notably on sanctions against Russia. These are deeply unpopular in Serbia, where memories of the Western sanctions that hit the population under the Milošević regime still run deep.

Recognition of Kosovo could also prove a sticking point with EU countries. Albanian Prime Minister Edi Rama warned that the new government is unlikely to change Serbia’s stance.

The Student List grew as a heterogeneous movement without a clearly defined leadership. Belgrade’s position on accession and foreign policy may become clearer once its MPs settle on the make-up of the new government.

For now, Baucal sees the party’s next challenge as turning the solidarity forged in the struggle into lasting trust in institutions.

“Citizens were able to set many differences aside. But in a democratic society, these interests and opinions will once again come to the fore. We believe that thanks to our differences, we will build a better system for our citizens,” he said.
