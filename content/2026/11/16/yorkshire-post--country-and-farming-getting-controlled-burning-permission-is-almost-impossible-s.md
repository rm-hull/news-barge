# Getting controlled burning permission is ‘almost impossible’ says land manager

## Getting permission to use controlled burning has become “almost impossible” according to a land manager who has started a petition calling for heather burning restrictions to be revoked.

More than 1,400 signatures have been added to the UK Parliament petition, started by a Peak District land manager, known only as “Robert”.

Critics argue that heavily restricting controlled burns leaves dangerous fuel loads of dry vegetation that feed massive wildfires.

England recorded 1,354 wildfires between January and August 2026, more than a third higher than the same period in 2025, according to provisional government figures.

![Moorland groups are calling for measures to enable controlled burning to cut wildfire risks.](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOmNiYjhmNGY0LTcyZDQtNDRmMy05NjdhLTFmOTk1OWY2MTc2NDozNTA0MWI1Ni00ZjhiLTQxMDgtOTkyZC01OWIwZTg2ZTY0Njc=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)


The petition, backed by Moorland Regional Groups, voluntary bodies representing gamekeepers, land managers, and sporting estates, calls on the Government to revoke the Heather and Grass etc. Burning (England) Regulations 2021, as amended in 2025.

Since 2021, burning on peat deeper than 40cm on some protected sites has needed a Defra licence.

In September 2025 this was extended to peat deeper than 30cm anywhere in a Less Favoured Area, roughly three times as much land.

Defra’s own figures, given to Parliament in September 2026, show that only two of 24 applications for deep-peat burning licences were approved in 12 months.

Robert said: “Over the years I’ve seen the heather and grass grow back faster than ever, so every summer there’s more fuel on the hill and the risk gets worse.

“Controlled burning is one of the best tools we have to stop a wildfire before it starts, and right now it’s almost impossible to get permission to use it.”

Heather burning has long been used to manage heather moorlands. Burning older, woody heather forces the plant to regenerate, producing fresh, nutrient-rich green shoots, which provide food for red grouse and livestock like sheep.

Its supporters argue that it also removes accumulated dry fuel loads, creates firebreaks across moors and reduces the intensity of any fire that follows.

However there are concerns burning can expose fragile acid uplands to erosion from rainfall, with a resulting influx of silt and peat into streams and watercourses.

Defra says over 80% of our peatlands are degraded, damaged, or dried out.

It said burning damages important ecosystems, and protecting peat from further damage is crucial to its restoration and recovery.

Responding to consultations on changes to the regulations Defra said: “Burning is only one of several options available to land managers trying to manage heather on peatland. Overgrowth of woody heather can be managed through the restoration of wet bog.

“In its natural state, heather is naturally limited on peatland and is just one of many species present. Prior to peatland restoration, overgrowth of heather can also be managed by cutting.

“Burning should be considered a last resort, used only where no sustainable alternatives are available.”
