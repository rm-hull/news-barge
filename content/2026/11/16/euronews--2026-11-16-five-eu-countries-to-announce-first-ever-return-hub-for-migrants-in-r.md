## Denmark, Austria, Germany, the Netherlands and Greece are in the final stages of negotiations with Kigali’s government. The first pilot project could be announced on Monday and become operational in mid-2027, amid controversy over respect for fundamental rights and compatibility with EU law.

Rwanda will host the first-ever EU return hub built outside the bloc, in an unprecedented move to send irregular migrants to countries with which they have no connection.

The agreement between Kigali’s government and five EU member states is set to be announced on Monday in Vienna, after months of negotiations, two people familiar with the matter told Euronews.

The group of migration hardliners comprises Denmark, Austria, Germany, the Netherlands and Greece.

Their interior and migration ministers are attending a conference in the Austrian capital, where they will be joined by Rwanda’s Interior Minister Vincent Biruta. A few metres from the venue, Austrian Chancellor Christian Stocker and Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen will hold a press conference in the afternoon to announce the deal.

According to the sources, the first hub would be a “pilot project”, set to host a few hundred people deported from the five EU countries, starting in spring or summer 2027.

It would consist of open facilities housing migrants, who would receive a residence permit and be free to move around the country, with the aim of integrating them into local society and the labour market.

In principle, return hubs are temporary accommodation for migrants awaiting deportation to their countries of origin. However, there is no time limit on stays, and those involved could end up stranded in Rwanda and never be sent home.

The EU border agency Frontex could also be involved in deportations to Rwanda once the European Parliament and member states approve a reform of its mandate, recently proposed by the European Commission.

## **Rwanda's return hub may face legal challenges**

Return hubs have raised concerns in Europe over their compatibility with EU law and the protection of human rights. Critics warn they could lead to violations, particularly when established in countries with limited democratic standards.

The law enabling EU states to set up return hubs outside the bloc establishes that only non-EU countries — “where international human rights standards and principles in accordance with international law are respected” — are eligible.

According to the UN, Rwanda has a questionable track record of respect for human rights.

A similar agreement between Kigali and the United Kingdom, under which Britain would send asylum seekers to Rwanda, was ruled unlawful by the UK Supreme Court in 2023 because of the risk of human rights abuses.

The five governments said the project would comply fully with EU law and international obligations, and would be run in partnership with international organisations such as the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) and the International Organization for Migration (IOM).

However, UNHCR and IOM are not directly involved in the negotiations on the procedures and could pull out if certain conditions are not met.

“IOM’s support requires a formal request from the receiving government, and assurances that adequate safeguards are in place,” a spokesperson for the organization told Euronews.

Legal complications may arise when deportations to Rwanda begin next year, as the European Court of Human Rights prohibits countries from transferring individuals to states or situations where they may face violations of fundamental rights.

“With this system, migrants may be subjected to torture and inhuman or degrading treatment,” Eleonora Celoria from Asgi, an Italian association of legal experts specialized in migration, told Euronews.

## **Costs are unclear**

The five EU countries will bear the entire financial burden of the deportations and of accommodation in Rwanda.

There are no current estimates of the total costs, and it is unclear what Kigali will get in return. In 2025, the Rwandan government signed an agreement to accept third-country nationals expelled from the US in exchange for roughly $7.5 million in financial support.

A similar project set up by Italy in Albania sparked controversy over its costs, with a total bill of €670 million over a five-year partnership. According to a study from an Italian university, detaining migrants in these centres is much more expensive for Italy than hosting them on its own territory.

Each available place in Albania costs over €153,000, whereas comparable facilities in Sicily could accommodate migrants for approximately €21,000 per place.

More EU countries may join Denmark, Austria, Germany, the Netherlands and Greece in deporting irregular migrants to unrelated countries in the future.

Nineteen of the EU’s 27 governments are convinced this is an “innovative solution” for migration management, as a joint letter sent to EU leaders last June made clear.

Other countries, such as Spain and France, have rebuked the deportation scheme, setting themselves on a collision course with a growing political majority within the EU.
