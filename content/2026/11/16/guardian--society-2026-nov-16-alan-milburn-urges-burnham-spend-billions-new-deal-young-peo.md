Andy Burnham is being urged to spend billions of pounds on a “new deal” for the next generation that would fund guaranteed jobs support for young people to offset a radical cut in benefits.

In a watershed report on Britain’s youth jobs crisis, Alan Milburn, the Tony Blair-era health secretary, said the country was at a “fork in the road” after a surge in the number of young people not in education, employment or training (Neet) to almost a million.

He said ministers had a choice to either carry on down a path of state and market failure or adopt a 10-year plan with an ambition to eradicate long-term youth unemployment.

Publishing a blueprint for change in a highly anticipated review, the report calls on the government to launch a sweeping shake-up of welfare, education, health and employment policy with the aim to ensure every young person has the chance to earn or learn.

If adopted by the government, Britain could halve the number of young people neither earning nor learning, helping about 600,000 more under-25s into education, employment or training each year by 2036, according to the review.

Milburn’s report comes as Britain’s young bear the brunt of rising joblessness as employers freeze their hiring plans amid the fallout from the war in the Middle East. Coupled with the legacies of the Covid pandemic and austerity, and a rise in mental ill-health and as AI upends the jobs market, the UK’s Neet rate has reached 13% – the highest level since 2014. Milburn said that rate could be lowered to 5% by 2036.

However, central to his plan would be a controversial shake-up of Britain’s benefits system to approach the under-25s as wholly separate from the working-age population at large.

Urging the prime minister not to shy away from welfare reform – after Keir Starmer was forced into a humiliating climbdown over a plan to cut disability benefits amid pressure from Labour MPs – Milburn warned the future of the next generation depended on the government making changes.

At a press conference in London on Tuesday to mark the launch of his report, he is expected to say: “It is time for a new settlement that makes work not welfare the future for the next generation.”

But Milburn also warned that crude benefit cuts would not work, arguing that changes would need upfront investment.

Burnham pledged on Sunday to intervene as early as childhood to tackle youth unemployment. Writing in the Guardian, he promised that changes to benefits he was considering in response to Milburn’s review would only be accompanied by a “root and branch rethink” of youth support.

At the heart of Milburn’s blueprint would be the creation of an entirely new welfare system for young people, involving tougher eligibility criteria than for older claimants. In exchange, however, there would be a drastic step-change in support to help young people find work.

This would involve some young people with health conditions and disabilities losing access to current benefits, although they would be able to apply to a local fund for short-term expenses and equipment. Those with the most profound health conditions who would be unable ever to work would still get more generous financial support.

To counterbalance the changes, Milburn said upfront investment to create a new “youth opportunity service” in England should be created to deliver a drastic boost in support for young people.

Accountable to regional mayors and local leaders, the service would combine employment, careers, health and practical support. Young people would receive a personalised participation plan and a named adviser, with access to support guaranteed by two new legal guarantees.

While education, skills and health policies are devolved in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, Milburn will say at the report launch that the underlying principles in his report should also apply.

Analysis in Milburn’s report shows that for every £1 spent on helping young people into work or education, the state spent £25 on youth benefits last year. Under his proposals that figure would be rebalanced to £2 to £1, bringing Britain closer into line with the ratios in other European countries.

The changes will, however, raise fears among some Labour MPs as well as charities and campaigners that slashing youth benefits could result in plunging more young people into poverty, particularly for those who require additional financial support to manage health conditions.

As Milburn prepared to launch his review, backbenchers from the left of the party – including Nadia Whittome, Bell Ribeiro-Addy, John McDonnell, Apsana Begum, Clive Lewis and Richard Burgon – warned in a letter that an entire generation risked becoming disconnected from work and wider society.

“What young people need is not more punishment, but more opportunity,” the signatories wrote, warning that an approach relying on benefit cuts “risks further disenfranchising young people who are already struggling”.

They added: “Placing young people under threat of losing essential financial support does not address the shortage of good-quality jobs, the barriers facing disabled young people, or the lack of meaningful routes into employment.”

In a bid to ease concerns among Labour MPs after last year’s welfare rebellion threatened to rupture the party and arguably began Starmer’s downfall, Milburn held meetings with MPs on Monday alongside the work and pensions secretary, Pat McFadden.

McFadden told backbenchers: “What is proposed is a new settlement for young people. This is not last year’s proposal with better PR. It’s about bringing together the help young people need in one place – treating them as a person not a departmental client.”

Milburn said Britain had faced a Neet problem for 25 years and had never made a plan to end it before. “We are at a fork in the road and half measures will not do. A complete system reset is needed. A lost generation is not inevitable. A work generation is possible.”

Labour has faced fierce criticism from business groups who say its decisions – including tax increases and a rising minimum wage – have added to Britain’s crisis in youth jobs, while the Conservatives and Reform have pushed for a tougher approach on benefits.

Helen Whately, the shadow work and pensions secretary, said Milburn’s review was “too cautious on reversing that damage and too often his answer is the big and expensive hand of the state”.

“Overall, it fails to bring down the welfare bill, proposes billions more in government spending and won’t make the slightest difference for young people stuck on welfare now – because nothing will happen for at least three years,” she said. “Even so, there’s a long road ahead to see if it can get past welfare-loving Labour MPs.”
