Between 2023 and 2025, I went to five funerals and a wedding.

One was for my dad, another for my close school friend’s husband, followed by my mother-in-law, a child’s mother from my role as School Chaplain, and my dearly loved neighbour.

The death of a loved one has changed me, and I am on a mission to support those who are grieving too.

In all these circumstances, I found that talking through the process of grief was far more helpful than keeping it bottled up inside.

As Brits, talking about death doesn’t come naturally to us; however, studies show that complex, unresolved grief can lead to substance dependency, amongst other things.

The relationship between substance misuse and complicated grief

In 2018, I studied a postgraduate certificate in Chaplaincy with young people, and the module that interested me the most was on dying and how people from different cultures respond to death.

Bereavement training with Ataloss.org, York Teaching Hospital and the Celebrant Training School has led me to recently qualify as a Funeral Celebrant, which enables me to be a gift to families when they are hardest hit.

It’s been a busy year as I became recognised as an authorised lay minister last month.

In the spring, a pilot bereavement cafe was set up in the Groves.

Funded by the Guildhall Ward in York, it provided a safe place for people on the grief journey to meet up with others who understood.

The hope is I can encourage members of the local community to talk about their grief journey with others who have been in the same position.

The Bereavement Community still exists, and last summer Lauren performed her poem to over 120 guests at my 50th birthday Charity Ball, which raised £4,410.50 for Papyrus and Menfulness.

I am aware that sometimes people may be afraid to ask about someone who has died for fear of them getting upset.

Normalising that grief and talking it through has been vital in trying to heal.

Sometimes with my sisters and parents we can't talk about it because feelings are strong.

With other people, trying to reach out at the bereavement group – even though I did it years later – was the best thing I could have ever done.

In terms of dealing with this grief, go to the groups.

Funded by St Thomas’s Church, the cafe will continue in the run-up to Christmas and until February 2027.

Drop-in sessions are on the second Friday of the month from 7.30pm to 9.30pm, with light refreshments provided.

Booking is not required.

The next Bereavement Cafe takes place at St Thomas Church Hall, 7.30pm–9.30pm, on Friday, November 13.

The following dates will be on Friday, December 11 and Friday, January 8.
