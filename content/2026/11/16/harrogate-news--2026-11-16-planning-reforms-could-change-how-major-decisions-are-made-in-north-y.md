**New Government rules are changing how planning applications are decided, reducing the ability of elected councillors to call applications before planning committees. The implications for local communities are illustrated by the Harrogate Spring Water expansion, which councillors unanimously refused earlier this year despite planning officers recommending approval.**

Changes to England’s planning system came into force on 31 October 2026, introducing a national scheme governing which planning applications can be considered by elected councillors and which must be decided by planning officers.

The Government says the reforms will make planning decisions more consistent, reduce delays and allow committees to concentrate on applications with the greatest significance.

However, the changes also reduce the circumstances in which local councillors can intervene in individual planning decisions.

Previously, councils had greater freedom to decide which applications should go before planning committees. Ward councillors could request that particular applications were considered by elected members, and some authorities used the number of public objections as a trigger for committee consideration.

Under the new arrangements, the number of objections alone will no longer be sufficient.

Certain categories of application must now be determined by planning officers. These include householder developments, minor commercial applications and smaller residential schemes, alongside specified reserved matters applications and other technical planning decisions.

For other applications, there is a presumption that decisions will also be delegated to officers unless specific criteria for committee consideration are met.

An application can be referred to a committee where it raises an issue of significant economic, social or environmental importance to the local area, or a significant planning matter.

However, referral requires agreement between a nominated senior planning officer and a nominated planning committee member. An individual ward councillor can no longer independently require an application to be considered by a committee.

## Angela Rayner rejects claims councillors are losing planning powers

Angela Rayner has defended the changes, rejecting criticism that the Government is removing decision-making powers from elected councillors.

Speaking to the House of Commons Housing, Communities and Local Government Committee on 11 November, Rayner was challenged by Conservative MP Lewis Cocking over the national scheme of delegation.

Cocking asked why the Government was taking away councillors’ powers to determine planning applications.

Rayner rejected that description, saying: **“We are not.”**

She argued that the reforms would make planning decisions more consistent and objective, with planning officers dealing with applications that meet established criteria, while councillors would retain responsibility for more significant developments.

Rayner said the Government had originally announced plans to recruit 300 additional planning officers but that more than 400 had now been recruited.

She told the committee that larger developments and other significant issues could still be referred to councillors for decisions.

However, Cocking challenged that position, pointing out that the new rules give planning officers greater powers and remove the ability of individual councillors to call in applications.

He also questioned why councils were cancelling planning committee meetings if councillors’ powers had not been reduced.

Rayner said that a small number of councils had failed to adopt the new arrangements by the 31 October deadline. She described their refusal as “regressive”, arguing that they had placed themselves outside the regulations established by Parliament.

She maintained that the purpose of the reforms was to ensure that development took place within a planned framework, with local authorities identifying where housing and infrastructure should be delivered through mandatory Local Plans.

The disagreement highlights the central issue surrounding the reforms. The Government argues that it is creating a more efficient and consistent planning system, while critics believe that reducing the role of elected councillors weakens local accountability.

In North Yorkshire, the Harrogate Spring Water application provides a practical example of why that distinction matters. Councillors unanimously rejected the proposal in April despite planning officers recommending approval. Under the new arrangements, that decision would ordinarily have been delegated to officers.

## Harrogate Spring Water illustrates the difference

The implications of the changes can be seen in the planning application for the expansion of Harrogate Spring Water’s bottling facility on Harlow Moor Road.

The application, reference HGT20/01539/REMMAJ, sought approval for the detailed design, appearance, landscaping, layout and scale of an extension to the existing facility.

Outline planning permission for the principle of the development had been granted in 2017. The subsequent reserved matters application dealt with the details of the proposed extension.

The application came before North Yorkshire Council’s Harrogate and Knaresborough Area Planning Committee on 17 April 2026 following significant public interest.

Planning officers recommended approval, subject to conditions and a Section 106 legal agreement.

Councillors disagreed and unanimously refused the application.

Their decision centred on two issues. They considered the proposed landscaping inadequate to mitigate the loss of existing planting and habitats. They also concluded that the appearance of the extension lacked local distinctiveness and was inappropriate in its setting, close to the Pinewoods and Rotary Wood.

The decision referred to policies NE3, NE4, NE5, NE6 and NE7 of the Harrogate District Local Plan, together with policy HP3.

It was a significant decision because elected councillors rejected the recommendation of their own planning officers following consideration of the concerns surrounding the development.

**Under the new planning arrangements, the same application would ordinarily have been decided by planning officers rather than the planning committee.**

The reason is that the national scheme specifically requires certain reserved matters applications to be determined by officers, subject to limited exceptions relating to particularly large outline permissions.

The large development exception applies to outline permissions involving at least 500 homes or buildings with at least 50,000 square metres of floorspace.

The Harrogate Spring Water proposal involved approximately 4,800 square metres of additional floorspace and did not fall within that exception.

Consequently, the application would normally have been delegated to officers. The level of public interest and the number of objections would not, by themselves, have provided grounds for referral to the committee.

This does not mean that planning permission would automatically have been granted. Officers would still have been required to assess the proposal against planning policies and relevant material considerations.

However, the final decision would ordinarily have rested with officers rather than elected councillors.

The case provides a clear example of how the new rules could change the decision-making process, even where a development attracts considerable local attention.

## What changes for residents?

Residents will retain the right to comment on planning applications and raise objections. Issues such as highway safety, drainage, flooding, access, environmental impact and the effect on neighbouring properties will continue to be considered.

What changes is the opportunity for those concerns to be heard directly by elected councillors at a planning committee.

Even a large number of objections will not automatically result in a public committee hearing. Where an application is eligible for referral, the new criteria and agreement between the nominated officer and councillor must be satisfied.

The reforms also introduce a maximum membership of 13 councillors for planning committees and sub-committees.

The Government’s intention is to make the planning process more efficient, with professional officers handling a greater proportion of decisions and committees concentrating on applications that meet the new referral requirements.

## North Yorkshire prepares its new Local Plan

The changes come as North Yorkshire Council begins work on a new Local Plan covering development across the county up to 2045.

The plan will cover the North Yorkshire Council area, excluding the North York Moors and Yorkshire Dales National Parks, which have separate planning arrangements.

It will establish the framework for future housing, employment, infrastructure and environmental protection, replacing the existing plans inherited from the former district and borough councils.

A scoping consultation opened on 26 October and runs until 7 December 2026.

Residents, businesses and community organisations are being invited to comment on what the plan should cover and the priorities that should influence future development.

Among the issues are housing requirements, the location of new development, employment opportunities, transport, infrastructure, biodiversity, community facilities and the protection of towns and villages.

The council is working towards adopting the new Local Plan by October 2029.

For Harrogate, Knaresborough, Ripon and the surrounding villages, the plan will have a significant influence on where development takes place and what infrastructure is required to support it.

The distinction between the new national planning rules and the Local Plan is important. While the Local Plan will establish the policies against which development is assessed, the national scheme changes who makes decisions on individual applications.

Residents will still be able to influence the preparation of the Local Plan and submit representations on individual planning proposals. However, they will have fewer opportunities to have contentious applications debated by elected councillors.

The Harrogate Spring Water decision demonstrates why that distinction matters. In April, councillors were able to reject a proposal despite the recommendation of planning officers. Under the new arrangements, that decision would ordinarily have been made by officers.

The Government’s reforms are intended to speed up development and remove unnecessary delays. Whether they achieve that without weakening local accountability will be an important question for communities across North Yorkshire.

## Have your say

Residents can submit their views on future development, local priorities and the issues they believe should be addressed in the new plan through the North Yorkshire Council Local Plan consultation.
