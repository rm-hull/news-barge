The UK government has not ruled out joining the European Union’s pilot scheme to deport rejected asylum seekers to Rwanda, years after its own initiative was axed.

The scandal-hit Rwanda plan introduced under the Conservatives was scrapped by Sir Keir Starmer immediately after he became Prime Minister.

It cost Britain £700 million despite only four volunteers being sent to Kigali, then-home secretary Yvette Cooper said, as she branded the policy the “most shocking waste of taxpayer money I have ever seen”.

But five European countries are set to unveil similar plans on Monday following discussions to create so-called “return hubs” with a non-EU country.

No 10 said it will watch the scheme “with interest” as part of efforts to find “innovative approaches” to crack down on illegal migration.

![EU countries could use facilities earmarked for the UK's failed Rwanda deportation scheme](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/08/01KT188MPB8VRAYXYXNXYR14C5.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Asked about the scheme, Prime Minister Andy Burnham’s official spokesperson said: “Obviously, it’s for the EU to make any announcements. I’m not going to get ahead of that.

“More broadly, though, you’ve got our position. The UK, like other European countries, is exploring innovative approaches to tackle illegal migration, including returns hubs, and will continue working with international partners to strengthen returns, remove those with no right to be here and restore control to our borders.”

When asked to rule out the UK joining the plan, he said: “The UK, like other European countries, is exploring innovative approaches, including return hubs to tackle illegal migration.“

“The public wants us to do whatever we can to deal with illegal immigration. We’ve made progress, as you know, on small boat crossings, but we’ve got further to go. So we’re considering our next steps, and we’ll keep those developments under review. We’ll watch that with interest, learning from what went on in the past.”

The EU plan forms part of a broader European push to increase deportations and curb irregular migration, following the approval of the EU's new Returns Regulation, which seeks to speed up deportations and allows member states to establish return hubs outside the bloc.

It differs from Britain's abandoned Rwanda deportation plan in one key respect: it would apply only to migrants who have exhausted all legal avenues to remain in the European Union, rather than to those whose claims are still being processed.

Rwanda has positioned itself as a destination for migrants that Western countries want to remove, despite longstanding concerns about its human rights record.

According to reports, during a September visit to Kigali, two European Parliament lawmakers were shown facilities earmarked for Britain's abandoned scheme that could potentially house migrants transferred from EU countries, including a hotel.

Leaders from the five countries have repeatedly said their plans would comply with EU and international law but critics have questioned whether migrants' rights could be violated if they are transferred to a country they have no connection to.

"What recourse do people have if their rights are violated and who is accountable?" said Céline Mias, EU director at the Danish Refugee Council. "If responsibility rests with the European member state, how would that work in practice?"

The Council of Europe, a pan-European human rights body, said in July that return hubs posed "considerable human rights risks."

European officials backing the hubs say the policy's main purpose is deterrence rather than large-scale removals. They argue that the prospect of paying smugglers thousands of euros for a dangerous journey to Europe, only to be sent to a third country, could dissuade some migrants from making the trip.

![Critics have accused Rwanda's government of supporting the M23 rebel group, which rights groups say has committed killings, rapes and other abuses](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/08/2252070369..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Critics, however, say there is little evidence that tougher enforcement measures and deportation schemes reduce migration over the long term.

“Enforcement changes how people travel, not whether they travel,” said Roberto Forin of the Mixed Migration Centre, a research institute that collects data along migration routes.

“Some pause or change route, and smugglers adapt and raise their prices.”

Rwanda President Paul Kagame has won praise from Western and regional leaders for helping end the country’s 1994 genocide and rebuilding the nation into a destination for investment and aid.

Critics accuse his government of suppressing dissent and supporting the M23 rebel group in neighbouring Democratic Republic of Congo.

Human rights groups say that the M23 has committed killings, rapes and other abuses in that country's conflict.
