## Nobel Prize-winning economist Philippe Aghion urged the EU to encourage new players in the AI market, ease restrictions on state aid and strengthen protections for workers facing technological disruption.

Artificial intelligence (AI) could drive economic growth in Europe, provided the EU’s competition rules allow new companies to enter the market, Nobel Prize-winning economist Philippe Aghion said on Monday.

His remarks come amid growing concerns over the dominance of a handful of US companies in the AI market and the technology’s impact on employment, with fears of widespread job losses in the coming years.

“With AI, more than ever, there is creative destruction because if you look at the rate of job destruction and job creation, it's being accelerated by the AI revolution – and that's where it's so important to let new firms come in,” Aghion said during an event in Brussels.

He added: “At the same time, it is important to have institutions like the flexicurity system that smooths out transition from old jobs to new jobs, because it helps manage the critical disruption.”

Aghion pointed to Denmark’s labour market model, which combines flexibility in hiring and firing with a generous safety net for unemployed workers.

A recent European Commission report shows that employment among highly skilled workers could grow by 20% by 2040 thanks to AI, while job losses among low-skilled workers could range from 20% to 60% across Europe.

Aghion shared the 2025 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences for his work on “creative destruction”, the process by which new innovations continually displace older technologies, businesses and jobs.

## **Growth and job creation**

Aghion said EU welfare systems are better equipped than the US system to manage AI-related employment changes: “We are ahead of the US when it comes to which labour market to accommodate AI revolution.”

However, he also said the EU should draw inspiration from the US in terms of industrial policy to ensure AI delivers growth and jobs.

“In the US, they have both competition policy and industrial policy. They have the DARPA - Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency - which is a competition-friendly way to do industrial policy.”

DARPA is a US agency responsible for developing emerging defence technologies. “In Europe so far we don't have the equivalent of the DARPA and I would very well imagine one to develop AI computing power and that would also encourage new firms to enter [the market].”

He added that the EU’s approach to competition policy was “precluding” the sector from receiving state aid, “which was a very anti-industrial policy.”

Aghion argued that EU competition rules should encourage new entrants and promote data sharing across the entire AI value chain, including cloud computing.

The dominance of US technology giants in the AI sector has already attracted scrutiny from EU regulators.

In December 2025, the European Commission launched an investigation into Google over concerns that the company could be distorting competition by imposing unfair terms on publishers and content creators or granting itself privileged access to their content.

The Commission, which enforces EU competition law, also opened an antitrust investigation into Meta to determine whether its policy restricting AI providers’ access to WhatsApp breached EU rules.
