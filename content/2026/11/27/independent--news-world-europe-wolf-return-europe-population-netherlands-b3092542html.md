The return of wolves to the Netherlands, roughly two centuries after being hunted to extinction, was initially celebrated as a fairy-tale triumph for conservation.

Yet the initial joy after a pair of wolves first bred in 2019 has rapidly turned sour. The resurgence of the apex predator has resulted in fatal attacks on livestock and troubling encounters with humans, inflaming existing societal tensions.

Wolf numbers are expanding right across Europe, though the population within the Netherlands remains modest — estimated at fewer than 150 animals divided into around 15 packs — compared to other European Union nations where they have been established for much longer.

Even so, the densely populated nation of 18 million people, home to an influential, multi-billion-dollar agricultural sector, forms the backdrop to an increasingly contentious debate over how successfully a protected animal can coexist with humans and livestock.

While established packs tend to stay in their territories, lone wolves drift right across the country. Tracking data from the Wageningen University and Research institute revealed one wolf covered more than 2,500 kilometres (1,550 miles) in three months, twice swimming across a major river and traversing several motorways.

The Dutch government announced this week that it intends to permit the culling of certain wolves, citing the need to reduce the risk of attacks on livestock and people.

### A farmer keeps most sheep in his barn

When Robertjan Feijen first began raising sheep in the rural northeast of the Netherlands about a decade ago, wild wolves were a distant memory. His sheep grazed on other farmers’ fields and pastures that are submerged during the winter to create speed skating ovals.

Things changed fast.

Since wolves returned to the Netherlands, the farmer has lost around 250-300 sheep in more than 50 wolf attacks.

The worst feeling for Feijen is if he has helped deliver a lamb and then sees that animal maimed or, worse still, “you see it lying dead, completely ripped open and then looking around you, it’s a battlefield with dead animals in ditches,” he said. “It’s indescribable.”

Researchers at Wageningen reported wolves killed more than 3,300 animals last year in the Netherlands, almost all of them sheep, and farmers were paid nearly 1.5 million euros ($1.6 million) for their livestock losses.

Farmers can also get subsidies for protective measures like electric fences. But Feijen said the government support doesn’t come close to covering his losses. He feels forced now to keep many of his sheep in his barn.

Photographer Liesbeth van Asselt has lived near the forests and heathlands that border the central town of Epe all of her 62 years and loves to walk the paths that crisscross shady groves of pine and oak.

But such outings are no longer a source of carefree relaxation.

Even though reports of wolves attacking dogs are rare, when Van Asselt walks with her dog, Raico, she wears a red whistle around her neck to scare off wolves that sometimes walk down the sandy roads near her garden.

“I think we can have an amount of wolves in the Netherlands, but not in between our houses, walking in our streets and in our gardens,” Van Asselt said.

![The sheep of farmer Robertjan Feijen are kept inside in the summer after several attacks by wolves in Oldeberkoop, Netherlands, Tuesday, June 16, 2026. (AP Photo/Peter Dejong)](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/10/Netherlands_Wolves_22392.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Long seen as a bastion of tolerance, the Netherlands has been roiled in recent years by protesting farmers angry at government plans to rein in nitrogen emissions; by black-clad demonstrators seeking to prevent towns opening asylum-seeker centers; and by people demanding action on climate change.

The debate about wolves opened up one more fault line. In December, when animal welfare activists held a symbolic funeral procession for the only Dutch wolf to have been shot in an officially sanctioned hunt, they were threatened by villagers. Activists said one opponent drove his car at them. Police intervened but made no arrests.

The wolf in question, nicknamed Bram by some locals, had menaced joggers and cyclists and was blamed for biting a child in a forest close to the university city of Utrecht.

Maarten Jacobs, a polarization expert at Wageningen, said wolves are being exploited by agitators seeking to deepen rifts in society.

“So there was existing polarization, and polarization needs fuel to keep the conflict going," he said. “And then the wolf arrived, and that’s perfect new fuel.”

![Demonstrators protest the killing of wolves, outside the provincial government building in Utrecht, Netherlands, Wednesday, July 8, 2026. (AP Photo/Peter Dejong)](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/10/Netherlands_Wolves__5_85.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

At De Hoge Veluwe National Park, a tourist destination in the central Netherlands, the return of the wolf is having a potentially devastating effect on the protected landscape of inland drifting sands.

Head of operations at the park, Jakob Leidekker, points to countless Scots pine saplings sticking out of a sandy plain. Wild sheep introduced from Cyprus and Corsica called mouflons used to graze on the young shoots before they could mature.

“All the mouflons are gone now because they were killed by wolves,” Leidekker said. “And what you see now is that all these pines are getting up."

The park is home to the Kröller-Müller Museum and its jaw-dropping collection of Vincent van Gogh paintings and other artworks. Some 570,000 people visited the park last year, many of them riding through forests and sand dunes on a fleet of white bicycles. Visitors are now handed a pamphlet explaining what to do if they encounter a wolf.

At least one wolf attacked a jogger and approached visitors. The park sought permission to use paintball guns to scare it off. By the time it was granted, the wolf was gone.

![A wolf hunts at De Hoge Veluwe National Park, Netherlands, Monday, June 15, 2026. (AP Photo/Peter Dejong)](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/10/Netherlands_Wolves_48458.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

While farmers and others lament the return of the wolf, conservation and animal welfare groups have welcomed it.

Nature organization Natuurmonumenten said it considers the animal “an enrichment to nature, but at the same time attaches great importance to cooperation between parties so that we can coexist with this animal with minimal conflict.”

While wolves are a threat to De Hoge Veluwe's protected landscape, experts say they generally have a positive impact on the environment by keeping large animals like deer on the move, ensuring they don’t overgraze areas.

“There’s a very nice saying in Eastern Europe,” said Hugh Jansman, an animal ecologist at Wageningen. “Where the wolf lives, the forest thrives.”

![](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/27/06/2227020836..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Wolves are thriving in Europe. The European Commission estimated the population across the continent almost doubled in 10 years to some 20,300 animals in 2023 with breeding packs in 23 countries.

But spurred by anger in communities where wolves prey on livestock, the EU last year relaxed the status of wolves from “strictly protected” to “protected” to give “more flexibility to member states to manage the populations of wolves.”

The Dutch move to allow for some culling will have to be hammered out with provincial governments. But State Secretary for Agriculture, Fisheries and Nature Silvio Erkens called it "an important step because there are now too many serious incidents with wolves.”

Some flout the protections anyway.

A report from the EcoJust consultancy estimates at least 41 wolves have been illegally killed in the Netherlands since their return.

Despite the competition for space in the Netherlands, Jansman says wolves have clearly demonstrated that they have a place in the Dutch landscape.

“I think the biggest challenge is, as humans, are we willing to share this landscape with a predator, the wolf?”
