In Mozambique and in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), a new body called the Global Centre for Peacebuilding and Business is working to prevent the expansion of mining for minerals and energy sources spilling over into conflicts over land and resources.

It is a platform to calm tensions by ensuring companies hear any concerns of local leaders – an initiative the Church of England established that can operate wherever there is a danger that business investments spark violence and worse.

Yet the stark truth is that, just as the Church embraces this task, governments – including our own in the UK – are pulling back from conflict resolution work as international development budgets are cut dramatically. This should alarm us all.

Few doubt the UK’s 2025 Strategic Defence Review was necessary in today’s age of disorder. I do not deny the importance of military deterrence, nor the debt we owe to our Armed Forces who risk their lives to protect us.

However, where is the equivalent study of the other side of the security equation: to assess whether the UK has the policies, capabilities and partnerships it needs to prevent conflicts? What one might call a Strategic Peacebuilding Review.

Britain has a proud record of peacebuilding, but as we finance our rearmament by salami-slicing the aid budget, we are retreating from tackling the root causes of conflict. It is a self-defeating false economy.

Much of this work has been done through the Integrated Security Fund, which brings together defence, diplomacy, and development experts – but it has lost almost half of its aid funding. Up to a dozen programmes to confront extremist threats in fragile places including Sudan, Somalia, Tunisia and Jordan have been dropped.

This retreat is happening at the worst possible moment, as more violent conflicts rage across the globe than at any time since the Second World War. Conflicts which have forced 120 million people from their homes and left 200 million in humanitarian need.

Those figures come from a report by international charities, called *Peace Works*, which also highlighted the cost to the world economy from failing to prevent this growing disorder: approximately £16 trillion, or a staggering 10 per cent of global GDP.

And yet we know it is so much cheaper to prevent or resolve conflicts than to pay the bills for their ruinous consequences. Every £1 invested in peacebuilding averts spending of up to £103 if conflict breaks out, research by the International Monetary Fund found – leaving aside the human misery avoided.

I am singing from the same hymn sheet as some of our most senior former military leaders in making this argument. Field Marshal Lord Richards, Major General James Cowan and James Cowan and General Sir Nick Parker have all warned “Britain’s security does not begin at our border”, but by confronting threats at source.

They know, from their military experience, that weak governments, institutional collapse and the conflicts that result are the recruiting ground for terrorists, and that

conflicts rarely emerge without warning – so why wait until violence has erupted before mobilising international efforts to prevent it?

At a recent event to mark a decade since the landmark accord to end the civil war in Colombia, the former president and Nobel Peace Prize winner Juan Manuel Santos highlighted the essential role of the international community in that success, through the painstaking work of mediation and supporting civil society, including that of the churches? It is a lesson we must heed.

There is already evidence that the withdrawal of US aid from conflict zones has led to more violence by further weakening governments. It will be a tragedy if the UK repeats that mistake.

Some argue that, if you want peace, you must prepare for war – but I fear the world is moving towards a new and far more dangerous mantra, namely that if you want peace, you must first wage war.

This will invite a new global arms race, more wars of conquest and the collapse of co-operation to confront shared threats. Only peacebuilding, which is not only a humanitarian cause but also an essential step to national security and economic resilience, can rescue us from that.

*The Right Revd Dr Guli Francis-Dehqani is the Lord Bishop of Chelmsford*
