## The hidden York cinema brought back to life for 'magical' Christmas screenings

February 1998 -The queue at Tempest Anderson Hall for the film Mrs Brown showing at the York City Screen. *(Image: NQ)*

*York's historic Tempest Anderson Hall to host Christmas films this December – within a magical setting, reports MAXINE GORDON*

As thousands of people prepare to watch Christmas classics inside Yorkshire Museum's Tempest Anderson Hall this December, many may be surprised to learn they are stepping into a venue with its own long cinematic history.

Long before streaming services, multiplexes and even City Screen on Coney Street, York's film lovers were gathering in the hall tucked away beside the Yorkshire Museum to watch everything from art-house productions to foreign-language films.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21764953.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Impression of how the Enchanted Christmas Cinema coming to York might look. Image supplied

Today, the grand auditorium is rarely seen by the public, but for decades it played an important role in York's cultural life.

Built in 1912, the hall was designed as a lecture theatre attached to the Yorkshire Museum and was named after York-born surgeon, explorer and scientist Tempest Anderson.

With its raked seating, impressive acoustics and theatre-style layout, it quickly became one of York's leading venues for lectures, concerts and public events. More than a century later, it still has capacity for around 300 people.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21766911.jpg?type=mds-article-620)January 2002 - Save City Campaign meeting at Tempest Anderson Hall

But for many York residents, the hall's most nostalgic chapter began in the late 20th century when it became home to one of the city's most beloved cinema ventures.

Before moving to its current riverside home, City Screen began life as a repertory cinema operating from Tempest Anderson Hall. Film enthusiasts flocked to Museum Gardens to see independent, international and specialist films that were rarely shown in mainstream cinemas.

At a time when York's cinema scene was dominated by commercial releases, the programme offered something different. Audiences could discover foreign-language dramas, acclaimed documentaries and critically praised films that might otherwise never have reached the city.

![Tempest Anderson Hall is part of Yorkshire Museum ](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21766913.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Tempest Anderson Hall is part of Yorkshire Museum. Image: Newsquest

The venture proved hugely popular and helped establish a loyal audience for independent cinema in York.

Press readers remember City Screen at Tempest Anderson Hall fondly.

Posting in our Facebook nostalgia group, Why We Love York - Memories, Raymond Baggaley recalled: "I saw the Abel Gance 1927 Napoleon around 1988/89. Live piano accompaniment. Magic."

Steven Clarke wrote: "We saw Get Carter but l cannot remember the year!"

While Helen Ball remembered: "We went to see Gosford Park and a film about Chuck Berry."

The success at the venue ultimately led to a major step forward in 2000, when City Screen joined the Picturehouse group and relocated to its purpose-built venue in the former Yorkshire Herald printing works on Coney Street, where it remains today.

![February 1998 -The queue at Tempest Anderson Hall for the film Mrs Brown showing at the York City Screen.](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21766910.jpg?type=mds-article-620)February 1998 -The queue at Tempest Anderson Hall for the film Mrs Brown showing at the York City Screen.

Today, few visitors passing through Museum Gardens realise the hidden auditorium once helped lay the foundations for one of Britain's best-known independent cinemas.

The building itself remains a fascinating part of York's heritage.

Attached to the Yorkshire Museum, it continues to host conferences, concerts, lectures, films and community events, keeping alive the purpose for which it was created more than 110 years ago.

This Christmas, the hall is returning to its cinematic roots as the Enchanted Christmas Cinema transforms the space into a woodland theatre complete with real trees and snowfall before each screening. Around 4,000 tickets have already been sold for the event.

For many visitors, it will simply be a festive night out.

But for those who remember the days when film fans regularly descended on Tempest Anderson Hall to catch the latest independent release, it will also serve as a reminder of a hidden York venue that has entertained generations.

**Who was Tempest Anderson?**

![Tempest Anderson rests during an expedition to Iceland, in August 1890, where he studied volcanic landscapes](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/10343292.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Tempest Anderson rests during an expedition to Iceland, in August 1890, where he studied volcanic landscapes

Tempest Anderson was a prominent figure in Victorian York. According to the Yorkshire Museum, he was a pioneer of town planning; Sheriff of York in 1894 and was the first man in the city to have a telephone.

Born in York in 1846, he studied medicine at University College, London, and returned to York to work in his father’s practice, specialising in ophthalmic medicine. He also worked in the County Hospital in York.

He was a keen mountaineer with an interest in glaciers which developed into a lifelong fascination with volcanoes.

By the turn of the 20th century, he had visited and photographed most of the European volcanoes as well as those of Iceland and travelled to the USA.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21766912.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Queen and Duke of Edinburgh visit to the York Mystery Plays 1957 - the Royal couple meet members of the cast in costume at the Tempest Anderson Hall in Museum Gardens

Back in York, he gave lectures using ‘magic lantern slides’ – an early form of slide viewer. His pictures gave people the chance to see landscapes they had never seen before. He also took many images of the people who lived in the countries where he stayed.

He died in 1913 on the Red Sea on the way home from his last trip and is buried at Suez. He never married and left half his estate to the Yorkshire Philosophical Society, the founders of the Yorkshire Museum.

Today the Yorkshire Museum has 5,000 pictures taken by him, of around 3,500 separate images (many are copies had made for different lecture sets.)
