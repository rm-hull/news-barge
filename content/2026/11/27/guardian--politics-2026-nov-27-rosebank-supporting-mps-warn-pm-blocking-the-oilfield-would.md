Dozens of Labour MPs in favour of Rosebank are putting pressure on the prime minister not to block the North Sea oilfield, warning that doing so could spell “lights out for Labour in Scotland”.

As many as 80 pro-drilling MPs are considering writing their own letter to Andy Burnham after nearly 100 of their colleagues called on the prime minister not to allow the project to proceed. Scottish Labour leader Michael Marra has also reiterated his support for Rosebank, cautioning that “working-class people in vocational jobs” were threatened by the decline of the North Sea basin.

Burnham now faces having to choose between two warring factions within his own party in what is likely to be the most sensitive decision he has taken so far as prime minister.

Anti-Rosebank MPs warn about the impact on the climate. They argue that the estimated additional 254m tonnes of carbon dioxide which Rosebank will produce over its lifetime are not worth the extra £500m in tax receipts and 500 jobs it will generate.

But those in favour of the project worry that a decision not to go ahead will damage Labour’s reputation with industry and Scottish voters in general.

One MP told the Guardian: “This is very much part of Scotland’s economy and supply chain, but crucially Scotland’s identity. It has become such a totemic issue that Labour will not be forgiven in Scotland if they don’t do this.”

The person added: “Andy needs to approve Rosebank or it’s lights out for Labour in Scotland.”

Marra said he recognised that the energy secretary, Miatta Fahnbulleh, had a “different” decision compared to the Jackdaw gasfield, but backed the project because of the jobs and economic activity it would support.

“The decline of the North Sea may be seen as something to celebrate by those who want us to meet our climate obligations.

“I understand that. But I ask those who call for a just transition to understand the anxiety in Aberdeen and the north-east of Scotland over a political establishment which often feels to them that it considers anything but clean energy a dirty word.

“This is people’s lives and livelihoods we are talking about. Not the millionaire shareholders of a billion-dollar multinational, but working-class people in vocational jobs,” the Scottish Labour leader said.

Several MPs have sent individual letters to Burnham urging him to allow the project to go ahead, but many are now in talks about whether to coordinate their efforts to counteract a letter against Rosebank signed by nearly 100 of their collegaues.

One Labour MP said: “The whips and the government know we have the support, we’ve made that clear behind the scenes. Conversations [over a joint letter] are ongoing over the weekend.”

Another added: “Reindustrialisation and energy jobs for young people are huge issues in Scotland, so the feeling is they need to approve it to show we’re serious about it.”

Henry Tufnell, Labour MP for mid and south Pembrokeshire said: “Failing to approve Rosebank would cost industrial communities jobs and investment, weaken our balance of payments and deny the exchequer vital tax revenues.”

Torcuil Crichton, the Labour MP for Na h-Eileanan an Iar (Western Isles), said: “We need the workforce, the supply chain and the expertise of the North Sea to build our renewable future.

“There are now more jobs in renewables than in the carbon sector, but we can’t abandon the workforce like other energy communities were abandoned by the Tories.”

He told BBC Radio Scotland: “It makes far more sense from a domestic security point of view and an environmental point of view to source those carbon fuels within our own boundaries. If we don’t, we’re importing a huge carbon footprint from other places.

“So there’s no point outsourcing our environmental conscience.”

Burnham has enjoyed early success in office by uniting his previously fractious parliamentary party and winning back voters who had defected to other leftwing parties. The Rosebank decision, however, will test his ability to keep his fragile coalitions of MPs and voters together.

While Labour has enjoyed a poll bounce since Burnham became prime minister, the improvement has been more modest in Scotland and the party remains behind the Scottish National party.

In recent months, the SNP’s stance on oil and gas drilling in the North Sea has shifted. Once strong opponents of new projects under Nicola Sturgeon, the party abandoned its “presumption against” new North Sea projects in October. John Swinney has also criticised the UK government’s energy independence bill, which aims to ban new North Sea drilling.

Polling by Survation last month found that 64% of people in Scotland say that the UK government should approve Rosebank, with 16% against.

“In Scotland, the public supports approval for Rosebank in every age group excepted 18-to-24s. But even that is very close: there’s just a 1% net difference. According to our polling, allowing the project to go ahead would make Scottish voters more likely to vote Labour. Any route to a majority for Burnham hinges on Scottish seats,” said PJ Stock, a research and data analyst with Survation.

Emma Hardy, the environment minister, acknowledged on Friday morning that her party was split over the issue.

She told Sky News: “I know from chatting to some of my friends and colleagues, some Labour MPs in Scotland feel differently to some Labour MPs elsewhere. But I know Andy [Burnham], as our prime minister, will be listening to everybody.”

While Labour went into the last election promising not to issue any more North Sea oil and gas licences, they could give the environmental approval to this project – the largest undeveloped field in UK waters – because it already has a licence.

Despite that, Fahnbulleh is understood to be leaning against granting approval on the grounds that it will emit an estimated 1m tonnes of carbon dioxide by 2042, but contribute relatively little to UK energy supplies.

While the decision technically lies with Fahnbulleh, most MPs believe that if Burnham signals either his approval or opposition to the scheme, she will be left with little option but to back the prime minister.

So far Burnham has said he wants to be “pragmatic” about decisions in the North Sea and won plaudits from Donald Trump after Fahnbulleh approved the smaller Jackdaw field.

Downing Street said on Friday: “The decision on Rosebank will be taken by the energy secretary through a quasi-judicial process. She will consider the relevant evidence, including environmental assessments and representations received during the consultation process, before reaching a decision.”

If the prime minister does push Fahnbulleh to approve the scheme, he could be faced with resignations from government, with more than one ministerial aide having indicated they are willing to quit over the issue.
