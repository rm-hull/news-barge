# Leeds company discriminated against Christian man over LGBT beliefs

## A company discriminated against a Christian social worker by not offering him a job because of his religious beliefs on homosexuality, the Court of Appeal has ruled.

Touchstone Leeds offered Felix Ngole a role as a hospital discharge mental health support worker in 2022, but later retracted the offer after it found news articles about a previous court case he was involved in.

That case related to Mr Ngole’s successful legal challenge against the University of Sheffield over its decision to expel him from his MA course in social work for comments he made on social media in 2015, including stating that “homosexuality is a sin”.

Touchstone told Mr Ngole his views went against its own, but offered to reconsider its position if he could give assurances his beliefs would not compromise his role.

The company then declined to offer Mr Ngole the role after a second interview, stating in a letter it felt his views could pose a “significant risk” to its users, who could find the comments “upsetting and offensive”.

In 2024, an employment tribunal (ET) ruled Touchstone directly discriminated against Mr Ngole by rescinding the initial job offer, but rejected further claims that requiring him to attend a second interview and not offering him the job were also discriminatory.

In February, the Employment Appeal Tribunal (EAT) judge found the ET’s decision about the second interview and the decision not to offer him the job was wrong, but sent the case back to the ET for reconsideration.

Mr Ngole challenged that decision at the Court of Appeal in September, with his barristers telling three senior judges that the EAT should not have sent the case back for reconsideration as it had found Mr Ngole had been directly discriminated against.

Touchstone also challenged the EAT’s decision, arguing the initial ET ruling should be restored or that the case should be sent back for reconsideration.

By a majority ruling on Friday, the Court of Appeal upheld Mr Ngole’s challenge and dismissed Touchstone’s, while also refusing the company’s bid to take the case to the Supreme Court.

Following the decision, Mr Ngole, who was supported by the Christian Legal Centre, told the Press Association it is an “important victory” for his family and “Christians across the country”.

He said he has “never discriminated against anyone”, adding: “There was no evidence whatsoever that I would treat any service user without dignity, compassion, and respect.”

He continued: “Disagreement is not discrimination, and holding biblical beliefs does not make someone a danger to others.

“I’ve never been a danger to anybody. Quite the opposite.”

Asked if he intends to seek compensation from Touchstone in light of the ruling, Mr Ngole said that “will be a conversation for another day”.

Bruno Quintavalle, for Mr Ngole, told the Court of Appeal earlier this year that making Mr Ngole attend a second interview meant Touchstone “imposed upon him requirements that were not imposed on anyone else”.

He said this represented a “very straightforward matter of direct discrimination”, despite the job description containing “simply nothing” related to LGBT issues and Mr Ngole never being the subject of a complaint.

Katherine Apps KC, for Touchstone, said in written submissions that it declined to offer Mr Ngole the job after the second interview because of a “lack of enthusiasm” for what the company “wished to see in the role, including actively promoting LGBTQI+ specific service and specifically tailoring services to the needs of LGBTQI+ service users”.

She also said Mr Ngole showed “resistance” to attending LGBT+ awareness training and that there was a “risk of complaints or claims” against the company by service users.

In a ruling, Lady Justice Elisabeth Laing said: “Mr Ngole’s ‘public manifestation of his beliefs’ happened long before he applied for the job.

“I cannot accept that that past public manifestation… which was lawful when made and which was no more than an accurate description of those lawful beliefs, could become ‘objectionable’ because of the nature of the job for which he applied.”

Agreeing with the decision, Lord Justice Miles said the “only tenable conclusion” was that Touchstone was “significantly influenced” by media reporting about Mr Ngole’s views.

But he said while Mr Ngole’s views “may not be generally accepted and indeed may offend many people”, they were expressed in an “objectively unobjectionable manner”.

Lord Justice Bean said he would have dismissed Mr Ngole’s appeal, stating Touchstone’s decision was taken “not out of concern about reputational damage, but out of concern for potentially devastating consequences for mentally unwell service users”.

He continued that he believed the company’s refusal to reinstate the job offer was “properly taken” based on Mr Ngole’s beliefs, which were “objectively inappropriate” in the context of the role he had applied for.
