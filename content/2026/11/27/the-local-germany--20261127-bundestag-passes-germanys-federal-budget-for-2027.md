After months of struggle, the black-red coalition has completed Germany's federal budget for the coming year, and on Friday the Bundestag voted to approve it.

Overall, the federal government is set to have more money available for the coming year that it ever has in a single year before, with 123 billion of that financed through debt.

The budget foresees expenditure totalling 556 billion euros in 2027, marking the second-highest borrowing level Germany has seen since World War II, just shy of the Covid-era budget in 2021.

A huge chunk of that expenditure is aimed squarely at defence, with spending expected to rise to 140 billion euros – reaching a level not seen since the end of the Cold War.

Additionally, more than a third of the budget is to flow into Germany's labour and social budget, which funds the government's allocations for pension insurance, for example.

Chancellor Friedrich Merz, whose coalition pushed through the spending plan, argued this week that Germany must bolster its military, which he said had been neglected for too long.

"Germany is more capable of defending itself now than it has been at any time in recent decades," Merz said, though he contended more needs to be done to deter a hostile Russia and defend NATO allies.

Ultimately the budget was passed with 317 votes in favour versus 260 against, with no abstentions.

To plug a budget hole worth around 34 billion euros, the 2027 budget creates a new consumption tax on sugar-sweetened drinks and also increases the tax rate applied to alcohol spirits. Tax rates for mixed alcoholic drinks are also to be adjusted.

Additionally the budget text confirms the fact that electricity taxes are to be reduced to the European minimum.

Still to be decided are a series of expected reforms aimed at Germany's income tax code. The Union (CDU/CSU) and Social Democrat (SPD) parties are reportedly still not in agreement about all details of the tax reform plan.

Many of the cuts originally planned by the government, including to the housing allowance, child supplement (*Kindersofortzuschlag*), and other social benefits were withdrawn by parliamentarians during the negotiations.

Regarding the 2027 budget overall, Chancellor Friedrich Merz admitted that the process to finalise it "certainly does not deserve a beauty prize".

Meanwhile, Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil has already hinted that after the amount of spending planned in 2027, the budget for 2028 will come with greater challenges and austerity measures.

Opposition parties in the Bundestag have already criticised the budget.

Left Party leader Ines Schwerdtner said the government constantly talks about emergencies and war, but largely ignores the "millions" of people in Germany for whom money gets tight every month.

Far-right Alternative for Germany co-leader Tino Chrupalla suggested too much money was being spent on defence for Ukraine, and said that Merz would "go down in the history books as the chancellor of Germany's insolvency".
