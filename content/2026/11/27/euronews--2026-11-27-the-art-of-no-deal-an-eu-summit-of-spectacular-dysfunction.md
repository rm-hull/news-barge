## Good morning. Leo Cendrowicz here with your Friday newsletter. They rush in, elbow each other out of the way, squabble over the price and emerge with precious little to show for it. But enough about Black Friday - we also have an angry EU summit to report on…

**A summit to forget.** Fourteen hours of talks, a boycott, a French presidential ultimatum, a bruised European Council president and virtually nothing to show for it. Yesterday’s special EU summit, convened specifically to break the deadlock over the bloc’s next seven-year budget, ended shortly after midnight in spectacular disarray. Leaders abandoned plans to continue negotiations today and went home, seemingly no closer to a deal than when they arrived.

As our Jorge Liboreiro and Luca Bertuzzi report, the summit was practically doomed from the outset. European Council President **António Costa’s**€1.78 trillion compromise, shaving €204 billion off the Commission’s original proposal, achieved the remarkable feat of antagonising almost everyone.** The great walkout.** The seven so-called frugals – Germany, the Netherlands, Sweden, Denmark, Austria, Finland and now Belgium – want deeper cuts and spending redirected towards defence, innovation and competitiveness. The 17 Friends of Cohesion want to protect traditional spending on poorer regions and agriculture.

Costa tried to shake things up by breaking leaders into smaller, geographically mixed groups. Instead, the frugals decided they were being unfairly targeted and boycotted the format. Costa carried on without them.

“We had a lot of physical movement, with all the meetings, but we did not really see any movement on content,” one diplomat told us.

**The science of disagreement.** With Italy’s**Giorgia Meloni** describing a deal as “light years away” and Hungary’s**Péter Magyar** putting the chances of an agreement at “pretty much zero”, you could be forgiven for thinking we were covering a conference of particle physicists rather than a gathering of European leaders.

Meanwhile, **Emmanuel Macron** decided what the summit really needed was more Emmanuel Macron. Breaking away from the negotiations to hold his own press conference, the French president cast himself as the great European dealmaker. “I'm trying to bring everyone closer together,” he declared.

His method? Threatening to shut down negotiations after 31 December. The implicit warning: his colleagues might find themselves negotiating with **Marine Le Pen** instead.

German Chancellor **Friedrich Merz**, unimpressed, pointed out that there were 27 bridge-builders, not just one. The Franco-German engine is not exactly purring.** The clock is ticking.**Leaders will try again on 17 December, but several seem remarkably relaxed about the timetable. “For me, there's no deadline,” said Dutch Prime Minister** Rob Jetten**. Yet the new budget must be operational by January 2028, Parliament still has to approve it, and next year’s electoral cycle could make the political arithmetic considerably uglier.** Remember Ukraine?**The other supposedly urgent item on yesterday’s agenda – a €45 billion loan to keep Ukraine afloat next year – was largely swallowed up by the budget brawl. Hungary, Slovakia and the Czech Republic are still refusing to participate. That, too, has been kicked into December.** Productive dysfunction?** There is an old Brussels theory that leaders need to exhaust themselves, trade insults and demonstrate to domestic audiences that they have fought tooth and nail before they can make concessions.

“You need to have a bad European Council to have a good one,” one diplomat told us. “It was a lot of political theatre.”

By that logic, December’s summit ought to be an absolute triumph.

## **In other newsFrom Madrid to Frankfurt.** There was one concrete result from the summit yesterday:**Pablo Hernández de Cos** was confirmed as the next president of the European Central Bank, becoming the first Spaniard to land the job. But perhaps his most remarkable achievement is to have earned the trust of both Spain’s conservatives and socialists – no mean feat in the country’s fiercely polarised political climate.

The 55-year-old economist is the consummate technocrat. Appointed governor of the Bank of Spain by **Mariano Rajoy’s** conservative government in 2018, he was subsequently championed for the ECB presidency by**Pedro Sánchez’s** Socialist administration.

Currently heading the Bank for International Settlements, Hernández de Cos has spent much of his career navigating the intricacies of monetary policy, banking supervision and financial stability. He is regarded as an orthodox economist, broadly aligned with the ECB’s hawks, although he advocated interest rate cuts in 2024. Expect a steady hand rather than fireworks in Frankfurt.

**Spain’s right turn?** Conspicuously absent from the EU summit,**Pedro Sánchez** is fighting for his political survival as Spaniards go to the polls on Sunday. He joined three other party leaders last night in a televised debate. His widely anticipated defeat would rob the EU of its highest-profile centre-left leader and further tilt the bloc’s political balance to the right. Polls suggest the conservative Popular Party will need the Vox party to govern, bringing the far right into national government for the first time since Spain’s democratic transition.**The price of solidarity.** How can the EU afford another €45 billion loan to Ukraine? Our Euronews Verify team crunches the numbers: the money would be borrowed on financial markets, backed by the EU budget rather than national balance sheets. Bruegel economist**Jacob Funk Kirkegaard** reckons the sum, equivalent to just 0.2% of EU GDP, is modest compared with the potential cost of defending Europe should Russia prevail in Ukraine.**Gulf tensions.** Two Italian technicians working for German defence giant Rheinmetall were killed in a strike on a facility in Kuwait, prompting Rome and Kuwait City to blame Iran. Tehran denied responsibility, describing the incident as suspicious. European Commission President**Ursula von der Leyen** called for increased pressure on Iran, warning that Gulf security was a concern for everyone. The deaths come amid growing fears of renewed escalation in the conflict.**Bardella under fire.** French far-right leader**Jordan Bardella** has denied fresh allegations of antisemitism after Le Monde and Mediapart published further messages he allegedly wrote as a teenager, including conspiracy theories about Jewish control of banks. Bardella denounced a media “persecution” campaign. The revelations are awkward for the National Rally, which has spent years trying to shed its antisemitic past and court Jewish voters.**Buyer beware.** EU Justice Commissioner**Michael McGrath** has warned Black Friday shoppers against bogus discounts and pressure-selling tactics. Checks last year found that 30% of online shops surveyed provided misleading discount information. McGrath’s proposed Digital Fairness Act would crack down on hidden fees, fake countdown timers and subscription traps. “A genuine deal should never depend on misleading the consumer,” he said.**Brexit break-up bill.** Britain’s new food and drink deal with the EU comes with an exit penalty: either side walking away within 10 years would have to cover the costs of restoring border checks. The clause looks like an insurance policy against another reversal in British politics. Reform UK’s**Nigel Farage** has already vowed to repeal Andy Burnham’s Brexit reset, while Conservative leader**Kemi Badenoch** has also opposed the deal.
