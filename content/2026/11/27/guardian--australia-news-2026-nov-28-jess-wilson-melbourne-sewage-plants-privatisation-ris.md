Experts have warned the Victorian Liberals’ plan to lease Melbourne’s sewage treatment plants is a “smoke and mirrors” way to pay off debt that risks compromising the state’s water resources and crucial wetlands for nearly a century.

On Thursday – two days out from the election – the opposition leader, Jess Wilson, announced plans for a 90-year lease of the Eastern and Western treatment plants, which process the vast majority of the city’s sewage and are run by Melbourne Water.

The lease is estimated to be worth $9.2bn, with Wilson saying there would be “no change” to water bills or the Essential Services Commission’s regulation of prices across the sector.

Experts are concerned the move could lead to worse services for Victorians, and compromise vital wetlands in Melbourne’s west.

Dr Erin O’Donnell, a water law expert at the University of Melbourne law school, said given the prices would still be regulated, it would be difficult for private companies to extract value from the services, and the money gained from the lease would amount to only about 5% of the debt held by the state.

“I wouldn’t even classify it as a sugar hit – it’s smoke and mirrors,” O’Donnell said.

“This will not make a significant dent in the debt that Victoria holds, and we are compromising the future of our water resources 90 years into the future.”

O’Donnell said Victoria already required corporations to pay significant dividends to the state government, with strict controls over pricing. It means there is no profit to be realised for private owners while also delivering the same quality of services, she said.

“Victoria has some of the lowest water charges of anywhere in Australia and the idea that there is unrealised profit that could be safely stripped out without compromising those services is a total furphy – I can’t say it any clearer.”

O’Donnell and Prof Stuart Khan, a water quality expert and head of the school of civil engineering at the University of Sydney, both pointed to the UK privatisation of water services in the 1980s under Margaret Thatcher as an example of how it can all go wrong.

The privatisation has been described as an “unmitigated disaster”, with hopes the Burnham Labour government will renationalise the utilities following years of declining services, and rivers polluted by private operators.

Khan said the UK’s services were privatised without guardrails around pricing or minimum standards of services.

“Practically every single river in England now has sewage contamination in it,” he said. “That’s the sort of situation that we need to make sure that we avoid because obviously private players are only going to be there if there’s a profit to be made.”

Victoria’s Labor state minister for the environment, Jaclyn Symes, pointed to the UK system and said it resulted in bills going up by 40% and “left wildlife swimming in a toilet”.

Khan said the NSW government’s privatisation of Sydney’s desalination plant could be a model that would work.

“It’s price regulated. It has requirements around managing water quality and they put drinking water straight into Sydney’s distribution system,” he said, adding it also has requirements from NSW Health and the state’s environmental protection agency.

Biodiversity Council member and waterbird and wetland expert Prof Richard Kingsford warned the privatisation could have disastrous consequences for Melbourne’s environment, including the wetland in the Western treatment plant at Werribee.

In 1983, the site was listed under the Ramsar convention as an internationally significant wetland, and more than 300 species of bird – including many threatened species such as the orange bellied-parrot – have been recorded there.

“It’s absolutely one of the more important wetland systems,” Kingsford said.

“I think it’s becoming increasingly important because we’ve had more wetlands drying up, less flooding and, particularly in dry times, it provides dependable habitat.”

Kingsford said privatisation “would likely drive different ways of management that might actually not have any consideration for the environmental value of the system”.

O’Donnell said it was “extraordinarily disingenous” for the Liberal party to announce the policy less than two days before the election when many Victorians had already voted.

“I think there is a real concern there, in terms of the openness around their policies [and] this will have significant implications for things like a swimmable Birrarung [Yarra River],” she said.

A Victorian Liberal party spokesperson said the criticism was “another desperate scare campaign by Labor.”

“We are not selling Melbourne Water. It was Labor that had a plan to privatise Melbourne Water. What we are proposing is a lease. When you lease something you still own it.

“The proceeds will be used to reduce Victoria’s debt and interest bill.”

The proposal has already been thrown into doubt after One Nation’s Victorian leader, Warren Pickering, vowed to block it should the party hold the balance of power after the election.

The Greens leader, Ellen Sandell, also promised to “fight Jess Wilson’s privatisation agenda tooth and nail should her Coalition form government”.
