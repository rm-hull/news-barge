The unique psychological challenges of elite level snooker

**It is perhaps one of the most visceral and deeply human interviews that any sportsperson has ever given.**

When Mark Allen, snooker's world number 11, spoke live on TNT Sports, external in October, he said he was in a "bad place", that he didn't see a future in the game, and that he was "just trying to survive".

Allen had just been beaten at the Northern Ireland Open. It was his first match since the World Championship, when in May he was one black ball away from a place in the final.

He has not spoken publicly since. In a post on social media in early November, Allen said the future is "looking brighter",, external although this week he explained he was having a "very tough" time, external and was "definitely stuck in a major low right now".

While Allen has not publicly discussed the reasons for his struggles, and if it is related to the sport, he had previously said "there is more to life than just a career".

And this would be far from the first time that poor mental health has affected a snooker player.

Right from the 1970s and the sport's first television boom, some of its great characters have spoken openly, and regularly, about addiction, isolation and snooker's unique psychological challenges.

So what is it about the sport that makes it so mentally difficult? And what is being done to help?

## Why is snooker so 'mentally destructive'?

In his emotional interview, Allen said he had spent 10 weeks in a mental health clinic and discharged himself to play in Belfast, though he planned to continue his treatment.

Other players have also previously spoken candidly about their own struggles, including multiple world champions Ronnie O'Sullivan and Mark Selby.

Selby considered retirement after a first-round defeat at the 2024 World Championship, while in 2025 O'Sullivan said many players "suffer in silence".

Snooker is, perhaps alongside golf, one of the most intensely individual sports, where players spend long hours alone in dimly lit practice rooms or travelling on the road, and where entire livelihoods can come down to tiny misjudgements.

"At least in golf you have your own ball. You don't have to worry about anyone else," 1997 world champion Ken Doherty told BBC Sport.

"In snooker, when you're sitting in your chair and your opponent is at the table, you can do nothing. Only sit and bear it.

"That is the toughest part about our sport and mentally that is quite difficult for people to comprehend."

Dr Paul Gaffney is a psychologist who has worked with Allen for the past four seasons. Snooker players, he says, can feel like they are inside a "colosseum".

"There are people looking down at you, and you can absolutely thrive, or be beaten in front of the world stage."

Gary Wilson, the world number 28, has also spoken openly about the bouts of depression he has suffered during his 22-year career.

"It's a very lonely sport," the 41-year-old told BBC Sport.

"You're practising in a club and you could be in there on your own for six, seven, eight hours a day in a dark room, with just a snooker light on.

"It can get unhealthy mentally in itself, but it's what you've got to do. You've got to dedicate yourself to it.

"I think it is a more mentally destructive sport, potentially, than a lot of other individual sports."

When asked how he felt watching Allen's interview, Wilson said: "We can all relate.

"There are a lot of lads who are a lot lower down on the tour struggling.

"Every couple of years, this coming out and nothing being done about it, isn't really good enough."

## What is being done to help - and is it enough?

Since 2021, snooker's governing body, the World Professional Billiards and Snooker Association (WPBSA), has put mental health provisions in place for players in the form of a partnership with Sporting Chance, a charity founded by former Arsenal and England footballer Tony Adams.

In the five years since the partnership was formed, Sporting Chance has provided more than 180 sessions of counselling for a wide range of issues.

Players experiencing mental health issues are encouraged to ring a confidential 24/7 helpline, and Sporting Chance will then arrange free treatment with a qualified therapist. This can take place over six to 12 talking sessions.

But some players believe that support could go further. In an interview in January 2025, O'Sullivan and Selby said they would like to see mental health professionals operate on-site at tournaments.

"I think players would have a lot more inclination to use somebody physically there at tournaments. I don't know how feasible that is," said Wilson.

There are benefits and drawbacks to the Sporting Chance service. Players receive a bespoke counsellor, rather than one who may speak to every player, but they are required to reach out themselves for this through a helpline. Doherty, who sits on the WPBSA players' board, believes it is the best solution.

"Players don't necessarily want to go and speak to someone at a tournament," Doherty said.

"We thought about having someone on hand, but then we thought, we don't think anyone would use it.

"This is a free service. Nobody has to know. Nobody will know on the circuit. It's just you and the person you're talking to.

"I know Ronnie [O'Sullivan] and Mark [Selby] spoke about that, but they obviously didn't know that service was there for them, that it's free, and that it's available for the whole tour."

WPBSA chairman Jason Ferguson, a former professional player himself, said: "The highs are very high and the lows are very low.

"If there's anything we can work on, it's continually getting the message out to other players to say those services are there."

In 2021, Wilson said he felt "totally gone" when the depression he explained he was suffering with came to a head in a match against John Higgins.

Wilson missed a pot, and while still down on his shot, thrashed the cue ball into a neighbouring red,, external sending the frame into chaos. He was fined £250 and received a formal warning.

But Wilson - known as the Tyneside Terror - believes there could be a more liberal approach to such displays of emotion in this most gentlemanly of sports.

"You're expected to keep your emotions completely in check at all times," he said.

"Now that's just not sport. When you look at any other sport that you play… you've got to be able to vent."

In response, Ferguson said that while snooker's authorities and fans love to see such passion, fundamentally it is a sport of "great etiquette".

Allen is next due to play in the UK Championship - a tournament he won in 2022 - which starts on Saturday. He has been drawn against qualifier Yuan Sijun but has not spoken publicly about whether he will play.

For Dr Gaffney, watching Allen's interview at Belfast's Waterfront venue was "painful". It made him feel "sadness, but also admiration".

"The brilliant thing about what he's done - his courage, his integrity, his class in the last few weeks - is there will be people watching snooker who wouldn't have been watching snooker before, because Mark Allen is playing," said Dr Gaffney, "and I think that says everything about the man."
