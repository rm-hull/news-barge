This past summer, the Greensboro Police Department in North Carolina conducted an usual survey on its X account: Should the official page be “more serious” or “unhinged”? Of the several hundred respondents, 88 percent chose the latter.

It was a ringing endorsement of an approach the department had long embraced, one which blends the vernacular of a terminally online millennial with the snark of a corporate brand jonesing for engagement.

“Some of y'all are like ‘free my boy’ expecting us to read it and be like ‘aww ok,’” read one post from April. When commenters complained, the account made punchlines out of them.

Other posts threatened lawbreakers with fictitious punishments, including Taylor Swift songs played on a loop. Some portrayed police as indifferent to those in need. Many simply offered random musings, such as: “Getting emails with like, a lot of words, is the worst.”

The irreverence has drawn an audience. Posts have garnered millions of views, and the department now has more than 300,000 followers across X and Facebook — roughly equivalent to the entire population of Greensboro.

![The Greensboro Police Department in North Carolina has gone viral on social media over its snarky and ‘unhinged’ tone, but not everyone is laughing](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/2/01/745235805_1355141670124112_3140049687613727357_n.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

But the social media tactic has divided viewers. Fans see it as a bit of harmless fun, while critics argue that the flippant tone is inappropriate.

Located 90 miles from Charlotte, and once considered the denim capital of the world, Greensboro is hardly alone is adopting a playful voice to vie for online attention.

“Humor is certainly an approach many other police forces have used in their social media posts,” Alyce McGovern, a criminology professor at the University of New South Wales, told *The Independent*. However it sometimes “backfires,” she added.

That warning became reality for Greensboro this past spring.

## **‘Unhinged’ posts**

Scrolling through the city’s police department feed on X or Facebook can create a sense of whiplash. Sober updates on drug busts, felony assaults and fatal shootings sit alongside its self-styled “unhinged” commentary.

“Beginning today, anyone arrested will be required to use Microsoft Teams instead of their one phone call,” one post states. Another declared: “If you did your own taxes and there's more than five digits in your refund, we’ll just go ahead and pick you up now.”

Elsewhere, the account invites people to report their own illegal activities, offering police assistance to drug buyers who didn’t get their money’s worth.

![One common them is threatening offenders with unusual punishments, such as requiring them to ‘use Microsoft Teams instead of their one phone call’](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/2/33/Screenshot-2026-11-18-at-9-40-53-PM.png?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)
![The account’s social media manager say they are not a ‘sworn police officer’](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/2/01/Screenshot-2026-11-18-at-9-41-19-PM.png?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“If your cocaine dealer shorted you and didn’t supply you with the full amount of product, contact us…to file an official complaint,” read a post earlier this month.

It amassed 5,500 replies, 350,000 likes and 18 million views on X alone.

The account also repeatedly pleads for an uneventful evening, casting police as more interested in staying in than fighting crime.

“We want to catch up on 1000-pound sisters tonight, so please take your fights and fender benders to a neighboring county,” the department wrote in June.

A January post struck a similar note.

“For the record, if you crash tonight doing donuts in a parking lot, that's between you and your maker,” the account wrote. “We’re cold and worn out.”

![Greensboro, located about 90 miles from Charlotte, has a population of roughly 300,000 people](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/18/17/489791188_983718767266406_2346226185831359813_n.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Days later, that reluctance gave way to a warning: if officers were forced into a “foot chase in this snow storm,” their “four-legged fur rockets will gladly take you down in the snow to ‘play.’” It was an apparent reference to deploying police dogs against fleeing suspects.

Not all of the department’s social media output is exactly work-related. Much of it consists of pop culture musings, touching on everything from Mariah Carey to Grand Theft Auto VI. Some posts contain just a single word, such as: “Hey,” “Night,” or, more confusingly, “Trains.”

The Greensboro Police Department did not respond to requests for comment from *The Independent*.

## **Divided community**

The posts have found an enthusiastic audience online, drawing thousands of replies— including from North Carolina Governor Josh Stein, who jokingly offered his thoughts on a speeding incident.

Many commenters welcome the laughs the department provides throughout the day. “I always stop my scrolling when I see your posts. Always a win,” wrote one satisfied commenter.

“It’s hilarious,” a Reddit user on a Greensboro page told *The Independent*. “You’re some boomer who doesn’t get it… Humor is funny, get over it.”

Some supporters say the schtick serves a practical purpose: it gets helpful public safety messages to people who might otherwise tune it out.

Not everyone is laughing though.

“As a former journalist and someone who spends a lot of time in Greensboro, I find it highly inappropriate,” another Reddit user told *The Independent*. “It’s simply toxic.”

Some question whether the account is a good use of police resources. “Find something useful to do like going after robbers, rapists, and murderers, instead of using our tax dollars to troll on twitter,” one X user wrote to Greensboro police.

Others have taken issue with posts inviting people to self-incriminate. “I find your attempts to undermine the 5th amendment reprehensible,” one commenter chimed in.

In the crowded contest for online attention, police departments across the U.S. and beyond have tried their hand at comedy. Their social media accounts serve up snark in cities large and small, from Bangor, Maine to Los Angeles, California.

McGovern examined the trend in her 2021 study titled “Memetic copaganda: Understanding the humorous turn in police image work.” She said her research found that police employ comedy online to “maintain visibility and drive public engagement” and to “divert attention from criticisms about their work.”

Greensboro police faced criticism last year after footage of a May traffic stop showed an officer pointing a gun into a vehicle and shouting at its occupants, according to *The News & Record*. The mayor later said the officer’s actions were appropriate and opposed firing him.

![Several of the Greensboro Police Department’s posts suggest tension between the account’s social media manager and department leadership](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/2/07/Screenshot-2026-11-18-at-6-02-46-PM.png?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Xiaochen Hu, a criminal justice professor at Fayetteville State University, identified similar benefits and risks in her own 2021 study.

“These posts usually draw public attention and receive many ‘likes’ and ‘comments,’” she told *The Independent*. “Our study also found that humor should not be overused. Overusing humor may cause the public to question if the police are serious in their work of protecting and serving.”

Police are far from the only public servants embracing the trend. A number of elected officials and government accounts have traded carefully worded statements for what some might describe as s\*\*\* posting.

New Jersey’s official X account has built a reputation for trolling, as has California Governor Gavin Newsom’s press office. Then there is President Donald Trump, whose seemingly inexhaustible supply of all-caps insults and AI slop puts him in a class of his own.

## **Saying sorry**

Several of the Greensboro Police Department’s posts suggest a conflict between the account’s social media manager — who has stated online they are not a “sworn police officer” — and department leadership.

In a February X post, the account wrote that “higher-ups” had instructed them to “cool it” with the “snarky comments and replies.” Their response was irreverent: a GIF of a tiny violin being played.

That tension came to a head this past spring.

“We’re going to try something brand new on our page!” the account posted on both X and Facebook on April 11. “Happy Attention Seeking Saturday!”

The post highlighted a man who had criticized the department online, and it included his name and photo, according to WFMY. “Thank you for your comments,” the account wrote. “And we are so glad that the internet is working again in your basement. Here’s the attention you wanted!”

![On April 12, the Greensboro Police Department issued an apology for a post it said had fallen short of community standards](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/2/20/Screenshot-2026-11-18-at-9-42-45-PM.png?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

After triggering backlash, the department deleted the post the following day and apologized, saying it had fallen short of community standards and was being reviewed by its standards division.

“On behalf of the department, we extend our apologies and commit to ensuring our social media pages remain a welcoming place for all,” the department wrote.

The post included a note from the social media manager: “I personally would like to apologize as that post was from me. I write most of these and take full responsibility, good or bad, of the outcome.”

Of the thousands of comments, most appeared to be in support of the unnamed social media manager.

Then, a few weeks later, the account’s signature style returned.

“The good news: The social team is back online,” the department posted April 27. “The bad news: There's now a policy named after us!”
