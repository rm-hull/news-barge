Erin Patterson has applied to the high court seeking leave to appeal after a failed bid last month to overturn her triple murder conviction.

Patterson, 52, was found guilty last year of the murders of her estranged husband’s parents, Don and Gail Patterson, and his aunt Heather Wilkinson, after she hosted them for lunch.

She was also convicted of the attempted murder of Heather’s husband, Ian Wilkinson, who survived eating beef wellington laced with death cap mushrooms after spending weeks in hospital.

On Friday afternoon, her lawyers filed her application to try to overturn her convictions and life sentence in the high court, a source confirmed to Guardian Australia.

Victoria’s court of appeal last month ruled Patterson’s appeal against her convictions should be dismissed. But it granted an appeal by the director of public prosecutions against Patterson’s sentence, finding that her original sentence of life imprisonment with a non-parole period of 33 years was too lenient. She was resentenced to life without parole.

“This offending was extraordinarily cold and calculated and stands to be denounced in the strongest terms,” court of appeal justices Lesley Taylor, Stephen McLeish and Peter Kidd found in their judgment on 30 October.

“Necessarily, we have given the question whether it is inappropriate to fix a non-parole period anxious consideration, in light of the gravity of such a conclusion and the different view to which the experienced sentencing judge came.

“But the features of the offending … lead each of us to conclude that this is a case of such shocking criminality that it is inappropriate to fix a non-parole period.”

Patterson had 28 days from that judgment to apply for special leave to appeal to the high court, a period that ended on Friday.

Her written application for leave to appeal is expected to be determined by all seven high court justices.

If they cannot make a determination based on the written application, the justices can refer the application to a hearing for oral argument.

Applications are typically referred to the justices the week before a sitting period and considered that Monday, before decisions are published the following Thursday.

The high court’s last sitting period of the year runs from 30 November to 11 December, meaning Patterson could know the fate of her application within the next fortnight.

If not, the court does not sit again until 1 February. The court would not comment on the application.

Patterson’s lawyers had previously argued in the court of appeal that her convictions should be overturned on seven grounds, all but two of which were dismissed without leave to appeal granted.

The two grounds for which Patterson was granted leave to appeal, but were also dismissed, related to the sequestration of the jury during their deliberations, and the telephone cell tower and iNaturalist evidence presented during her trial.

The court heard that the jury in the case stayed at the same motel as the police informant and DPP staff while they were deciding on their verdicts in the trial.

Patterson argued that this represented “a fundamental irregularity … that has fatally undermined the integrity of the verdicts and requires the quashing of the convictions and an order for a re-trial so that justice can not only be done but be seen to be done”.

The second ground was that cell tower evidence, which suggested Patterson could have been in Loch and Outtrim after death cap mushroom sightings were reported in the towns on the citizen science website iNaturalist, should not have been adduced in her trial as it was either irrelevant, or its relevance was “outweighed by its unfair prejudice”.
