## Exhausted by weeks of street protests, French police officers are demanding better pay, more resources and greater protection. Meanwhile, students and trade unions are determined to maintain their mobilisation, despite what they deem as a strategy of repression aimed at stifling the movement.

Wrapped up against the November cold, several dozen men and women stand outside the police station in Paris's 12th arrondissement, blowing into their hands to keep warm. At a signal from their union representatives, the blue and white flags of the Alliance police union are raised in front of the cameras.

Legally barred from striking, these police officers were protesting during their break, as part of a nationwide mobilisation called by their main unions Alliance, Un1té and UNSA-Police.

After weeks of successive demonstrations, police representatives call the government to urgently address longstanding grievances.

## **Police officers at breaking point**

“We're demanding that our risk allowance be raised to 35%. It's currently at 28.5% and has been since 2019. It's non-negotiable.” Yannick Landreau, Paris regional representative of the Alliance police union, told Euronews.

Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez, has offered to gradually raise the allowance to 33.5% of their basic salary by 2029.

Too late, and too little, say the unions, demanding immediate action

“More and more of our colleagues are being injured,” Landreau said. « In just the first seven days of the protests in Paris, we recorded 50 instances of injured colleagues per day. The reality now is that mortar rounds are being fired directly at law enforcement » said Landreau.

Intervention units, he said, still lack fire-resistant uniforms, while officers drafted in from local police stations to reinforce crowd-control operations do not always have adequate protective equipment.

Citing a toll of more than 2,600 injured officers, Landreau also pointed to the cancellation of leave and rest periods.

“My colleagues are exhausted and they're hurt in their flesh,” he said. “At some point, this will have to stop. Because we don't know how much longer we'll be able to ensure people's safety.”

The extent of the discontent became apparent last week when some 1,200 members of France's CRS riot police reportedly called in sick on a single day of protests, according to an internal police count.

Officers have complained of shifts lasting up to 15 hours and prolonged periods away from their families.

## **A decade of successive crises**

The current unrest has compounded grievances that have been building for years.

From the 2015 terrorist attacks and the prolonged Yellow Vest protests of 2018 and 2019 to successive waves of social unrest, French police forces have been repeatedly mobilised on a large scale.

“Never any respite. But we've never given up,” said Landreau.

France relies on approximately 150,000 national police officers and 100,000 gendarmes, whose duties range from everyday policing and maintaining public order to criminal investigations and counterterrorism

Newly qualified police officers earn around €2,160 net a month, rising to approximately €2,340 in the Paris region, according to the National Police's official pay scale.

## **Discontent extends beyond frontline policing.**

« We signed an agreement in 2022, and it hasn't even been fully implemented,” Georges Knecht, secretary general of SNIPAT, a union representing administrative, technical and scientific personnel at France's Interior Ministry, told Euronews.

“Some staff are now earning below the minimum wage and need a compensatory allowance just to reach it. This cannot go on.”

According to Knecht, administrative, technical and scientific personnel account for around 15% of police staff in France, compared with 25 to 30% in most European police forces.

He pointed in particular to the shortage of forensic police personnel, numbering just 3,300 nationwide, despite their increasingly important role in criminal investigations.

“We've moved from a culture of confession to a culture of evidence,” he said. “From minor burglaries to terrorist attacks, they're the ones on the front line.”

Knecht also warned that the lack of professional status for forensic personnel was prompting staff to seek transfers to other government departments.

“We feel we're being put off, that they're playing for time until the next elections.”

## **France's controversial approach to policing protests**

While police officers are demanding greater protection, the methods used to police demonstrations have come under renewed scrutiny.

The use of rubber-ball launchers and explosive crowd-control grenades in France has long drawn criticism from human rights organisations, particularly since the Yellow Vest protests, during which a number of demonstrators suffered life-changing injuries.

The latest protests have reignited the debate over the proportionality of police responses and the right to demonstrate, further fuelled by Parliament's approval last Friday of controversial legislation presuming the lawful use of firearms by police officers and gendarmes unless proven otherwise.

Denounced by critics as a “licence to kill”, by opposition MPs, the legislation has been challenged before France's Constitutional Council.

In an opinion issued on 5 November, the human rights office of the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE) also warned that the measure could undermine the effectiveness of judicial investigations, victims' access to justice and the protection of the right to life, urging France to reconsider its adoption.

## **Students vow to keep protesting, accusing government of repression**

Barely an hour after police officers had ended their rally on thursday, students and trade unionists started marching from Bastille towards Nation in eastern Paris, vowing to continue their mobilisation, and chanting slogans denouncing police violence.

“The government is trying to wear down the movement. It's closing high schools and universities,” Mathis Aversenq, a member of the student coordination **Interfac Île-de-France**, and of the far left NPA-Révolutionnaires party told Euronews, citing the deployment of gendarmes to monitor high school students in Nancy and the use of tear gas at a high school in Nantes.

“They're hoping the mobilisation will end through repression alone. But the movement is continuing, with general assemblies bringing together hundreds of high school students.”

Coppélia Moreau, also member of the Interfac mobilisation committee, and president of the UNEF student union Paris Nanterre University branch slammed what she described as an unprecedented police presence in universities, the heavy armament of police officers and police violence during demonstrations.

“Why is the government doing this? To evade its own responsibilities, which means responding to our demands,” she said.

Students' demands include increased university capacity, the recruitment of teaching staff, improved educational facilities and learning conditions, higher student grants and an end to selective university admissions.

In late October, the government announced a series of concessions, including €4 billion in low-interest loans to regional authorities to renovate school buildings and €1 billion for university infrastructure by 2029.

It has since reportedly increased the number of additional places promised in higher education from 15,000 to 20,000, pledged to replace the controversial Parcoursup university admissions platform by 2028 and announced that student grants would be adjusted in line with inflation.

But student organisations want the immediate scrapping of Parcoursup and Mon Master, the national admissions platform for master's degree programmes, and a much more substantial increase in higher education admission capacities.

“We know the money is there to create university places and renovate high schools,” said Aversenq. “But the government is throwing crumbs in our faces along with its tear gas.”

As long as we don’t get a response to our demands, namely, the immediate end of the Parcoursup system and the creation of 300,000 additional places in higher education, we will continue our mobilisation. »

## **Seeking to broaden the movement**

With university exams approaching and turnout at demonstrations declining, the ability of student organisations to sustain their mobilisation is being put to the test.

But student activists rejects claims that the movement is running out of steam.

“Despite all the measures taken by the government to cause the movement to lose steam, including orders banning demonstrations, gatherings, general assemblies, and meetings of high school students in front of their schools, there has been no return to normal. » said Coppelia Moreau.

Inter-university assemblies are continuing to coordinate actions, while student activists are seeking to broaden their movement by linking their demands to those of workers and trade unions.

“We want funding. Not for war, but for schools, high schools and universities. And we want higher wages, because we cannot accept workers being paid €1,400 a month, which isn't even enough to live on. So, we will continue the mobilisation, and we will organise ourselves to amplify it. »

The next major test of the movement's momentum will come on Tuesday, 1 December, with another day of nationwide strikes and demonstrations.

“We're calling on the whole of society to join us because we know that together, we can force this government to back down and even give in.” said Aversenq.

Social discontent shows little sign of subsiding at a time when the government is struggling to reduce France's budget deficit. Reconciling fiscal constraints with mounting social demands is becoming an increasingly difficult political balancing act as France heads into the election year.
