Proposed legal changes being debated in Italy could redefine hunters as wildlife managers while expanding the range of targetable animals. The plans have drawn sharp concern from conservationists, who caution that the policy might result in extensive culling and endanger protected wildlife.

Supported by Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni’s ruling conservative coalition, the draft law represents the most significant update to the nation's hunting regulations since 1992. It comes after sustained pressure from agricultural and field sports lobbies in the lead-up to next year’s general election.

Under the draft proposals, regional councils would gain powers to extend hunting seasons and permit shooting across wider areas. Formerly protected birds, such as wild geese and pigeons, would become permissible targets, alongside new provisions to manage wolf numbers.

"It is an indispensable law," said Francesco Bruzzone, a lawmaker from the League group, part of Italy's ⁠governing coalition, who heads the party's wildlife and hunting department.

"Wildlife populations have changed, ​and ⁠their impact creates problems for many people," he added.

Farming groups, a key part of Meloni's support, say hunting needs to be expanded to limit crop damage caused by growing wildlife populations, particularly of wild boar.

There ⁠were an estimated 1.5 million wild boars in Italy in 2021, according to the national environmental agency ISPRA. The cost of ​agricultural damage ⁠caused by boars averaged more than €17 million ($18.6 million) ‌a year between 2015 and 2021, agency data shows.

Animal rights group LAV says wild boar numbers have risen in recent decades for several reasons, including crossbreeding with more fertile Eastern European animals.

The Senate has already approved the bill, which describes hunting as a ‌national tradition. The lower house began debating it this week, though no date ‌has been announced for a vote.

Environmentalists staged a protest in central Rome on Thursday, waving red flags and banners featuring animal silhouettes.

"The overwhelming majority of Italians are opposed to this brutal, archaic practice, which should now be consigned to history," Luciano Di Tizio, the Italian head of conservation group WWF, said.

According ⁠to a survey by pollster Piepoli last year, 39% of Italians support abolishing hunting or sharply reducing it, while 32% believe it should be restricted to genuine wildlife-control measures.

Italy has around 430,000 registered hunters, according to data gathered by the country's regions, fewer than neighbouring France, which has around 900,000. The highest concentrations are in the northern Lombardy region and in central Tuscany.

Hunting groups say their members can help manage wildlife by preserving the land where they operate.

The Italian Hunting Federation (FIDC) said its members plant crops for game, creating habitats that also benefit birds and insects.

"If someone is willing to invest their own time and resources to pursue their passion ‌while also helping to manage the environment, why should they not be allowed to do so?" FIDC president Massimo ​Buconi said.

Lipu, Italy's main bird protection group, runs a wildlife rescue centre in Rome near Villa ‌Borghese park. Some of the birds it treats have suffered ⁠severe gunshot injuries and will never fly again.

"Italy's wildlife is already under pressure from the climate and biodiversity crisis. ⁠Opening up hunting would effectively allow daily slaughter," Lipu president Alessandro Polinori said.

The group says it has documented injuries to peregrine falcons, kites and kestrels, and has ‌also treated eagles, hobbies and sparrowhawks. wild boar"Killing animals is never the preferred solution," Polinori said.
