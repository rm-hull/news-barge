Black Friday is well-associated by now with discounts and deals, but a month or so ago few would have expected such cut-price offerings to have extended to the stock market.

It seems to be the case though, after a rough month for tech and AI-associated companies in particular, whose share prices have taken a hammering to the extent they could be splashed across shop windows in bright neon cardboard cutouts.

Nvidia, a third off! Google, 15 per cent extra free! Anthropic, save 25 per cent now!

But, just as with buying an electrical appliance, a year-long subscription or a bunch of workout gear we definitely won’t be using two months from now, it’s important with stocks and shares to identify the genuine discounts from the sneaky price traps.

What’s actual value, what’s mis-priced...and what has had a long-overdue re-rating?

## Supermarkets and stock markets

Black Friday is a big deal, of course, for retailers, small businesses and plenty of gift-buyers trying to pick up bargains ahead of Christmas.

Some data for this year from VoucherCodes suggests more than £3.5m will be spent on goods every single minute on 27 November by Britons - that’s £3.41bn over the course of the day, mostly online.

Sounds a lot.

But rewind the tape to 23 November and Jensen Huang’s reappointment as Nvidia CEO and the chipmaker company gained more than $900bn (over £700bn) in a single day, in market capitalisation value.

Stocks move fast both up and down, which might have potential investors who have been warily watching on the sidelines wondering if this is the time to get involved.

Amish Patel, head of equity research at Raymond James, says it’s less about individual stocks bouncing back when trying to assess a change of momentum.

“Investors head into Black Friday with plenty of stocks already marked down. The question is whether they’re bargains, or about to get cheaper still,” he said.

“Wednesday felt like a market trying to work out whether it had fallen far enough. Fundamentals don’t change that quickly, sentiment and positioning do.

“The interesting bit was beneath the surface. Around 80 per cent of S&P 500 stocks actually rose. If that continues, I’d find it much more encouraging than simply seeing the big technology stocks bounce.

“It’s too early to say the sell-off is over, but some very good businesses are considerably cheaper than they were. We’re starting to ask where the selling has created opportunities.”

## Are Britons investing more?

The opportunity is there, then - plus there’s a wider national push to get people investing. That’s much more focused (and rightly so) on a long-term approach, regardless of market ups and downs.

But is it working?

The government’s Savvy the Squirrel-led adverts launched in April, but it appears to have had limited direct impact so far. The campaign’s Instagram account has amassed just 930 followers so far and doesn’t appear to have posted anything in the past six weeks.

Meanwhile, research from Moneybox shows barely one person in eight (12 per cent) has ever heard of Savvy, highlighting how much further the government will need to go to show they are backing the movement.

Only 40 per cent of respondents in that survey were confident about investing, with the usual candidates proving why: fear of losing money, not having spare money and not knowing enough about the subject being the stumbling blocks for most.

Despite that, numbers are on the up - just sporadically, unevenly and clearly with a lot more work to do from all involved.

Holly Mackay, of Boring Money, reports that almost a quarter (24 per cent) of all investors in the UK have been investing for three years or fewer - so a big clutch of new people joining the scene.

A couple of things may help - firstly, the chancellor rowing back on plans to essentially force people to invest by way of cutting the cash ISA allowance. Investing should be something to aspire to get involved with because of positive outcomes, not something people are herded towards if they don’t want to pay more tax on savings.

![Chancellor John Healey and Prime Minister Andy Burnham](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/21/00/5d6442f0f7bd0441128237ea29a3614aY29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzk1MzA0MDI0-2.86547416.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Another investing platform, IG, has launched a proposal which would see the UK government match the first £1,000 invested by UK adults who had never previously opened a stocks and shares ISA.

This type of carrot - rather than stick - approach is generally considered more likely to get people at least *thinking* about investing for the first time, compared to simply funnelling them that and hoping they do it of their own accord.

Further research by IG suggests barely more than one in ten (13 per cent) believe the government is doing enough to encourage more people to invest.

And automation is another one.

Moneybox are asking the government to extend “auto-enrolment beyond pensions to saving and investing”, which Brian Byrnes, director of personal finance, says would help “remove friction and build long-term habits” - just as it has done for millions saving for retirement.

## Is now the right time to invest?

So markets have been falling, they might go back up or they could keep dropping. In short, nobody knows when the absolute perfect time to start putting money into stocks will be - but if you are new to investing, hear this: that’s always the case.

It’s why so many experts recommend drip-feeding money into your ISA (which gives tax-free gains on share growth and dividends) and why they urge to keep consistently doing it regardless of whether the wider market is up or down.

As for what’s next? Chris Beauchamp, chief market analyst at trading platform IG, says history can give a couple of different lessons.

“It pays not to try and stand in the way of US markets around Thanksgiving. The week of the feast usually delivers a positive return, and so it has done once again. Normally this would set up markets nicely for December and the rally into Christmas, but this year things look a bit different,” he said.

“While some have clearly been using recent losses to go bargain hunting, they should be wary: 2000 and 2007 also had strong Thanksgiving weeks. It makes sense not to read too much into the holiday trading period - next week will be the real test for this bounce.”

With individual stocks, what goes down doesn’t always have to go back up. But zoom out on a long-term price chart of an all-world fund, for example, and you’ll see the reason why Brits are so strongly urged to invest: the direction of travel is often upwards.

*When investing, your capital is at risk and you may get back less than invested. Past performance doesn’t guarantee future results.*
