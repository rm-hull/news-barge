## Austria´s Green Tech Valley Cluster hosts 300 companies — including 20 global tech leaders — shaping the innovative and circular technologies of the future. They are developing solutions in sectors like hydropower, solar thermal, wind, recycling, batteries or electro-mobility — all made in Europe.

In southern Austria lies the nerve centre of a world-leading green technology provider.

Here and across 280 locations over 80 countries, 30,000 employees are developing new solutions for renewable energies — especially hydropower, but also in the pulp and paper, metal and energy storage sectors.

The company claims to be a key global actor in the green transition, helping industries worldwide to enhance efficiency, reduce waste, and minimise emissions.

We were granted special access to their manufacturing facilities — and an equally rare interview with their CEO, Dr. Joachim Schönbeck.

“We have a responsibility to develop the technologies we can because that is what we can provide to society”, he told us. “But our target is to make it economically viable, because we learned that if it's too costly, nobody can afford it; it will not fly. So that's where we see our part”.

The group´s latest available annual revenue hovered around €8 billion — and growing.

“The roadmap is definitely this drive to electricity”, Mr Schönbeck said. “So we have the renewable energy. We have the extension of the grids that will come. We will see much more applications into electrical motors, into batteries, into transformers. And this is where we all can supply technology into. We have about almost 40% of our revenue that is related to that area and we see that growing”.

Not far from there, we visit another big European actor in green solutions.

With 4,000 employees and active in 9 countries, Saubermacher AG company recycles all sorts of residues from 40,000 clients in 1,600 municipalities.

At its Graz site alone, around 10,000 tonnes of used textiles are recycled every year — currently manually, but soon, also automatically, according to CEO Andreas Opelt.

“Currently, just 1% of the textiles worldwide are recycled, and so the technology for textile recycling is not as advanced and upscaled as it should be”, Mr. Opelt says. “30 years ago, we started with plastic recycling, also in the similar stage that we started with manual sorting. Then we created our first pilot plants, and we have also our first pilot plant being set up here. And I'm sure in two, three years, we will have big plants fully automated, doing the job at a big scale because we want to reach much more than this 1% of recycling, but a degree that is really sustainable.”

A huge global provider of green technologies. A big European name in the recycling sector. What do they have in common?

They are both members of a green technology cluster headquartered at the futuristic Science Tower in Graz, a 60-meter-tall architectural and technological landmark in the city's *Smart City* district.

Under a strict public-private partnership, the Green Tech Valley Cluster gathers 300 companies shaping the innovative green and circular technologies of tomorrow.

This includes 20 global tech leaders, as well as many medium-sized companies and start-ups, all developing technical solutions in sectors like hydropower, solar thermal, wind, recycling, batteries, energy storage, charging devices or electromobility, explains Bernhard Puttinger, the cluster’s CEO.

“We see, of course, at the European Union, we have high wages, high energy costs, but we are very pioneers in these technologies, and we see, of course, that global competition sometimes is not a level playing field”, he acknowledges. “But to overcome these challenges, innovation and new ways of collaboration are the answer and this is what we as a cluster try to foster.*”*

The cluster claims that one seventh of the world's green electricity production is done through technologies developed by its members.

“It's about their needs, the opportunities they see. We bring together their joint strategies, their future business opportunities, and with that we start projects and programmes to initiate joint future projects”, Mr Bernhard tells us while driving around Graz. “At the end of the day, our key performance indicator is how many innovation projects have been started on an industrial level. Within the last five years, a quarter of a billion euro R&D investment budget was initiated starting from ideas from the cluster. So, we see there's a good impact from our side.”

A short tour around the region takes us to one of the clusters´ key members, a company combining tradition with engineering and innovative research to develop and mass-produce wood hybrids that replace classic materials for industry, mobility or logistics.

Aiming to increase its innovation drive, the company has heavily invested in a state-of-the-art research facility called Wood Vision Lab, due to begin operating by 2027.

“On the one hand, wood is a millennia-old raw material of mankind. But on the other hand, thanks to the *digital twin*(technology) that we have built, we can calculate and design it in such a way that we can really use it for current and future high-tech applications”, Martin F. Karner, CEO at Weitzer Woodsolutions, says. “In principle, it is about using the* digital twin* on really lightweight components; to maximize the advantages of wood as a raw material in the product component.”

The Green Tech Valley Cluster also hosts 2,300 specialised researchers working to shape future green solutions in different fields.

Martin Sagmeister, for example, leads a team working to develop technologically sustainable solutions across the whole value chain of hydrogen — from production, to storage, to transportation.

“The main thing about hydrogen is its versatility,” explains Martin Sagmeister, Senior Researcher and Project Manager at HyCentA Research GmbH.

“So, you can use it as a chemical compound, you can use it as an energy carrier, you can store it, you can transport it, you can use it for mobility. In Europe, we are using it now — hundreds of thousands of tonnes or millions of tonnes that we already need — and we need to replace this grey hydrogen with green hydrogen. That would be our first priority.”

Meanwhile, researchers at Graz University of Technology are developing paper-based solutions to replace chemical-loaden isolation membranes in batteries or fuel cells.

“Paper was already thought to be dead in the 80s. So, everybody was thinking about the paperless office and that paper wouldn't play a role anymore in the future”, explains Stefan Spirk, Associated Professor at the Institute of Bioproducts and Paper Technology at TU Graz.

“But paper can have applications that are basically never-ending. So, we have papers, not only like in here, when we use it in a separator as a membrane, in batteries, in fuel cells, in electrolysers. [But] we also have commodity products. We have packaging applications. We are replacing plastics with more and more paper products. And that's why I think this is a very important area of research for the future of Europe.”

Spirk is also CEO at Ecolyte GmbH, a start-up exploring new industrial applications for paper materials.

The Green Tech Valley Cluster claims 600 green tech solutions developed by its members are already in the world market.
