Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman stepped in this summer to persuade the Trump administration to stop pressing Venezuela over its OPEC membership, two people familiar with the matter said. Saudi Arabia is the group's top producer and its de facto leader.

The intervention came after Washington urged two of OPEC's founding members, Venezuela and Iraq, to consider quitting the organization earlier this year, according to ten people familiar with the matter. The administration also prevented Venezuela from paying its annual membership dues.

These moves, previously unreported, amount to a direct challenge to a body that President Donald Trump has long accused of keeping oil prices artificially high.

![The Trump administration encouraged Venezuela and Iraq to consider leaving OPEC earlier this year](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/27/10/2306499435..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

At the time, U.S. officials were pressing both countries to raise oil production and investment, and some worried that OPEC's production limits could eventually constrain those ambitions, according to people familiar with the discussions.

In Iraq, Prime Minister Ali Faleh al-Zaidi raised the possibility of leaving OPEC at a government meeting this summer after discussions with US officials who encouraged the idea, two people familiar with the matter said.

Iraq's oil ministry pushed back, persuading al-Zaidi to keep Iraq in the group and instead seek a higher production quota, the people said. The United States holds powerful leverage over both countries.

It controls Venezuela's access to state funds following the January ouster of Nicolas Maduro, while Iraq's oil revenues continue to pass through an account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, a relic of the 2003 Iraq War.

![This view shows a cake featuring an image of former President Nicolas Maduro on the day of his 64th birthday](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/27/11/2306106767..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The State Department did not answer questions about whether it had prevented Venezuela from paying its OPEC membership fee. But it confirmed Washington has the power to decide on Venezuela's use of state funds, now held in a U.S.-controlled account set up after Maduro's removal.

"The Department receives disbursement requests from the interim Venezuelan government and reviews the requests, including against a list of approved and prohibited uses of ​the foreign Government Deposit Funds," a State Department official told Reuters.

The official said lower-level U.S. officials may have raised the question of leaving OPEC with Venezuela's government earlier this year, but that "it has ⁠not been among the issues raised by high-level officials."

The State Department's conversations with Iraq, meanwhile, "have focused on improving energy infrastructure to increase production and transportation of energy, not OPEC," the official said. Representatives for OPEC and for the governments of Venezuela, Iraq, and Saudi Arabia did not respond to requests for comment.

The U.S. maneuvers add strain to an organization that has already lost four members since 2019, and whose share of the global oil market recently hit a low on the back of disruptions caused by the US and Israeli war on Iran, another OPEC founding member.

![The previously unreported moves targeted two of OPEC's five founding members, marking an unusually direct challenge to an organization](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/27/11/2026-11-27T012926Z_1476667629_RC2VXIAEMB5S_RTRMADP_3_USA-OPEC-PRESSURE.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

"The risk of OPEC fracture is rising," said Dan Alamariu, chief geopolitical strategist at Alpine Macro. The United Arab Emirates announced its exit from OPEC earlier this year after growing frustrated with the group's production caps, while Angola, Ecuador and Qatar quit in 2024, 2020 and 2019, respectively.

There are no indications the U.S. was involved in those decisions.

OPEC was founded in Baghdad in September 1960 by oil-producing countries seeking to assert more control over a global market dominated by a handful of Western oil companies.

## **How America went from buying OPEC oil to becoming the cartel’s biggest rival**

Founded by Iraq, Iran, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and Venezuela, OPEC now has 11 members. It has also formed an alliance with several other producer countries, including Russia, called OPEC+, which regularly meets to set coordinated production levels.

Trump has repeatedly attacked OPEC throughout his decades-long business and political career, calling it a cartel and a monopoly profiting from the rest of the world. Over the past decade, though, the U.S. has become the world's largest oil producer, turning it from an OPEC customer into its main rival for oil market share.

![After the U.S. seized Maduro in January, the State Department opened discussions with Venezuelan officials in Washington on whether the country should leave OPEC](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/16/2306104728..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

After the U.S. seized Maduro in January, the State Department opened discussions with Venezuelan officials in Washington on whether the country should leave OPEC, five people familiar with the matter told Reuters. They did not identify the individuals who took part in those discussions.

Trump wanted US oil majors to revive Venezuela's oil output, something he said would shore up energy security in the Western Hemisphere, and worried that OPEC policy decisions could hinder that plan, the sources said. In August, Trump said it was up to Venezuela whether to leave OPEC.

U.S. officials also held talks this summer with Iraqi Prime Minister Ali Faleh al-Zaidi, encouraging Iraq's departure from OPEC as US oil majors Exxon Mobil and Chevron planned major new investments there, two people familiar with the matter said.

Al-Zaidi later raised the issue at a government meeting. His oil ministry persuaded him to stay and instead potentially seek a higher OPEC quota, they said. The issue could come up again, as some elements of Iraq's ruling coalition favor leaving OPEC and OPEC+, which they view as dominated by Saudi Arabia and Russia, the people said.

There is no indication Washington has sought to block Baghdad's OPEC dues. After the U.S. invasion of Iraq in 2003, the United States began routing Iraq's oil revenues through an account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York.

That structure remains in place more than two decades later. This year, the US threatened to starve Iraq of its oil money if it didn't cut Iran's influence in its government.

## Washington blocks a $3 million membership bill and sparks fears Venezuela has become an American protectorate

OPEC membership fee payments are typically due in the first quarter of each year and fund staff salaries, research, and travel at the OPEC Secretariat in Vienna. Unlike the United Nations, where dues are scaled to the size of a country's economy, OPEC splits the fees equally among members at around 2.8 million euros ($3.08 million) each.

![OPEC membership fee payments are typically due in the first quarter of each year](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/02/25/23/40/Screenshot-2026-02-25-at-6-36-10-PM.png?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The price tag is tiny compared to OPEC oil revenues, but paying it carries symbolic importance as a marker of continued membership in an organization Caracas helped create. Washington argued Venezuela could spend the money on more important matters, one person familiar with the country's relationship with OPEC said.

OPEC has the discretion to disregard unpaid membership fees, and is not planning to expel Venezuela over the issue, two OPEC sources told Reuters. Washington's control of Venezuela's oil revenues and finances has led some in the South American country to refer to their country as a U.S. protectorate, instead of a sovereign nation.

The Government Accountability Office opened a formal investigation in July into the administration's management and disbursement of Venezuelan oil revenues, following congressional requests for transparency.

The Trump administration has said Venezuela's oil revenues are being used to meet the needs of the Venezuelan people, including to pay government salaries and fund essential services. Reuters could not establish when exactly the U.S. rejected the OPEC dues payment request.

Venezuela is currently exempt from OPEC+ oil output quotas as its production has collapsed from historical highs due to years of sanctions and mismanagement. The country pumped about 1.2 million barrels per day in September, according to OPEC figures.

Its state oil company, PDVSA, said it plans to reach 1.5 million bpd by the end of next year. OPEC last regulated Caracas' output with quotas in 2017-2018 when the country's target was around 2 million bpd. The country's return to the quota system is unlikely until it hits that level again, a source familiar with Venezuela's OPEC position said.
