**Dr. Alexander Wolf, Leiter des Hauptstadtbüros der Hanns-Seidel-Stiftung**

## The EU summit on the Union's common budget for 2028-2034 has initially failed. Some want to protect established spending, others to cut costs and mainly finance new strategic tasks, warns foreign policy expert Alexander Wolf of the Hanns Seidel Foundation in Berlin.

Europe wants to become more independent, competitive and secure. Yet it is precisely the tasks that are crucial to this that face the deepest cuts in the draft for the next EU budget. Behind this lies a structural problem in European politics.

The special Brussels summit on the European budget for the years 2028 to 2034 ended without agreement. On 17 December, the heads of state and government will meet again. Until then, the main question will be how much Europe is prepared to spend.

## Costa wants cuts above all in new strategic priorities

The European Commission has proposed a financial framework of just under €2 trillion. Council President António Costa has reduced this to around €1.78 trillion. Germany and other net contributors still consider that too high.

More revealing than the overall figure, however, is where Costa makes the cuts. Compared with the Commission’s draft, he removes around €95.5 billion from competitiveness, prosperity and security, and around €54 billion from agriculture, cohesion and related tasks. That amounts to reductions of 16 and 5% respectively. In defence industry, security and space, the cuts are almost 30%.

Costa’s draft does foresee substantial increases for defence and competitiveness compared with the current budget. Even so, the new strategic priorities are being cut disproportionately.

## The logic of national interests

Germany and other net contributors want to limit their payments and focus the budget more strongly on competitiveness and security. Poland and other cohesion countries defend the funding for their regions. France wants an ambitious European budget while also protecting its agriculture. Italy takes a sceptical view of additional European levies.

Each of these positions is understandable from a national perspective. The problem arises from how they interact.

The European financial framework requires unanimity in the Council and the consent of the European Parliament. Every government can make its approval conditional on its own interests being safeguarded. This favours spending whose benefits can be clearly attributed to individual member states.

A French president can explain to farmers which subsidies he has secured in Brussels. A Polish prime minister can point to new roads and modernised regions. But which head of government can claim the benefit of a joint European air defence system purely for his or her own country?

Joint European tasks do have political advocates. But their benefits are less easily mapped onto the national balance sheet that underpins almost every budget negotiation in the end: how much did we pay in, and how much do we get back?

## Agriculture or defence? A budget for a new world

About 60% of the current European financial framework is devoted to agriculture and cohesion. Both areas serve core purposes of European integration: food security, economic development and the reduction of regional disparities.

However, Europe now has additional tasks to tackle that did not carry anything like the same weight when this budget structure was created.

Russia is threatening Europe’s security order. China has built up significant technological and industrial capacity in strategic sectors. The United States is pursuing its own economic and security interests, which do not necessarily coincide with Europes.

The conditions under which the bloc has built its prosperity in recent decades are changing fundamentally.

That cannot be addressed simply through higher national defence spending. It is equally about joint defence research, industrial production capacity, secure supply chains, cross-border energy networks, military mobility and European expertise in key technologies.

In 2024 Mario Draghi set out what would be needed: catching up the innovation gap with the United States and China, combining decarbonisation with competitiveness, and strengthening Europe’s security by reducing strategic dependencies. He put the additional private and public investment required at €750 to 800 billion a year.

This sum cannot and should not be financed solely from the European budget. However, the budget can mobilise national and private investment and make joint projects possible where individual member states cannot achieve the necessary scale on their own.

## The substance is shrinking: what Europe should finance jointly

I believe the European budget should be shaped much more by its tasks. The decisive factor should be what the member states can achieve better, more efficiently or more cheaply together than alone.

Agriculture and the less developed regions of Europe still need reliability. But their shares of the budget should, with appropriate transition periods, gradually yet clearly decline in order to create more scope for joint investment in the future.

Anyone who thinks Europe can simply carry on as before is living off a substance that is steadily shrinking. A Europe that wants to become more sovereign and more independent also has to get ahead of events.

That calls for honesty on the revenue side. Costa’s draft sets aside €158 billion solely for repaying the EU debt taken on during the pandemic. This money is not available for other tasks.

Costa therefore proposes additional European sources of revenue, including levies on digital services provided by large companies, on tobacco and on non-recycled electronic waste. Anyone who rejects additional own resources has to explain whether they prefer higher national contributions or corresponding cuts. And anyone who demands more European tasks has to say how they should be funded.

The contribution rebates also belong in this context. They are the expression of a budget logic that measures the success of European policy by national returns. As long as this calculation dominates, funding joint tasks will remain structurally more difficult.

## The price of an agreement: established or new tasks?

Germany and France are pushing for an understanding before the end of the year. Given the French presidential election next spring, that is understandable.

One possible compromise would be an even smaller overall volume combined with concessions to the net contributors, agriculture and the cohesion countries. Particularly contentious sources of revenue could be postponed, and funding for further aid to Ukraine could be negotiated separately.

Such an agreement would carry weight. It would create planning certainty and show that 27 governments, despite differing interests, are capable of reaching a compromise.

The crucial question, however, would be the price at which it is achieved. If the main effect is to protect established spending while once again making the new strategic tasks bear most of the savings, the underlying problem would remain.

Europe would still have to tackle many of its new tasks largely through national budgets, loans and additional financing instruments. Especially for long term investment in security, infrastructure and technological competitiveness, a lack of joint planning can lead to higher costs and avoidable duplication.

We do not have to turn all our challenges into European ones. But we should organise and fund at European level those we can no longer adequately handle at national level.

Anyone who wants a more sovereign and more independent Europe must also be prepared to draw fiscal policy conclusions from that.
