A Palestinian woman who wants to study architecture in York to “help rebuild Gaza” has won a High Court challenge against the government over its refusal to grant her a student visa.

Nour Ismail, 20, was offered a fully funded scholarship to the University of York to undertake an undergraduate degree in architecture at the beginning of the current academic year.

This came after she was unable to accept a scholarship to University College Cork, Ireland, following the Irish Government’s decision to block “a wave” of Gazan students obtaining visas without government scholarships in July.

The Irish High Court has since overturned that decision, but the Home Office used it to justify its refusal to provide “diplomatic assurances”, known as an onward travel assurance (OTA), to allow her to travel to Jordan and obtain her visa to study in England.

Ms Ismail challenged the decision as unlawful, while the Home Office defended the claim.

On Friday (November 27), Deputy High Court Judge Guy Vassall-Adams KC overturned the Home Office’s “irrational” and “unfair” decision, which the department must now reconsider.

In a 19-page judgment, he found that Ms Ismail had a “potential life-changing opportunity”, had met the criteria for an OTA, and had raised the “likely illegality” of the Irish Government’s decision with the Home Office.

But he continued that the department’s decision “did not substantively engage with any aspect of the claimant’s application except the Irish refusal, which it adopted and treated as decisive”, despite its “potential unlawfulness”.

He said: “In my judgment, given the importance of this decision for someone in the position of the claimant, the reasons given by the defendant were wholly inadequate.”

The judge said that Ms Ismail had studied at the Department of Architectural Engineering at the Islamic University of Gaza and achieved “excellent” results, but her education has been “severely disrupted” by Israel’s war on Gaza.

In a statement for a hearing earlier this month, Ms Ismail told the court that academic resources have been destroyed and several professors killed, while the destruction of buildings meant students had to study at home without consistent access to electricity or the internet, leading her to seek to study abroad.

Following Ms Ismail’s offer to study in York, the Home Office refused her student visa application in October, for reasons including its concern over the genuineness of her intent to move to the UK to study.

As a result, she missed the University of York’s enrolment deadline earlier this month, and her place was deferred to the 2027 academic year.

Lawyers for Ms Ismail told the High Court that the fact her application for an Irish visa was refused could not cause the government to doubt her intention to study in the UK.

Sonali Naik KC also said the government did not provide sufficient reasons to deny Ms Ismail’s application and that she should have been allowed to respond to the department’s concerns.

Jack Holborn, for the Home Office, told the High Court that the Irish visa refusal was not the only reason for the government’s decision but that it was rational to consider it relevant.

He also said it was reasonable to have concerns about whether Ms Ismail was genuinely seeking to enter the UK for study, and denied any unfairness.

Judge Vassall-Adams ruled in favour of Ms Ismail, stating that the Home Office’s conclusion “that there were reasonable concerns as to the claimant’s genuineness as a student was irrational”, and that “the process adopted by the defendant was procedurally unfair”.

He noted that as a result of the Irish High Court action, Ms Nour was granted an Irish visa and “will now be able to take up her place at University College Cork, if she chooses to do so”.

He also pointed to a statement from an academic at the University of York who works on the Gaza Scholarship Initiative, who said that it is likely the government will reach out to universities “with a view to persuading them to extend their final enrolment dates for students from Gaza”.
