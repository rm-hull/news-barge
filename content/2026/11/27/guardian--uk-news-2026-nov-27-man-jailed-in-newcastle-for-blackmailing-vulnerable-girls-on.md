A 20-year-old man from north-east England has been jailed for blackmailing vulnerable girls around the world, prompting a senior police officer to urge parents to regularly check their children’s devices.

Beau-Jack Mensforth admitted 17 offences of making and distributing indecent photographs of children, blackmail, and participating in the activities of an organised crime group.

Newcastle crown court heard that Mensforth coerced girls into harming themselves, making indecent photographs of themselves and physically harming their pets.

At his sentencing on Friday, victims spoke of feeling scarred for life, being deprived of a normal childhood, needing psychiatric care and attempting suicide.

Judge Bindloss jailed Mensforth for 14 and a half years, describing him as “a calculating predator who groomed and blackmailed young girls”.

Mensforth was part of a global group, known as the Com, which is involved in a broad range of cybercrime, including targeting vulnerable girls online. If the children resisted Mensforth’s wild requests they would be blackmailed, said DCI Steve Brown of Northumbria police.

The court heard that Mensforth used cyber-attack threat techniques called swatting and doxing to try to get his way. Swatting is the term used when someone falsely reports a major crime at the victim’s house so that the emergency services turn up in numbers, sometimes armed. Doxing is threatening to put all of someone’s personal details online.

Brown said Mensforth was “targeting vulnerable females across the globe in order to carry out acts at his behest. If they didn’t, he would swat and dox them.

“As he was doing this, he was also having chats in his own online forums and groups … bragging, discussing, encouraging others to do the same.”

Police say Mensforth, using the name Yuri, encouraged and incited girls to self-harm themselves by cutting his name into their body.

At his sentencing, the court heard statements from some of Mensforth’s victims around the world. All of them were given lifelong anonymity by the judge.

One teenager, from the US, said her ordeal with Mensforth had made her attempt suicide. She had become “incredibly depressed and nihilistic” and her self-worth was “ripped to shreds … I felt I was being hypnotised into believing I was nothing”.

She said she was physically scarred for life. “I can’t look at my own body without getting flashbacks to the gaping wounds he made me carve,” she said.

Another victim said Mensforth was a “psychopath” and spoke of how he had “deprived me of a normal childhood and a normal life”.

A third girl spoke of her feelings of isolation, loneliness and paranoia after her experiences with Mensforth. “I have a lot of anger towards him,” she said. “I hate him … I just want him punished for the pain he has caused me and my family.”

A girl from Australia said she was 12 when she first encountered Mensforth and he made “her believe it was OK to self-harm for your pleasure”. She said she now suffered “overwhelming anxiety” and Mensforth had ruined her ability to trust others. “My ability to feel safe was stolen from me,” she said.

In police interviews Mensforth spoke of how the group would encourage girls to kill pets “or any stray animals”. In one email exchange he suggested that a girl should be encouraged to punch a cat.

Northumbria police started investigating Mensforth, from Sunderland, in October 2023 when he was 17. Brown described it as a “very complex” investigation, with a “vast” amount of digital material recovered.

There were nine victims in the criminal case, three of them based in the UK, with others in the US, Greece, the Philippines and Australia. More than 30 other victims had been identified, but where prosecution had not been possible, Brown said. “It’s been a hard slog because there’s been a lot of gruesome and horrendous material that the investigation team had to go through. It would take its toll on anybody.”

Brown said he wanted people to be aware of what could happen online. Police are encouraging people to “support the young people in their lives by looking at their devices, monitoring and checking what they are doing online”.

Brown said warning signs that a child might be a victim could include a change in attitude or personality, wanting to just stay in their room, hiding their devices, not letting you access them, “and clearly at times people are self-harming”.

The defence counsel Robin Patton said psychiatric evaluation had revealed Mensforth had autistic spectrum disorder, which reduced his capacity to exercise appropriate judgment.

His descent into “moral squalor” started after, as a socially isolated 15-year-old, he lost his father, Patton said.
