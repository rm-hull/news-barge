Newly released documents suggest that the CIA waterboarded more detainees during the post-9/11 “war on terror” than previously acknowledged, utilizing a site north of Kabul, Afghanistan, for the practice of simulated drowning.

The agency has acknowledged waterboarding three men: Abu Zubaydah, believed to be a high-ranking member of al-Qaida; Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, suspected architect of the 11 September 2001 attacks; and Abd al-Rahim al-Nashiri, accused of planning the 2000 bombing of a navy destroyer.

But declassified documents obtained under a freedom of information lawsuit by the New York Times, includes an account from a former CIA contract translator that appears to support the belief that waterboarding was more common and may have occurred beyond two acknowledged “special rendition” sites in Thailand and Poland.

The outlet reported on Friday that the linguist worked at a secret prison, or black site, one north of Kabul, Afghanistan, known as Salt Pit or Detention Site Cobalt.

The translator told an FBI agent in 2012 he had seen a man he called “Bahir” strapped to a board tilted downward into water.

“There were approximately three to four people ‘doing this,’” the FBI agent summarized in the account, saying that the witness “saw the incident in passing but did not participate himself. The waterboard session was in the hallway and there was not a lot of room.”

The account comes from an investigation into the CIA’s use of torture and its destruction of waterboarding videotapes from 2008 to 2012 conducted by then-special prosecutor John Durham. No charges were ever brought.

In the new documents produced by the Times, the linguist told the FBI in that “Bahir” was “a relative of Hekmatyar”, seemingly a reference to Gulbuddin Hekmatyar, the former Afghan prime minister.

That may correspond to a man named “Ghairat Bahir”, who was apprehended in a raid in Islamabad in 2002, appears in CIA records, and was subjected to “enhanced” interrogation techniques.
