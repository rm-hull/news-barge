**Net migration falling to the tens of thousands is a landmark moment in our collective national conversation about immigration over the last couple of decades.**

There is a simple reason for this. We have had years and years strewn with the litter of politicians promising to cut net migration to under 100,000 annually.

But it never happened, until now.

In the latter decades of the last century, net migration was broadly net zero. Sometimes more folk left than arrived. But, around the time that I first arrived as a reporter in Westminster in 2004, the story was changing and would change rapidly.

I have followed every twist, turn, argument and campaign since, both from Parliament and from Brussels.

On 1 May 2004, the European Union expanded hugely, from 15 to 25 members, the biggest expansion in its history.

The bloc's population grew by 74 million people overnight as countries including Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Slovakia, Slovenia and what was then the Czech Republic signed up.

And remember, a core plank of EU membership is freedom of movement: the right to travel, live and work anywhere in the club.

Transitional controls on workers from these former communist states could be applied at the time, but the then-Labour government led by Tony Blair chose not to.

The consequences of that decision were as substantial as they were consequential.

Ministers had predicted a minimal impact. But net migration cranked up.

Crucially, this began to ground arguments about sovereignty in the swirling debates about the UK's membership of the EU.

At the time I was a Europe correspondent based in Brussels and a frequent visitor to the European Parliament in Strasbourg.

The UK Independence Party, led by Nigel Farage, were on the rise. But Eurosceptics would tell me privately that their deeply held concerns about the loss of sovereignty, of power, to EU institutions had been a hard sell to many back home. It was an argument, regardless of its merits or otherwise, that felt theoretical and intellectual to many, rather than practical and grounded in day-to-day concerns.

But that changed for many given the scale of immigration people could see.

Plenty of places, particularly beyond the big cities, felt the rapidity of change. Immigration took on a greater political salience.

Should all control over migration be re-imported to Westminster? Such was the growing strength of feeling among many, a sequence of prime ministers began promising to reduce net migration to the tens of thousands.

David Cameron made the promise. The Conservative election manifesto in 2010 harked back to those numbers before the turn of the century. "We will take steps to take net migration back to the levels of the 1990s – tens of thousands a year, not hundreds of thousands."

Five years later, Cameron renewed the promise, in the Conservative manifesto of 2015. The party would "keep our ambition of delivering annual net migration in the tens of thousands, not the hundreds of thousands," it read.

By 2017, Theresa May was prime minister and while the UK had voted for Brexit, it hadn't happened yet. In the Conservative manifesto that year, she promised "controlled, sustainable migration, with net migration down to the tens of thousands."

At the 2019 general election, Boris Johnson was in Downing Street. He didn't repeat the tens of thousands promise, post Brexit, instead arguing for an "Australian-style points-based immigration system" and, crucially, that "overall numbers will come down."

They didn't. Instead, they soared by almost a million – a giant spike on the graph of recent net migration, branded by his critics as the "Boriswave."

Johnson argued that Brexit would mean Westminster would take back control of migration, as leaving the European Union would mean the end of freedom of movement among the member states.

And yes, Brexit did repatriate that power. But this delivered choices, not a guaranteed outcome that the numbers would fall. And Johnson's choices led instead to a spectacular rise.

It took Rishi Sunak's government and the then-Home Secretary Sir James Cleverly to substantially change the rules and so limit the number of visas granted. The incoming Labour government in 2024 inherited these rules and added further to them.

The question now, with the net migration number now in the tens of thousands, is whether immigration will now shrivel as a topic of widespread worry.

How migration feels can be influenced by world view and wealth, politics and geography. Let's take just one aspect of it: the number of asylum seekers being housed in Scotland has increased by 20% over the past year, despite a fall in numbers across the UK as a whole. Glasgow remains the council area with the largest number of people in asylum accommodation in the UK, and there have been increases in areas from Aberdeen to North Lanarkshire which buck the UK-wide trend.

And a lower net figure also does not necessarily mean that the scale of immigration and emigration are low. Large but similar numbers merely serve to almost cancel each other out, numerically. The underlying official statistics set out that an estimated 738,000 people immigrated to the UK in the year to June, while 661,000 emigrated.

But just as previous political choices still have consequences now, a persistently low net migration number, depending on who is coming and going, could have consequences in the future. Take Andy Burnham's desire to shake up social care in England, a sector traditionally reliant on arrivals from overseas for much of its workforce. How might it cope? It's one of many potential questions for the future.

For now, as we examine this latest set of statistics, many will reflect that we have collectively as a country – if not everyone individually - exuded an anguish, sometimes an anger at the scale of immigration. It has been one of the standout demographic, social and so political issues of recent times. Perhaps now that conversation turns to a new chapter.
