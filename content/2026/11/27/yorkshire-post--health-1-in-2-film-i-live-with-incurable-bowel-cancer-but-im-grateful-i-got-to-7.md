# 1 IN 2 film: 'I live with incurable bowel cancer - but I'm grateful I got to 70'

## Filmmaker David Wilkinson, who grew up in Leeds, has made his most personal documentary yet after being diagnosed with bowel cancer. Laura Reid reports.

It wasn’t long after he was diagnosed that David Nicholas Wilkinson turned to what he knew. First as an actor, and then as a writer, producer, distributor and filmmaker, the screen industry has been his stomping ground since 1970.

So, two years ago, after being told he had stage four bowel cancer, he told his oncologist he wanted to create a film about the disease. “I think of ideas for films all the time and it came to my mind quite quickly,” says the 70-year-old, originally from Leeds. “But I didn’t want to do a film that was just old man gets cancer.”

The idea for 1 IN 2, a documentary exploring the growing impact of bowel cancer, began to take shape after David’s first experience of cancer treatment. Opposite him, as he underwent his first dose chemotherapy, was a man in his early forties, the father of three young children.

“He was so extraordinarily fit physically was this man, in his shorts and his t-shirt and I was just so shocked that he was 42 and that he was at the same stage as me,” David explains. “As I went in each cycle I was seeing more and more young people and that really did shock me and I thought that's what I want to make the film about.”

1 IN 2, named after the statistic that roughly one in two people in the UK will be diagnosed with cancer at some point in their lifetime, follows David through his own treatment, where he discovers a rise in bowel cancer among people too young to be screened for it as part of the national programme. Among those who tell him their stories is a bowel surgeon whose own symptoms were dismissed by her oncologist colleagues and in part by herself because she was only 44.

In this country, bowel cancer screening is currently offered to people aged 50 to 74 every two years. The NHS says this is because the risk of getting bowel cancer gets higher as people get older. Younger people aren’t without risk, however. Research is pointing to a rising number of younger adults being diagnosed with bowel cancer globally and only last month, researchers from King’s College London began fronting a new trial to help discover why.

The major PROSPECT-UK community study aims to contribute to understanding around what may be driving the rise across different countries and populations, following thousands of adults over time to explore how diet, lifestyle, daily habits, gut health, and environmental exposures can influence risk.

Featuring patients, surgeons and oncologists, David’s film challenges some of the misconceptions surrounding bowel cancer, including the belief that it is an older person’s disease, or that it will always reveal itself through visible blood in the stool.

By sharing his experience and the stories of those he meets along the way, he also aims to encourage people to recognise the signs and symptoms of bowel cancer, and potentially save lives. Speaking on the film, he says: “1 IN 2 is my most personal film yet, dealing with my terminal cancer diagnosis, and because so many in the medical profession have said it will save lives, it is possibly my most important film.”

“What any filmmaker wants is for people to be affected,” he later tells The Yorkshire Post. “And it would be great if sometime when I'm no longer here, somebody meets one of my children and says ‘Oh, my uncle John watched your dad's film, went to the doctor, and they caught his bowel cancer in time’.”

David’s own diagnosis came from a colonoscopy, a year after a routine FIT (Faecal Immunochemical Test) through the screening programme came back clear. FIT tests look for blood in a sample of poo; when people present with colorectal symptoms, FIT can detect trace amounts of blood with a low threshold for a positive result, but the tests are also used for asymptomatic screening with a higher positive test threshold.

Even if a FIT result suggests there’s no need for further tests, it is not a guarantee that an individual doesn’t have bowel cancer. That’s why, says David, who also has prostate cancer, it is so important to know the symptoms and to not delay seeking medical help.

“It’s about constantly letting people know that that strange rumbling you have in your stomach, for example, may not be because you're intolerant to eating cabbage or something,” he notes. “If you keep getting it, it might actually be early onset bowel cancer. So for goodness sake go and talk to the doctor.”

Despite living with incurable cancer, London-based David says he tries not to let the disease define him. “I think anybody who has a serious illness, you have to be positive,” he reflects. “You've got to believe that whatever they're treating you with is going to work...and I just go through life thinking forward all the time”

“The film itself has been therapy,” he continues. “David Wilkinson the person would be depressed by news that the cancer spread to five other body parts. David Wilkinson the filmmaker is going oh that's quite interesting because that could be the end of act two – just when you think he's getting better, the cancer has spread around the body. So I've got this perverse thing going on all the time.”

His overall thought, however? “I'm lucky,” he says earnestly. “Three score years and 10. I’ve been able to live a biblical lifetime. And because I’ve spent the last two years with people a lot younger than me who will not see 70, I really do feel that.”

- 1 IN 2 has already been screened in some UK cinemas as part of Maggie’s 30th Anniversary Celebrations, with proceeds contributing to the work of the cancer charity, which has supported David.
