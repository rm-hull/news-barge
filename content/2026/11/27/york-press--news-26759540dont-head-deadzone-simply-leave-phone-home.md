AMONG the hundreds of emails that fall into my inbox every week one caught my eye.

It drew my attention to what was described as the latest trend - deadzoning.

At first I wondered whether I was being invited on a trip to a cemetery. Was Undercliffe opening its gatehouse as an Airbnb? Maybe a visit to London’s Highgate was on the cards, or, even better, a chance to explore Pere Lachaise in Paris.

Then I wondered whether I was being asked to be an extra in one of those zombie apocalypse films. That would be fun.

But no. It turns out that deadzoning means taking a holiday in a place where there is little or no wireless signal - a place where you cannot use your mobile phone, laptop or any other Wi-Fi device. Such places are now called deadzones.

The email’s suggested destinations included remote corners of the French Alps, the shore of Peru’s Lake Titicaca, and a string of tropical islands off the coast of Panama. In such spots travellers can truly be off-grid, with no notifications whatsoever pinging in your pocket.

Sound good? Such holidays not only cost a pretty penny, but you must do your homework beforehand.

I was aghast to find dozens of websites telling people how to prepare for a deadzoning trip.

‘Give your brain time to adjust,’ one advises. ‘The first 24-48 hours are the most difficult. The brain continues to seek out the usual stimuli: notifications, feeds, pending responses. After this initial period of ‘abstinence’ something loosens up. The mind begins to produce its own thoughts instead of processing those of others.’

Others advise taking along sufficient ‘analog gear’ to keep you occupied while phone-free: things such as books, a notebook and pen, card games, a paper map, or a non-smart camera. These are described as ‘offline entertainment’, as though they are something from the Dark Ages.

The guidance to deadzone holidays stresses the need to ‘prep offline essentials’ - download and print maps, tickets, and bookings before you go, and carry local cash in cash. And, of course, to let friends and family know where you are heading and for how long.

What strikes me is, aren’t these all things we did automatically in the pre-internet years? It wasn’t so long ago that everywhere in the world was a deadzone. Whether in New York’s Times Square or on a remote Polynesian atoll, nobody had a Wi-Fi signal, no one had a mobile phone, and we hadn't heard of laptops. We had none of these things, yet we managed to book holidays, head off and and enjoy ourselves. We didn't need to be in contact with others 24/7.

I can’t help thinking that, while technological advancements have no doubt helped us in many ways, they have set us back in others. We now have to be re-educated on how to live internet-free.

The thing that amuses me most about these so-called deadzones is that people don’t need to travel to them to have a signal-free vacation - they could go anywhere and simply leave their phones at home.

You only have to watch Race Across the World - where contestants are banned from having access to technology - to see that it is possible to travel without these trappings. Participants travel across continents without flights, smartphones, internet access, or bank cards. They manage very well indeed, fully immersing themselves in the different cultures without distractions from TikTok or Instagram. They actually look out of bus and train windows, taking in the sights and sounds, which few travellers do nowadays.

Unless you are a lone traveller keen on daily check-ins with family back home, people can manage without WiFi signals on holiday. When you set off stick your phone in a drawer and not in your bag. The world is your deadzone.
