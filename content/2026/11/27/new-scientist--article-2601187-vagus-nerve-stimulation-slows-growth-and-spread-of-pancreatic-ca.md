An electrical implant that stimulates the vagus nerve slowed the growth and spread of pancreatic tumours in mice. The approach seems to work by helping cancer-killing immune cells infiltrate tumours, enabling a treatment that doesn’t normally work for pancreatic cancer to become effective.

“It’s very exciting,” says Timothy Wang at Columbia University in New York, who wasn’t involved in the research. “It’s the first study I know where electrical manipulation has been used to treat tumours outside the brain.”

The intervention made the tumours susceptible to immunotherapy, which helps immune cells destroy cancer but usually fails against pancreatic tumours. “That could be very significant for patients,” says Wang. “Getting immunotherapy to work is the holy grail right now for pancreatic cancer. It’s thought to be key to long-term survival.”

One way to potentially make immunotherapy work is through electrical stimulation. This is already used to treat some kinds of brain cancer, such as glioblastoma. Now, Lu Gan at the Huazhong University of Science and Technology in China and their colleagues have tested it for pancreatic cancer. More than 500,000 people are diagnosed with pancreatic cancer a year, of whom only 13 per cent survive 5 years, making it one of the deadliest forms of cancer.

Prior research by Wang and his colleagues has also shown that blocking electrical signals in the vagus nerve, which connects the brain to other organs, increases the growth of pancreatic cancer in mice. To see whether stimulating the nerve has the reverse effect, Gan’s team developed an electrical device – roughly 1-centimetre long – that wraps around part of the oesophagus.

When activated by ultrasound waves, sent from outside the body, the device sends electrical signals along a branch of the vagus nerve that runs from the oesophagus to the pancreas.

To put it to the test, the researchers implanted the device, which wasn’t yet activated, into mice. Six days later, cancerous cells were injected into their pancreas. After tumours had formed, the team activated the device for 20 minutes a day in half the mice. It remained inactive in the remaining mice, acting as a control.

About two weeks later, the tumours in the control mice weighed about 230 milligrams, on average, compared with about 190 milligrams in the mice that received electrical stimulation – an almost 20 per cent difference. “I don’t think anyone would have predicted that big an effect,” says Wang.

The pancreatic cancer cells had also spread to more than 10 other parts of the control mice’s bodies, on average – about double the degree of spread that occurred among those that received electrical stimulation.

The more tumours spread, the deadlier they tend to be, however, the researchers didn’t measure the mice’s survival outcomes. This is probably because that would involve tracking a large number of mice over a longer period, and this was just a proof-of-concept study, says Wang. The researchers did find, however, that the device had no known short-term safety issues.

In another part of the experiment, the researchers found that the electrical stimulation seemed to boost the number of cancer-killing immune cells, called T-cells and natural killer cells, in the tumours. It also reduced the abundance of other kinds of immune cells, called MDSCs, which suppress T-cells.

Exactly how this occurred is unclear, but prior studies suggest that stimulating the vagus nerve increases the release of a neurotransmitter called acetylcholine, which can bind to receptors on the surface of immune and cancer cells, altering their behaviour, says Wang.

Finally, the team explored whether the approach could make pancreatic tumours respond to an immunotherapy drug, called anti-PD1, which activates T-cells but is usually ineffective against this type of cancer. “Pancreatic cancer is notorious for having an almost zero response to immunotherapy, and most believe that’s because pancreatic cancer has a tremendously fibrous structure, which stops immune cells getting in,” says Wang. If electrical stimulation allows T-cells to infiltrate the pancreas, immunotherapy stands a better chance, he says.

The researchers found that anti-PD1 alone had no substantial effect on tumour growth in mice with pancreatic tumours. But combining it with electrical stimulation slowed tumour growth and stopped cancers spreading more than electrical stimulation alone.

This suggests this combination treatment could improve survival outcomes, says Wang. “Long-term survival in pancreatic cancer really does require T-cell immunity and getting immunotherapy to work.” It may be even more effective when combined with a non-immunotherapy drug, called daraxonrasib, that roughly doubles survival time in people with pancreatic cancer, says Wang.

Further research should first test whether the findings can be replicated in other mouse models of pancreatic cancer that better mimic what happens in people, for instance, by using mice that have been genetically engineered to develop pancreatic tumours, says Wang. These studies could also assess survival outcomes, he says. If successful, trials in people could start within the next couple of years, he says.
