Poor working relationships with direct superiors have driven large numbers of managers to leave their jobs, according to new research.

A survey polling over 1,000 managers revealed that two out of three have either departed or thought about leaving an organisation.

The Chartered Management Institute (CMI) noted that its findings demonstrate how inadequate line management directly damages staff retention.

The CMI highlighted that although companies frequently concentrate on salaries, benefits and hiring to keep talent, they endanger workforce stability by ignoring a critical factor in employee loyalty – the rapport workers share with their line manager.

Petra Wilton, director of policy at the CMI, said: “People often say they don’t leave jobs, they leave bad bosses.

“Our research shows just how much truth there is in that and it’s a huge warning sign for employers.

“Businesses spend enormous amounts of time and money recruiting talented people, offering competitive salaries and attractive benefits.

“But none of that can make up for a manager who doesn’t listen, doesn’t support their team or makes coming to work miserable.

![A survey of more than 1,000 managers found that two in three said they have considered leaving, or have left, an organisation](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/15/32/iStock-2242952093.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“A good manager can be the reason someone stays in a job, while a bad one can be the reason they walk out of the door.

“What’s particularly concerning is that so many organisations still aren’t investing enough in management training and support.

“Good managers aren’t simply born, they need the right skills and development.”

Earlier this week, new research found how workplace conflict is costing employers an estimated £51.2 billion each year.

Findings from conciliation service Acas, produced alongside researchers at the University of Westminster, indicate that 14 million employees experienced conflict in 2025.

More than one in 10 of those affected subsequently left their jobs, forming the largest share of costs, with resignations and dismissals accounting for an estimated £29.5 billion a year, Acas stated.

Baroness Maggie Jones, chair at Acas said: “Workplace conflict is becoming more complex and comes at a substantial cost.

“Our research shows that 14 million employees across the UK experienced conflict at work in 2025, costing employers £51.2 billion annually.

“Conflict can do serious damage to working relationships, productivity, wellbeing and staff retention.

“Rising demand for Acas services, growing case complexity and more discrimination-related disputes all point to the need for prompt action.

“These costs are not inevitable. Investing in training and effective management, and promoting healthy relationships and early resolution, are good for people and business.”
