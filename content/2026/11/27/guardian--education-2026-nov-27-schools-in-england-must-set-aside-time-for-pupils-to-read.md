Schools in England will have to allocate time for children to read for pleasure as part of Labour’s proposed reforms of the national curriculum.

The education secretary, Lucy Powell, is expected to announce to parliament on Monday that primary schools must earmark time each day for children to read.

Secondary schools will be required to set aside time several days a week as part of the measures to tackle declining literacy rates and increasing screen time.

There will be a statutory reading test for children in year 8, and secondary schools will be expected to ensure pupils focus on entire books, poems and other literature.

The education secretary told the Times: “Across the world, we are seeing a ticking timebomb for our future generations as reading and literacy levels are falling, with too many children seeing reading as a chore or missing out altogether.

“We cannot afford to stand idly by and let this continue. Reading fluently is the gateway to lifelong learning, opening up every subject in school.”

Powell added that in addition to setting up children for success, reading should be “enjoyed and every child should have the opportunity to discover that”.

Powell is also expected to announce the programmes of study for the curriculum in England, which could include shorter exams in some GCSE subjects.

History, geography and modern foreign languages are considered the most likely courses to be slimmed down.

Laura Trott, the shadow education secretary, said she welcomed reforms that encouraged children to read more but said they “must go hand in hand with a serious effort to reduce screen time in our classrooms”.

In July, an education committee inquiry into reading for pleasure concluded that the National Year of Reading should be extended to a National Decade of Reading.

It called on the government to commit to a national reading guarantee that would ensure all children have regular opportunities to enjoy reading.

Research last year found that reading enjoyment among children and young people in the UK had fallen to its lowest level in two decades, with a sharper decline among teenage boys.

While boys’ reading enjoyment fell across most age groups – particularly among those aged 11 to 16 – for girls it remained relatively stable or slightly improved.

The National Literacy Trust’s survey found that among girls aged eight to 18, 39.1% said they enjoyed reading in their free time, compared with 25.7% of boys.
