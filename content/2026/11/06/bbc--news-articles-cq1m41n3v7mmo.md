**Ballots are still being counted in the US midterm elections but the political landscape is already pivoting to the 2028 White House race.**

Buoyed by major gains in Congress, Democrats will have momentum as they prepare for the contest to succeed President Donald Trump.

But a good showing in the midterms does not guarantee a White House win two years later. Barack Obama was re-elected in 2012 despite huge Republican gains in 2010.

No one has yet entered the presidential race but potential candidates will be preparing behind the scenes.

Vice-President's JD Vance's reflection on where the midterms went wrong for Republicans has been interpreted as him paving the way for a 2028 run.

The results of Tuesday's election and the historic size of Democratic gains could shape the White House race in several ways. Here are four takeaways.

## 1. Big winners in the midterms now have momentum

Several prominent potential presidential candidates were running in the 2026 midterms - and some emerged looking strong.

Georgia Democratic Senator **Jon Ossoff** won by a sizeable margin in the politically competitive state that Trump won in 2024.

While he has previously said he has no plans to run for president, his successful campaign, with several viral moments, helped boost his national profile.

Another potential Democratic nominee, Pennsylvania Governor **Josh Shapiro**, swept aside his Republican challenger.

Shapiro has not committed to a 2028 run but analysts point to his broad appeal in swing-state Pennsylvania as a sign that he could be competitive on the national stage.

Illinois Governor **JB Pritzker** also won after a campaign pundits in his state described as a "tune-up" for a presidential run.

California Governor **Gavin Newsom** could not run for re-election in the midterms but he is invested in the outcome of a ballot initiative to tax the state's wealthy residents which he campaigned against.

On the Republican side, there were two potential 2028 names running in Arkansas.

Republican Senator **Tom Cotton** and Governor**Sarah Huckabee Sanders** - a one-time Trump administration press secretary - were able to stay in office by a narrower margin than before, both earning just above half of the votes in the conservative state.

## 2. JD Vance and Trump's allies under pressure

Exit polls suggest the swing against Republicans was partly due to anger felt by voters at the actions of the Trump administration.

That could present a challenge to his potential successors who come from the ranks of the current leadership, including Vice-President **JD Vance** and Secretary of State**Marco Rubio**.

"The one person who has been hurt badly by this election campaign is Vance," said Karen Beckwith, a professor of political science at Case Western Reserve University in Ohio.

Vance has already sought to distance himself from defeat - and from a foreign policy adventure in Iran that was politically disastrous for his party.

"The reason we lost is the war," he wrote on X.

Rubio has denied reports he is considering leaving his post ahead of a 2028 presidential run.

Other possible Republican candidates may benefit from not being part of the administration. Some have been staking out a position on what went wrong and what next.

In a lengthy post on X, Senator **Ted Cruz**, who was not running this year, said that too many Republican politicians seem like "they didn't care" about the economic issues faced by voters.

"It is dark right now, but it is always darkest before the dawn, and morning is coming," he wrote. "That is my fervent prayer, and that is my commitment to you."

Florida Governor **Ron DeSantis**, who abandoned his 2024 presidential run after being trounced by Trump early on, was similarly critical and pointed to Republican victories in Florida as a sign of what can be accomplished.

## 3. Left and centre Democrats in fight for future

Both the centrist and left wings of the Democratic Party have claimed victory from the results as each side sifts through possible 2028 contenders.

Dozens are already making moves, Obama's former adviser Jim Messina has told the BBC.

El-Sayed's win in Michigan was a breakthrough moment for left-wing Democrats because he triumphed in a swing state.

His supporters now claim his win could supercharge left-wing candidates in the 2028 election.

"The old notions of electability are out the door, and bold economist populist outsiders proved they can win in battleground states," said Adam Green, the co-founder of the Progressive Change Campaign Committee.

Green added that the "water is now warm" for candidates such as New York Democratic representative **Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez** to run in presidential primaries and "know that they can win."

Moderate Democrats, however, claimed victories across the country, with the "Blue Dog Caucus" of moderate and conservative Democrats gaining at least 14 seats in Congress, nearly doubling its current size.

This has led many within that wing of the party to conclude that centrist candidates that bill themselves as pragmatic and willing to work with conservatives could woo Republican voters.

Given the success of Democratic candidates who focused on cost of living issues, an economy-first approach will likely be a focus again in two years.

Amirah Siquierah, the director of federal affairs at the Working Families Party, said successful candidates in 2028 will combine economic populism with newer concerns such as data centres.

## 4. Support of Hispanic voters must be earned

Both parties must contend with a changing political map and a shifting coalition of support for 2028.

Once "safe" Republican parts of the country can no longer be considered conservative strongholds.

Texas is left with only one Republican at a statewide level, which means its 40 electoral college votes could be up for grabs in 2028.

Christina Pascucci, a political commentator and one-time Senate candidate in California, said the midterms "challenge long-held political assumptions" and show that "neither party can afford to take its traditional strongholds for granted".

That is true of both Democrats and Republicans who will need to win over swing voters, added Pascucci, now the CEO of Atlas Global, a global communications and advisory firm.

"Neither party owns these voters," she said.

The Trump coalition of working class and Latino voters faltered, reversing many of the gains he had made with those electorates in his successful 2024 campaign.

Driena Sixto, a former spokesperson for Turning Point USA and a conservative political commentator, told the BBC that she believed that Republicans will have to change their messaging to Latino voters ahead of 2028 for any future candidate to be able to reverse the trend.

"Last time, the campaign message was one of safe communities and fighting back against trafficking," she said.

"After the president was sworn in, I think a lot of messaging from the White House did too much to highlight deportations. That was the divide."
