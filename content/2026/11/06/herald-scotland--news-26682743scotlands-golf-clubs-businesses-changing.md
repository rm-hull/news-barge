There is no single story of the business of golf in Scotland.

Over the past year, *Around the Greens* has continued to explore an industry in which businesses are growing around the game, successful clubs are confronting the pressures created by demand, and smaller venues are finding inventive ways to secure their futures.

Few stories illustrated the commercial opportunities around golf better than GolfClubs4Cash, whose journey from an idea sparked by a set of stolen clubs to a business valued at £50 million provided one of the most striking entrepreneurial tales of the year.

Up in the Highlands, Royal Dornoch offers a very different illustration of success. Demand has become so strong that the historic club is having to confront the question of how much more golf it can accommodate.

![Around the Greens logo](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/18712832.jpg?type=mds-article-620) *(Image: Damian Shields)*But* Around the Greens* has never just been about Scotland's best-known courses.

At Lybster, we discovered how a small Caithness club celebrating its centenary has used digital marketing and visitor income to help strengthen its business. Innerleithen, meanwhile, provided an insight into the less glamorous but equally important economics of running a community golf club, where visitors and volunteers play crucial roles in keeping the numbers working.

The people running Scotland's golf clubs are changing too, along with ideas about what the job involves. At Pollok, the arrival of a new general manager formed part of a wider effort to modernise both the management team and the culture of one of Glasgow's traditional clubs. Hollandbush offered a radically different model after taking control of its former municipal course, while at Kings Golf Club in Inverness, manager Graham Ramsay has helped oversee membership growth while developing the visitor side of the business.

![Graham Ramsay of Kings Golf Club](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21648593.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Graham Ramsay of Kings Golf Club, where growing membership and visitor business reflect the changing demands of golf club management *(Image: Christopher Young/Scottish Golf)*

Together, they provided a glimpse of the changing demands of club management, as those running Scotland's courses balance the expectations of existing members with the need to attract visitors, generate income and plan for the future.

Innovation has increasingly become part of the picture too. Scottish start-up Irrigate App secured £100,000 in funding for technology aimed at the golf sector, another example of the businesses developing around a game that remains one of Scotland's most recognisable industries.

And some of the year's most memorable stories have been about opening that game to more people. Phoenix Disability showed what can happen when golfers are given opportunities to progress, taking players from their first experience of the sport through to competition on the world stage.

Together, those stories underline something encountered repeatedly while travelling *Around the Greens*: Scottish golf is not one market moving in one direction.

There are internationally-renowned clubs dealing with extraordinary demand and small community courses working hard to remain sustainable. There are entrepreneurs building businesses around the sport, technology companies finding new opportunities within it, club managers adapting to an increasingly complex job, and people trying to make golf accessible to a wider audience.

Every week, *Around the Greens* tells one part of that much bigger story.
