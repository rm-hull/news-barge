Inverclyde has been named one of Scotland’s top five council areas for recycling, with more than half of its municipal waste being reused or repurposed.

The latest figures from the Scottish Environment Protection Agency (SEPA) show that 53.1% of municipal waste in the area – more than 14,000 tonnes – was reused or repurposed.

The report also found that Inverclyde produces the lowest amount of household waste per person in Scotland and has the country’s lowest household carbon impact.

The council said recent improvements to its recycling services have helped increase the amount of waste diverted from landfill.

Last year, around 4,000 properties, mostly tenements, were added to the council’s kerbside recycling scheme through a partnership with Zero Waste Scotland.

In 2024, the council also signed a long-term contract with waste management company Viridor to ensure waste is recycled, reused or converted into energy to power homes. The arrangement made Inverclyde one of the first areas in Scotland to stop sending biodegradable waste to landfill.

The council has also expanded its blue-bin kerbside recycling service as part of its wider environmental ambitions and net zero strategy.

Inverclyde recorded a carbon dioxide rate of 0.71 tonnes per person, compared with the Scottish average of 0.96 tonnes.

Councillor Michael McCormick, convener of environment and regeneration at Inverclyde Council, said: "To have some of the best recycling rates in the country is something we should be proud of.

"It’s down to the hard work of council staff who are committed to making it as easy as possible to recycle at home and at our recycling centres and the commitment from residents.

"But we never rest on our laurels and are always looking to make further improvements in line with the council’s net zero strategy and ambitions.

"We’ve seen good examples of that in the last couple of years with the expansion of the blue bin kerbside recycling service and partnership with a new contractor that means biodegradable waste is no longer sent to landfill and is instead either reused or used to create energy for homes."
