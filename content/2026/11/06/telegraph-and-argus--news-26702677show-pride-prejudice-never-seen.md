THERE have been many adaptations of Pride and Prejudice on stage and screen - but maybe none quite like this one.

Jane Austen’s much-loved novel gets an irreverent girl band-style makeover in the Olivier Award winning West End sensation Pride and Prejudice\*(\* sort of).

Written and directed by Isobel McArthur, it’s an audacious but affectionate retelling of the great love story - where men, money and microphones are fought over and the romance stakes are high. The show is set to pop classics including Young Hearts Run Free, Will You Love Me Tomorrow and You’re So Vain. It’s the 1800s. It’s party time. Let the matchmaking begin.

A global success, with a production recently opening in Australia, the show is heading for Bradford next year.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21675321.jpg?type=mds-article-620)An all-female cast takes on multiple roles

Leading the all-female cast as Elizabeth Bennet is Georgia Firth, who calls the role “a homecoming”. Georgia worked on the previous tour as assistant stage manager and understudy across all five roles. “When it got to being able to do rehearsals or go on stage a few times, that was where all the lights would be flashing. It was amazing,” she says. “Now I keep expecting to have to go and clear the set. I’m just so grateful for the crew, because I’ve seen what it’s like on the other side of the wings.”

With cast members playing multiple roles, there’s a whirlwind of quick changes offstage. Playing Elizabeth, she notes, gives her “by far the easiest ride” costume-wise, compared with cast-mates dodging clothes being ripped off in the wings.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21675319.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The show is accessible for Austen fans and those new to the book

What draws her most to Elizabeth is her refusal to shrink herself: “Women are so often taught from an early age to be sorry for existing in any space - to feel apologetic for talking, or being too close to you on the Tube. This production, and Elizabeth, is so resistant to that idea. Why is it that a Mr Darcy can walk in and send a work email that just says ‘do this by tomorrow,’ while women are disposed to say sorry for asking for help? We need more shows that tell women they don’t have to apologise for existing.”

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21675322.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The show is an audacious re-telling of the beloved novel

Above all, she hopes audiences have fun with the show. “You don’t realise quite how connected you’re getting to the characters until you’re crying over them, wondering when you started caring so much about these lunatics running around the stage,” she smiles.

A show with five actors and a dozen characters between them will, at some point, require someone to argue with herself mid-scene. That someone is Roxanne Morgan, tackling the roles of Mary and Lydia Bennet and Mrs Gardiner.

Which of her characters would she most want at a party? “I’m not a Lydia fan, she wants to be the centre of attention and I never want that. But I’m not as much of a wallflower as Mary either,” she says, landing instead on Mrs Gardiner. “She’s the in-between, she’s a bit of a matchmaker too.”

How are the quick-change demands of playing two sisters in the same scene? “There’s a lot of disappearing behind something and coming back out as the other sister, and everyone goes ‘Where’s that one?’ and I go ‘Okay, I’m back as someone else.’ It’s a bit wild.”

Roxanne promises a looser take on the period than audiences might expect. “These aren’t your Bridgerton Regency people - they’re your country Regency people. They’re allowed to have fun, be a bit more relaxed.”

She’s full of praise for the female-dominated creative team, led by director Isobel. “Isobel brings in people she trusts - the MD, choreographer, fight director - and because you trust Isobel, you trust them implicitly.”

Also taking on the gender-flipped, multi-role format is Julia Murray, playing anxious matriarch Mrs Bennet and the brooding Fitzwilliam Darcy. For Mrs Bennet, whose every waking hour is devoted to marrying off her five daughters, Julia draws on “some formidable women in my life”. Darcy demands a different approach: “Physicality is the big challenge. I learned quickly how much we tend to make our bodies very apologetic, while a man like him, so high status, just doesn’t apologise. Knowing where to hold your power and your strength is alien to me, but really interesting.”

Julia saw the show on its first UK tour and was floored by it. “And here I am now,” she says. “We were straight into chemises and boots from about day two of rehearsals. Normally it’s tech before you’re doing that.”

Eva Hope plays Jane Bennet, Lady Catherine de Bourgh and George Wickham. Of the three, she feels the strongest pull toward Jane: “She’s bright-eyed, loves life, loves romance - 18-year-old me is very much Jane, so I can relate to her.”

The costume-change pace is unlike anything she’s tackled before and, as Eva has dyslexia, she says the sheer volume of lines and characters has been the biggest challenge - and its biggest relief as things click into place. “Having it in my hand and reading it is a bit of a struggle, so I’m excited for it to finally sit,” she says.

Eva says a key appeal of the show is accessibility: “Someone might walk in thinking, ‘I’m not smart enough to understand it’. But Isobel’s done it in such a magnificent way that anyone will follow the plot and laugh, whether an avid reader of the book or new to it.”

A show needs an optimist and a hater - and Phoebe Marshall plays both, taking on grounded realist Charlotte Lucas and scheming Caroline Bingley. Asked if she’s romantic or realist herself, Phoebe lands somewhere in between: “I’ll read a romantic novel and cry on the Tube. But I’m a realist in that I don’t believe in love at first sight anymore.” As for which of her characters would last longest on Love Island: “Miss Bingley, 100per cent. She’s cutthroat. She’s not there for friendship.”

Of the female creative environment, Phoebe says: “It’s shockingly rare how often the room is mostly women, and it’s so refreshing. It feels like a safe space.”

Isobel McArthur is still marvelling at the show’s success. “We’re all astounded, grateful and just so full of joy,” she says. She’s proud of how the cast has grasped the emotional truth beneath the comedy: “Invested in making those characters real, so we don’t tip over into spoof for its own sake.”

Her message to audiences considering the show is simple: “If you love this novel, I promise you we’ve taken good care of it. And if you’ve never heard of it, or of Jane Austen, you don’t need to research a thing. We’ll take you by the hand and tell you the story - and I guarantee you’ll have a laugh.”
