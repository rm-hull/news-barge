York and North Yorkshire Mayor David Skaith and Deputy Mayor for Policing, Fire and Crime Jo Coles have called on the Government to make Strategic Mayoral Authorities the default structure for future police reforms.

Their intervention follows renewed calls for major changes to policing in England and Wales, including proposals from Metropolitan Police Commissioner Sir Mark Rowley for a small number of much larger regional police forces.

Sir Mark has argued that the existing structure of 43 police forces is no longer equipped to deal effectively with modern crime, and has called for a move towards between eight and ten regional forces, supported by local policing commands and a National Police Service.

The Government has already set out its ambition to reduce the number of police forces through its January 2026 white paper, From local to national: a new model for policing.

An independent review, led by former Metropolitan Police Commissioner Lord Bernard Hogan-Howe, is examining the future structure of policing, including the number and geographical boundaries of forces.

The outcome could have significant implications for North Yorkshire Police, which could lose its status as an independent force if it becomes part of a larger regional organisation.

## Mayor warns of impact on rural policing

David Skaith said that larger forces could result in resources being drawn towards major urban areas, potentially leaving smaller towns and rural communities with a weaker policing presence.

> **David Skaith, Mayor of York and North Yorkshire, said:**
>
> ‘Mega forces’ are a threat to neighbourhood policing in York and North Yorkshire, especially in our rural communities. They would inevitably see resources diverted to large cities, leaving towns and villages at risk of becoming hotspots for crime. That is unfair to North Yorkshire taxpayers, who provide almost half of the funding for policing.

He said he was concerned that removing policing from the principle of aligning public services with mayoral boundaries would undermine the wider approach to prevention and early intervention.

He argued that policing was closely connected to other public services, including health, education and employment, and that Mayors were well placed to bring those organisations together.

Mr Skaith said reform was necessary, but should be based on collaboration rather than top-down reorganisation.

> **David Skaith added:**
>
> Local accountability has never diminished the police’s ability to work across boundaries to tackle cross-border issues such as organised county lines crime, fraud, cyber crime and counter-terrorism. That is why I support the creation of a strengthened national police service, but not at the cost of the crucial and intrinsic link between the police and the communities they serve.

He also described North Yorkshire Police as an efficient force that provides good value for taxpayers, saying officers should be able to continue their work without the distraction of unnecessary restructuring.

## Deputy Mayor questions whether bigger forces would be better

Deputy Mayor for Policing, Fire and Crime Jo Coles said the changing nature of crime required national capabilities and effective collaboration, but did not necessarily mean larger police forces were the answer.

> **Jo Coles, Deputy Mayor for Policing, Fire and Crime, said:**
>
> Policing must adapt to the changing nature of crime. Cybercrime, fraud, child exploitation and organised criminal networks do not respect force boundaries. But it does not follow that bigger police forces are the answer. Serious and organised crime requires strong national capabilities and effective collaboration.

Ms Coles said policing could not tackle modern crime alone and that Mayors could help bring together police, probation, youth justice, children’s services, safeguarding, housing and health organisations.

She also questioned how the effectiveness of a police force should be measured, asking whether comparisons should be based on budgets, officer numbers or geographical size.

North Yorkshire covers a large geographical area, with around 6,000 miles of roads and approximately 32 million visitors each year. The Combined Authority said those circumstances created particular demands for policing that needed to be recognised in any restructuring.

![Jo Coles](https://harrogate-news.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Jo-Coles-Headshot-2-1.webp)
> **Jo Coles added:**
>
> Police reform will not automatically create more police officers, shorten our 6,000 miles of roads or remove the demands of 32 million visitors into York and North Yorkshire.

## North Yorkshire already works across force boundaries

The Combined Authority said regional collaboration was already taking place across Yorkshire and the Humber, including work on organised crime, exploitation, cyber threats and major investigations.

It said North Yorkshire Police and its partners had dismantled 23 county lines in the past two years, with prosecutions resulting in more than 74 years of prison sentences.

The authority argued that serious and organised crime required national capabilities, regional collaboration and strong local intelligence, rather than necessarily requiring the abolition of existing forces.

It also pointed to the potential costs and disruption of mergers, arguing that money spent on unnecessary restructuring would not improve frontline policing.

Ms Coles said the test for reform should be whether changes made the public safer, rather than simply whether policing could be organised differently.

## Earlier concerns about the future of North Yorkshire Police

The possible loss of North Yorkshire Police as an independent force has already prompted criticism locally.

On 13 October, The Harrogate Informer published an article by Philip Allott, the former North Yorkshire Police, Fire and Crime Commissioner, under the headline Bigger isn’t better: why North Yorkshire needs its own police force.

Mr Allott argued that merging North Yorkshire Police into a larger regional force could weaken local accountability and result in resources being directed towards larger urban areas.

He also questioned whether the anticipated savings from mergers would justify the cost and disruption of reorganising police services.

His article referred to the latest inspection of North Yorkshire Police, which rated the force good in six of the nine areas examined. He argued that the force’s performance and existing improvement work did not make a compelling case for its abolition.

Mr Allott also highlighted concerns about rural response times, neighbourhood officers being diverted from their duties and the potential consequences of having local policing decisions made further away from the communities affected.

He supported stronger national arrangements for serious and organised crime but argued that this did not require North Yorkshire to lose its own police force.

## Devolution and local accountability

Policing responsibilities in York and North Yorkshire transferred to the elected Mayor when David Skaith took office in May 2024. Jo Coles subsequently took on the role of Deputy Mayor for Policing, Fire and Crime.

The Government’s wider reform programme also proposes changes to police governance. Its white paper sets out plans to replace Police and Crime Commissioners with Policing and Crime Boards, involving Mayors of Strategic Authorities and local council leaders.

The Government says the intention is to strengthen local accountability while creating a more consistent national and regional policing structure.

However, the Mayor and Deputy Mayor argue that the relationship between policing and devolved public services should be retained as forces are reorganised.

They say local knowledge is particularly important in areas such as North Yorkshire, where rural communities, market towns, major visitor destinations and large geographical distances create different policing demands from those found in major cities.

The Combined Authority also raised the importance of public confidence and accountability, pointing to past cases that have damaged trust in policing.

It said the performance of a force should be judged on its ability to prevent crime, protect vulnerable people, catch offenders and maintain public confidence, rather than its organisational size.

The debate over the future structure of policing is continuing as the independent review considers how forces should be organised across England and Wales.

For York and North Yorkshire, the central question is whether reform can deliver stronger national capabilities while retaining a police service that remains accountable and responsive to the communities it serves.
