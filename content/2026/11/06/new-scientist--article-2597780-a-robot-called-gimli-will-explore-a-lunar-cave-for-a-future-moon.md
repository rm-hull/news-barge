In video-game terms, the moon is the first level in an unfathomably giant, never-ending realm. It is the place where we, the players, learn the controls and try very hard not to die, in preparation for safely exploring the rest of the solar system. At a convenient 385,000 kilometres from Earth – a stone’s throw in cosmic terms – our planet’s companion is a great testing ground. And the race is on to claim a piece of it for a base.

Unfortunately for us, the moon is also a preposterously dangerous place to be. The volcanic dust there is adhesive, abrasive and corrosive, able to eat through spacesuits and machinery like a swarm of voracious locusts. Its daytime temperature can reach 120°C, while its benighted craters can plunge to -245°C, which is close to absolute zero, the lowest possible temperature in the universe. There are moonquakes, which (unlike Earth’s own tremors) can last for tens of minutes. And its lack of an atmosphere means that its surface is unprotected from asteroids (which can splatter you) and space radiation (which can give you cancer).

There are so many awful ways to perish on the moon. China knows the risks. So does the US. And yet, these two countries – along with their many friends and enemies – aren’t just keen on sending astronauts to the lunar surface; they are also planning to establish their own nuclear-powered bases, home to rotating astronaut crews.

I know what you’re thinking: this sounds like the stuff of science fiction, in the sense that you think it will never happen or, if you do, you suspect that it will go horribly, dramatically wrong. But I have been covering these plans for several years now, and I can promise you two things. The first is that, because this new space race is being (partly, perhaps largely) driven by nationalistic zeal, a moon base is much closer to being realised than you might think. The second is that you’re right: if space agencies aren’t exceedingly careful, things could go wrong.

Neither side wants to see a dead astronaut on the lunar surface, which is why these bases must be nothing short of impregnable fortresses. To that end, NASA recently announced that it has selected three new uncrewed scientific missions to the moon, each designed to further the goals of its Artemis programme. The first is a sophisticated seismometer, which will listen out for moonquakes and meteorite impacts. The second will hunt for signs of ice that can be turned into potable water, breathable oxygen and rocket fuel.

So far, so expected. But the third robotic mission really stood out to me, because it is going to investigate a subterranean lava tube.

Yes, you read that right: a tube, burrowed out by actual lava, on the moon.

Lava tubes are extremely cool – and I’m not just saying that because I’m a trained volcanologist. I have walked through several on Earth. They’re serpentine, cavernous things. They often form when a scorching-hot river of molten rock is rushing through a channel. When the river runs out of momentum or magma, the top – exposed to the atmosphere – cools quickly, forming a roof. The rest of the lava drains out elsewhere, leaving an eerie, inky-black hollow behind. Some of them even have nice acoustics, which makes them great alternative gig venues.

The moon is a great big ball of frozen magma. It’s geologically comatose today, but a stegosaurus could have looked up and seen the crimson glow of freshly erupted lava painting the lunar surface. And where there’s an abundance of lava, there are oodles of lava tubes. The problem is that, from the perspective of NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter satellite, suspected lava tube sites just look like deep, shadowy pits.

The Marius Hills pit, on the moon’s nearside, is a prime example. It looks like someone punched a hole in the lunar surface, which is why it’s thought to be a natural skylight for a lava tube. Keen to find out for sure, NASA is sending a robotic mission there to scout it out – and not just to settle a few bets between lunar geologists.

NASA notes that lava tubes offer “natural, ready-made shelters that provide crucial thermal stability and shielding from deadly ionizing radiation and micrometeoroid bombardment”. That’s right: one day, NASA’s astronauts might be living in ancient volcanic vaults to avoid spending too much time on its truly terrible surface.

The mission to the Marius Hills pit – the first plan to explore a lunar lava tube – doesn’t have a launch date just yet. But it does have an excellent name: the Geophysical Instruments for Marius Lunar pit Investigation, or GIMLI. Yes, *that* one, the warrior dwarf from Middle-earth who loves his axe, despises evil rings of power and frequents all kinds of dangerous caves.

I reached out to Nathaniel Putzig at the Planetary Science Institute, the principal investigator of GIMLI. He swears that the name wasn’t inspired by J. R. R. Tolkien’s *Lord of the Rings*. He says that he simply wrote down words describing the mission plan and arranged them to make a pleasing sounding acronym.

“At the time, I was only vaguely aware of the Tolkien association, not fully realising that I had happened upon the name for legendary dwarf Gimli, member of the Fellowship of the Ring and the first Lord of the Glittering Caves,” he told me. The name, he said, “won out in the ranked choice voting by team members”. Thank goodness, because it’s literally perfect for this mission.

GIMLI is a joint effort between the Planetary Science Institute, NASA, the Norwegian Space Agency and US tech firm Honeybee Robotics, a subsidiary of Blue Origin. It won’t abseil into the pit itself. Instead, it will land next to it and use its suite of instruments – including a ground-penetrating radar, seismic sensors and a device that measures subtle differences in the moon’s gravitational field – to map out the hidden subsurface, while its cameras take shots of the pit and its walls.

Presuming the skylight does lead to a lava tube, GIMLI won’t just scratch a scientific itch. It will inform NASA’s plans to keep its astronauts from being murdered by the moon.

Car-sized asteroids burn up in Earth’s atmosphere without consequence. But on the moon, they slam, unimpeded, into the ground, and explode like a pile of dynamite. Very rarely – as happened in 2024 – an asteroid the size of a couple of football stadiums crashes into the lunar surface with the force of an atomic bomb. Astronauts are far more likely to be imperilled by pebble-sized asteroids, which sound like less of an issue until you realise that they travel at tens of thousands of kilometres per hour, effectively making them hypersonic space bullets. Fortunately, the roof of a lava tube would handily absorb their impacts.

If the ceiling of frozen lava was thick enough, it would also offer protection from a major solar storm. When the sun is feeling irritable, it can launch a salvo of super-charged particles our way. Earth’s magnetic field and substantial atmosphere deflect and soak up many of them. But the exposed moon takes these bombardments head on. In the event of an especially intense solar outburst, astronauts there could receive a fatal dose and die weeks or even days later of acute radiation sickness. Hiding in a lava tube could save their lives.

Thank goodness, then, that the GIMLI project exists, and can help us answer the many questions we still have. What are the moon’s lava tubes like? Are they roomy? Do they look stable? Could you fit an astronaut habitat in them, or a small nuclear reactor that powers a nearby base? Seeking these havens out is, in NASA’s words, “important groundwork for safe, long‑term living on the moon”.

NASA is targeting the lunar south pole for its upcoming crewed Artemis missions. This is where it wants to establish the first moon base, largely because it is thought to be home to crucial reservoirs of water. Should we expect that base to be at least partly concealed in a lava tube?

![](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI_319405714.jpg?w=840)

“Lava tubes necessarily form in volcanic terrain, and there is little or no evidence of such terrains in the heavily cratered highlands of the south polar region,” says Putzig. So, probably not. But, he adds, “other outposts may well be established elsewhere that lava tubes may be identified”.

Most lava tubes will also require something like rappels, or makeshift elevators, for astronauts to access them. This creates its own hazard: if a clumsy moonwalker trips, falls and gets stuck in a lava tube, or the machinery malfunctions, there’s a chance that they could die down there. Considering all the other ways the moon could take them out, this would be a pretty embarrassing end.

Lava tubes may be a bit of a hard sell: they could be onerous to find, troublesome to access and potentially a bit bleak to live in. I’m willing to bet that, if you’re going to camp on the moon for several months at a time, you would probably prefer to eschew a dark, volcanic labyrinth for a spot on the surface. Even if you’re at risk of being blasted by tiny space rocks, you at least get to see Earth, and the stars cresting the lunar horizon. Even on the moon, the call of the night sky may prove too bewitching a prospect to pass up.
