*Seven pounds today buys a sandwich and a coffee in most British cities, and not much more. It was all**DC Singh** had when he left Punjab for the United Kingdom, and it remains the figure he reaches for when asked where his business began. “It was all I had, so I made it last,” he says. “Every pound had a job to do.”*

DC Singh is now the founder of Edinburgh Cashmere, a Scottish heritage brand that makes scarves, wraps and ponchos in cashmere, lambswool, and merino wool. Behind it sits an older and far quieter enterprise: a wholesale textile company that he built over many years, largely one email at a time, and that came to supply leading high-end fashion labels.

DC Singh arrived with no capital, limited formal education and only a basic command of English. The work available to him was manual and modestly paid. The industry he would eventually serve runs on relationships, reputation and track record, and he had none of the three, nor anyone in the trade who knew his name.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21680860.jpg?type=mds-article-620)DC Singh has been covered by international lifestyle and fashion magazines such as Vogue, Grazia and Tatler *(Image: DC Singh)*

What he acquired, over time, was an understanding of how cloth moves: from fibre to mill, from mill to maker, from maker to shelf. Somewhere along that chain, he concluded, there had to be room for a newcomer who could deliver quality reliably and was willing to work his way in.

Earning his place took three years of unanswered emails and a steady stream of samples, the evidence of quality that a wholesale textile or garment buyer demands before committing to an order, until one at last caught the eye of a leading fashion brand.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21680862.jpg?type=mds-article-620)DC Singh had always intended to build a brand of his own. *(Image: DC Singh)*###**The Warehouse Years**

His first job in the country was in a warehouse, the sort of position that asks for stamina and punctuality rather than credentials. DC Singh took the shifts, covered his rent and began setting money aside. It was steady, unremarkable work, and it left room for the venture he was already imagining. The habit of saving would prove more valuable than the wage.

Alongside the work, his attention turned to the wholesale side of textiles: the buyers, the specifications, the orders placed in volume season after season. It is a trade with high barriers and few public entrances, and he had no introductions to any of it. So he began writing his own. “I had no one to introduce me, so I introduced myself, one message at a time,” he says. Every day he sent a dozen emails to prospective buyers, each one a request for the chance to quote on an order.

His English was halting, his credentials thin and his company existed largely on paper. Buyers who receive hundreds of approaches have little reason to open a message from an unknown sender, and less to answer it, particularly when the sender is competing with established mills that have decades of references behind them. A dozen emails a day over three years comes to roughly ten thousand. By his own account, none of them produced an order.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21680899.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Edinburgh Cashmere is a major brand in the global knitwear market *(Image: DC Singh)*

He did not leave the warehouse, and the reasoning was plain. The job paid for his living costs and, in effect, funded the experiment, which could continue only as long as the wages did. “I could not afford to stop,” he says. “The warehouse let me keep going.” Each day began with a shift and ended with another round of outreach, a routine that leaves little room for anything else, and one he held to for the full three years.

In wholesale textiles, an email rarely wins an order; a sample does. Buyers commit to large quantities of fabric only after they have handled it, judged the yarn, the feel and the finish, and satisfied themselves that the maker can reproduce that quality at volume. The sample is the first gate every supplier must pass, and for a newcomer it is the only evidence there is. It is also costly to produce. DC Singh spent heavily, putting a large share of his savings into sample after sample, with no guarantee of a return should none of them earn the attention of a leading fashion brand.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21680864.jpg?type=mds-article-620)With DC Tasty due to launch, DC Singh is entering the food delivery market *(Image: DC Singh)*###**The Sample**

The turn, when it came, came from the samples. One of the largest high-street fashion retailers in Europe examined what DC Singh had sent and liked one of them. In a trade where the sample decides whether an order follows, that approval was the opening he had spent three years and much of his savings working toward. It is what set the wholesale business in motion. “I could not say much in English at the time,” DC Singh recalls. “The cloth could say it for me.”

In wholesale, approval from a name of that scale travels quickly. Buyers watch one another, and a supplier who has satisfied one demanding customer becomes a lower risk to the next. For three years, DC Singh had been sending introductions that went unread. Now his work was being introduced on his behalf.

Orders followed, and from the upper end of the market. The next buyers to come to him were leading high-end fashion brands, customers who specify fibre, finish and delivery schedules in close detail and are slow to forgive a lapse. For a company that had gone years without a single order, the change in standing was steep. DC Singh was no longer a hopeful sender of emails. He was a supplier with a roster of exacting clients, and each season he would be judged again.

The discipline that demanded proved the more lasting reward. Luxury wholesale prizes consistency: the same quality on the fortieth order as on the first, delivered when promised. DC Singh's operation learned to work that way, and the habits it formed would shape how the company behaved when conditions later turned against it.

Growth at that level is expensive: larger orders mean larger purchases of yarn, more production capacity and the working funds to carry both until payment arrives, often months after the cost is incurred. DC Singh's savings, already thinned by years of sampling, could not stretch that far.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21680867.jpg?type=mds-article-620)DC's flagship luxury cashmere brand has been built through years of hard work, research and forging strong client relationships. *(Image: DC Singh)*###**Capital**

The answer came from the other asset he had been building: a network. Across the years of selling and supplying, DC Singh had formed relationships with buyers, makers and others in the trade, and those connections led to outside investment. Backers of a trade business tend to look for repeat orders and dependable delivery, and by then he could point to both. The funding gave the wholesale company room to take on commitments far beyond what his own resources could have supported.

With the business on firmer footing, an older ambition came back into view. DC Singh had always intended to build a brand of his own: not a supplier's name hidden behind a client's label, but a company that spoke directly to the people who wore its products. The materials were familiar by then. Cashmere, lambswool, and merino wool were what he had spent years sampling and selling, and knitwear was the natural form. “I always knew I wanted something of my own,” he says.

He started that company and named it Edinburgh Cashmere, a choice he made with care.

Scotland is closely associated with fine woollen cloth, and a name that invokes it comes with expectations about quality and craft that a customer grasps without explanation. DC Singh chose to meet those expectations rather than borrow them. Edinburgh Cashmere is a Scottish heritage brand, and he has built it to carry that standing through its materials, its patterns and the craft behind each piece.

The wholesale business continued alongside it, and was still running when the pandemic arrived.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21680897.jpg?type=mds-article-620)DC Singh chose the name Edinburgh Cashmere to reflect the quality and craft in traditional Scottish woollen cloth *(Image: DC Singh)*###**A Scottish Thread**

Edinburgh Cashmere draws its character from Scottish heritage, which shapes the line of each silhouette, the depth of each colour and a standard of finish that is felt long before it is noticed.

Its centre of gravity is the scarf, made in cashmere, lambswool, and merino wool, and offered in plains that sit easily beneath a tailored coat and in checks that carry a quiet signature. Around it sit wraps and shawls, ponchos and capes, and lambswool throws for the home. The three fibres give customers a clear choice between degrees of softness, warmth and structure, and allow the range to move between everyday wear and something more considered.

A signature check serves as the label's visual identity, recognisable at a glance and carried across the collections. The Exclusive Collection, which includes the DC Scott and DC Check patterns, shows the brand at its most distinctive. The label is designed for customers who value quality of cloth and a sense of provenance, and who prefer discretion to display.

The identity DC Singh has chosen is a continuation of Scottish craft rather than a novelty built on its imagery. The distinction is practical, since a brand that leans on symbols must keep inventing them, while one that stays consistent in its materials and finish only has to keep its word. “Scotland already sets the standard,” he says. “My job is to meet it.”

It is a measured position, and an unusual one for a founder who came to the trade from outside and from a long way away. DC Singh treats Scottish heritage as something to be served in the detail of the work: in the cloth, the proportions and the finish.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21680898.jpg?type=mds-article-620)DC Singh, the successful self-made businessman is expanding into new markets around the globe *(Image: DC Singh)*###**Tested, Then Made to Keep**

When the pandemic arrived, fashion's supply chain stalled. Stores closed, orders slowed and the steady movement of cloth from mill to maker to buyer was interrupted. For a wholesale business the damage is immediate, because costs are incurred long before demand returns, and DC Singh's company felt it financially, as did much of the trade.

He kept paying his suppliers on time. Spinners, dyers and weavers in the trade often extend credit on little more than a promise, and DC Singh, who had once depended on the willingness of strangers to take a chance on him, held to the agreed terms through the downturn. The decision was costly, yet it was consistent with how the company had operated since its first demanding clients, and in a trade where relationships outlast any single season, it is the kind of conduct that is remembered. “They trusted me when I had very little to show for it,” he says. “Paying them on time was the least I could do.”

The consumer brand reflects the same sensibility. Edinburgh Cashmere is built around fibres that DC Singh knows from the inside, after years of sampling and selling them, and its range favours pieces meant to last over pieces meant to be replaced. The emphasis shows in the details a customer notices: the weight of a scarf in the hand, the finish at its edges, the way a pattern holds its shape.

Years of working to the specifications of demanding clients taught DC Singh to read cloth as a buyer does: for the evenness of the yarn, the consistency of the finish and the discipline of delivering the same standard each time. The label applies that scrutiny to pieces that carry his own name.

For DC Singh, the measure of a piece is its endurance: whether it is still chosen, season after season, long after the day it was bought.

It is a considered standard, and a natural extension of DC Singh's own history. The patience and exacting care that carried a wholesale company into the supply chains of some of fashion's most exacting names now give shape to a label of his own, built on the understanding that refinement is established slowly and recognised in time.
