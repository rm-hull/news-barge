DAVID James Buckley’s second historical crime novel, Surviving Angel, is set in 1958 and follows Acting Chief Inspector Jim Bible as an investigation into the murder of a husband and wife, and the disappearance of their young son, draws him into a nationwide mystery involving wartime secrets and powerful figures determined to keep the past buried.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21602153.jpg?type=mds-article-620)David's new crime novel, Surviving Angel features Acting Chief Inspector Jim Bible *(Image: David Buckley)*

Here David reveals how the novel - following his debut crime novel Stone and Water, which first introduced Jim Bible - is inspired by real historical events and David’s memories of growing up in Bradford:

Cows. I can remember cows being herded up Wibsey Park Avenue when I was a child growing up in a terrace house opposite Park View Mills. Further back, my late Aunt Muriel remembered skylarks and lapwings on Wibsey Slack. But perhaps it was the ‘waste ground’ that inspired me to set crime novels in my original home patch.

How carelessly we used that dismissive phrase in the fifties when we played cowboys and Indians, or hid face-down among the willow herb playing hide and seek, in my case obsessively hiding for too long until everyone had gone and I wandered home forlorn at teatime.

The waste ground around Wibsey features in both the first two 1950s novels in my Jim Bible series. In Stone and Water a young boy is found dead on ground opposite Wibsey Infant School near the mounds of blue/grey slack. In the new one, it’s Scaley Hills, that area of still wild hillside covered with broom, holly and brambles that slopes from Briarwood Grove and joins up with Brownroyd Hill where the first murders take place.

Not the real Brownroyd Hill, but Highroyd Hill. Mine is a fictionalised Bradford, never named. Wibsey becomes Chapel Heights. I throw in a few real place names and I’ve kept the police station in the Town Hall below the magnificent clock tower, but relating my adjusted geography to the real geography is like trying to solve a jigsaw puzzle by thumping the pieces with your fist to make them fit.

Unboxing history gave me another selection of jigsaw pieces to play with. The never explained disappearance of Bradford priest Father Borynski was one. That was in 1953, but I’ve brought a fictional priest’s disappearance forward to 1958. Another unsolved mystery is the 1945 murder of farmworker Charles Walton in a Warwickshire village where there were rumours of witchcraft. Even Fabian of the yard, a famous detective in his day and so smart he had his hair cut once a week, couldn’t solve that one.

As a writer, you squirrel things away not knowing when you’re going to retrieve them. I first came across a fictionalised version of the Charles Walton case when I was teacher of English and used it as a basis for GCSE assignments. I like reading history and years ago discovered the London Cage, a Second World War interrogation centre where prisoners of war were questioned with a rigour bordering on torture, and which the government tried to keep secret for years. This gave me a peg on which to hang a story which takes Jim Bible travelling around the country - including to Doncaster where there was a ‘Cage’ on the site of the racecourse - following leads which shock him with the scale of government skulduggery.

He’s shocked because Bible is a decent man. When I started the Jim Bible series I wanted to avoid the cliché of the detective with a permanent hangover or wrecked personal life. Jim Bible wants to be a good man. He would do the right thing if only he could decide what it was. The story for Bible is discovering what the right thing is.

It seems heartless to call playing with historical tragedies fun. But a good deal of writing is fun. At school, there’s always someone in the class who’s really good at drawing. At Grange Boys’ School in the early 1960s it was a boy called Carter (Hello Carter, if you’re reading this; Buckley here). In one art lesson, doing a powder-colour painting of workmen on the road, he suddenly announced, ‘I’m going to put a Bradford City scarf on this bloke.’ I find the playfulness of that remark quite inspirational, showing you can have fun in art while still producing something you want to be taken seriously.

So in Surviving Angel there’s a scene on Baildon Moor in which Bible’s eight-year-old daughter hears a cuckoo in Spring Woods below, as I once did when with my parents. I’ve brought in Fort Ojibwa, the cottage on Fair Road with a front yard sporting a totem pole and other Native American artefacts, though I’ve renamed it Fort Navajo and rebuilt it on Highroyd Hill.

Driving through Harrogate in our 1936 Austin Ruby my dad picked up a National Service soldier hitch-hiking to Catterick Camp, so he’s got in the car again.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21675208.jpg?type=mds-article-620)David with National Service soldier Uncle Donald in the back yard at Wibsey Park Avenue *(Image: David Buckley)*

The steam train that puffed its way from Queensbury past Grange School gets another puff and my Uncle Vic’s prized Wharfedale Speakers pump out a few more tunes. I give one of the characters my dad’s technical skills. He made our first TV and the neighbours trooped in to watch the Coronation.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21675201.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The Buckley family’s 1936 Austin Ruby features in the book *(Image: David Buckley)*

So perhaps my novels are fuelled by nostalgia, like remembering a holiday and wishing you were still there. But the past is another country and you can’t stay in it forever. It would be nice, though, if skylarks and lapwings came back to Wibsey.

l Born in Bradford in 1949, David James Buckley grew up in Wibsey and has lived in Sheffield since 1979.

A former English teacher, he has also written for The Guardian, The Observer and The Independent, and has had three radio plays produced by the BBC, including two for Radio 4.
