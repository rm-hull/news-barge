A non-verbal Autistic boy was left without the specialist education support he needed to help him communicate for more than a year because of faults by City of York Council, a watchdog has found.

The boy, who has not been named, uses a sign, symbol and speech system called Makaton to communicate, along with other methods such as pointing to objects and gestures.

But the Local Government and Social Care Ombudsman found the council failed in its legal duty to make sure the boy's teaching staff were properly trained in Makaton.

Martin Kelly, the council’s corporate director of children’s services, said it has made “considerable efforts” since the ombudsman’s report into the case almost three years ago “including making further investment in the local workforce”.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/13748822.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Martin Kelly, City of York Council’s corporate director of children’s services

The ombudsman launched an investigation following a complaint from the boy’s family.

It found the council “failed in its legal duty to make sure the boy's teaching staff were properly trained in Makaton”, the ombudsman said.

The ombudsman said the boy had an Education, Health and Care (EHC) Plan which stated it was necessary for every member of staff in his classroom be trained to levels three and four in Makaton.

But for more than a year, the ombudsman said it found either no staff member, or at most one, held the level of qualification required.

“Training was patchy and slow, with staff still learning Makaton while they were teaching the boy,” an ombudsman spokesperson said.

They said the ombudsman rejected the council’s argument that the impact was “reduced because staff were using other approaches, including gestalt language processing, as part of a broader ‘total communication’ method”.

The ombudsman said it also had concerns about the way the council handled the family's complaint.

“It took too long to issue a formal response, and the mother was repeatedly left chasing for updates, at one point even being asked by the council to confirm herself whether the problems had been fixed,” the ombudsman spokesperson said.

“When the boy’s mother complained, the council arranged and paid for Makaton training and offered the family a substantial financial remedy.

“The ombudsman welcomed this but said it still did not reflect the significance of what the family had been through and recommended a further payment.”

Julie Odams, chief executive at the Local Government and Social Care Ombudsman, said she recognised that the council has “taken steps to put things right before the family approached us”.

But she said: “Unfortunately that doesn't change the fact that for more than a year, a little boy who relies on Makaton to be understood was left without it.”

“A legal duty to a disabled child is not something a council can work around,” Ms Odams said. “If an EHC Plan says a child needs Makaton to communicate, finding other ways to navigate the school day is not good enough. That duty was non-delegable and absolute.”

She added: “Every council holding an EHC Plan should check provision is in place when the plan starts, check it every year, and act quickly when someone raises a concern. That is how councils can be sure a child is getting the support their EHC Plan expects."

## Council's 'absolute priority' is to provide 'best possible start' for each child, says director

Mr Kelly said the council works with thousands of families across York each year and its “absolute priority is always to provide the best possible start for each individual child” in the city.

He added that the local authority “constantly” reviews its work, “and that of our partners, to ensure the support we provide is of the highest standard and is right for each child”.

“Where families feel that their child is not receiving the right support at the right time, we aim to work with them and our partners, to evaluate and address any concerns,” he said.

“We acknowledge the ombudsman’s Report, which was based on a unique set of circumstances almost three years ago.

“Despite the challenges – in part due to insufficient funding – we have made considerable efforts since this time, including making further investment in the local workforce.

“We will continue to further improve support for young people with Special Educational Needs and /or Disabilities (SEND) and their families in York as part of our plans to implement the government’s SEND reforms.”
