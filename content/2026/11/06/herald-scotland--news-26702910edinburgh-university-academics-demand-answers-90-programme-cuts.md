**Academics at Edinburgh University will confront senior managers over controversial restructuring plans that have resulted in the closure of more than 90 programmes.**

A special meeting of the university’s Senate will be held on November 11, two weeks after academics published a report accusing principal Sir Peter Mathieson and his leadership team of breaching statutory requirements to “push through cuts without proper oversight or regulation”.

Staff and student senators are expected to pass a motion calling for clarity on the latest round of cuts, which began in mid-September.

The motion states: “Senate requests a report listing all programme closures approved since 1 August 2025, and recording for each: the date of consideration by the relevant Board(s) of Studies; the approving College committee; any further body involved in the decision; and evidence of student involvement.”

A number of senators told The Herald that they feared senior managers had not adhered to university policy or governance procedures in enacting the cuts, a position the university rejects.

![Sir Peter Mathieson appearing at a Education Committee hearing in June 2025.](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21501913.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Sir Peter Mathieson's leadership has been heavily criticised by academics. *(Image: Scottish Parliament)*

They have been kept anonymous due to fears of reprisals.

“Unfortunately, over the last few years we have seen our statutory role, enshrined in Scottish law, ignored or circumvented over and over again by senior leadership with no accountability,” Senator A said.

“The Higher Education Governance Act is clear that the role of Senate is to ensure that our academic provision and reputation are not undermined by the business interests of our senior management team and the university Court.

“However, senators have not had any power to shape the proposed restructuring and subsequent job losses, despite asking multiple times to be involved.”

Senator B echoed these claims, telling The Herald colleagues were "immensely frustrated” by the governance impasse.

“[We have been] told time and again, without evidence, that all the proper processes and policies are being followed for sweeping changes to the academic offer.

“Frankly, it's not clear to us that everyone involved in these closures is even aware of the full set of applicable rules, much less that they have been rigorously seeing that they are respected and documented.”

Senator C agreed, adding: “Senators are not opposed to course or programme closures, and recognise that these may be necessary, but these are decisions that have academic as well as financial aspects, and that require academic involvement - something university policy recognises.

“We've reached a point where there is now significant mistrust in the senior management of the university, and deep frustration with what appears to be a strategy to avoid academic scrutiny of decisions that have a significant impact on our educational offering as a university which states a commitment to societal relevance, diversity, inclusivity and accessibility.”

The Herald understands that next week’s meeting will focus on how programme closures in the College of Arts, Humanities, and Social Sciences (CAHSS) were decided.

Senator A alleged that the cuts were made “without proper consultation and without following the processes laid out in policy”, while Senator C said the meeting will be “an attempt to ensure decisions are taken through proper procedural channels”.

“It is difficult to say how long Senate members' patience will hold out,” Senator C added.

“The recent report on the governance crisis was indicative of this, and was written, in part, as an appeal to parliamentarians and members of government in Scotland to take seriously the implications of poor governance and accountability at the heart of an institution with such an important place in Scottish society and public life."

Senator A went a step further, directly appealing to Scotland’s parliamentarians.

They said: “We urge the Scottish Government to intervene before the reputation of our institution is decimated by a restructure designed by consultants, with no pedagogical knowledge or expertise, and 1,800 of our colleagues lose their jobs and livelihoods.”

In September, The Herald reported that 90 programmes in CAHSS would stop enrolling new students from 2027, in a decision which managers defended as an academic ‘refresh’.

The shock announcement led to five weeks of strike action by members of the University and College Union (UCU), who warned that an accompanying £140 million budget savings drive could lead to widespread staff redundancies.

Despite backlash from staff and students, Sir Peter said that work to make the university ‘financially resilient’ must continue.

“Urgent, difficult but strategically important decisions need to be taken now,” he wrote in a staff-wide email.

“That is the role of responsible leaders: aiming to safeguard the future as well as taking care of the present."

Edinburgh University declined to comment.
