*Scottish Government must balance realistic market expectations against bus operators’ claims, while clarifying public control proposals says our columnist*

Politics is politics and politicians will sometimes wish to make a splash. One way of spicing up a major speech, such as a party conference, is to announce a new policy or two. Everyone understands how it works.

But there is a downside. The way in which policies enter the public realm matters. Policies which require wider consent for effective delivery might be doomed from the start if key stakeholders are blindsided or needlessly antagonised. And this is precisely what has happened over recent months.

First, the commitment to cap the prices of everyday supermarket items – a centrepiece of the SNP’s 2026 manifesto - was announced without any prior consultation or, crucially, a plausible route to delivery. Anyone doubting the delivery point need only familiarise themselves with the Scottish government’s recent consultation paper.

Next, the ‘proposal’ to completely overhaul local and regional government in Scotland amounted to less than 300 words, inevitably raising more questions than it answered. The 3-month period of limited consultation (it seems only COSLA and Parliament Committees are considered full consultees in this instance) might prove sufficient to cobble together a political fix but is highly unlikely to allow for the stress-testing of its consequences.

Latterly, the First Minister’s speech to SNP conference included a commitment that ‘*by the end of this parliament, through franchising and regulation, the SNP will bring the bus network back into public control’*. It would be unreasonable to expect granular detail in a conference speech but the imprecision seemed almost designed to set hares running. Inevitably, if erroneously, the policy was immediately and widely reported as a commitment to ‘nationalisation’.

There are legitimate questions: what precisely does ‘public control’ mean? What are the preferred models of franchising and regulation? Why was this policy not included in the SNP manifesto published only 6 months ago? The SNP must sharpen its approach to the full policy process if it wants to improve a less than sparkling record on delivery.

Still, the response from some quarters was predictably hysterical. Deciding that there would be little merit in posing legitimate questions in a sober fashion, Sandy Easdale, owner of McGill’s buses, observed that Mr Swinney ‘appears to have transformed into the next Idi Amin or Robert Mugabe’ noting that these men ‘bankrupted their countries through catastrophic financial policies".

Good grief. The UK’s model of deregulated bus services is an international outlier. Does the potential implementation of a regulatory model that is absolutely the norm in most other European nations really justify comparing the First Minster to homicidal tyrants?

I wrote here recently about the quality of Scottish business representation. If the system was functioning properly, Scotland’s main business representative organisations would be screaming at these guys to get a grip and stop embarrassing the wider community.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21134667.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Stephen Boyd *(Image: Supplied)*

There is a genuine problem here. Any possibility of developing a broad consensus on economic development is undermined by unhinged reactions to policies which would be considered moderate or centrist in comparable nations.

If altogether less ridiculous and offensive, some arguments on the other side of the bus debate also fail to convince. The week after the First Minister’s speech, Ross Greer, Co-Leader off the Scottish Greens was on BBC radio discussing buses (and other matters). He argued that bus services could be funded through bonds - similar to Glasgow City Council’s climate action bonds - on the basis that bus services like housing and energy infrastructure would provide safe returns to investors.

This is far from obvious. Proponents of more ‘public control’ - or, as Mr Greer would prefer, full public *ownership* – are likely to demand the highest standards of employment for bus workers, affordable fares, more services (including many on routes that could not reasonably be expected to turn a profit), and investment in more environmentally friendly buses (preferably purchased from domestic manufacturers). These entirely reasonable ambitions will combine to significantly increase the costs of running bus services.

GCC’s climate action bonds pay 4% annual interest over a period of 5 years. Could bus services be run in a way that guarantees such returns to civic investors? The case needs to be made, not assumed.

The first two phases of the Glasgow bond programme were expected to raise £2m in total. By way of comparison, running the excellent Lothian Buses currently costs around £175m a year. Lothian Buses may be a profitable enterprise but its profits are small when compared to the average for non-financial enterprises across the UK.

And Scotland isn’t Edinburgh. Providing excellent bus services across many of Scotland’s geographies is intrinsically more expensive than it is in a wealthy city with relatively high population density and a high employment rate.

Greater public control, however defined, will necessarily require higher subsidies and these will have to be funded through general taxation. Its proponents should recognise this reality and tailor tax policy demands accordingly.

Political debate over economic and social issues leaves a lot to be desired in Scotland and the quality seems to deteriorate further when matters of ownership and control are in the spotlight. Some of the reaction to the First Minister’s conference speech seemed to suggest that regulating bus services is one step short of full central planning. There is insufficient appreciation of the limits of what the market can deliver.

Writing in the Fraser of Allander Quarterly Review in 2009, when debate was raging over the tendering of the Clyde and Hebrides ferry services, the widely respected Strathclyde University economist – and competition specialist – Professor Neil Kay noted that the Scottish government was failing to engage with economic reality:

*“…as we teach in Economics 101, private interest is not always fully reconcilable with the public interest, especially where the provision of essential services by private local monopolies is concerned. The failures of successive administration to recognize and deal with that simple point are likely to be felt by vulnerable and fragile communities for generations to come*.”

He was right then and his comments remain highly relevant. Politicians need to assess the shrill claims of bus operators in the context of what it is reasonable to expect the market to deliver in the provision of essential bus services. Those who rely on these services may well welcome greater public control but it is incumbent on Ministers to spell out what this means and how it will be paid for.
