Palantir’s controversial £330m health contract is to be extended before ministers draw up plans to replace it with a British alternative, the FT has reported.

Health secretary Yvette Cooper has faced calls from MPs and campaigners to axe the US tech firm’s contract to deliver the flagship government AI system.

The system aims to connect scattered medical files, speed up hospital discharges and improve surgery scheduling among other potentially life-saving improvements. But a review has concluded that removing Palantir from NHS data systems by triggering a break clause in its contract would be too disruptive, the FT reported.

Ministers instead plan to launch a procurement process for a UK tech alternative, with Palantir to potentially be replaced in two years.

Palantir’s UK and Europe chief executive, Louis Mosley, said ending its contract would be “putting politics above patients” and accused critics of “Palantir Derangement Syndrome”.

The company has contracts to supply AI to the Israeli military and Donald Trump’s homeland security crackdown, while its billionaire founder, Peter Thiel, has questioned democracy, said tech companies can “unilaterally change the world” and called technology an “incredible alternative to politics”.

The plan is expected to be signed off soon but Green party leader Zack Polanski accused Labour of “betrayal” and said it was “allowing the corporate capture of our most beloved institution by a genocide and Donald Trump supporting tech corporation.”

He added:

The reports today that Labour is to keep Palantir in the NHS shows that the huge sums of money paid to the likes of former Labour ministers, Peter Mandelson and Tom Watson, looks to have paid off.

Any evidence of patient benefit is highly contested by NHS staff and many NHS trusts have rejected the use of Palantir’s platform.

‘Profound anger’ among black voters over Kemi Badenoch’s slavery remarks

Chris Osuh

The Conservative leader, Kemi Badenoch, has caused “profound anger, hurt and disbelief” among many black voters with her comments about transatlantic slavery, according to the chair of Operation Black Vote (OBV), who urged the prime minister, Andy Burnham, to apologise for Britain’s involvement without delay.

David Weaver, a former government adviser, said the Labour and Tory leaders risked alienating black voters with their handling of the issue of a state apology for slavery, while saying Badenoch’s remarks showed a “troubling absence of moral leadership”.

In a week in which the UK political agenda was dominated by postcolonial legacies, Badenoch said Burnham should not apologise for Britain’s historical role in slavery “because it is a lever to wedge open the door to ask for cash”, while criticising Commonwealth leaders on X.

Burnham, while saying he was open to making a formal apology on behalf of the British state, has ruled out reparations. Weaver said he welcomed the prime minister’s apparent willingness to apologise, but said the government’s “almost reflexive insistence” that any apology must come with assurances “reparations are off the table” was concerning.

Weaver said only the prime minister had the power to address the hurt felt by descendant communities and show Britain had the “moral courage to apologise without qualification”.

UK government considers smart glasses ban over secret filming concerns

Nadeem Badshah

The government is considering placing restrictions on smart glasses amid concerns the technology is being used to surreptitiously film women, a minister has said.

Speaking in the House of Commons, the safeguarding minister, Satvir Kaur, hinted at a ban on selling the technology “to certain users”.

Kaur said:

The benefits of smart glasses must be weighed against the harms they can cause. The government is already looking into options to restrict smart glasses in certain settings and restricting the sale to certain users.

The minister said people who used smart glasses for filming already risked breaking the law in certain settings, such as in a court where cameras are prohibited.

Kaur added:

Other settings, such as healthcare and education, have existing safeguarding or professional policies that are relevant to smart glasses and any kind of digital recording. NHS England and the Department for Education are currently looking into wearable technology in certain settings as well.

Anna Sabine, a Liberal Democrat MP and a culture spokesperson for the party, who held Wednesday’s debate, said:

Women are reporting waking up after nights out to find videos of themselves have gone viral online, filmed without their knowledge or consent by men wearing smart glasses.

To me, these devices not only have the potential to erode safety and privacy in public spaces, but raise really important questions about consent.

Minister tells energy suppliers to do more to help households tackle unpaid bills this winter

Jillian Ambrose

An energy minister has told the bosses of Great Britain’s biggest energy suppliers to do more to help households get on top of their unpaid energy bills this winter, before the government spends almost £2.5bn to help write off the country’s record high debts.

Energy bosses were summoned to a roundtable meeting on Thursday to discuss the government’s plan to pay down the energy debts of about 2.4m of the hardest-hit households.

The debt relief scheme is part of a wider “circuit-breaker” strategy put forward by the chancellor in the annual budget statement last month. This includes measures to tackle household energy debt, which could reach £7bn by the end of the year.

The minister for energy consumers, Polly Billington, is understood to have told bosses from energy companies including Octopus Energy, British Gas and EDF Energy that suppliers would still need to play a “crucial role” in tackling the longstanding issue of problem debt alongside the government’s multibillion-pound support package.

Suppliers are expected to proactively identify customers who may be under financial strain and offer help such as debt payment plans or pointing them in the direction of debt advice services.

The government is also planning to work with the industry to make it easier to spot households that may need extra help, and to modernise payment systems to make it easier to pay. The government has backed the use of smart meters and smart pre-pay meters to help households track their spending and avoid getting into arrears.

Burnham to force employers to publish salaries on job ads in latest government 'small fix'

Hello and welcome to the UK politics live blog.

Employers will be forced to publish salaries on job adverts, Andy Burnham has announced, in the government’s latest “small fix” to help improve people’s lives.

The prime minister said his government would bring in a legal requirement for companies to say what they pay when listing new jobs, which campaigners say will help young people and women in particular achieve more equal pay.

Burnham said the move would help deal with the frustration of applying for a job without knowing the salary, while officials say that publicising pay will help those in work know if they are being paid fairly in comparison with others.

The prime minister said:

Whether you’re a young person starting out or a parent trying to support your family, you should know what a job pays before spending your time and effort applying for it.

That’s why we will make it a legal requirement for all employers to say what you pay.

But breaking down barriers is only one part of the job. We also need businesses to open more doors and give young people the experience and confidence to take that first step into the world of work.

Paul Nowak, the general secretary of the Trades Union Congress, said: “Anyone who’s searched for a job knows how frustrating it is to read ‘competitive salary’.”

Recent research by the Chartered Institute of Personnel and Development found less than half of employers always published salaries ranges on job adverts, while less than a fifth always published specific salaries.

Experts say omitting pay details from job adverts makes it harder for young people to know which jobs to apply for, and makes it easier for employers to give lower pay offers to some candidates, including women.
