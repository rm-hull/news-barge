The Scottish Government is facing demands from MSPs to explain the “rationale” behind plans for a new company dedicated to traditional music and address financial black holes faced by the country’s five existing flagships.

Culture secretary Màiri McAllan has been told to clear up “significant uncertainty” over the new national performing company.

Holyrood’s culture committee is seeking an explanation of which organisations were consulted before the trad music company was announced by the Scottish Government in the summer.

It has demanded full details of how much it will cost to set up the outfit and the expected annual running costs of the new company, which has been personally championed by First Minister John Swinney.

MSPs are also seeking an “assurance” that additional funding previously promised for Scotland’s national performing companies is “not directly entirely” towards the trad music outfit, instead of providing long-awaited uplifts to the five existing companies.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/19258938.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The Peatbog Faeries emerged from the traditional music scene on the Isle of Skye. *(Image: Ryan Buchanan)*

Ms McAllan has suggested that the new company is being created to address a “considerable anomaly” in Scottish culture.

She told the Scottish Parliament: “It is to offer parity to something that is defining of music and performance in Scotland. I should not like to go on any longer without traditional music having such parity.”

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21653925.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Traditional music acts are celebrated every December at the MG Alba Scots Trad Music Awards. *(Image: Paul Campbell)*

The committee, which has made a series of recommendations to Ms McAllan ahead of the next Scottish Budget, has raised concerns with her about the impact of “deep and sustained real-terms cuts” on the five companies - Scottish Ballet, Scottish Opera, the National Theatre of Scotland, the Scottish Chamber Orchestra and the Royal Scottish National Orchestra.

It has urged the culture secretary to secure the financial future of the five companies after hearing evidence that the overall budget available to support them, which currently amounts to almost £25m, has been cut in “real-terms” by 45.8 per cent since 2013-14.

In its own report for MSPs, arts collective Culture Counts said: “How can Scotland realistically plan to expand its national company model while the companies already delivering it are being asked to do more with significantly less?”

MSPs were recently told that all five companies are projecting a significant deficit in the next financial year after being left on “standstill funding” in the last Scottish Budget.

At the time of the budget announcement in January, they issued a joint statement saying they were “baffled” by the government’s “inexplicable” decision given a previous commitment to invest an additional £100m in culture. They suggested they had been “singled out” for treatment by the government “after effectively 15 years of standstill funding”.

Plans for the National Performing Company for Traditional Music emerged three months later in the SNP’s Holyrood election manifesto. According to the party’s spending plans, it is expected to have an annual budget of £5m.

Mr Swinney confirmed plans for the new company at a private reception for some of Scotland’s leading traditional musicians at Bute House in August, which was held before an official industry consultation.

Concerns about the proposed new company were raised with Ms McAllan at a “roundtable” event she hosted with traditional music organisations after the consultation got underway.

Speaking at the culture committee, Fiona Campbell, convener of the Traditional Music and Song Association of Scotland, said: “We have heard from a number of people, so I will be voicing concerns and views that we have heard from across the sector. People do not know why that was in the manifesto, to be honest. That was a common comment. We do not know where it came from.

“It is not that we do not like the idea of promotion or recognition; the issue is how and why it is done and how it is resourced. It actually almost feels like it needed to be talked about before it was created.”

The Herald recently revealed further concerns about the government’s plans for the new trad music company, which has sparked fears that it will “privilege” certain artists and their material if they go ahead.

More than 150 Scottish singers, musicians, writers, broadcasters, event organisers and traditional arts organisations have signed a letter to Ms McAllan raising concerns about the potential impact of the new company on Scotland’s traditional arts “ecosystem”.

Writing to Ms McAllan, Patrick Harvie, the convener of the culture committee, said: “We recognise that there remains significant uncertainty surrounding the rationale and funding for the new national performing company.

“We note the cabinet secretary’s assertion that the current absence of a national performing company for traditional music represents an ‘anomaly’. However, we ask that the Scottish Government sets out in greater detail the rationale behind the establishment of a new company.”

Mr Harvie said the committee wanted to know how the traditional music sector was involved in developing the proposals for the new company before it was announced, including the details of all organisations that were consulted, what other options were considered for the support and promotion of traditional music, the expected cost of establishing the new company and its annual running costs.

Mr Harvie said the “timing and certainty” of additional funding for the existing companies was key to ensuring that it had “lasting benefits”. He urged Ms McAllan to take steps to ensure the five organisations could “avoid or minimise any deficits or losses” in the next financial year.

He added: “In light of concerns raised by stakeholders, we request that the Scottish Government provides assurance that the new national performing company will be funded in addition to the existing companies, and that any funding uplifts will be directed to the existing companies and not directed entirely towards the new company.”

Speaking at the launch of the traditional music company in August, Mr Swinney said: “Traditional Scottish music has a unique, rich sound reflecting our history and regional diversity. It connects our past to our present and is loved across Scotland and around the world. Giving it the same platform as other performing arts will support established and emerging talents to grow.”

A spokesperson for the government said: “We have provided an uplift of £1 million in funding across our five national performing companies in 2025-26 and they received an additional £700,000 in 2024-25. This investment takes their core funding to the highest level since 2011-12.

“We will continue to support the internationally acclaimed work of our national performing companies in 2026-27 with a view to providing them with additional funding in future financial years as part of the commitment to delivering £100 million for culture. As part of the Scottish Government’s budget process, we will continue to engage with the companies on their funding requirements for 2027-28.”
