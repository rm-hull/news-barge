Beavers have returned to the Loch Ness catchment for the first time in around 500 years, with a family of six released as part of a major rewilding project in the Scottish Highlands.

The animals were released into a lochan at Trees for Life’s Dundreggan estate in Glenmoriston on November 4, after being relocated from elsewhere in Scotland under a licence from NatureScot.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21675439.jpg?type=mds-article-620)One of the beaver kits exploring its new home at Dundreggan loch. *(Image: Beaver Trust)*

The Ness Beaver Project aims to establish at least 15 beaver family groups across lochs, lochans and rivers around Loch Ness over the next three years.

NatureScot granted a licence for the releases on October 30, following an application by Trees for Life earlier this year.

The project aims to help beaver populations grow and connect across a landscape with suitable habitat, including woodlands and extensive river and loch banks.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21675440.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Alan McDonell and Tobias Leask of Trees For Life, carrying a crate containing one of the beaver family down to the release site. *(Image: Beaver Trust)*

Beavers can shape their surroundings by digging channels, coppicing trees and building dams, creating pools, wetlands and open areas in woodland.

These habitats support a range of wildlife, including frogs, dragonflies, birds and bats. On smaller watercourses, dams can slow water flows and trap sediment, improving water quality and reducing downstream flood risk.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21675446.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Guests and team members opening the crates, releasing the first family of wild liing beavers into the Ness Catchment at Dundreggan as part of the Ness Beaver Project. *(Image: Beaver Trust)*

Wetlands created by beavers can also retain water during dry periods and provide damp refuges for wildlife during wildfires.

The project is being delivered by Trees for Life in partnership with Beaver Trust and Five Sisters Zoo. Future release sites will be announced after the animals have been safely released, to allow them to settle without disturbance.

Alan McDonnell, head of nature restoration at Trees for Life, said: "The Ness Beaver Project is now set to be a major milestone for nature recovery in Britain.

"Hunted to extinction centuries ago, beavers are reclaiming their place in our rivers and wetlands – breathing new life into precious landscapes, boosting biodiversity, and bringing eco-tourism opportunities.

"Five hundred years since Loch Ness became the last recorded site of Scotland’s beavers before they were eradicated, they are back with the space and habitat to flourish – bringing with them the potential for new wetlands, more wildlife, and healthier rivers.

"The return of these habitat-creating, biodiversity-boosting, flood-preventing animals is now one of Scotland’s biggest rewilding success stories."

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21675447.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Releasing the first family of wild liing beavers into the Ness Catchment at Dundreggan. *(Image: Beaver Trust)*

Dr Roisin Campbell-Palmer, head of restoration at Beaver Trust, said: "It has been profoundly rewarding to release the first beavers at Dundreggan, marking the start of their return to the Loch Ness catchment.

"The extensive woodland and suitable habitat here offer real opportunities for the animals to thrive and bring lasting biodiversity benefits.

"We look forward to seeing the changes the beavers bring and to working with Trees for Life, local communities, and land managers over the next three years to support further releases and help people live alongside them.

"This project is an important step towards delivering Scotland’s National Beaver Strategy by expanding the population into suitable areas, with monitoring and practical support to help address any challenges along the way."

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21675438.jpg?type=mds-article-620)One of the beaver kits exploring its new home at Dundreggan loch. *(Image: Beaver Trust)*

Gary Curran, collection manager at Five Sisters Zoo, said: "Since Scotland’s first official reintroduction trial in 2009, growing wild populations of beavers have shown what is possible when people work together to restore a lost species.

"The return of beavers to Loch Ness, one of Scotland’s most iconic landscapes, marks a major turning point in this extraordinary national recovery, and we are delighted to be playing a role in this."
