The SNP has been urged to launch disciplinary action against its own chief executive over the cancellation of a controversial abortion event at its annual conference.

A formal complaint has been lodged accusing Callum McCaig of bypassing SNP rules and governing structures after he withdrew permission for a conference fringe event organised by anti-abortion group the Society for the Protection of Unborn Children (SPUC).

The event was due to discuss coerced abortion and what the SPUC describes as gaps in Scotland’s legal protections for women pressured into terminating their pregnancies.

The SNP has already been threatened with legal action after party officials, led by chief executive Mr McCaig, cancelled the event after complaints from party members.

It is claimed SNP chiefs raised no concerns about the nature of the event when it was booked for a fee in August.

The party later said it had been “wrong” to allow the event to go ahead, saying SNP conference should be a place where all members could “feel comfortable”.

Now SPUC chief executive John Deighan has escalated the row by lodging a formal complaint with SNP business convener Angela Constance, demanding an investigation into Mr McCaig’s handling of the cancellation.

In the letter, seen exclusively by The Herald, the SPUC asks Ms Constance to refer the matter to the party’s Member Conduct Committee and relevant disciplinary body to investigate whether proper National Executive Committee (NEC) procedures were bypassed.

It accuses McCaig of making an “arbitrary” decision and claims responsibility for conference arrangements rested with the NEC and business convener rather than the chief executive.

The complaint cites comments from Mr McCaig in which he said he “withdrew permission” and that his “decision has not changed”.

The anti-abortion group claims he bypassed proper governance channels by cancelling the event six days before it was due to take place and argues it was “unreasonable and unfair” that the organisation was not given an opportunity to respond to concerns raised by SNP members.

The complaint states: “By capitulating to outside petition pressure and issuing an unprocedural cancellation a mere six days before the event, Mr McCaig bypassed proper governance channels.”

It also accuses him of bringing the SNP into disrepute and exposing the party to potential legal and financial consequences.

The SPUC claims the cancellation breached Scots law, including provisions of the Consumer Rights Act 2015, although the allegations have not been tested in court.

The group had previously claimed the decision could breach the Equality Act 2010 and said it was seeking formal legal advice.

The Herald understands legal action remains firmly on the table pending the outcome of any SNP disciplinary investigation.

The SNP is now being asked to investigate the authority under which Mr McCaig acted and establish clearer rules for future conference fringe bookings.

SNP chiefs withdrew the event, which was supposed to be held at SNP conference in Aberdeen last month, following opposition from Women for Independence, while a petition calling for the fringe’s removal attracted more than 250 signatories.

SNP MSPs Emma Roddick and Karen Adam backed the calls.

Critics of the event said it was being “presented as raising awareness on the ‘reality of coerced abortion’” but was in fact a “progressive veil of the outdated and bigoted beliefs”.

The row also drew significant criticism from John Keenan, Bishop of Paisley, and president of the Bishops’ Conference of Scotland, who told The Herald that freedom of speech had “little meaning if it extends only to those with whom we agree”.

An SNP spokes­per­son said: “Fol­low­ing con­cerns being raised by SNP mem­bers we took the decision to revoke per­mis­sion for SPUC to hold a fringe event at our con­fer­ence.

“We want SNP conference to be a place where all members can feel comfortable and it is clear our initial decision was wrong as it did not achieve that.”

The Herald asked the party for additional comment.

Founded in 1967, SPUC has long drawn controversy for its hard-line campaigning.

Beyond calling for abortion to be recriminalised, the group opposes emergency contraception and certain forms of birth control, sex education in schools and the introduction of buffer zones around abortion clinics.

Critics, including women’s rights organisations and medical bodies, have repeatedly accused the group of spreading misleading claims about reproductive health and staging intimidating demonstrations outside clinics.
