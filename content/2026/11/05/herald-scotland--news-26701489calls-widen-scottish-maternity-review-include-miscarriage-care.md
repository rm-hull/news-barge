Scottish Labour's Jackie Baillie has accused the Scottish Government of being "complacent" over failures in miscarriage care, warning of "considerable disquiet" among women and families about maternity services across Scotland.

Her comments come just days after First Minister John Swinney ruled out a public inquiry into maternity and neonatal deaths, despite growing pressure from bereaved families.

The deputy Scottish Labour leader said women's health was not being given the priority it deserved as she called for an independent maternity review to explicitly include miscarriage support.

Speaking at Holyrood's Citizen Participation and Public Petitions Committee, Ms Baillie raised concerns about access to treatment, continuity of care and safeguards for women navigating gynaecology, fertility and maternity services.

She urged MSPs to write to Professor Christine McCourt, who is leading the maternity review, asking her to consider concerns raised in a petition brought by Elizabeth Crammond.

She said she was refused an urgent face-to-face assessment despite experiencing bleeding and paid for private scans before losing her baby.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21287551.jpg?type=mds-article-620) *(Image: Supplied)*

She has warned that bereaved women are being "completely ignored" in efforts to reform Scotland's maternity services.

"If I were ever to have a future pregnancy, I would be terrified," she told The Herald.

Ms Crammond's petition calls for GPs to have a clear duty of care when women move between gynaecology, fertility, early pregnancy and maternity services.

It also seeks a reactive auditing system to identify delays, safety concerns and inconsistencies in treatment across health boards.

She argues women can find themselves caught between services, with responsibility for their care unclear and support fragmented.

Ms Crammond's case was raised by Ms Baillie at First Minister's Questions in September, when Mr Swinney acknowledged the service had not worked as designed.

Addressing the committee today, Ms Baillie said: "There are lots of examples of lack of access to care, lack of continuity of care, effectiveness of safeguards seem to be absent, and she is one of a considerable number of women who have experienced these.

"I can only describe it as trauma with miscarriage care as it currently operates across Scotland."

She added: "The Scottish Government response, which I've read several times, is just a little complacent in my view, and I say that respectfully.

"And there is no mention, for example, of the review by Professor Christina McCourt, and maybe that's because the review appears to be quite limited in scope.

"There is considerable disquiet out there, not just about miscarriage support, but about a whole range of gynaecology, maternity, and neonatal support in Scotland."

Ms Baillie also pointed to families demanding a public inquiry following the deaths of babies linked to serious adverse events.

She said: "We have patients, as you know, mothers and fathers demanding a public inquiry because of the loss of their baby as a result of a serious adverse event, and there is huge dissatisfaction there.

"It is clear that women's health is not the priority that it should be."

The Herald revealed that NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde recorded 736 serious adverse events in maternity and neonatal services between 2019 and 2025, including 406 classified as involving a death.

As part of a four-day long investigation into maternity services, the paper also revealed that nearly £100 million has been paid out by NHS Scotland in maternity negligence claims over the past five years. Maternity and obstetric negligence claims accounted for just over two-fifths of all NHS Scotland compensation payments over the four most recent financial years.

More than 100 parents attended a protest in Glasgow on Sunday demanding accountability, including Julie Keegan and Angus McLean, whose son Mason died aged three days at the Queen Elizabeth University Hospital in January 2023.

Ms Baillie recently raised their case at First Minister's Questions, warning that families did not believe existing investigations were working or that Professor McCourt's review went far enough.

However, Mr Swinney rejected calls for a public inquiry, insisting the independent review would provide recommendations for improvements.

"I don't support a public inquiry," he told MSPs.

Bereaved parents have criticised the maternity review's forward-looking approach, arguing it will not adequately investigate historical failures or establish whether deaths could have been prevented.

Professor McCourt's review is expected to report in July 2027.

In its response to the petition, the Scottish Government said GPs already had responsibilities for assessing, managing and referring patients, making a reinstated duty unnecessary.

Ministers also pointed to ongoing work with NHS boards and Healthcare Improvement Scotland to improve maternity safety.

But Ms Baillie urged the committee to ask Professor McCourt "whether she would explicitly consider miscarriage support and many of the issues raised within this petition".

MSPs agreed to keep the petition open and consider a wider evidence session on women's health.

They also backed writing to ministers about action taken following the First Minister's acknowledgement of problems with maternity care.

Committee convener Paul Sweeney suggested the petition could be considered for an early parliamentary debate.
