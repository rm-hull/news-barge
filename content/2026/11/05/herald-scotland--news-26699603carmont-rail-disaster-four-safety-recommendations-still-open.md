Rail safety issues from the Carmont disaster remain unresolved more than six years after the crash that killed three people, The Herald can reveal.

Four recommendations remain open, covering control-room decisions during abnormal weather, safety assurance, weather-related infrastructure failures and measures to reduce the consequences of a derailment. They are still listed as open by the Rail Accident Investigation Branch (RAIB).

One union official said the outstanding work “makes you wonder if they actually care about stopping another disaster”.

The official added: “Until every single safety recommendation is completely locked in, the victims, their families, and the travelling public are going to remain deeply suspicious.”

The four outstanding recommendations stem from the derailment near Carmont, Aberdeenshire, on August 12, 2020, when a ScotRail train travelling from Aberdeen to Glasgow Queen Street struck debris washed on to the track during heavy rain.

Driver Brett McCullough, 45, conductor Donald Dinnie, 58, and passenger Christopher Stuchbury, 62, died when the 6:38am high speed Aberdeen to Glasgow Queen Street train left the tracks after hitting washed-out landslide debris near Carmont during heavy rainfall. Six other people were injured.

They were the only nine people on the train due to pandemic restrictions.

A fatal accident inquiry found that basic safety checks could have prevented the disaster. Sheriff Lesley Johnston ruled that Network Rail, which is responsible for the infrastructure including the tracks and signals, should have ensured a newly built drain was constructed properly and that the required safety documents were handed over.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/13803930.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Flashback to the Carmont rail crash *(Image: RAIB)*

The inquiry found that the drain could not cope with the heavy rainfall because it had not been built according to its design. Debris was carried on to the track, where it caused the train to derail.

There had also been a warning during construction. Water had flowed through a gully and caused slight erosion to the surface of the new drain before the work was complete, but no action was take The RAIB identified that as a missed opportunity to recognise how the drain’s earth bank affected the flow of water, and said it may have contributed to the crash.

The four recommendations still open address different parts of the railway’s response to the risks exposed by Carmont. They include how control-room staff make decisions during unusual or dangerous conditions, how Network Rail checks that safety procedures are being followed, how the railway assesses weather-related risks to infrastructure, and how the consequences of a derailment might be reduced.

One recommendation that has not been resolved calls for Network Rail, working with train operators, to review whether route-control rooms can manage complex incidents such as abnormal weather and multiple infrastructure failures.

The review is meant to examine whether control rooms have enough staff with the right technical and decision-making skills, experience and knowledge, and whether responsibilities are clearly defined. It also calls for appropriate training and a system to check that staff remain competent in their roles.

The Office of Rail and Road (ORR), which monitors rail safety, said Network Rail had set out a plan involving staff training, competence frameworks and work to improve understanding of the effects of abnormal weather.

But the regulator said it would need evidence that the new or refreshed frameworks were in place and that Network Rail could show its training and competence system gave staff the confidence and ability to make difficult decisions during adverse weather.

Another recommendation that remains open concerns the way Network Rail checks whether safety-critical work in route-control rooms is being carried out properly.

It calls for a project to identify weaknesses in management arrangements and the reasons for any failures to comply with safety requirements. The work is also intended to produce a structured programme of improvements, endorsed by the Network Rail board, with changes explained to frontline staff.

![Three people died in the crash in 2020](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/20463003.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Three people died in the crash in 2020 *(Image: Jane Barlow/PA Wire)*

The ORR said Network Rail had established a working group to identify the activities that need to be checked and develop a better assurance process. The work is linked to the review of staff competence required.

Another that has not been resolved concerns the risks of weather-related failures involving earthworks, drainage and structures.

Network Rail and the Rail Safety and Standards Board are required to carry out a systematic risk assessment of the measures intended to limit the effects of such failures, including the operational response. The assessment should identify weaknesses and lead to a time-limited plan to address them.

The ORR said the assessment was being undertaken and that it would consider closing it off once Network Rail had produced a suitable plan to deal with any weaknesses identified.

Another that remains open concerns the risk of a train derailing and the measures that might help guide or contain it after it leaves the rails.

Network Rail, the Rail Delivery Group and the Rail Safety and Standards Board are required to consider lessons from Carmont when assessing train and track design. The work includes the risk of derailment following small landslips or washouts, the position of the track in relation to nearby ground, and the possible consequences of a derailed train striking structures such as bridge parapets or tunnel entrances.

The recommendation called for a risk-based strategy to assess whether additional measures should be installed on the railway or incorporated into trains, and for any necessary changes to industry standards.

The ORR’s assessment said the work was being carried out through an industry research project examining methods of derailment mitigation. The project included modelling how a derailed train might move and how different safety measures could affect the outcome.

The regulator said the research had shown that some measures might not be practical to retrofit to existing trains or infrastructure, while others could be effective only in particular circumstances. It also noted that some devices could make the consequences of a derailment worse if they deflected a train in the wrong way.

The project was expected to produce a strategy and related guidance, with work initially forecast to be completed by the end of 2025. The recommendation remains listed as open in the latest status summary.

One union official said the delay raised questions about accountability.

“Families would be rightly asking how they can expect the exact same bosses who enabled these fatal errors to be the ones tasked with fixing them? It makes no sense that the people who caused the problem are now stalling the solution.”

The official added: “Accountability cannot happen behind closed doors.

“For the victims and their families, the sheer mystery of how this catastrophe was allowed to happen was agonising. Their entire worlds were shattered in the public eye, while the people whose blunders caused the derailment just went back to business as usual.”

ScotRail has said it worked closely with infrastructure owner Network Rail to implement recommendations made by the RAIB, “to do everything possible to reduce the risk of something like this ever happening again”.

A Network Rail Scotland spokesperson said: “The FAI report records that the vast majority of the RAIB recommendations have been implemented and that the remaining open recommendations are being actively addressed by the industry.

“We continue to work closely with the ORR, train operators, and other stakeholders, to fully implement our response to Carmont and we have made significant changes to improve safety on Scotland’s railway.

“Since the accident, we have changed how we operate services during periods of extreme weather and have increased our investment in new technologies to help manage the impact of climate change on Scotland’s railway. Scotland’s integrated control room is also the first in Britain to establish a 24/7 specialist weather team to help manage weather events in real-time.”
