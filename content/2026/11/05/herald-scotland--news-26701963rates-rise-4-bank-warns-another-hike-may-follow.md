The Bank of England has raised interest rates to 4% from 3.75% and forecast that inflation will rise to a peak of around 5% next year.

It is the first hike in more than three years.

Andrew Bailey, the Bank’s governor, said the decision “shouldn’t be seen as the first in an inevitably long sequence of hikes”, but warned: “Unless the conflict in the Middle East is resolved, neither does it seem likely to be the last increase.”

All members of the bank's nine-person Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) voted for a hike, although one member, Catherine Mann, preferred a bigger jump of 0.5 percentage points.

The MPC said many companies had so far been absorbing higher energy costs into their profit margins or had hedged against large spikes since the war began around nine months ago.

But it warned that such “buffers would only prove temporary and these cost pressures would eventually pass through into higher consumer prices if the energy shock persisted”.

Interest rates are typically increased in a bid to bring down the rate of inflation, amid hopes that higher borrowing costs will weigh on demand and therefore cause firms to limit price increases.

The Bank of England said on Thursday that it currently expects inflation will continue to accelerate in the coming months before peaking at “around 5%” in the second quarter of 2027.

It is then likely to steadily fall until it reaches the central bank’s 2% target in the third quarter of 2028.

The projected rise in the cost of living comes despite efforts from Andy Burnham’s Government to support households in last week’s budget.

Bank research indicated that measures from the Budget, such as the removal of VAT from household electricity bills, are likely to reduce the inflation rate by around 0.25% in the second quarter of next year.

The inflation spike is primarily driven to higher energy prices, linked to the conflict in the Middle East.

Household energy bills are set to be a particular driver of inflation, with expectations that regulator Ofgem will increase its price cap for household bills by almost 25% to an average annual bill of £2,150 in January.

The Bank’s report indicated that higher energy costs could feed further into supply chains by next year, as some firms see their hedged energy prices unwind.

It predicted that British households will also face more expensive food, with food inflation expected to rise as high as 4.8% next year.

This was linked to higher energy costs for manufacturers as well as potential supply chain pressure linked to the El Nino weather system.

Nevertheless, the Bank said the UK economy has been more resilient than previously predicted in recent months.

It said higher business investment, partly linked to AI, helped to drive stronger growth so far this year.

It said the UK economy is on track to grow by 1.6% this year, up sharply from a previous forecast of 1.1%.

The economy is then likely to see growth slightly to 1.3% amid pressure from higher inflation, but this is still above the 1.1% growth projected in July.

However, the Bank said it downgraded its growth forecasts for 2028 and 2029 as elevated inflation and interest rates weighs on potential demand.

Responding, Chancellor of the Exchequer John Healey said: “The Iran conflict is reaching deep into the pockets of families and businesses here at home.

“In my Budget I acted to give some breathing space to families and businesses. Twenty million households will get a £100 energy payment, I extended the tax cut on electricity bills and froze fuel duty.

“I also set out the course ahead to get costs down for good. That includes big changes to our energy system so Britain is better protected from global shocks in the future.”

The SNP’s economy spokesperson, Kirsty Blackman, described the rise as “another hammer blow to hard-pressed households who are facing yet another year of soaring bills under the Labour government”.

She added: “Andy Burnham promised he would cut the cost of living but every major household bill has risen on his watch. Energy bills will soon be more than £1,000 higher than the Labour Party promised, the cost of food and fuel is sky-high, and mortgage rates are already at 6% – adding an extra £5,000 a year to average mortgage interest payments compared to 2021.”

Nathan Emerson, chief executive of Propertymark, said there was “little surprise” in the rise.

He added: “While these decisions are never straightforward, the Bank of England must carefully balance the need to control inflation against the wider impact that borrowing costs can have on households.

“Last week’s Autumn Budget acknowledged several important consumer concerns, including rising energy costs and the ongoing challenges facing many first-time buyers.

"Measures aimed at addressing some of these pressures are focused on stability, particularly where they could help improve access to homeownership and ease the long-term financial burden on households.

"Over the coming months, much will depend on how inflation and consumer confidence evolve.”
