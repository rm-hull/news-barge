One of the privileges of writing about business is that you frequently discover the question you started with is not quite the question that matters.

That happened repeatedly last month as I found myself writing about everything from luxury golf tourism and car dealerships to business rates, artificial intelligence and a deeply contentious golf development. Underneath, however, were variations on the same question: what happens when some of the assumptions on which a business, or an investment, has been built begin to change?

That was particularly striking in St Andrews, where the Old Course Hotel reported softer demand from North America. Revenue fell 2% to £30.3 million and the hotel reported a £1.15m pre-tax loss, with its directors pointing to weaker North American demand.

For a business at the luxury end of one of Scotland's strongest tourism markets, it was an interesting reminder that even apparently dependable sources of international demand cannot simply be taken for granted.

![Chris Clark, group managing director of John Clark Motor Group](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21634099.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Chris Clark, group managing director of John Clark Motor Group *(Image: Kabir Aram Zaman)*

The pressures were rather different John Clark Motor Group, one of Scotland's biggest car dealers. The company remained a £1 billion-plus business last year despite a fall in pre-tax profits, which dropped from £19m to £12.9m.

Its results reflected pressures including employment costs, supply disruption and weaker used-car values. But there was another story in there too: the group's expansion of its representation of Chinese car brands including BYD, Geely and Changan. It was a useful example of an established Scottish company adjusting its business as the wider market changes.

Sometimes, of course, the pressure comes not from customers or competitors but from the environment in which companies operate.

That was at the heart of our Reset the Rates campaign, where I delved into Scottish Government data to examine how the business rates burden is distributed around the country. My analysis found Edinburgh had overtaken Glasgow in its contribution to Scotland's non-domestic rates pool, contributing £397.5m in 2025-26 against Glasgow's £396.8m.

The work also underlined why data can add something important to business reporting. Looking beneath the national totals exposed significant differences between individual council areas and helped put the experiences of businesses caught up in the rates debate into a wider context.

Technology provided another variation on the theme.

When Barclays announced plans to cut around 5,000 jobs globally, the bank would not say whether Glasgow would be affected. But comments from its chief executive about artificial intelligence helping employees become more productive pointed towards a much bigger issue.

The interesting question is increasingly not whether AI will change white-collar work, but what work is left for people to learn their professions on. If technology takes on more routine junior tasks, how do today's recruits acquire the experience needed to become tomorrow's senior professionals?

![Coul Links](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21625797.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Ministers have refused permission for the proposed Coul Links golf course near Embo *(Image: Ramblers Scotland)*

Perhaps the most difficult choices I wrote about, however, arose at Coul Links in the Highlands, where ministers rejected plans for a new golf course. Planning reporters concluded that the economic benefits did not clearly outweigh significant effects on important natural assets.

Investment decisions rarely happen in isolation. Jobs matter, and so does growth. But so do taxation, technology, environmental concerns and changing patterns of demand.

None of these stories offers a simple argument that change is good or bad. What interested me was how businesses respond when circumstances they cannot entirely control begin forcing decisions upon them.

Costs rise, customers change, technology advances and public policy shifts. Perhaps the businesses best equipped for what comes next will not necessarily be those that predict the future correctly, but those that recognise soon enough when the assumptions that got them this far no longer hold.
