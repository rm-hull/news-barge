Since 2023, the Scottish Business Monitor, run by the Fraser of Allander Institute at the University of Strathclyde, has asked firms each year about their relationship with the Scottish Government. In previous years that relationship has looked strained, and this year it has moved in a more negative direction.

That raises a question: can the start of the Seventh Session of the Scottish Parliament provide the Scottish Government with a chance to improve its standing with businesses?

Seven in ten firms told us they do not believe the Scottish Government understands the business environment (69%), and a similar share do not believe it engages effectively with businesses on policy issues (71%).

Businesses are often frustrated with governments, regardless of party politics, but this relationship needs to be tended carefully because faith in policymakers’ ability to manage the economy is a driver of business sentiment. Sentiment in turn shapes decisions about hiring, expansion, and investment.

The detail behind those headline figures is stark. Just 12% of firms agreed that the Scottish Government understands the business environment in Scotland, while two in five firms (40%) strongly disagreed. This level of disagreement is the highest we have seen since we began asking these questions. Last year 41% disagreed, and in 2023 and 2024 the figure was 62–64%, so this year’s 69% reverses the improvement we reported in 2025. Much of the change comes from firms that previously gave a neutral answer now taking a view, and more of them have come down on the negative side.

This matters for more than goodwill. If a firm feels poorly understood or cannot anticipate how a policy announcement will affect it, they are more likely to see government as a source of volatility. The rest of this quarter’s survey bears that out: almost every firm (99%) said economic uncertainty was an important concern for the months ahead, and 93% said the same about political uncertainty.

This uncertainty particularly affects investment decisions, which require long-term commitments. This matters because business investment is a key driver of long-term growth yet remains the weakest of our domestic indicators, with a net balance (i.e., share of firms responding ‘higher’ minus the share responding ‘lower’) of -18%. It has been negative in every quarter of the past two years, and firms expect it to fall further over the next six months.

More broadly, expectations for sales over the next six months fell from a net balance of 10% to 3%, below the post-pandemic average of 6%. Three-quarters of firms expect weak or very weak growth in the Scottish economy over the coming year, representing middling levels of sentiment.

None of this means the picture is entirely bleak. All six of our headline activity indicators improved on last quarter. For the first time in over two years, more firms reported higher sales volume in the previous quarter than those who reported lower. Turnover improved similarly, which suggests the pick-up is real activity and not simply higher prices. Cost pressures, though still widespread, also eased slightly. The weakness in sentiment is therefore less about how firms are trading today and more about their confidence in what comes next. Their relationship with The Scottish Government is part of that.

There are also some signs of progress on that relationship. Although negative responses have risen, so too have positive responses: agreement that the Scottish Government engages effectively with businesses has doubled from 6% in 2024 to 12%. Notably, more than one in five firms (22%) now say they know an effective route to influence Scottish Government policy when an announcement is likely to affect them. That is up from 14% last year and the highest share since the question was introduced.

Taken together, these findings point to a business community that wants to engage but feels that engagement is not being met halfway. A majority of firms (53%) still say they do not know an effective route to influence policy, suggesting channels into government are either unclear or not seen to be effective. The start of the new Scottish Parliamentary Session seems the natural moment for further improvement.

So, what do firms want the new Parliament to focus on? This year we asked them to choose the single devolved policy area that should be the main business priority for the session: a third of firms (34%) chose business rates, more than twice the share selecting any other option.

Skills and training came second (14%), which is no surprise when 88% of firms cite staff availability as an important concern. Income tax followed at 12%, reflecting worries about the effect of Scotland’s higher rates on recruitment and retention. Meanwhile very few chose regulation (3%) or digital support (1%).

The strength of feeling on non-domestic rates is understandable. Rates are one of the most direct and visible costs the Scottish Government places on a business, and they must be paid whether or not the business is profitable. With 82% of firms still reporting rising costs this quarter, and 86% expecting costs to rise further, a tax that firms see as unresponsive to their circumstances is likely to provoke frustration.

However, there are no easy answers here. Non-domestic rates raise significant revenue for public services, so any reform involves trade-offs that the Scottish Government and Scottish Parliament will need to weigh openly. But the clarity of the message gives the Scottish Government something it does not often have: a clear signal of what businesses consider the priority.

With the Scottish Budget approaching, the new Scottish Parliament has an opportunity. Clearer engagement and more predictable policy could reduce some of the uncertainty that firms say is holding back their confidence and their willingness to invest. How the Scottish Government approaches business rates, and how well it explains its choices, will be an early test of whether it can rebuild trust with Scotland’s businesses.

*Josh Hampson is an assistant economist at the Fraser of Allander Institute, at the University of Strathclyde.*
