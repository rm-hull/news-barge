*Spending just £1 more locally could generate millions for Scotland’s makers and economy*

What if the greatest gift we could give Scotland this Christmas was the chance for thousands of small businesses to grow?

As Black Friday approaches, our inboxes fill with discounts, social media becomes an endless stream of advertisements and we are encouraged to buy more, spend faster and think less. Yet beyond the noise, talented Scottish makers are creating beautiful things, often working alone, trying to build businesses around skills they have spent years developing.

I wonder how often we consider what our Christmas spending could mean to them, or to Scotland's economy.

According to calculations published by Scotland's Towns Partnership in August 2025, if everyone in Scotland spent just £1 more each week with local businesses, an additional £282.7 million could be directed towards our regional economies annually. Just £1 a week. Imagine what even a modest change in our Christmas shopping habits could mean.

For many independent makers, Christmas is not simply an opportunity to increase sales. It can determine whether their businesses survive the quieter months that follow.

David McDiarmid creates colourful artwork and homewares inspired by food, from ceramic pasta-shaped fridge magnets to paintings of favourite ingredients. Everything is made in his East Glasgow studio, with materials sourced from the UK wherever possible. His work is playful and distinctive, but behind those cheerful creations lies the less glamorous reality of running a small business.

David estimates that more than half his studio time is spent on administration, marketing, accounts and packing orders. Having built my own sustainable fashion brand, I recognise that frustration. The joy of creating something can quickly become overwhelmed by everything required to keep the business going. You are the designer, manufacturer, marketer, accountant and, quite often, the person cleaning up at the end of the day.

"The three-month period leading up to Christmas is the most sustainable part of the year for me," David explains. "If I could make what I do in that three-month period throughout the rest of the year, then I'd be running a comfortable, sustainable business."

His experience is echoed by Edinburgh jeweller Eilidh Geraghty, founder of MAIRIDH, meaning everlasting in Scots Gaelic. A former PE teacher turned self-taught jeweller, Eilidh creates timeless pieces as an alternative to disposable costume jewellery.

Each piece demands meticulous finishing and polishing, often leaving her looking, in her own words, *"like I work in a coal mine"*. A reminder of the considerable work behind something so beautifully finished.

Christmas has previously accounted for just under half Eilidh's annual turnover, making those few weeks crucial to sustaining her business. *"Choosing an independent maker means the money spent on your gift goes directly to another family,"* she explains. Behind every handmade gift is someone working hard to turn their skills into a livelihood.

But while Christmas helps sustain businesses like David's and Eilidh's, I believe Scotland is missing a much bigger opportunity. Scotland's economic ambitions often focus on attracting major investment and nurturing the next high-growth technology company. Yet across the country, talented entrepreneurs are already creating exceptional products, many struggling to turn their skills into a sustainable income. What could be achieved if greater attention and support were directed towards helping these businesses grow?

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21642950.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Edinburgh jeweller Eilidh Geraghty, founder of MAIRIDH *(Image: Supplied)*

That is the ambition behind Makerteers, a new Scottish accelerator founded by Ryder Meggitt, Dr Matt Benning and Leanne Anderson. Both David and Eilidh have secured places in its first cohort.

Speaking to Ryder and Leanne, I was struck by the potential scale of this opportunity. Ryder asks us to imagine helping a maker grow their annual business turnover from £10,000 to £60,000, potentially transforming a part-time venture into a full-time livelihood. Multiply that across thousands of Scottish makers and the collective economic impact could be substantial, without a single business becoming the next billion-pound success story.

Of course, turnover is not personal income, but the principle matters. Growth can come from thousands of smaller businesses becoming stronger, buying materials, supporting suppliers and creating employment.

Having participated in business accelerators myself, I know how poorly conventional support can fit those who manufacture physical products. Advice often centres on technology and services, while makers must finance materials, manage production, hold stock and find customers, often before seeing any financial return.

Makerteers aims to address this gap through support with confidence, branding, production and technology. Its introductory events in Glasgow and Edinburgh both sold out, while applications exceeded available places.

Around 80 per cent of applicants identified sales and cashflow as their greatest challenge, with marketing and visibility close behind. These are people who know how to make things. What they need is help turning that creativity into commercial success.

And consumers and retailers have a role to play. As Leanne observes, *"Makers aren't the ones that have got these flashy ads."* Independent businesses cannot compete with the advertising budgets of global brands, particularly during Black Friday.

They need greater visibility, opportunities to sell through retailers and access to customers beyond Scotland. Supporting Scottish manufacturing should never mean thinking small. We should be proudly making things here and selling them to the world.

We also need to reconsider how we value what people make. Handmade products cannot always compete with mass production on price, nor should we expect them to. The skill, time and care involved are fundamentally different. While local production is not automatically environmentally sustainable, choosing something well-made and designed to last offers an alternative to disposable consumption.

Eilidh encourages people visiting Christmas markets to take time to discover the stories behind the products rather than rushing through a shopping list. *"All we want is to say hello and get the chance to tell as many people as possible what we do,"* she says.

I love that idea of shopping as a conversation, a discovery, a connection with the person who actually made something.

Of course, Christmas is expensive, and I am certainly not suggesting families should spend more than they can afford. But where we choose to spend the money already in our Christmas budgets matters.

And this brings me back to the importance of supporting independent Scottish businesses like David's and Eilidh's. As Scotland's Towns Partnership highlights, spending just £1 more a week with local businesses could generate an additional £282.7 million annually across Scotland. That means more than increased sales.

It means businesses growing, jobs being created, wages supporting local families and greater economic activity generating tax revenues that help fund our NHS, schools and public services. The benefits of that small change in spending could reach far beyond the businesses themselves, strengthening the communities in which we live.

For me, this is what economic growth should ultimately deliver. Not simply impressive figures on a spreadsheet, but decent livelihoods, stronger communities, thriving high streets and opportunities for the next generation to build their futures here in Scotland.

And that is the real power of choosing local this Christmas. We don't need to spend more to make a difference, just think more carefully about where our money goes. Because every pound we choose to spend with a Scottish maker is more than a purchase. It helps build the jobs, opportunities and thriving communities on which Scotland's future depends.

*Dr Antoinette Fionda-Douglas is co-founder of Beira, and assistant professor at Heriot-Watt University*
