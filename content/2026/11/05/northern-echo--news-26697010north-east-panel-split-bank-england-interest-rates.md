A split decision has emerged from business leaders in the North East regarding the future of interest rates.

The North East Shadow Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), made up of business leaders from across the region, met to debate the Bank of England’s base rate ahead of the official MPC’s meeting later today..

The panel’s vote was divided, with two members calling for a rate cut, three in favour of holding steady, and four backing a small increase.

The North East Shadow MPC is a partnership between Recognition PR, the Northern Echo, and Mercury Wealth.

Oliver Bourke, managing director of Mercury Wealth and one of the members favouring a cut, said: "I'd favour a cautious quarter-point cut.

"There needs to be a bit of relief on the pressure that borrowers are having thinking about mortgage costs and people coming out of their long-term fixed rates that they might have had during the pandemic.

"I think we're starting to see some of those bite now.

"But I do think that we do need to recognise that there is that inflation risk.

"I think at the moment we have cost-push inflation, and I think that's as a result of energy and fuel."

James Dale, partner at Anderson Barrowcliff, also voted for a cut. He said: "I think the economy just needs to be stimulated.

"I think the government's doing everything it can against stimulating the economy, and I just think business owners need more cash in the pocket to go and make investment decisions.

"So I appreciate the inflation argument, but I just think it's getting critical now for small business."

Others felt it was too soon to reduce rates.

Simon Bailes of Simon Bailes Peugeot said: "I think it’s unrealistic looking for a cut basically.

"That's the reason I decided on the hold.

"And certainly from the major borrowings that we need to do from a customer point of view, which is the motor company's finances, we are not going to see a cut in rates."

Claire Maith, chief financial officer at Eastgate Engineering, agreed, citing a slowing economy and global factors behind inflation.

She said: "From my perspective, the economy is already slowing.

"OBR's predicted 1 per cent growth, which obviously we don't want to hurt business investment, which is why I think we shouldn't increase interest rates."

Discussing the potential impact of an increase, Dr Oliver Jones, head of sustainability – North East at Cundall, warned that higher rates could hurt regeneration projects.

He said: "Adding a further rise would increase the cost of that sort of development finance and make already marginal schemes in the North East, particularly the regeneration schemes, harder to finance, potentially delaying that investment, the investment the budget was trying to stimulate in the first place.

"I guess the counter argument would be inflation and rates may need to remain restrictive if those inflationary pressures persist."

Others saw a rise as necessary to keep inflation under control.

Martyn Pullen, partner at FRP Advisory, said: "I could see the argument for a hold, but we cannot let inflation get away from us.

"The factors that are affecting our inflation rates are probably outside the UK and most of the Western governments' control and are in one man's hands to a large degree."

"And there are people on fixed incomes who are going to take interest from their savings and be taxed more.

"So putting the rate up just a smidge will maybe ameliorate that."
