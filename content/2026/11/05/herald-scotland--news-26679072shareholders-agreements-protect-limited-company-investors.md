Limited companies come in all shapes and sizes. Likewise, their owners can range from an individual with a handful of shares to a partner in the business or a large institutional shareholder, such as a pension scheme.

One of the features of a limited company is the flexibility of its ownership. Existing shareholders may wish to increase their stake in the business, while new shareholders can also buy into an established company.

There is no general legal requirement for anything in writing beyond the shares themselves. It is possible to buy and sell shares, or issue new shares to shareholders, and simply carry on based on verbal agreements, trust or informal understandings.

Let's remember that a company has its own legal identity, separate from its shareholders and directors. Owning a slice of the business does not automatically give or imply control, unless an individual, either alone or together with other shareholders, holds sufficient voting rights to influence the direction the company takes.

Common sense and good practice dictate that anyone investing money or other assets in a company should have some protection and the ability to have their voice heard in the management of the business.

One standard way of regulating the rights and responsibilities of shareholders is through a shareholders' agreement. This is a legal contract signed by the shareholders, setting out their relationship with the company and with each other.

It should cover all stages of share ownership, from the initial purchase, through the ongoing operation of the company, to the eventual sale of shares or winding up of the company, whatever the reason. This could be a successful buyout or, at the other end of the scale, business failure and liquidation.

Signing a shareholders' agreement is not the first step in buying into a company. Like other legal documents, such as a property lease, commercial property purchase or mortgage agreement, it is the final stage of a negotiated deal and reflects the rights, responsibilities and financial arrangements agreed between the parties.

This means two things. Firstly, negotiations should be thorough and cover all likely eventualities in the life of the company, without leaving gaps or loose ends. Secondly, there are a number of important matters that need to be checked before committing to an investment.

These include the financial value of the business, its legal structure, any charges registered against it and its Companies House filings. It's also worth looking at less technical matters, such as the company's ethos, personnel, reputation and standing in the business community. All of these should be properly investigated before any agreement is signed. If you're unsure what to look for or don't fully understand the legal or financial implications, it's sensible to seek professional advice, including legal representation from a solicitor who can help protect your interests.

Shareholders' agreements all serve the same basic purpose of governing relationships between shareholders and with the company, but there is no single template. Each agreement can be tailored to the circumstances of the business and its shareholders.

Typically, an agreement will cover matters such as the number of shares purchased, the price paid, the shareholder's rights to company information and voting rights, and how shares will be valued if someone wants to buy or sell. It may also set out rights to dividends, confidentiality obligations and a process for resolving disputes, whether over share valuations or other disagreements.

And there's plenty more that can be included. In a growing company, for example, there may be provisions allowing shareholders to acquire more shares or the company to buy back shares under certain conditions. These rights often come with detailed procedures, specific circumstances that trigger them and strict deadlines that must be followed. Missing a deadline or failing to follow the correct process could mean losing an opportunity or a particular right.

This example reflects an important feature of shareholders' agreements. Unlike a business partnership or an active joint venture, investing in a company as a shareholder often involves a more distant or formal relationship. The company needs capital from investors, but its management may not want additional people looking over their shoulders or telling them how to run the business.

This means there can be some tension between the interests of shareholders and those running the company. Shareholders want to know how their money is being used and may want some influence over decisions. Meanwhile, management wants the freedom to get on with running the business, without having to report on every decision or pay out more in dividends than necessary.

A shareholders' agreement helps establish a fair balance between these interests. It sets out everyone's rights and responsibilities, helping to prevent or minimise disagreements and providing a way to resolve them if they arise.

A well-drafted shareholders' agreement is therefore an important protection for anyone investing in a limited company. There are no shortcuts when it comes to protecting an investment. Shareholders need to understand their rights, how their money is being protected and what options they have if they eventually want to sell their shares or recover their investment.

At the same time, fellow shareholders and company management have interests to protect too. A good shareholders' agreement recognises this and strikes a fair and effective balance that works for everyone involved.
