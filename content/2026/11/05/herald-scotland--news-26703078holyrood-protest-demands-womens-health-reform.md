*(Image: Newsquest)*

Bereaved mothers and women's health campaigners gathered outside the Scottish Parliament demanding urgent action to improve healthcare, warning that women across Scotland are being left to suffer.

Protesters called for the reinstatement of a dedicated women's health minister, improvements to miscarriage and maternity care, and greater investment in services for conditions including endometriosis.

The demonstration came as Holyrood's Citizen Participation and Public Petitions Committee considered concerns about gaps in care for women navigating gynaecology, fertility, early pregnancy and maternity services.

Among those taking part was Elizabeth Crammond, 43, from Kinross, whose experience of miscarriage has prompted calls for greater accountability and reform within NHS Scotland.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21287551.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Elizabeth Crammond and her daughter. *(Image: Supplied)*

Ms Crammond suffered her third pregnancy loss in March after repeatedly seeking help from NHS Fife's early pregnancy unit while experiencing bleeding.

She previously told The Herald that she was refused an urgent face-to-face assessment and paid for private scans before miscarrying at home.

Her case was raised by Scottish Labour's Jackie Baillie at First Minister's Questions in September, when John Swinney acknowledged that the service had not worked as it should.

Speaking outside Holyrood, Ms Crammond said the time for apologies was over.

"You have seen what I went through personally, you’ve been questioned by the Labour party, there have been apologies by you but there needs to be action and we just want you to listen to the people of Scotland," she said, "The action needs to come soon because more women can’t suffer."

She also called for the Scottish Government to reverse its decision to remove the dedicated women's health minister role during a reshuffle earlier this year. Minister Maree Todd has since insisted she is the Women's Health Minister, however, the role is not recognised in her title.

"There needs to be a women’s health minister because there needs to be somebody there on that platform that’s speaking for the people of Scotland," Ms Crammond said, "Saying your cabinet is all female doesn’t actually cut it. We do need to see a women’s health minister to look at the issues and get them sorted as soon as possible."

Ms Crammond has launched a petition calling for GPs to have a clearer duty of care when patients move between gynaecology, fertility, early pregnancy and maternity services.

It also seeks an auditing system to identify delays, safety concerns and inconsistencies in treatment between health boards.

Her concerns were examined by Holyrood's petitions committee, where Ms Baillie accused the Scottish Government of being "complacent" in its response to problems with miscarriage care.

The Labour MSP urged the committee to ask Professor Christine McCourt, who is leading an independent review of maternity services, whether miscarriage support and the issues raised in Ms Crammond's petition could be explicitly considered.

MSPs agreed to keep the petition open and consider a wider evidence session on women's health.

They also backed writing to ministers about what action had been taken following the First Minister's acknowledgement of problems with maternity care, while committee convener Paul Sweeney suggested the petition could be considered for an early parliamentary debate.

Ms Crammond welcomed the committee's response.

"It did come across as quite positive. They do recognise the problems in women’s health which is impacting gyanecology and maternity care."

She also welcomed the prospect of Professor McCourt examining miscarriage care as part of the wider maternity review.

The protest brought together campaigners raising concerns about conditions including endometriosis, which can leave women experiencing debilitating pain and facing lengthy delays before diagnosis.

June Smillie, founder of Nicola's Supporting Women in West Lothian, attended to support Ms Crammond and highlight her own campaign for compulsory women's health training across NHS Scotland.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21676026.jpg?type=mds-article-620)June Smillie, founder of Nicola’s Supporting Women, joined campaigners outside the Scottish Parliament to call for urgent improvements to women’s healthcare, warning that women are being left unheard and dismissed by NHS services. *(Image: Newsquest)*

She said: "These are also important issues and awareness around women’s health is on the increase. It’s a public crisis.

"We want to see women’s health get the recognition as there hasn’t been the funding needed put into it. Women are really struggling. They are leaving their jobs, they are having to pay privately for operations. We are not getting healthcare we really need."

Ms Smillie established her charity in memory of Nicola Thyne, a 36-year-old mother of two from Bathgate who suffered from stage-four endometriosis.

Her family said she endured years of severe pain and repeatedly felt dismissed by healthcare professionals before receiving her diagnosis.

Ms Thyne died by suicide in January 2024 after an operation she had expected to undergo was cancelled shortly beforehand.

Her death inspired Nicola's Law, a campaign demanding mandatory and standardised women's health training for GPs and other NHS professionals, covering conditions including endometriosis, chronic pain and the impact of physical symptoms on mental health.

Ms Smillie said the Scottish Government needed to take the experiences of women seriously.

"Listen to us. We know women feel unheard and dismissed. Please, listen. We’ve lost Nicola because of endometriosis, chronic pain and her operation being cancelled. Nicola felt unheard and dismissed for years and years. This is serious. It’s a public crisis."

The Herald previously revealed that just 51 Scottish GPs had recorded completing a national NHS menstrual health training module covering endometriosis since its launch in September 2024.

That was equivalent to just 1.1% of Scotland's qualified GP workforce, although doctors may have undertaken other relevant training.

The Scottish Government has rejected calls to make women's health training mandatory for all GPs and healthcare professionals, arguing that such a requirement would not be practical or achievable.

Another protester, Becky Ardent, spoke about losing her daughter Esme at just over 11 weeks of pregnancy.

Ms Arden said she had felt dismissed and ignored after experiencing persistent bleeding before miscarrying the following day.

"I am here because every baby matters and every mum deserves the best care," she said.

Asked whether the Scottish Government was doing enough to improve women's healthcare, she replied: "No. I was robbed of precious time with my baby."

Candice McKenzie, founder of the East Lothian advocacy group Endo Warriors, was also among those demanding change.

She is campaigning for a national database to record endometriosis treatments and patient outcomes and supports calls to reinstate a dedicated women's health minister.

"We are here to support Elizabeth Crammond but also to highlight two separate petitions running alongside theirs," she said.

"One is my personal petition to create a national database for endometriosis treatments and outcomes."

Ms McKenzie said that despite government commitments to improving women's health, the experiences of patients suggested significant problems remained.

"I think the Scottish Government is making good noises about looking like they are addressing the health of women, however, I’m afraid to say, with the lived experience of women’s health, we don’t necessarily agree with the outcomes we are experiencing of women’s health."

She said women were facing an average wait of 10.2 years for an endometriosis diagnosis and called for ministers to work more closely with campaigners.

"We are obviously hopeful of getting a round table to bring these petitions together but we do urge the Scottish Government to take these matters seriously."

The demonstration comes amid growing scrutiny of women's healthcare in Scotland, including concerns about maternity safety, lengthy gynaecology waiting lists and delays in diagnosing conditions such as endometriosis.

The Scottish Government has introduced a second phase of its Women's Health Plan, aimed at tackling inequalities, improving access to care and reducing diagnosis times.

Ministers have also commissioned an independent review of maternity services, led by Professor McCourt, which is expected to report in July 2027.

However, campaigners say the measures must translate into meaningful improvements for women experiencing difficulties accessing treatment.
