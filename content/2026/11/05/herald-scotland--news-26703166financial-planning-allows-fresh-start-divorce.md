*By Laura Crowe, Group Director at Acumen Financial Planning*

By November, cosy season is back, the evenings are dark, and someone is inevitably letting off fireworks way earlier than they should be! Bonfire Night is a reminder that change can be noisy and unsettling, but it can also mark the start of something new.

Divorce is rarely a change anyone would choose. Alongside the emotional strain, there are practical questions and issues to deal with. Where will I live? What can I afford? What happens to the pension, the mortgage and the savings? It can feel like a lot to tackle at once.

Once the smoke clears, you need a plan. Many people make the mistake of trying to replicate their previous financial life on a single income.

This is rarely sustainable. Instead, this is the time to build a new, bespoke financial blueprint for your new life. A useful first step is simply to get a clear picture of your finances.

Gather details of your income, outgoings, savings, debts, pensions, insurance and any settlement arrangements.

If you have shared accounts or liabilities, understand what needs to be untangled and when. Keep copies of important paperwork, and make sure you know what has and has not been formally agreed. These informal agreements can raise their head, long into the future.

That clarity matters because a settlement is not just a headline figure. A house, a pension and a cash sum may all have different implications for your future.

Before making big decisions, consider how each part fits with your needs and wants, both now and later. If you are still negotiating, get appropriate legal advice. In addition, a financial planner can help you understand the longer-term consequences of different options and provide an unbiased professional opinion, to help separate decisions made with the heart rather than the head.

Once you know what you have to work with, make a budget for your new circumstances. Start with the essentials: housing, bills, food, transport and any commitments to children or other family members. Then look at what is left for saving, debt repayments and the things that make life enjoyable.

A budget is not there to make you feel bad for buying a coffee, it is there to ensure you are armed with all the facts before deciding what to spend your money on.

Try not to rush into major decisions because you feel you have to have everything sorted. Selling a home, changing investments or taking pension benefits can have lasting consequences. Give yourself time where you can and get advice before acting on anything you are unsure about.

Be particularly cautious about making decisions based on a single number or a quick online calculation, or indeed because you feel pushed into it by someone else.

It is also worth reviewing the details that are easy to overlook. Check who is named as a beneficiary on pensions and insurance policies and review your Will and Power of Attorney.

Update contact details and consider whether your protection such as life cover or income protection still makes sense for your circumstances. These are not the most exciting jobs, but they can save confusion later.

If you have children, the financial plan should reflect the realities of family life after separation: where they will live, regular costs, and how you will manage shared responsibilities. Clear communication and a practical approach can help, even when the wider situation is difficult.

A Financial Planner can help you bring the moving parts together: cash flow, pensions, investments, tax considerations and longer-term goals.

The role is not to make decisions for you, but to help you understand your options and build a plan around what matters to you.

That can be especially helpful if money has mostly been managed by your former partner, or if you are facing financial decisions for the first time on your own.

Life after divorce may not look like the future you once imagined. It can take time to adjust, and there may be false starts along the way.

But a clear view of your finances can make the next steps feel more manageable. You do not need a perfect plan on day one; you just need a sensible place to begin.
