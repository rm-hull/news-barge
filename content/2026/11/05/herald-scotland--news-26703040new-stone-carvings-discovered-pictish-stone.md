Archaeologists have revealed unexpected new stone carvings discovered during the excavation of a Pictish standing stone in Angus.

A remnant of an older Pictish stone and a mysterious medieval head was found by archaeologists from Historic Environment Scotland (HES) and heritage group AOC Archaeology under Aberlemno III’s base stone and in the topsoil around where it stood.

The Aberlemno III stone is a famous ninth-century Class II Pictish cross-slab made of old red sandstone, located by the roadside in the village of Aberlemno.

Archaeologist say that the discovery of a fragment of an older Pictish stone with part of a fish motif, and an intriguing fragment of a carved face in relief which may be Early Medieval, shows that Aberlemno III still holds many secrets yet to be unveiled.

![Pictish stone fragment](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21675976.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Pictish stone fragment *(Image: Matt Carney)*

The fragment of the older Pictish stone has been incised with what seems to be a salmon motif, one of the most recognisable naturalistic motifs in the Pictish symbol repertoire. The salmon motif is only found on stones across northern and eastern Scotland, usually on older Pictish stones, and the fragment was found propping up the base stone. According to archaeologists, this means that the stone it comes from may already have been in pieces by the time the base stone was added to Aberlemno III, but analysis of the other finds and samples taken during the excavation is needed to further determine when this all could have taken place.

A relief carving of part of a human head was also found in the topsoil during the excavation. This carving seems to be unrelated to any known Pictish monument in the area, as it does not seem to fit on the existing slab of Aberlemno III. While its form and appearance strongly indicates that it is Early Medieval in origin, archaeologists say further research is needed.

The head suggests that further carved monuments may lie in wait under the earth for experts to uncover in Aberlemno, an area steeped in Scottish history and archaeology.

Under the base stone, the team discovered two features which may have been settings for erected stones. One of these pits could be an earlier location for Aberlemno III, which would have meant the stone initially faced north-south and was orientated towards Aberlemno Kirk, where another Pictish stone stands. If this is an earlier setting, the stone then either fell or was moved and the base stone was added for Aberlemno III to sit in, and the orientation of the stone was changed to east-west, the standard Christian alignment.

Stefan North-Sagrott, Senior Cultural Resources Advisor at HES, said: “The initial results of the excavation by AOC have already exceeded our expectations. The discovery of the Pictish stone fragment, as well as the carved head, give us new questions to answer.

“We have learned that Aberlemno III has likely remained in its original location, but that it was moved or fell and the base stone was added later, while a piece of an older Pictish stone was used to prop it up. We are unsure how the head fits into this, and we are excited to start the next phase of post-excavation analysis and research to see how much more we can discover.

“The base stone has now been brought to Edinburgh for further analysis and to retrieve the section of Aberlemno III which is still embedded in the stone where it broke. This will likely reveal further carvings not seen for hundreds of years. Analysis of the base stone will also give further insight into Aberlemno III’s history, though the excavation itself has already revealed a previously unknown chapter in its story.

“It is clear that the place of Aberlemno still holds many secrets. We would like to thank the Aberlemno community for their support during the excavation, and we will continue to share our findings as we learn more."
