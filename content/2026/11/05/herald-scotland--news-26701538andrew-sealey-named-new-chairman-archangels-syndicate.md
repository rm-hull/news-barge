Today's appointment of Andrew Sealey as chairman of Archangels comes 34 years after he became an investor in the Edinburgh-based syndicate that his father, Barry Sealey, helped establish.

The younger Mr Sealey, who joined the Archangels board in 2021, succeeds Ian Macleod who had chaired the organisation since 2022 and been a director since 2018.

The family connection gives the appointment a sense of continuity. More relevant to what happens next, however, is Mr Sealey’s long career in international private capital.

He joined private-capital adviser Campbell Lutyens in 1990, took responsibility for managing the business in 2003 and remained chief executive until 2024. The firm grew during that period to more than 300 staff across 13 offices.

Mr Sealey is now non-executive chairman of Lazard CL, formed after Lazard acquired Campbell Lutyens earlier this year and combined it with its existing private-capital advisory operation.

That experience is relevant at Archangels, where the money provided by its members is only part of the funding picture.

In 2025, Archangels helped channel £41.1 million into Scottish companies, up 50% from £27.3m in 2024. Members provided roughly £12.8m while another £28.4m came from co-investors. Less than a third of the total, therefore, came directly from Archangels members.

The balance is similar this year. Of £27.8m invested across nine new and existing portfolio companies so far in 2026, £11.1m has come from members and £16.7m from co-investment partners. Recipients have included University of Edinburgh spin-outs Biocaptiva and Bioliberty.

For a syndicate with around 130 members, attracting money from elsewhere increases the sums available to the companies it backs. It also means Archangels’ ability to attract co-investment matters at least as much as the headline amount committed by its own members.

The scale of Archangels’ involvement in the market has been built over more than three decades. Since its formation, the group says it has invested more than £200m on behalf of members and supported 26 businesses through successful exits. Those exits generated gross proceeds of more than £400m for all shareholders.

The syndicate currently has 17 companies in its portfolio. Over the past five years it has invested £54.6m of members’ money and arranged a further £88.1m of co-investment, making outside capital a substantial part of the funding attached to its deals.

There are limits, though, to what can be read into rising investment totals. The amount of money going into young companies says little by itself about what ultimately comes out.

On that measure, Mr Macleod’s tenure included the sales of three portfolio companies: Optoscribe was sold to Intel, Blackford Analysis to Bayer, and Reactec to Ideagen. Archangels’ 2025 annual report said investments made and realised over the previous decade had achieved a post-tax realised internal rate of return of 27%, returning 3.1 times the capital invested.

The same report described a difficult investment environment, with subdued merger, acquisition and investment activity amid geopolitical and economic uncertainty.

Mr Sealey therefore takes over an organisation that has operated for 34 years, but in a market very different from the one in which Archangels began in 1992. Its future will depend not simply on how much its own members are prepared to invest, but on its ability to use that cash to attract additional capital and, ultimately, generate enough successful exits and returns to keep investors coming back
