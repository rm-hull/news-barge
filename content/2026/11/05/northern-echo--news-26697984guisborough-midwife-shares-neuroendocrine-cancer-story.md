A midwife with neuroendocrine cancer is raising awareness of the rare and often misunderstood disease.

Anna Temke, 59, from Guisborough, was diagnosed in July 2025 after cancer was found in her lungs, just a month after successful treatment for tongue cancer.

Neuroendocrine cancer affects around 6,000 people in the UK each year and takes an average of nearly five years to diagnose.

Ms Temke said: "When I received the second diagnosis, you would think that maybe I’d be expecting it, but to be honest, it was just as devastating as the first one.

"I was feeling healthier than ever and to be told I had cancer — again — turned my world upside down."

Neuroendocrine cancer develops in hormone-producing cells and can affect multiple organs.

Ms Temke has worked in the NHS since she was 17 and has spent the past 21 years as a midwife.

Despite her diagnosis, she continues to work in the role with the support of her colleagues.

She said: "This cancer is often missed, misdiagnosed, and misunderstood, with more than 50 per cent of patients diagnosed at an advanced stage.

"Unfortunately, in my journey, I have lost count of the number of times I have had to explain what neuroendocrine cancer is to people, and that includes healthcare professionals."

Ms Temke is now an ambassador for Neuroendocrine Cancer UK and is determined to raise awareness.

She said: "I am on a mission to ensure that as many people as possible know about this cancer, from GPs, medical teams and nurses to the public.

"This will help ensure that lives are saved while reducing the time to diagnosis and ensuring treatment is started earlier."

She plans to host an awareness stand at James Cook Hospital on November 10, which marks World Neuroendocrine Cancer Day.

Group chief nursing officer Emma Nunez said: "Anna's determination and resilient nature to keep raising awareness of neuroendocrine cancer while living with the condition herself are truly commendable.

"It is particularly inspiring that Anna continues to work as a midwife, providing compassionate care to our patients despite the challenges she is facing.

"I would like to thank Anna for her courage in sharing her story and hope her journey raises some much-needed awareness about the condition."
