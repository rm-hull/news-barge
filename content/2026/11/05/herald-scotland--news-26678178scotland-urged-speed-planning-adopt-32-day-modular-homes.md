*The successful evolution of rapid and effective off-site construction offers solid foundations when predicting the future of housebuilding. So why is the industry still hampered by short-sighted processes?*

Scotland first declared a housing emergency four years ago, although perhaps ‘emergency’ is not the right word, as it was neither sudden nor has it received the immediate action an emergency warrants.

But then what ails Scotland’s ability to build more homes faster to meet demand is like a compound fracture: extremely complicated and in need of more than a plaster.

Factors looming large in the mix include the planning system and long delays for planning approvals, a shortage of skilled workers, escalating cost of materials, inflation, and economic and political uncertainty.

![Coldwells Build-Passivhaus-WoodendofGlassel-Credit-ColdwellsBuild](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21642094.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Coldwells Build’s proposed Passivhaus development *(Image: Supplied)*

Arguably, the biggest hurdle of all is of our own making. Those in the industry cite affordability and over-regulation amongst many other things, which impact hardest on smaller SME companies and self-builders for whom protracted planning and funding delays can sound the death-knell.

Until 1947 there was no mandatory planning system. Houses were easier to build and faced far fewer hurdles, although basic controls still existed. Amongst other things, that has given us an aesthetically diverse architectural landscape, including listed homes and many outstanding follies.

Would MacCaig’s magnificent ‘colosseum’ make the cut today? Or the Earl of Dunmore’s famously bizarre Pineapple House? Planning is a paid-for service designed to facilitate not frustrate but unlike building warrants, which are compliant and purely objective, the planning system at present is subjective and therefore open to personal sway.

It shouldn’t be this complicated. We live in an age of technology other industries have been quick to embrace – cars that can drive themselves, for example. Yet incongruously, 90% of private sector homes are still built as they have always been - on a building site out in the open and subject to the vagaries of the weather. How can this be when we have an existing alternative in offsite modern methods of construction (MMC) that delivers more homes, faster, more economically and with fewer workers, in a warm, dry, factory environment?

In one sense we already are, as all traditionally constructed homes in Scotland start with a timber frame, which is factory built. But in the main, that’s where it ends. Adopting MMC seems like a logical progression and is undoubtedly a powerful driver for change - until you balance it against the barriers to adopting MMC offsite.

Factories require heavy capital investment and high up-front costs compared to traditional onsite builds that benefit from land and profit margins. Moreover, once a factory is up and running, its economic survival depends on guaranteed demand and a steady flow of orders, which is not conducive with a private open-market housing market.

Even then, there are still the same hurdles with planning delays, cautious lenders, increasing costs and increasing skill shortages, which could be partly addressed by providing more apprenticeships and incentives for young people to enter the industry. Nor does it help that, as a nation, we have become so averse to risk we are verging on standing still – and unlike social housing where demand can be quantified, private open-market housing lacks a guaranteed buyer at completion. For volume housebuilders, as things stand the risks outweigh the benefits and there are no incentives to change.

Modular construction is not new and those old enough to remember the 1950s will recall the prefabricated bungalow-style homes erected in response to the massive shortage of homes after the war. Yes, prefabs had their flaws (asbestos being one) but for many they were palaces compared to the slum tenements of the day. Residents loved them and although deemed a temporary solution at the time, they were only completely phased out in the late 1990s.

![Ardbeg Modular on site erection](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21642096.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Ardbeg Modular on-site erection of its energy-efficient homes *(Image: Supplied)*

Interestingly, back in 2015 sector body Homes for Scotland published an in-depth research document into Mainstreaming Offsite Modern Methods of Construction in House Building, However, at the end it concluded: ‘The evidence suggests the business case for offsite construction still requires to be established.’ More than a decade on, we are only painstakingly slowly making headway.

Nowadays MMC follows the framework outlined by the Scottish Government Building Standards Verification. These methods are categorised by structural types, the two main categories being complete, three-dimensional modules fully manufactured, insulated and fitted out in a factory then transported and assembled on site; and panellised construction consisting of flat two-dimensional panels for walls, floors and roofs fabricated off-site and assembled on-site.

There are also sub-sections, such as factory-built bathroom pods, utility cupboards and kitchen units that can be craned in onsite and plumbed into traditionally or modularly built superstructures.

Despite the advantages and potential to address current challenges, MMC remains very much a niche market hampered by misconceptions, such as limited design variety and aesthetic standardisation. More important, there still seems to be a disconnect between what is familiar and the option to build better.

The case for the latter has not been helped by publicity given to the closure of recent modular start-ups – Ayrshire’s Wee House Group which included Connect Modular, and most notably, Legal & General Modular Homes, which was well-funded yet closed in 2025 having failed to become profitable due to Covid and long planning delays. Conversely, industry successes are passed over.

Arguably one of Scotland’s greatest success stories in the use of modern methods of construction is CCG (Scotland), which has one of the most advanced off-site manufacturing facilities in the UK. Having pioneered offsite manufacturing almost two decades ago, CCG has since established the build method as mainstream, with an output of more than 1000 homes per year and a current all-tenure housing pipeline valued at over £500m.

At the heart of this capability is CCG’s ‘iQ’ Timber System, an enhanced closed panel wall solution that combines with floor or roof cassettes to create the superstructure of a home or building. Clients can have flexibility in the choice of specification and components, such as insulation, internal wall linings, windows and doors, are incorporated under factory-controlled conditions prior to assembly on-site.

![CCG's award-winning Shawbridge Arcade](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21642098.jpg?type=mds-article-620) CCG’s award-winning Shawbridge Arcade in Pollokshaws, *(Image: Keith Hunter)*

A precision-engineered environment guarantees greater consistency and quality, in addition to delivering a product of vastly improved thermal performance, which ultimately serves to reduce energy costs for the end user- whether homeowner or tenant.

Equally important, sustainably sourced materials, zero waste practices and fewer vehicle movements help lower carbon emissions at every stage of build. However, it’s the combination of this building method with CCG’s partnership delivery model that sets the business apart and enables them to cater for developments of any scale.

“Offsite manufacturing provides significant advantages in terms of quality, sustainability, and efficiency and it is by now our DNA - but its full potential is realised when combined with our proficiency as a contractor,” explains Calum Murray, CCG (Scotland) director and former chair of Offsite Solutions Scotland.

“Offsite demands early client engagement and by collaborating from the outset with our customers, designers, and supply chain, we can influence specification, improve buildability and construction timescales, remove risks, and create efficiencies long before site start. It’s about bringing the right expertise together at the right time to deliver better, faster, and more cost-effective outcomes,” he adds.

By increased use of public sector frameworks and direct award, this partnership-led approach sustains an essential manufacturing and construction pipeline. Reducing uncertainty and improving deliverability, it is fundamental to CCG’s business model and in a period of housing emergency, is key in supporting housing associations and local authorities to accelerate essential affordable housing delivery.

While delivering vital homes is fraught with frustrations, Scotland is a country where ‘vital’ means different things in different regions – and no-where is the need more urgent than the Highlands and Islands, where local businesses dependent on workers are blighted by the lack of affordable homes. And homes are only part of the problem…

![CCG factory](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21642099.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Above, home construction underway in CCG’s factory *(Image: JIM)*

Fuel poverty in the north of Scotland is over 42% higher than the rest of Scotland and accounts for one in three Highlands and Islands’ households, rising to more than four in 10 in remote rural communities and around half in some island areas, meaning delays in delivering new homes is even more critical. Inverness-based MAKAR is an award-winning offsite sustainable, SME manufacturer with the drive and ability to make a difference.

MAKAR designs and manufactures healthy, net zero homes built to a Passivhaus equivalent in a workshop appropriately surrounded by woodland. These are not just homes; they are highly insulated, energy efficient, eco homes - made from Scottish timber and all-natural materials – that cost £6 a week to heat (not a misprint).

A three-bedroomed Paradigm prototype house erected in the grounds outside the workshop is open for viewing and enables visitors to see, touch and breathe in the difference.

Developed over two decades, the company’s technology and design-led integrated manufacturing process is poised and ready to help boost rural economies by building much needed homes in rural and remote sites where traditional housebuilders either can’t or don’t want to go. So why is its order book not overflowing?

“Rural Scotland needs businesses like us willing to deliver two homes here, four there, six somewhere else,” says chief executive Neil Edgar. “Unfortunately, the system we have at present doesn’t understand or cater for rural differences. To give an example, we have four local authority homes with planning and building warrant in place.

"We could have built two of them in the hours spent trying to get the contractual element over the line and it’s still not completed. We are working in completely disconnected time zones where we can build a house in a week, yet it has taken two years so far to get to technical audit with the Scottish Government for funding. And the longer it takes, the more the price escalates.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21642116.jpg?type=mds-article-620)MAKAR House assembly onsite2 *(Image: Supplied)*

“On another project, 598 days passed before the build started. Our manufacture, delivery and assembly take 32 days – that’s 95% process and 5% physical build. Since the first housing emergency was declared in May 2024, we could have built 60 homes, which is not significant for the city, but it is in the rural highlands and islands. We already know how to build better homes; what we need now is the urgency and ability to be able to deliver them.”

For Edgar, the crux is not only about building more, but building better to alleviate fuel poverty, boost local business, and ultimately benefit the Scottish economy. “For those only interested in the bottom line, delivering the same houses erected by the same builders is only ever going to deliver the same results; it’s not going to address the fuel crisis. A MAKAR house will.

“Change,” he acknowledges, “must come from the top, otherwise it’s business as usual. We need to change the product requirement if we are going to eradicate fuel poverty at source.” On a recent visit to the MAKAR workshop and a tour of its workshop and Paradigm showhouse outside, First Minister John Swinney was not only impressed, according to Edgar “he totally got it, particularly when he learned a Paradigm home could potentially save up to £50,000 a year in heating costs over a 25-year mortgage term.”

When you dig down into it, Scotland’s housing market has numerous SME companies delivering variations on an MMC theme, thereby extending choice for home buyers.

HebHomes on the Isle of Skye is one company manufacturing homes offsite from precision engineered closed panel systems and structural insulated panels.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21642114.jpg?type=mds-article-620)MAKAR Workshop-drone image *(Image: Supplied)*

The company grew from award-winning Dualchas Architects, founded in 1996 by brothers/architects Neil and Alasdair Stephen, whose house designs include linear houses echoing the style of traditional longhouses that blend seamlessly into the rugged rural landscape.

Designed by Dualchas, these are kit homes, which range from supply and erect packages to full turnkey, with erection typically completed in one-to-two weeks. With offices on Skye and in Glasgow, the company operates across Scotland and south of the border.

“Since we built our first house in 1995, regulations around sustainability and low-carbon construction have become much more complicated and much more expensive,” says Dualchas architect Neil Stephen.

“Affordability is key but so too is placemaking and building homes to last. Doing it as cheaply as possible means you build communities that won’t be cherished and looked after in the future and will lose value. We also need better joined up thinking and a more holistic approach to housing.”

In Aberdeenshire, Coldwells Build is an award-winning, family-run construction company specialising in prefabricated Passivhaus homes and custom builds across Scotland and the UK. It has recently been granted planning permission for a five-house project in private woodland near Banchory. The mix of four and five-bedroomed homes – designed by John Gilbert Architects in Glasgow – will be Passivhaus certified and prefabricated in Coldwells Build’s workshop in Insch.

Other successful MMC companies include SIPS Homes in Dalgety Bay, which manufactures homes offsite built from structurally engineered, highly insulated panels that form floors, roofs, external and internal walls without the need for a timber frame or trusses.

Also, Ardbeg Homes and its sister company Ardbeg Modular, which deliver high-quality, energy efficient homes constructed offsite in a precision-controlled manufacturing facility. Adopting a modular approach, each house is fully completed offsite - including fitted kitchens, bathrooms, plumbing and electrics - then split into modules and transported to site where they are craned into position, re-assembled, and finished externally and internally ready for occupancy.

Ardbeg’s design process is tailored to the needs of housing associations and local authorities and allows for full customisation to suit unique project requirements (currently luxury cabins for the tourist industry), while Ardbeg Homes has a private residential development at Stronachlacher near Loch Katrine within the Loch Lomond and Trossachs National Park.

Scotland needs more homes, regardless of whether they are built offsite or on. And while there is no one-size-fits-all solution, key would be to simplify and expedite the planning and approval processes, improve communication between various government departments and create greater awareness and understanding as to why things must change - and the consequences if it’s not forthcoming.

Hope is hovering on the horizon: earlier this year, First Minister John Swinney made a speech at Prosper’s Annual Forum in Edinburgh where the key theme was ‘Growth Starts Here.’ In it he stated…

*‘At the moment, systems and structures are far too complex. There is unnecessary overlap. Often what appeared to be good reasons a rule is put in place, on top of another, and then another, and we find ourselves with a labyrinth of regulation that has become costly and cumbersome to navigate. Regulation is important, indeed vital, but it must be regulation that both enables and protects, that creates pathways to investment - not unnecessary obstacles.*

*‘The planning system offers a good example of what is possible. One of the clearest messages I have heard, that the Government has heard, is that our planning system needs to work better to enable growth. That means increased capacity and resources like the National Planning Hub to support and enable faster-decision making at a local level. And it also means streamlining the planning process, removing unnecessary obstacles and processes in our consenting systems as we work to make Scotland’s investment ecosystem more attractive.’*

Next year will see the launch of More Homes Scotland, a new national housing and land delivery agency designed to bring simplicity, scale and speed to housing delivery.

To date, there are few details but if between them, Homes for Scotland, the Scottish Government and More Homes Scotland can bring simplicity and sense where currently there is chaos, it would be a start and might lead to an upturn in starts and completions down the line.
