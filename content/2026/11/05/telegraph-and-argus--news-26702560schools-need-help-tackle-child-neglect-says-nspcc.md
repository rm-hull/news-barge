THE NSPCC exists to protect children across the country from abuse, but we also aim to support children who are experiencing or at risk of neglect too.

Experiencing neglect in their early years can have lasting effects on a child’s development, physical health and wellbeing, and is linked to an increased risk of mental health concerns later in life, so identifying and addressing neglect as early as possible is crucial to improving long-term outcomes for children.

Outside of their families, the only people to see young children on a daily basis who could pick up on the signs of neglect are primary school teachers, who play a vital part in safeguarding and are often the first people to spot early signs of neglect.

A new survey of more than 11,000 members by teacher’s union NASUWT found more than half of early years staff (53per cent), and 48per cent of primary school teachers said they had seen a rise in safeguarding referrals related to child neglect in their schools.

For both early years staff and primary school teachers, neglect was the biggest reported increase in safeguarding referrals of any form of abuse, including sexual abuse, emotional abuse and physical abuse.

One early years professional, who reached out to the NSPCC Helpline with concerns about a child, said: “We had an awkward situation at pick up today where a little girl’s mum turned up drunk to take her home. She’d driven so we couldn’t let them go together and the mum just wasn’t in any state to be responsible for a two-year-old.

“We got one of her friends to come and get them and the friend said mum had been having a hard time, and the child’s father had recently left the picture.

“I don’t know how often mum’s drinking is a problem, but she needs some help.”

Taking into account the whole education sector, more than one in three teachers in the UK (35per cent), said they had witnessed an increase in neglect-related referrals in their school and 53per cent had seen an increase in safeguarding referrals overall.

However, despite teachers reporting increased safeguarding concerns, the NSPCC knows that their referrals often fail to result in the support families need because the concerns do not reach certain statutory thresholds.

With this in mind, the NSPCC is calling on the Government to introduce a new national child neglect strategy.

While we have welcomed its recent investment in children’s services and greater focus on prevention and early intervention, we want to see more done to offer robust support at a local level to help children and families where neglect is a concern before problems in the home escalate.

A national neglect strategy should also allow for improved training opportunities for all safeguarding partner agencies, such as teachers and education professionals, to ensure a shared language across all workforces encountering children where neglect is a concern.

This work is vital to achieve the Government’s ambitions to strengthen and support education’s role as a safeguarding partner.

Neglect remains one of the most overlooked forms of child abuse, and teachers are among the first people to notice when something is wrong, but if concerns are reported and go no further, support may not be offered, and the problems can persist or worsen.

Matt Wrack, General Secretary of NASUWT, has echoed the NSPCC’s calls, saying teachers and school leaders work hard to help children as early as possible, but the external support to deal with neglect-related concerns was rarely available.

He said: “Teachers are trying to do their best for the children they teach, but neglect is a serious child protection issue that needs a national level response.

“Schools and education professionals must have access to timely advice, specialist support and effective referral pathways, enabling children and young people’s needs to be addressed by the most appropriate service or professional.”

For non-professionals without safeguarding training, it can be difficult to recognise the signs of neglect or know where to turn with any concerns.

The NSPCC Helpline is available on 0808 8005000 or by emailing [help@nspcc.org.uk](mailto:help@nspcc.org.uk) to offer support and guidance to any adult concerned about a child, and resources about child neglect and how to support a child are also available on the NSPCC website.

Looking forward though, the NSPCC and NASUWT believe that without the Government recognising neglect as a growing crisis and implementing a new child neglect strategy, many children and families may continue to go without the vital support they need.
