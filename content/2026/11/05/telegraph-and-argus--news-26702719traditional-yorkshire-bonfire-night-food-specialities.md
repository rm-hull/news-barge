Yorkshire’s traditional Bonfire Night foods connect today’s celebrations with centuries of local history.

Fireworks, sparklers and bonfires draw the crowds on November 5, but food remains central to celebrations across Yorkshire.

## Parkin is perhaps the best-known treat

The rich cake is made with oats, black treacle, golden syrup, ginger and butter, and is closely linked with Guy Fawkes Night and autumn in northern England.

Food historians believe parkin developed in Yorkshire during the 17th and 18th centuries.

Oats grew widely in Yorkshire’s uplands and were cheaper and easier to obtain than wheat.

For working families, parkin provided a filling source of warmth and energy as winter set in.

![Traditional ginger Parkin](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21675489.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Traditional ginger Parkin *(Image: T&A)*

Ginger was prized for its warming qualities, while keeping parkin in an airtight tin made it softer, darker and stickier.

The tradition continues, with parkin often made before November 5 rather than eaten straight from the oven.

## Bonfire Toffee

Treacle toffee, also known as Bonfire Toffee, is made with black treacle, sugar and butter.

Its cheap ingredients made the hard sweet a family favourite.

For many children, smashing the slab was part of Bonfire Night alongside fireworks and other November 5 activities.

Shops and market stalls also sold cinder toffee and toffee apples.

## Jacket potatoes

Jacket potatoes offered a savoury alternative to the sweets.

People buried them in bonfire embers and left them to roast slowly, giving the potatoes a smoky flavour.

Butter or cheese turned the potatoes into a cheap, filling meal for people spending hours outside.

![Baked potato](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21054631.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Baked potato *(Image: Newsquest)*

## Pie and mushy peas

Mushy peas and pea pudding, often flavoured with mint, were also served at local galas and firework events.

Hot sausages and pork pies reflected Yorkshire’s farming heritage and featured in celebrations in some areas.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/20642158.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Four Seasons Mushy Peas

Yorkshire’s Bonfire Night connection is especially strong because Guy Fawkes was born in York in 1570.

The date marks the failure of the 1605 Gunpowder Plot, when Fawkes and other conspirators tried to blow up Parliament.

Modern events may serve burgers, pizzas and street food, but traditional dishes still link families to the past.
