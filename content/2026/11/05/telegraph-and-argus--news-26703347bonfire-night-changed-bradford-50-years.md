"Don't put fireworks in your pocket."

It sounds less like official safety guidance and more like the sort of lesson someone learns the hard way.

Yet it is advice that has remained a part of Bonfire Night safety messages for half a century.

In November 1976, the Telegraph & Argus published a "Firework Code" on its Young World page.

Nestled between colouring competitions and features for children were a series of safety warnings from West Yorkshire Police aimed at keeping youngsters out of trouble on Guy Fawkes Night.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21676360.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The Bonfire Night safety guide published in the T&A in 1976. *(Image: T&A Archive)*

## Advise remains eerily similar

Looking back at the list half a century later, readers might expect some wonderfully outdated advice.

After all, this was an era when children travelled in cars without seat belts, rode bikes without helmets and thought nothing of disappearing for hours without a mobile phone.

Instead, much of the guidance would fit perfectly on a modern fire service Instagram post.

Young readers were told to keep fireworks in a closed box, take them out one at a time, read the instructions, stand well back after lighting them and never throw them.

Fast forward to 2026 and West Yorkshire Fire and Rescue Service is still advising people not to throw fireworks, to follow instructions carefully and, yes, not to carry them around in their pockets.

The differences are mostly in the presentation.

The advice from 76' appeared alongside cartoons aimed at children.

Today's guidance arrives in slick social media graphics complete with approved retailers, safety standards, hashtags and warnings about the impact on pets and wildlife.

## The follow-reporting

Just days after publishing the Firework Code, the T&A was reporting on the consequences of ignoring it.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21676361.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The post-Bonfire Night reporting on November 6, 1976. *(Image: T&A Archive)*

On November 6, the newspaper revealed that Bradford Royal Infirmary had treated a number of youngsters for firework-related injuries during Bonfire Night celebrations.

Burns to faces, hands and eyes were among the injuries recorded.

Evidently, some children had viewed the safety code as more of a suggestion than a set of rules.
