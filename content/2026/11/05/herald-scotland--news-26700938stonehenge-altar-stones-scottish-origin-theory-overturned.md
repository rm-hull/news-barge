*(Image: PA)*A theory that the six tonne Altar Stone at Stonehenge came from Scotland has been overturned.**BBC Scotland reports** that a new study has ruled out suggestions that the 4.9 metre sandstone slab travelled the length of Britain during the Stone Age and was used in the construction of the neolithic monument.

Findings of a separate report in 2024 caused a stir when hey indicated a striking similarity between the stone and similar rocks in the Orcadian Basin in north-east Scotland

But after further Scientific tests were carried out in Scotland, researchers failed to find a location with the same characteristics of the stone in that area.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/16594319.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Stonehenge's mystery endures *(Image: Newsquest)*

The new study's lead, Professor Duncan Pirrie from the University of South Wales, said the latest investigation has left them "far better equipped to focus on the areas most likely to yield an answer."

Pirrie said his latest publication on the Altar Stone has "built the clearest picture yet" and was "the most comprehensive geological investigation of the Altar Stone ever undertaken".

Professor Nick Pearce from Aberystwyth University, who worked on the previous research, said "ruling out potential source areas is just as important as identifying new ones".

The geochemistry expert said: "Every location eliminated narrows the search and brings us closer to discovering where the Altar Stone originated.

"The stone is unique among those at Stonehenge, and tracing its source would provide valuable new insights into its remarkable journey to Salisbury Plain thousands of years ago."

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21673053.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The Altar Stone at Stonehenge could have come from Scotland *(Image: Image: Professor Nick Pearce/PA Wire)*

The world-famous Wiltshire monument was constructed in multiple phases over thousands of years, between 3000 and 1600 BC. However, not all stones come from the area.

The smaller bluestones travelled around 225km from west Wales with research suggesting a few were taken directly from another circle.

The latest study combined new observations and looked at fragments of the stone in museum collections, examining structure, minerals and composition.

It has been published in the Journal of the Geological Society.
