Andy Burnham is “determined” to bring the UK back into the European Union, and believes it is what the majority of the British public want, according to reports.

Speaking to *The Times*, a senior ally said the prime minister “deeply” believes the UK should rejoin the bloc after a summit on Friday saw Brussels open the door for a year of negotiations over the UK’s relationship to the EU.

The briefing confirms a direction of travel for the prime minister since September, when his intentions were revealed by *The Independent* during Labour’s conference.

It also comes as foreign secretary Ed Miliband said “all the options will be on the table” during the negotiations, including the possibility of rejoining just hours after the prime minister unveiled a deal on ending food and drink checks at the border and setting up a youth mobility scheme with the EU.

“He (Burnham) wants to rejoin. That’s the truth of it,” the source said. “He feels it very deeply and thinks he is on the right side of public opinion. The idea that this is being driven by anyone other than him or it’s just clever positioning or tactical is wrong. It’s just what he believes.”

Mr Miliband told BBC’s *Sunday With Laura Kuenssberg* the world had changed “massively” since the 2016 referendum vote to leave the EU.

“Europe is changing too, so we’ve got to look at what that means,” he said. “It’s not about this parliament, we’re respecting the red lines in this parliament.”

At Friday’s summit in Brussels, Mr Burnham made three significant agreements with the EU, including allowing 100,000 young Europeans to live and work temporarily in the UK each year.

The youth experience scheme will allow UK and EU citizens aged 18 to 31 to live and work in each other’s jurisdictions for up to three years. The first young people to travel under the scheme will do so “no later than January 2030”, according to government documents.

A food trading deal, officially known as a sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) agreement, was also agreed, and will end most post-Brexit checks and restrictions on food and agricultural products from March 1 2028.

An emissions trading systems (ETS) agreement, applied from the start of 2028, will mean £6.5 billion of British exports, including from the steel and aluminium industries, will be exempted from EU carbon border taxes.

But the government has hit a potential stumbling block over the issue of the tuition fees charged to students from the EU in British universities.

A six-month deadline has been set to reach agreement after EU member states called for their students to be charged the same rate as British counterparts.

That would mean a cut from fees of up to £38,000 a year to £9,970 – something the UK has resisted because of the financial impact it would have on universities.

![Foreign secretary Ed Miliband said 'all options' are on the table](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/21/09/2305461673..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The row led to doubts being raised in Brussels about whether the rest of the deal would be implemented unless the UK gives in to the EU’s demands.

But Mr Miliband said it was “absolutely clear” the agreements would apply.

The foreign secretary said: “The dates are set out in 2028 when these things are coming in, we’re introducing legislation in Parliament, so definitely.”

He said European Commission president Ursula von der Leyen was clear “there are those three agreements and then there’s a separate piece of paper and a separate issue, which is looking at the issue of tuition”.

Reducing fees for EU students to the same level as domestic undergraduates could cost around £500 million a year and may reduce places available for young Britons, the Government has been warned.

Cabinet minister Dame Angela Eagle said the Treasury and Department for Education would work with universities during the negotiations on fees.

The environment secretary told *BBC Breakfast*: “I don’t think anybody wants to do deals that negatively impact anything. We want win-win deals.”

The UK claims the deals done on Friday will add 0.5 per cent to gross domestic product – a £17 billion boost to the economy – in 2040.
