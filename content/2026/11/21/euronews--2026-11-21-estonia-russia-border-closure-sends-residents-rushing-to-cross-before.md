## Families and travellers are queuing to cross from Russia into Estonia before all five road border crossings are shut on 1 December. The decision follows an alleged Russian-backed sabotage attempt, but has also raised concerns among people with relatives on both sides of the border.

People carrying suitcases, backpacks and plastic bags have been gathering in the Russian border town of Ivangorod, hoping to cross into Estonia before the Baltic country closes its land border with Russia next month.

The queues reflect the impact of a decision that will affect not only cross-border travel but also families with relatives living on opposite sides of the frontier.

Estonia, a member of NATO and the European Union, announced on 20 November that it would suspend road crossings at all five road border checkpoints with Russia from 1 December. The restriction is due to remain in place for six months, after which the government will review the security situation and decide whether to extend or amend it.

## Why is Estonia closing the border?

The decision follows an attempted arson attack on the premises of Estonian drone manufacturer Threod Systems in Viimsi, north of Tallinn, on 14 November.

According to Estonia's Internal Security Service, Russia's military intelligence agency, the GRU, was behind the attempted attack. Eight Latvian citizens were detained, with investigators suspecting they had been recruited to carry out the operation. The company's security staff intervened before significant damage was caused.

Estonian authorities described the incident as part of a wider pattern of sabotage *(source in Estonian)* and hostile activity against the country and other European states. The government said the border closure would make it harder for Russian intelligence services to recruit and influence people crossing into Estonia, as well as help prevent sanctions evasion.

Moscow has rejected the accusations. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov described the allegations as unfounded, while Russian Foreign Ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova accused Estonia of infringing the rights of Russian citizens and threatened political and diplomatic countermeasures.

Zakharova also compared the planned closure to the construction of a new Iron Curtain.

## Families divided by the border

For people living in the border region, the decision has consequences extending far beyond security policy.

Narva, an Estonian town of more than 50,000 people, lies directly opposite Ivangorod on the other side of the River Narva. The two towns have long-standing family, social and economic ties, with many residents having relatives on both sides.

The crossing has also been used by people travelling from other European countries to reach St Petersburg.

Russia suspended vehicle traffic through the Narva crossing in February 2024, but pedestrians have continued to cross.

Narva Mayor Jaan Toots has called on the Estonian government to consider humanitarian exemptions for residents who need to visit elderly relatives, attend funerals or deal with other urgent family matters. He has also warned of economic consequences for the town, including potential losses for local hotels, restaurants and businesses.

The closure is also expected to affect employment in the region, where border guards and customs officers are among the local workforce.

## Crossings have already fallen sharply

The number of people travelling between Estonia and Russia has declined considerably since the start of the war in Ukraine.

According to Estonian public broadcaster ERR, annual crossings fell from more than four million before 2022 to around 760,000 in 2026. Of those, approximately 447,000 were made through the Narva-Ivangorod crossing, according to the figures cited by AFP.

Reuters reported that Russian nationals accounted for 307,000 of this year's crossings, while 175,000 were made by Estonian citizens and 54,000 by Finnish passport holders.

The planned restrictions will apply to all five road crossing points: Narva-1, Narva-2, Koidula, Luhamaa and Saatse. Rail freight will continue, subject to existing restrictions and customs checks.

Travellers will have to use alternative routes through neighbouring countries, particularly Latvia, although journeys are likely to become longer and more expensive.

For those without access to a car, travelling by air may become the only practical alternative.

The closure is currently planned for six months, rather than indefinitely. Estonia's government says it will reassess the measure after that period, depending on the security situation.
