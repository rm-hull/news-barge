While serving a federal prison sentence for his involvement in the Jan. 6 attack on the U.S. Capitol, Oath Keepers founder Stewart Rhodes got a tattoo showing a defiant Donald Trump after the Butler, Pennsylvania shooting. It marked an extreme display of loyalty to the Republican president but that allegiance has now entirely vanished.

Speaking at a yellow table outside an Irving, Texas gastropub, Rhodes delivered a scathing reassessment of the leader he once sought to keep in office.

"I am absolutely done with Trump," Rhodes said, labeling him not only a traitor but "one of the very worst in our nation's history."

![Stewart Rhodes at a Jan. 6 House Judiciary Select Subcommittee meeting in January 2026](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/08/05/09/2255685299..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

His rejection shows how support for the president has frayed even among his most fervent past defenders. Six years ago, Rhodes was convicted of seditious conspiracy after attempting to block the transfer of power following the 2020 election, attributing Trump’s loss to Democrat Joe Biden to widespread fraud.

Following recent historic Democratic electoral victories in Texas, however, Rhodes pointed to a different cause of his complete reversal.

"The lion’s share is because Trump pissed off his own base," Rhodes said.

Dressed in a black cowboy hat, he wore an eye patch over his left eye—the result of a previous firearms accident—leaving a single blue iris visible. Nearby, attendees gathered with drinks during his Christian Warriors After-Party, held alongside a three-day event titled "Christ is King: America After Trump." Several visitors stopped to greet him, inquire about joining the Oath Keepers, or sign up for training sessions in community defense and disaster response.

## Rhodes’ split from Trump

Rhodes traced the initial fracture in his loyalty to Trump's clemency decisions. While the president granted full pardons to hundreds incarcerated over the Jan. 6 Capitol assault, he merely commuted Rhodes’ prison sentence.

"That was the first inkling," Rhodes stated.

Further policy decisions deepened the rift. Rhodes condemned Trump's military actions involving Iran as a betrayal of his commitments to end overseas military conflicts. He also expressed anger when Trump brushed aside calls to publish government files connected to Jeffrey Epstein, dismissing those making the demands as "PAST supporters." Ultimately, Rhodes concluded that the administration had abandoned its core campaign pledge.

"We elected him to end the corruption in Washington, D.C. ‘Drain the swamp.’ Unfortunately his administration has been one of the most corrupt administrations in U.S. history," Rhodes said, adding, "The pardons are pay-for-play and everything is pay-for-play."

![Rhodes got a tattoo of Trump while serving a federal prison sentence for his involvement in the Jan. 6 attack](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/21/15/55/GettyImages-2218290769.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The White House didn’t respond to *AP’s* requests for comment about Rhodes' remarks. Sam Jackson, an associate professor at the University at Albany who researches anti-government movement dynamics, noted that such reversals fit a broader ideological pattern.

"They went from seeing Donald Trump as their ally in this fight against the conspiracy, to actually being co-opted by the conspiracy," Jackson said, adding that the shift "points to deeper consistency in that understanding of the world as driven by hidden, evil actors."

A graduate of Yale Law School who previously worked for former U.S. Rep. Ron Paul of Texas, Rhodes established the Oath Keepers in 2009. The group aimed to recruit military and law enforcement personnel to refuse orders they deemed unconstitutional, including gun confiscation or searches conducted without a warrant.

Rhodes eventually aligned his organization with Trump’s political agenda. After the 2020 election, the Oath Keepers mobilized behind efforts to prevent the certification of the results. According to federal prosecutors, members assembled weapons caches, conducted tactical exercises, and traveled to Washington, where a number of them joined the breach of the Capitol building.

Though Rhodes never entered the Capitol building, he was sentenced to 18 years in prison—among the longest handed down in connection with Jan. 6—after claiming in court that he was a victim of unfair prosecution and "illegality."

During sentencing, U.S. District Judge Amit Mehta highlighted the risk Rhodes posed: "You are smart, you are charismatic and compelling and frankly that’s what makes you dangerous," the judge said. "The moment you are released, whenever that may be, you will be ready to take up arms against your government."

## Watching from prison when Trump was shot

While serving his sentence, Rhodes watched Trump launch his political comeback. He recalled seeing the Butler assassination attempt on a jail TV alongside fellow prisoners. Watching Trump raise his fist and shout "fight," Rhodes initially believed divine intervention had protected the candidate.

Following his victory in the 2024 presidential election, Trump issued broad pardons to hundreds of individuals prosecuted for Jan. 6 offenses, including those convicted of assaulting police officers. Rhodes, however, received only a commutation, leaving his criminal record intact at the time.

![Rhodes says he will now get his Trump tattoo ‘modified’](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/21/14/download..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

"He let us out of prison. We were grateful. I didn’t get a pardon though," Rhodes said.

Earlier this year, the Department of Justice vacated Rhodes’ conviction, restoring his legal standing. He is now focused on reviving the Oath Keepers, which once claimed tens of thousands of members nationwide.

Upon regaining his rights, Rhodes prioritized obtaining firearms. Though he did not register to vote before the latest election, he insisted he had no intention of participating.

Rhodes said that "the Republican Party has taken us for granted," adding that he refused to select between "a half a dose of poison or a full dose."

Regarding the prison tattoo honoring Trump, Rhodes said he has no intention of leaving the artwork unchanged.

"I will have this prison tattoo of Trump ‘modified,'" he wrote in a text message, "to reflect that reality. That treason."
