A diamond ring that belonged to one of the most famous survivors of the Titanic’s sinking has fetched £150,000 at auction.

Lucy, Lady Duff Gordon was wearing the solitaire diamond ring when the liner went down on April 15, 1912, on its maiden voyage, resulting in around 1,500 deaths.

It was the only piece of jewellery of significance that she owned that didn’t go down with the ship. The family knew it as The Titanic Ring, and it was believed to have been her engagement ring.

The ring went under the hammer at Henry Aldridge & Son in Devizes, Wiltshire on Saturday, sold via a direct descendant. It blew past estimates after being expected to sell for up to £80,000 at auction.

“It’s exceptionally rare for jewellery related to a Titanic survivor to come to market but almost unprecedented for such a personal item from such a high-profile passenger to be sold at auction via direct descent,” Andrew Aldridge, of Henry Aldridge & Son, said.

The ring had been given to Lady Duff Gordon's grandson, John, the 3rd Earl of Halsbury, a British crossbencher peer and scientist.

![The diamond ring was worn by one of the most famous survivors of the sinking of the Titanic](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/20/10/40/01M7W8DRKZCFJRQPDZ119QWSRW.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

After his death, it stayed within the family and has been treasured ever since.

Lady Duff Gordon had travelled to the US in 1912 with her husband, Sir Cosmo, as first class passengers on board the Titanic.

The Duff Gordons and their secretary, Laura Mabel Francatelli, escaped the sinking in Lifeboat No 1.

The boat was designed to hold 40 people, but it was lowered with only 12 aboard, seven of them crew.

On board RMS Carpathia, the Cunard liner that rescued Titanic survivors, Sir Cosmo gave each of the men from Lifeboat No 1 a cheque for £5.

This was to help them after the White Star Line stopped their wages when the Titanic sank.

It led to gossip that the Duff Gordons had bribed their lifeboat's crew not to return to save swimmers out of fear the vessel would be swamped.

## Additional Titanic memorabilia

Elsewhere in the sale, a unique day ticket to view the Titanic on the day of its departure from Southampton sold for £40,000.

The ticket allowed the mother of a second class passenger to watch the liner's departure. The card bears the White Star burgee and is addressed to the officer in charge.

It states: “Please allow Mr Talbot and 2 friends to view the S.S. Titanic.”

It is stamped “second-class” and is dated April 10, 1912. A handwritten note on the reverse says: “Mrs Dibden plus two sisters.”

![A day ticket to view the Titanic on the day of her departure was also up for sale](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/21/20/e80f8f94751ea1510e45d37e942e9d63Y29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzk1Mzc3MzA4-2.86444030.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Eliza Dibden, the mother of 18-year-old William Dibden, from the New Forest, Hampshire, carried the ticket onto the Titanic. Her son died in the disaster.

Accompanying the ticket is her son's first communion certificate, dated Easter 1908.

A postcard written by the Titanic's senior wireless operator while the liner was docked at Southampton also went under the hammer, selling for £32,000.

Jack Phillips, 25, wrote the card, which features a photograph of the Titanic on the front, to his sister Elsie Phillips on April 6, 1912.

Phillips, described by auctioneer Andrew Aldridge as “one of the true heroes of the disaster”, sent wireless messages enlisting other ships to assist with rescuing passengers and crew as the Titanic was sinking.

The postcard, addressed to Miss Elsie Phillips of Godalming, Surrey, features a photo of the Titanic at Southampton.

It reads: “Thanks very much for your letter. Having glorious weather, went to Cowes yesterday. Will write later before we sail.”

The sale totals include buyer's premium.
