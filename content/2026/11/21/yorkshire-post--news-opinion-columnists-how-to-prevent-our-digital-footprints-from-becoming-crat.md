# How to prevent our digital footprints from becoming craters: Joanna Goodwin

## The recent US midterm elections saw the digital world crash into the physical world, with exit polls suggesting opposition to data centre expansion was a significant concern for many voters.

Republicans can point to many reasons for their losses, and the data centre issue may not have delivered a Chicxulub-level impact (some dinosaurs are more resilient than others), but the GOP, and the rest of us, should take note – the cloud doesn’t live in the sky, and its environmental footprint is very real.

When we stream a film, save a photograph, send an email, ask AI a question or upload a document, it can feel as though (by magic) nothing physical has happened at all. But everything digital is physical.

The magic happens because of devices, cables, networks, servers and enormous data centres. These are all manufactured.

They need minerals extracted from the earth. They need electricity. Their manufacture and operation can consume significant quantities of water. And eventually, they become waste.

The International Telecommunication Union estimates that the world generated 62 million tonnes of electronic waste in 2022, but only 22.3 per cent was formally collected and recycled. Contained within that waste were around 31 million tonnes of valuable metals estimated to be worth billions.

And then there’s water. The Government Digital Sustainability Alliance has specifically highlighted the growing relationship between AI, data centres and water security. Meanwhile, the latest National Framework for Water Resources warns that, without action, England could face a public water supply deficit of up to five billion litres a day by 2055, with additional pressure expected from sectors including energy, food and data centres.

Energy is also an issue. The International Energy Agency estimates that data centres consumed around 485 terawatt-hours of electricity globally in 2025. By 2030 that could rise to around 950 terawatt-hours – approximately double today’s level and around 3 per cent of global electricity demand.

Does that mean we should stop using digital technology? Of course not. Digital services can reduce travel, improve access to services, connect communities and help us solve environmental problems. However, we do need to stop treating digital resources like they are infinite. The digital world will always be limited by the physical world.

But what can we do? Isn’t it all in the hands of politicians and big business? Many US voters will beg to differ. They made their views very clear this month.

As for business, it serves demand – and we have agency over that. We all need to think about our digital clutter. How many thousands of images and videos that we’ll never look at again do we need? But more importantly, we should be keeping devices longer. Look after phones, laptops and tablets, repair them where practical and resist replacing functioning technology simply because a newer model is available.

Recycling is good; reuse is better. A functioning device often has far greater value as a device than as recovered raw material. Pass equipment on, refurbish it, donate it through an appropriate scheme or redeploy it elsewhere in an organisation.

For businesses, design digital services to do less – better. Remove unnecessary pages, steps, tracking scripts and duplicated systems. Make information easy to find and tasks easy to complete so people do not have to repeatedly search, reload pages, contact support or start processes again.

A sustainable digital service does not have to mean a stripped-back or inferior experience. Offering a clean user interface with well-optimised images that make pages faster is unlikely to annoy customers.

And using less data can reduce hosting, storage and processing costs, as well as the infrastructure needed to deliver digital services. Individually, such savings may seem small, but across large services, thousands of users and years of operation, they can become significant.

Digital sustainability isn’t about abandoning technology or sacrificing convenience. It’s about recognising that every digital service depends on finite physical resources.

Better management of those resources and smarter choices can help prevent our digital footprints becoming craters.

Joanna Goodwin is CEO and founder of Y Cewri, a consultancy, coaching and training business.
