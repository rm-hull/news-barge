On a train from Aberdeen to Glasgow during a summer trip to Scotland, I sat across from a Scottish man who offered me a can of lager. It was 11am and I politely declined but I smiled at the gesture.

It turned out there was a German man from Berlin sitting opposite us. After discovering his nationality, the Scottish man explained that his daughter was soon getting married to a German guy, and that he was struggling with his wedding speech. Could this new German acquaintance perhaps suggest a few German phrases or give him some jokes?

"I don't know what I would say," said the German man, who seemed lovely but was visibly panicking. "It's so personal."

On hearing this, I piped up: "Oh, I live in Berlin! I can help too!"

The three of us couldn't believe it. Suddenly we were all chatting, laughing and handing round the Scottish guy's phone typing German words in it and trying to explain how to pronounce them. *Prost!* was an easy one to say, I offered.

"This would *never* happen on a train in Germany," the German man said to me, signalling his disbelief at the situation.

"I know," I laughed. At the end, we shook hands and wished each other well.

**Berlin can be a cold city**

While people in Scotland are not friendly all the time – I've seen my fair share of grumpy bus drivers – that interaction reminded me how much I enjoy sparking up a chat in unlikely places.

It's no secret that Germans are not big fans of small talk. And Berlin, in particular, has a reputation for being a difficult place to make social connections.

In the 2024 InterNations Expat Insider survey, the German capital ranked last out of 53 cities for local friendliness. And the latest 2026 survey found that just 32 percent of foreign residents considered Berliners generally friendly, compared with 63 percent worldwide.

It's not that everyone is unfriendly, and I rather admire the famous *Berliner Schnauze* (Berlin snout) which provides a welcome dose of wit and humour.

But let's be honest, some people in Berlin really don't need to storm into you while walking on the street or look at you like you have just tried to steal their favourite sandwich.

This cold culture can take a long time to get used to, particularly if you come from somewhere where striking up a conversation with a complete stranger is part of everyday life.

For me the lack of spontaneous positive interactions is one of the hardest things about living here.

I was reminded of this last week when I met up with a Scottish friend for coffee and we realised that we were both feeling starved of these human moments.

It's not that we don't have lovely friends in Berlin who come from all over the world, including Germany, of course. It's more that we don't get enough of the little moments of connection with strangers.

It's the little things that get to you – like drivers not stopping to let pedestrians cross the road, or when someone walking in front of you doesn't hold the door open.

I feel like I'm wandering around desperate for a stranger to return my eye contact. 'Do I exist?', I sometimes ponder on the particularly bad days where I feel like the harsh edge of Berlin is getting me down.

When light moments do happen, I treasure them. Just the other day, an older German woman on the S-Bahn made a joke about the train changing its route. We exchanged a bit of banter and I wished her a good day when she got off. I was smiling ear-to-ear after that.

I find it gets worse in the winter months. In the summer, the cafe culture paired with sunshine makes the city come alive and there are more opportunities to talk to strangers as well as lots of visible smiles. It just isn't the same come November when it starts getting dark in the afternoon and the ping pong tables remain empty.

![A composite photo shows an S-bahn window view as seen in summer and winter.](https://apiwp.thelocal.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Lines-in-space-and-time-Berlin-S-Bahn-in-the-summer-and-the-winter..jpg)

After living here for so long, I've developed a few strategies to deal with this.

My main tactic is to smile more. In fact, I would even describe it as aggressively smiling at people.

Sometimes I walk around grinning at strangers, even if they look at me as though I've completely lost the plot. And perhaps I have but that I can live with.

I'll also start conversations even if some people initially look a little started. More often than not, they eventually come around. And chatting with varied generations also helps me improve my German.

In a way it's a little selfish (maybe it's my own version of the *Berliner Schnauze*?) because it helps me feel better, but I hope a few others enjoy it, too.

I've also realised how important it is to get to know your community. My neighbours are wonderful and I really enjoy the banter and gossip on the stairwell.

I go to my local cafe often and chat with the Gen Z staff who work there. They keep me up-to-date with the best queer parties while I pet the neighbourhood dogs.

I have even started talking to the people who work at the local bakery, asking them their weekend plans.

One place I have not tried to spread my aggressive friendliness is the supermarket because I value my life and I'm not sure what would happen if I tried to make small talk at the *Kasse*.

While I want to bring a little more warmth to Berlin, I also know my limits.
