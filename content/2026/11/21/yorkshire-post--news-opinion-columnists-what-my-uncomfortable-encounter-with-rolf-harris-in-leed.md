# What my uncomfortable encounter with Rolf Harris in Leeds hospital explains about the silence around Russell Brand’s behaviour: David Behrens

## There’s a reason you’ve never seen prison newsletters from Ronnie and Reggie Kray or any of the current crop of crooks serving their time at HMP Wakefield and just longing to tell the world of the injustice that has befallen them. It’s forbidden, that’s why, and even the Krays knew that lawbreaking has its limits.

Putting someone behind bars means not only denying their liberty but removing them from society to a place where they can do their victims no more harm, at least not physically. It’s a fundamental tenet of British justice from which no criminal is exempt. Russell Brand’s KC was apparently too busy reporting himself to the Bar Standards Board to give his client the message.

So last weekend on their monetised online ‘show’, Brand’s wife Laura blithely read out the protestation of innocence he had composed in jail, to the audience of Christian extremists he has befriended. He always was a shapeshifter, pandering to both the political left and the hard right as it suited him.

Now his wife is also his accomplice and it’s hard not to see her as another victim, gaslit by the firestorm of his unchecked personality. It makes her a walking metaphor for the industry that gave him life.

The entertainment world has always protected its stars because stars bankroll the studios. Secretaries, make-up girls, scriptwriters are replaceable but stars are indelible; that has been the business model since they hushed up Charlie Chaplin’s marriage to a pregnant 16-year-old, 102 years ago. Errol Flynn and Alfred Hitchcock were among many grateful for the protective embrace of their studios long before the days of Harvey Weinstein and Bill Cosby.

In Britain we worked off the same script. TV stars who brought huge audiences to their networks were protected by unwritten rules which discouraged junior staff from filing complaints against ‘the talent’. David Walliams is the latest on a long and ignominious list.

It is tempting to think – and producers and politicians have been quick to say it – that Walliams’ outing and Brand’s imprisonment mark a turning point. But it took 17 years to bring Brand to justice and it is 22 years since Jonathan Ross joked on TV about Walliams’ penchant for teenage girls. That points not to contrition but to a creative community revelling in its own reputation.

The comedy sector in particular must see the hypocrisy of slapping down unfashionable performers like Benny Hill for objectifying women on screen but backslapping profitable ones like Walliams for allegedly doing it in real life.

I can’t exclude myself from these double standards. In 1988, the year before Hill’s show was cancelled by Thames TV, I was running an entertainment series at ITV in Yorkshire and we booked Rolf Harris to perform his three-legged Jake the Peg routine to an audience of children in a hospital ward in Leeds.

Given what we know now about Rolf Harris, the idea should have raised more red flags than a May Day parade but at the time he was arguably Britain’s best-loved children’s entertainer and his presence, we thought, would brighten the blighted lives of the young patients.

More importantly still, his career was in a lull and his reduced fee fell within our measly budget. Therein lies the truth at the heart of all television production.

During a break in filming while Rolf kicked his three heels, he made an extremely lewd remark about a passing nurse. I wasn’t on the spot when it happened but I was told about it a few minutes later. I don’t think the nurse heard it.

I mentioned it to colleagues but none of us took it seriously. The person who told me had couched it as an anecdote, not a complaint. No crime had been committed and lewd remarks were just part of the working language.

But of course our silence was cancerous, a void in which bad behaviour could incubate, a protective shield around the perpetrator. There must have been hundreds of such incidents and each one would have emboldened Rolf Harris to go on pushing the envelope of impropriety until, eventually, someone got hurt.

The same equation of acquiescence and inaction is what enabled Russell Brand – and if he were the reborn Christian he claims to be, he would know that.

This week some 400 people in the TV industry signed an open, eloquent letter warning that without an end to the culture of ‘open secrets’ in their workplaces there could be no cultural turning point.

It was a timely intervention, especially with Russell Brand still apparently free to martyr himself on social media. Silence was what empowered him and silence is what must now be forced upon him.
