Business leaders can learn a lot from the latest UK government reshuffle, as it says something important about where AI is heading. On 21 July the UK government took the decision to abolish the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology.

Three days later, it announced a Prime Ministerial AI Taskforce positioning AI no longer as a standalone technology bolted onto government but as an integral part of the operating structure of every department.

As AI officially takes its seat at the UK Cabinet table, businesses must now consider what the equivalent shift looks like inside their own operations. For most, AI is already running inside software teams, tools and daily workflows.

The real question will be how to organize it with the same clarity of remit, budget and accountability as any other function.

President, New Enterprise, at Cognition.

For most, it will mean making a fundamental shift and treating AI as something that can take on defined responsibilities, operate within clear boundaries and deliver measurable outcomes alongside human teams.

Software engineering is where this gap between use and organization is most visible. Teams are already relying on AI day to day, but few have restructured around it, so the immediate opportunity is disciplined deployment, not first-time adoption. The larger effect will be a change in the economics of what businesses can build.

To deliver such transitions will require software. Whether it's a new service connecting with legacy systems or manual processes becoming reliable workflows, data needs to move from old to new platforms and this is often where transformation plans slow down. The business may understand the opportunity perfectly well, but the lack of engineering capacity is often a bottleneck.

## Ambition isn't the problem

The UK does not lack ambition, with a growing array of research and startup companies. The government's Technology Adoption Review estimated that embracing AI could increase UK productivity by 1.5% a year, worth as much as £47 billion annually over the next decade. That's the scale of the opportunity on offer, but the gains will only appear once the innovation moves from pilots into everyday operations.

The IET's 2025 skills survey found that 76% of engineering employers struggle to recruit for key roles, with software engineering among the hardest disciplines to fill. Autonomous software engineering doesn't close that gap by producing more engineers, it closes it by introducing greater scalability and flexibility into their daily operations.

We get it, every proposed project must have a clear commercial implication, and its expected value must outweigh the cost to plan and build it. Autonomous software engineering changes part of that equation, cutting the backlog at a fraction of the cost.

## From bottlenecks to positive workflows

AI software engineering is different from an autocomplete tool. It can receive an objective, inspect a codebase, plan the work, use engineering tools, write and test code, respond to failures and produce an output for human review. Suddenly engineers are setting the direction of work, making architectural decisions and resolving any ambiguities in the process.

Multiple tasks can now run in parallel, with longer pieces of work continuing in the background while the human team works elsewhere. That creates additional capacity that can be deployed as new tasks arise, without needing to permanently grow headcount.

Engineering capacity is not becoming free or unlimited as a result of this capacity growth, as security, infrastructure and maintenance remain real, constant costs. What autonomous software engineering delivers is more flexibility in the system, while at the same time reducing overall costs, enabling businesses to explore new areas of growth sooner than previously possible.

## New ways of working become the norm

Historically, larger companies have been able to support dedicated engineering teams while smaller businesses have had to concentrate limited technical resources on a narrow set of priorities. This matters particularly in the UK where smaller businesses (SMEs) account for 99.85% of the private-sector business population.

However, with autonomous engineering, smaller teams can maintain more integrations and automate more of its operations without the need to employ more engineers or radically grow the business. SMEs won't suddenly operate like large businesses, which still hold advantages in capital, distribution and customer relationships. But access to greater engineering capacity may become less of a barrier to competition.

Autonomous engineering also creates different opportunities for larger organizations. The increase in capacity means that long delayed or lower value projects that lacked immediate commercial benefits, can be actioned. Human architects and domain experts remain essential to any project. AI simply expands what they can act on, which lowers the barrier to success.

The sectors most likely to feel this first are the ones where legacy technology has kept transformation slow and expensive. Areas such as insurance, logistics, and certain parts of financial services and healthcare administration are all areas where core systems have historically needed scarce, specialist engineering time. As that constraint eases, modernizing legacy systems stops being a multi-year program, opening opportunities for companies regardless of size.

## Managing the new expectations

Making software easier to produce does not equate to creating more value. To ensure that new ways of working deliver for the business, new software management controls are required. This means investing in staff, so they fully understand how to work with the new tools, including explicit standards for when human approval is required.

Measurement must change as well. Tokens consumed, prompts submitted and lines of code produced say little about business value. Leaders should look at useful work accepted, time to production and cost per completed outcome.

Productive hours are not the same as full business ROI. That depends on whether the organization chose the right work in the first place. But measuring useful output is a far stronger starting point than measuring AI activity.

## Fewer bottlenecks

The UK government's restructuring won't solve Britain's productivity problem on its own. But it recognizes something businesses can't ignore: AI now belongs inside every economic and business strategy.

For businesses to find a new way ahead they will need to make the same logical leap and embrace what AI has to offer. Autonomous software engineering won't solve all the problems within an organization, but it will enable them to consider a larger set of opportunities.

Those that can give AI systems the context and the controls to streamline operations once deemed too time consuming, or too low value to be of worth, while retaining clear human accountability, will readily find a world of reduced bottlenecks.

Agents are really great at well scoped work, particularly if they have deep knowledge of your systems and code bases. The new bottleneck is new work and initiatives. We've seen startups have no shortage of roadmap to quickly execute on, but larger companies need to reorient themselves to scope and deliver work to agents in a new way.
