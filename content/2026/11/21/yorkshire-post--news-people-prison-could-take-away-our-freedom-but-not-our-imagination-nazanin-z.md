# 'Prison could take away our freedom but not our imagination' - Nazanin Zaghari-Ratcliffe on how sewing helped her through Iran detention

## Nazanin Zaghari-Ratcliffe tells Laura Reid about how crafting helped her during her detention in Iran and what life has been like since returning to the UK, ahead of an appearance in Harrogate.

![](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOmI2MDAxOTIwLTUxYzgtNGE5OS1hODY0LWQ3N2UyOWEzMWI5Mjo1NmJlYzYwZS00MDJlLTRjYjItOGE1NC0xMTg3NWFiZjY4ZTg=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=990&quality=70&enable=upscale)


It was still prison. But after nine months of being “deprived and disconnected” in “the silent torture” of solitary confinement, when Nazanin Zaghari-Ratcliffe was moved to a general detention ward for political prisoners, her first thought was ‘I can endure this’.

There were books, there was opportunity for creativity, and there were other people - united somewhat through their collective suffering. British-Iranian national Nazanin was detained in Iran for nearly six years, jailed on false espionage charges in 2016 after being arrested whilst attempting to return to the UK following a visit to see family with her then young daughter, Gabriella. “Creativity kept me going,” she says. “Crafting and books. I don’t know what I would have done without either of them.”

Today, former charity worker Nazanin is appearing in Harrogate for the Knit + Stitch show. She’ll be speaking at the event about how sewing became a lifeline for her during her detention and how she fought for permission to have fabric sent into prison so she could make clothes for her daughter.

![The Knit + Stitch show is taking place in Harrogate this weekend.](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOjBkMzI4ZmRmLWM4OWYtNDlhMS1iZGU1LWIyOTg4MGI0MWEzYTo3NzViOGVjOS1jYTg1LTQyYjUtYjQxYy00MWM3YzdlNjIwMmM=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)


Like for many of those she encountered, crafting was a way to pass the time. Inside prison walls, it also presented an opportunity for her mind to be engaged elsewhere. “I think time was one of the main troubles in prison,” 48-year-old Nazanin eeflects. “It stretches in prison and loses its meaning. You have to find different ways to fill it up.”

“Being creative was mainly, collectively, about us trying to find a safe space to just disconnect from where we were,” she continues. “It was also about affirming our identity. Prison can take away our freedom but it cannot take away our imagination...It was a way to connect ourselves to the life we had before and the life we would hopefully be going to.”

Nazanin, who had been interested in crafting for a long time having been taught to knit and crochet by her mum, spent hours of her early days in prison tinkering with scrap pieces of leather to fashion necklaces and bookmarks. But it was sewing that really captured her imagination. “We had one single sewing machine in prison, an industrial very old one, which was mainly used for mending as opposed to making things,” she recalls. “It was old, creaky, not at all easy to work with, but it would do the job...We didn’t have a huge amount of fabric either. We had to upcycle, use bed linens, table cloths.”

Nazanin was supported by one of the prison teachers, who ran various workshops in skills such as leather work and jewellery making and also worked as a seamstress outside the prison walls. She recognised Nazanin’s keenness to learn and helped her to make a base pattern, which she then went on to use in sewing and embroidery projects. But she needed more fabric. “It was very tricky to send fabrics to prison because you were only allowed to have made garments and a limited number,” Nazanin says. “So any fabric would eat up my allowance.”

In the end, she managed to get special permission to have fabric brought to her; a precious assortment of Liberty material which she’d been collecting back home for “a special occasion”. “With the bigger pieces, I made a couple of matching dresses for my daughter Gabriella and myself,” Nazanin says. “But we never got to wear those dresses at the same time.”

“Seeing my daughter in them was very uplifting,” she adds. “I think the other women were impressed I’d made pretty little dresses for her from the Liberty fabric. She was the youngest child to be visiting her mum in prison…So everyone would adore her. She would come with so much attitude and personality wearing these dresses. It was magical. It almost felt like we weren’t in prison for that period of visiting time. That was my intention, to do something that escapes me, takes me out of where I was and for a brief period of time, takes me to where I wanted to be.”

Back in the UK, Nazanin’s husband Richard led a long campaign to raise awareness of his wife’s detention. After a period of house arrest with her parents in Iran, Nazanin was finally released and returned to the UK in 2022. Whilst in Iran, even outside of prison walls, she says was scared to acknowledge her ‘freedom’, worried she would be incarcerated once again. “I think psychologically I was still stuck in prison because I had lived there for such a long time, I had made my own routine, I had made my own projects. I was used to that life,” she says.

Even returning to the UK was bittersweet; relief she was finally back home in London but sadness for those she had left behind in prison and for how difficult she knew it would now be to see her family, who lived in Iran. Release was “the best feeling in the world”, she says, but freedom was “complicated and a lot more difficult than I expected”. “When you go through such an experience and you come out of it, you underestimate how things have changed. I thought I would come back where I left. It wasn’t anything even close to that.

“I had come back after six years and people had changed, my daughter wasn’t a toddler anymore, she was nearly eight. The world had gone through covid, the world had changed. I underestimated how different my life, and my connection with society, with my friends, with my family, would be.”

“I feel like I can’t keep up with the speed of life that everybody else feels very comfortable with,” she continues. “I spend a lot of time at home…I cannot complain because I am a free person but I spend a lot of time pondering and thinking about things.”

Having lived through such a traumatic experience, Nazanin, who is still undergoing therapy, says she can find global events “triggering”. “I am still recovering; I wasn’t expecting that to take so long but considering what has been happening in the world and in Iran, I think recovery has taken a lot longer than what I had imagined. I spend a lot of time thinking about the people in Iran, the people in the world, but also my parents, my family.”

“I think what’s happening in Iran is so heartbreaking,” she adds. “I am a peace maker, I don’t really believe that any democracy can be built on dead bodies. I don’t believe a military attack is a route to freedom. But even for those who felt that was the case, the situation in Iran has actually got worse. People are dealing with a lot of poverty and not being able to make ends meet, rocketing inflation…It’s also horrific living under uncertainty, not knowing what’s going to happen tomorrow morning…They can't have a normal life. Everything is put on hold and that’s heartbreaking.”

Since returning to the UK, Nazanin has split her time between writing, public speaking and creative work, including making her own clothes. “Sewing at the moment is one of the most exciting parts of my day,” she says. “It is still one of my joys.”

One of the highlights was her involvement a project with Liberty and the Imperial War Museum which explored how creativity can flourish even in the most challenging circumstances and saw the creation of three fabrics inspired by motifs of conflict and confinement. Some of the material was donated to the charity Fine Cell Work, which offers paid, craft opportunities to people in prison to support rehabilitation.

Nazanin is now set to work on a project with the charity using the fabric inside prisons. “What will be interesting for me to see is this circle being complete,” she says. “I received Liberty fabric in prison and then fast forward these prisoners will receive Liberty fabric, this time with (stories of incarceration) within the fabric. That’s the most exciting part of this project, to see the loop complete.”
