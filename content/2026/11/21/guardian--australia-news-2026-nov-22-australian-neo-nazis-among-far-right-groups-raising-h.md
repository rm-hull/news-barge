Australian neo-Nazis have raised hundreds of thousands of dollars on the global crowdfunding platform GiveSendGo, which has been accused of facilitating the financing of hate and extremism.

Of the 68 Australian fundraisers on the site, the White Australia party has raised the fourth-highest amount of money with $200,000 for its high court battle to overturn its ban as a designated hate group.

Neo-Nazi Thomas Sewell’s legal defence fund is sixth at almost $110,000. Sewell pleaded guilty to violent disorder in October. He and others allegedly stormed Camp Sovereignty in 2025 after taking part in an anti-immigration march. Five people were injured.

GiveSendGo was set up after GoFundMe started cracking down on hate groups. It says its aims are to “share the Hope of Jesus through crowdfunding” and to “stand for freedom”.

Most crowdfunding on the site is for uncontroversial causes – medical procedures, funerals for relatives, saving endangered species or creative projects.

Other asks are overtly political – the top Australian fundraisers are from anti-trans and anti-abortion activists.

Last year a Minnesota woman who admitted calling a child the N-word raised more than US$750,000 on the site to “protect [her] family”.

The co-founder of GiveSendGo, Jacob Wells, defended hosting that fundraiser, saying he believed in “freedom of speech, freedom of association” and that those were foundational tenets of society.

In 2022 the US Anti-Defamation League (ADL) Centre on Extremism found GiveSendGo hosted 230 campaigns “operated by or for extremists and their causes”. The centre found crowdfunding platforms had raised more than US$6m for extremist campaigns, and GiveSendGo was responsible for collecting more than 86.5% of that money from 2016 to 2022.

“Extremist campaigns found on these platforms espouse hateful rhetoric including antisemitism, white supremacy, QAnon conspiracies and anti-LGBTQ+ extremism, as well as rhetoric from antisemitic sects of Black Hebrew Israelites,” it found.

In 2023, the Australia/Israel & Jewish Affairs Council (Aijac) called on the Conservative Political Action Conference (CPAC) Australia – which that year featured the Coalition senator Jacinta Nampijinpa Price and former prime minister Tony Abbott – to dump GiveSendGo as its gold sponsor because of its use by neo-Nazis and other extremist groups.

A spokesperson for GiveSendGo said it did not “condone racism, discrimination, hate speech, or violence of any kind”.

“We also do not allow fundraisers that promote or glorify violence, raise money for illegal activity, or mislead givers. We follow the laws of the countries we operate in, and we review every reported fundraiser against our terms of service,” the spokesperson said.

“Fundraisers that violate those rules are subject to moderation or removal.”

Dr Matt Wade, a senior lecturer in social inquiry at LaTrobe University, has studied the interaction between crowdfunding platforms and extremist movements.

Speaking broadly about GiveSendGo, not specifically about Australia, he said platform’s policies and how they are enforced had “gravely serious implications” and that GiveSendGo’s claim to be a free speech defender made it a “safe haven for white supremacist and neo-Nazi causes”.

Hosting a fundraising campaign tacitly lent any cause legitimacy, he said.

“Crucially, in key cases that are harbingers of growing far-right influence, these donations are not simply expressions of sympathy or support, but outright endorsements of the actions that the beneficiaries took,” he said.

GiveSendGo says in its terms of service that it reviews every fundraiser and verifies the recipient’s identity, and that funds are disbursed to the recipient only once they have completed a compliance process.

It expects donors to give only to those they know and trust, and does not confirm that every statement in a fundraiser is true.

It bans any fundraisers for abortion, gender reassignment for minors and anything illegal.

The spokesperson said people had a right to ask for help, others had the right to decide whether to give, and that GiveSendGo was “not a place of judgment but a place of generosity”.

Wade said GiveSendGo’s claim to ban the promotion of racism was questionable.

“Many would argue that, at best, the qualifier of ‘stated or demonstrable purpose’ is wilfully naive and far too narrowly construed, given the ultimate aspirations and raison d’être of some of the figures they host on their platform,” he said.

The top Australian fundraiser currently on the site is for Sall Grover, founder of the Giggle for Girls app, with almost $2m raised. Grover is fighting the federal court finding that her app discriminated against trans woman Roxanne Tickle.

Next is the anti-abortion activist Joanna Howe, who has raised almost $400,000 to pursue legal action under the Fair Work Act against her employer, Adelaide University, because of its investigations into almost 200 allegations against her.

Her husband, James Howe, has raised another $180,000 for a potential defamation case.

Both Howes say on the site they would use any money left over to “transition” their careers.

The Sedra family has raised about $340,000 for help to “move to a safe home”. Andrew and Noleen Sedra, a married couple who are both pastors at the Pentecostal Echo church in Sydney, claim they were threatened at their home by “LGBTQ left-wing activists”. Other reports have cast doubt on that claim.

The Sedras were contacted for comment.
