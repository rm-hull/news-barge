# The Yorkshire Vet: Our cutting response to a log jam​

## My cousin has recently acquired a small wood. He’s always been interested in conservation, so the main purpose of the purchase was to become the custodian of trees, flora and fauna within its boundaries. Another was that it would provide a constant and never-ending supply of firewood. At a family event earlier this year, he explained the logistics.

It was fine to cut up trees which had been blown down by gales, but if a healthy tree was felled, the rules dictated that a larger number of new trees be planted by way of regeneration. However, it was also very much “safety first” and he explained that he didn’t like to go down to the woods alone. Trees partly fallen and resting on a neighbour were to be avoided at all costs. He referred to them as “widow-makers” on the basis that felling them was dangerous since the teetering trunks could fall in unpredictable ways. The ill-effects of a chain-saw catastrophe were all too obvious. Without another human around to raise the alarm and apply a tourniquet, the consequences could be dire.

My ears pricked up and I quickly volunteered to go along whenever he next needed a visit. I love the idea of foraging from nature, whether it’s blackberries, apples or logs for the fire. We arranged a rendez vous on the next Sunday when we were both available. Firstly, we circumnavigated the wood, identifying likely trunks that were lying horizontal and especially those close to the footpath. These were the priority, but not before I’d had a lesson on chainsaws. How I’ve reached my mid-fifties without having got my hands on such a manly tool I do not know. Brought up in Castleford, I didn’t really have access to woods and there was no concept of burning anything other than coal, so there wasn’t much need for a chainsaw. My youthful memories of cold, damp November evenings are of nostrils filled with sooty and sulphurous coal fumes so one of the joys of living in a more rural place is the amazing smell of wood smoke from a countryside fire. Anyway, now was the time to learn some power tool moves. The chainsaw cut through the fallen tree trunks like a knife through butter and we soon had a pleasing pile of logs to move.

Moreover, the space that we had cleared seemed like a canvas for new life. Saplings and small bushes, which had gone previously unnoticed appeared to stretch out their branches as if free to thrive. I couldn’t wait to return to the woods in the future to see how this ecosystem would evolve as the mini shrubs developed. It was hard work and, after three hours of sawing, lifting, wheelbarrowing and loading the trailer, it was time for our picnic lunch. As we found a spot to sit down, we reminisced over outdoor adventures that we had shared when we were kids. Forty years ago, we might have been doing something similar, though without the power-tools. As we opened our snap, we realised with amusement, that we both had both brought a flask of wholesome pumpkin soup that we had made ourselves! It was fitting given the season and aptly confirmed our resourcefulness. Amusingly and earnestly, we compared our potages. Mine was a more rustic affair, with added lentils, bits of blitzed pumpkin skin and a chilli kick. It contrasted with the more refined, smoother and distinctly more curried alternative. But there wasn’t much time to compare culinary creations (though we did marvel at how two soups could taste so different). There were more trunks to section and more heavy lifting to be done. In mid-November, the sun doesn’t linger for long and its orange beams were already casting longer shadows, so it was time to get back to work.
