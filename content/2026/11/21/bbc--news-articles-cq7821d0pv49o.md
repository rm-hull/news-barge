Phoebe Bridgers' concerts have inspired some fans to explore their artistic talent

**Phoebe Bridgers has drawn in fans over the years with her raw, vulnerable lyrics and indie sound that can make even the messiest emotions feel less lonely.**

The US artist's third album Lost Weekend, released this summer after a six-year wait, delves into falling in and out of love, grappling with change and the confusing feelings that come with grief.

On her tour of the Grammy-nominated record, Bridgers has introduced a no-phone policy - a move that's had an unexpected side effect.

Instead of taking photos and videos of the shows, some are creating artwork as a reminder of the gigs.

Massive Bridgers fan Olivia MacDonald, from Massachusetts, attended the singer's show in Boston last month.

She was initially "shocked" by the no-phone rule, as employed by artists including Bob Dylan and Jack White, but became a convert.

Without the distraction of phones, she says, fans chatted, danced and sang along with each other.

"Once I got to the concert, I realised this is such a unique way to connect and bring everybody together," she says.

MacDonald, a pen and ink artist, brought her sketchbook along to the show after spotting fellow fans' interpretations on social media.

She relived the gig by drawing standout moments such as Bridgers' dripping, electric blue eye make-up and her guitar, originally owned by the late American musician Elliott Smith.

"Everybody takes away something different," says 28-year-old MacDonald.

"That's the beauty of it, that everybody can have a different interpretation."

Sharing her designs on social media helped her connect with other fans, who reached out to discuss their experiences of the show.

"I feel like with photos or videos, you capture a moment in time, and with drawings, you really capture a feeling and people can connect to that feeling," she says.

"It sparks more conversation too."

Bridgers has navigated an emotional whirlwind since she was propelled to stardom with the release of her emotionally charged and critically acclaimed album Punisher in 2020.

The 32-year-old Californian has toured and won three Grammys with supergroup Boygenius, lived out a high-profile romance and breakup with Irish actor Paul Mescal, and lost her dad in 2023.

Fans believe she explores some of these experiences in her latest album.

MacDonald says Bridgers explained to the Boston crowd that the no-phone rule would allow her to talk openly with her audience like friends, without the worry of those moments ending up online.

Some fans expressed worries the policy could exclude people with disabilities or those who might need to contact someone in an emergency.

MacDonald says she shared the reservations, but points out fans were able to keep their phones on them and access the sealed pouches they were stored in at special designated areas inside the venue.

Fan Anna Meyer, who attended the New York show, agrees the device-free aspect made the show a better experience for artist and audience, and allowed the special lighting effects to be fully appreciated without being distorted by phone lights.

The graphic designer, 35, chose to capture this element afterwards - focusing on a poignant moment when a kneeling Bridgers played piano in the glow of a spotlight.

"It was so cathartic to feel so inspired," she says.

"And I do wonder, if I did have my phone, would I have felt this need to sit down and immediately draw something from the show?"

Meyer feels the trend is particularly special as it's put a focus on techniques, such as pen and paper, in an age when traditional art feels under threat.

"We're now in an era of AI where they want us to believe if you want to create something visual, you have to use a computer and AI programme," she says.

"And it's just not true.

"We all have the ability to create, and our own creations that come from our own mind and hand are far better than anything that a computer can create.

"And sometimes even far better than anything that a camera can capture."

Phoebe Bridgers fan Anna Meyer's time-lapse of her artwork inspired by the singer's gig had a positive response online

Followers of the new trend argue it also helps to guard against gig spoilers, where big moments go viral on social media before crowds get to see them in-person.

Lillie Collins, a fan from Michigan, feels artwork offers "a little teaser" rather than giving everything away.

The 20-year-old painted her own interpretation after seeing Bridgers' show in Detroit and her videos revealing the piece blew up on TikTok.

She says the wave of positive responses was "very exciting" as she'd never had that reaction before, and it's now made her consider that creating art could be a successful career route.

Bridgers herself appears to approve too, as her team has shared several posts praising the artworks, external.

MacDonald and Meyer were among those who got shout-outs on the official Lost Tour Instagram page, as was Collins, who says the idea Bridgers may have seen her artwork had her "screaming".

"I really appreciate how she was able to bring this level of creativity and humanity into her performances," she says.

Bridgers will kick off her UK run of shows at Manchester's Co-op Live on Thursday, followed by dates in Glasgow, Birmingham and London.

Ayanoh Nakamoto was one of the fans who attended a special intimate show at Union Chapel, London, where Bridgers tested the no-phone policy.

She will be back in the crowd for the first London show next month, and says the "excitement has built up so much because we haven't been able to see what is really happening in the US right now".

"It's just so cool to have an experience where it feels like it isn't spoilt for you," she says.

Despite Nakamoto running a TikTok page where she shares her concert video content, she admits it feels liberating to put her phone away and be in the moment.

She jokes she doesn't have an "artistic bone" in her body, but thinks this might be her chance to try her hand at drawing, even if it is a stick-figure sketch.

"I wonder if this is going to be a stepping stone for artists to maybe have this at more concerts and just feel that connection back to the audience in the future."
