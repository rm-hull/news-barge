## Starling murmurations: Best places to see autumn's favourite spectacles near York

Wonder of nature - a starling murmuration. Image: Andy Louch

*Best places to see starling murmurations and other wonders of nature near York this autumn and winter, by AMY COOPER of the Yorkshire Wildlife Trust*IT is a crisp, clear day as I wind my way between the fringed reedbed pools; cold enough that the wind goes right through me, but more than made up for by the reflection of the low sun in the glassy water, the cacophony of birdlife squabbling on the edges, and the feeling that something very magical is just around the corner…**Starling murmurations**

A fluttering, bubbling noise immediately makes me look up. Starling murmurations are one of nature’s most awe-inspiring spectacles, the calm skies filled with a whirling, liquid-like mass of birds turning in a gentle patter of wing-beats and stunning synchronicity.

No one knows for certain why starlings put on such mesmerising aerial acrobatics during the winter months, but avoiding predators through safety in numbers is one common theory. Autumn roosts usually begin to form in November, and last until early January.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21681078.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Wonder of nature - a starling murmuration. Image: Andy Louch

More and more birds will flock together as the weeks go on, and the number of starlings in a roost can swell to around 100,000 in some places.

Describing a starling murmuration rarely does it justice – it is best to witness for yourself. Yorkshire Wildlife Trust’s Ripon City Wetlands nature reserve, just 25 miles from York is one of the best places in North Yorkshire to see this season’s favourite spectacle. The birds start to gather around 30 minutes from sunset and can be seen performing across the reserve until they descend into the reedbeds to roost – flattening them in the process. Watching a many thousand strong flock ripple and dance across a dusky pink winter sky is a wonderful way to welcome in the New Year. Nearby Staveley nature reserve, even closer at 20 miles from York, also hosts the occasional starling murmuration, easily enjoyed from the central hill on the reserve, as are North Cave Wetlands near Hull and Potteric Carr in Doncaster.

Murmurations are not easily predictable, and starlings do not murmurate every evening. I would recommend reaching your intended reserve early to enjoy the rest of the winter wildlife in case the starlings are a no-show. Do also take a look at Yorkshire Wildlife Trust’s website for advice on where to see starling murmurations, timings, car parking and staying safe in the half light.

**Pink footed geese**

The autumn migration is in full flow and pink footed geese – one of the easiest migratory species to spot - have arrived from the Arctic to spend the next few weeks in east Yorkshire. Some birds will remain here for the winter whilst others will head further south to overwinter in East Anglia and the east midlands. About 360,000 individuals spend the winter in the UK, feeding on our wetland and farmland habitats.

Each morning, the birds travel inland from their overnight stay on the banks of the Humber to feed and rest in the fields of the High Wolds above Pocklington and Market Weighton. As a result, several of Yorkshire Wildlife Trust’s wetland reserves are the ideal place to watch pink footed geese pass over – definitely a fantastic early-morning spectacle as the geese fly in fairly large numbers. Chance a dawn visit to Wheldrake Ings and North Cave Wetlands and turn your eyes to the skies, or visit Paull Holme Strays and Kilnsea Wetlands on the banks of the Humber of an evening to see them settling down for the night.

**Kingfishers**

Kingfishers are an exotic sight at any time of year – but particularly during the winter months. The zip-past of a bright flash of blue can be a real highlight against the drab and colder days and fewer leaves can make them easier to spot, though they can still blend in surprisingly well with their surroundings.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21681079.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Kingfisher. Photo: Sean Jones

They are often heard before they’re seen, giving a high-pitched piping call as they fly, and can sometimes be spotted sitting quietly on low-hanging branches over the water and searching for prey. When a kingfisher spots a small fish, it dives into the water to catch it, bringing its prize back to its perch to eat. The biggest surprise of the kingfisher is that it isn’t actually blue! Their feathers are a dull brown, and appear blue because tiny cavities in their features scatter and reflect just blue light back.

Kingfishers are a familiar sight at many of Yorkshire Wildlife Trust’s nature reserves. Adel Dam in Leeds is so famous for its resident kingfishers that it has a wooden kingfisher sculpture, but regular sights are also reported at Potteric Carr in Doncaster, North Cave Wetlands and Staveley near Boroughbridge.
