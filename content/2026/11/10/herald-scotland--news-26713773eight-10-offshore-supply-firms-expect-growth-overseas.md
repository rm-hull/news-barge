Eight in ten supply-chain companies expect a greater proportion of future investment and growth to happen outside the UK, according to a new report by an energy sector body.

Confidence, according to the Offshore Energies UK (OEUK) report, is low. Despite the £123 billion of risked offshore energy investment, just 35% of the 60 companies surveyed are confident about their UK business outlook over the next 12 months.

The report, OEUK supply chain and people director Katy Heidenreich writes in its introduction, is “a warning”.

“Companies,” she observes, “see opportunity ahead, but too many are increasingly looking overseas for growth. Once investment, skilled people and assets move elsewhere, they are difficult to bring back.”

“If we allow today's capability to erode before tomorrow's markets are ready to sustain it, we will make our energy ambitions harder and more expensive to deliver.”

The report, titled Backing Britain’s Offshore Energy Supply Chain, sees oil and gas as “an important part” of the picture of producing homegrown energy and industrial strength.

It outlines how the energy transition has not yet replaced oil and gas as the economic foundation of the supply chain.

Nine in ten companies derive more than 20% of revenue from oil and gas. Offshore wind exceeds that 20% threshold for only about one in ten respondents. However, climate change campaigners argue that putting oil and gas at the heart of net zero risks locking in fossil-fuel dependence, delaying cleaner energy, increasing cumulative emissions and relying heavily on unproven carbon capture.

For instance, following the Scottish Government’s decision to approve the new Peterhead gas-fired power station, Oxfam Scotland’s head, Katherine May, said: “Our political leaders seem trapped in a fossil fuel fantasy where every new project is somehow sold as climate progress.”

Ahead of the launch of the OEUK report, Heidenreich said: “Oil and gas activity and the revenue it generates remain crucial to sustaining the UK’s offshore supply chain, supporting skilled jobs and giving businesses the means to invest in the energy transition.”

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/12249838.jpg?type=mds-article-620) *(Image: Getty Images)*

The report notes that oil and gas “provide energy today, but they also sustain much of the capability, infrastructure and investment that the wider offshore energy system relies upon.”

Heidenreich explained: “It must be a managed transition that keeps businesses working, retains value in the UK and supports secure, affordable, lower-carbon energy.”

Rystad Energy analysis commissioned by OEUK found that the existing UK oil and gas supply chain holds between 50% and 80% of the capabilities required across renewables and carbon capture and storage (CCS).

“Uncertainty,” Heidenreich told The Herald, is one of the biggest issues across all areas of the energy sector. In the report she writes: “We need clearer and more credible project pipelines. Better procurement and commercial behaviours. Earlier engagement with the supply chain. Investment targeted where the UK can genuinely compete. And stable fiscal, regulatory and market conditions that give businesses a reason to invest here.”

The report includes quotes from companies within the sector outlining the problems. “We are a proud UK company,” says one, “but current uncertainty in the market, including regulation, policy, taxation and consents, means that our investments overseas present less risk and better returns.”

Another describes: “We are being squeezed at both ends: more taxation from government and more punitive terms and conditions being cascaded through the supply chain, including bank guarantees, warranties and liabilities.”

But the report notes that offshore wind represents the “largest source of prospective capital investment in the UK offshore energy pipeline”. Among OEUK members, decommissioning generates “the strongest growth expectations” and “oil and gas ranks second, showing that respondents continue to see growth opportunities in the sector”.

“Offshore wind,” it says, “follows closely behind. To justify investment in additional people, equipment and facilities, suppliers need confidence in the timing, continuity and commercial viability of future work.”
