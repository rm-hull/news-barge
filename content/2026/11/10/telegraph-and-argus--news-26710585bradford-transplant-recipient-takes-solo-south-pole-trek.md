A Bradford man is aiming to become the first organ transplant recipient to complete a solo expedition to the South Pole.

James Garnett, from Eldwick, is planning the world-first challenge nearly 20 years after receiving a life-saving kidney transplant at St George’s Hospital in London.

He said: "I’ve chosen to take on such a tough expedition and world-first to not only raise as much money as I can but to also raise as much awareness about this hospital, chronic organ donation shortages, and to push my boundaries and the boundaries and perceptions of what organ transplant patients can achieve."

Mr Garnett will set off this month on a 50-day unsupported journey across Antarctica.

He will cover 566 miles while pulling 100kg of food and equipment in sub-zero temperatures.

He is taking on the challenge in memory of his mother, who died of kidney failure.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21687415.jpg?type=mds-article-620)James Garnett on a previous expedition in Greenland *(Image: UGC)*

He hopes the expedition will inspire others and raise awareness of organ donation.

Mr Garnett says he could make the journey only because of the care he received at St George’s Hospital.

He received a kidney from his younger sister when he was 21.

He said: "The tireless staff at St George’s saved my life when I received my kidney transplant from my sister back in 2008, and the staff at BRI Hospital in Bradford fought very hard to try and keep my mum alive while seriously ill with kidney failure."

His sister, just 18 at the time, became the youngest kidney donor at the hospital.

This year marks the 20th anniversary of his transplant.

Mr Garnett said: "Everyone has donated so generously to my past charity expeditions across India, Sri Lanka, Svalbard and Greenland, helping me raise over £4,000 for the NHS and other charities, so I’m heading to Antarctica and aiming to raise at least £3,000 this time for the hospital and NHS which have done so much for me, my family and countless others."

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21687425.jpg?type=mds-article-620)James Garnett is taking on a expedition to ski 566 miles to the South Pole *(Image: UGC)*

The money raised will support St George’s renal department.

The hospital is working to build its first dedicated renal unit.

Mr Garnett’s journey to the South Pole will take him from the northern ice shelf of Antarctica to the pole itself.

He said: "Over 40 to 50 days (depending on storm delays), I will be skiing and climbing 2,800 metres from the northern ice shelf of Antarctica to the bottom of our planet, the South Pole.

"Skiing on average 14 miles per day, in -10c to -40c and in 24/7 daylight, I will haul all my food and equipment (weighing around 100kg) in pulks (sledges) in high winds, storms, whiteouts, snow and hopefully some blue skies."

He said he is taking on the expedition to help address organ donation shortages and to show what transplant recipients are capable of achieving.

Mr Garnett’s health problems began suddenly when he was 21.

He was preparing for his first international tour as a drummer when doctors diagnosed him with end-stage kidney failure.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21687417.jpg?type=mds-article-620)James Garnett on a previous expedition in Greenland *(Image: UGC)*

He said: "After a very stressful year for my whole family, dark days on kidney dialysis and seemingly endless tests, the amazing staff at St George’s Hospital successfully performed a kidney transplant, with the kidney being donated by my unbelievably brave sister who at the age of 18 became the youngest ever donor at St George’s."

The experience changed his outlook on life.

He is now determined to live in a way that honours those who helped him.

Since then, Mr Garnett has rebuilt his music career and travelled the world.

He has performed alongside artists such as David A. Stewart, Paloma Faith, and Leona Lewis.

His expeditions are his way of giving back to the NHS.

He also hopes to inspire other transplant patients to live life fully.

He said: "Years of talking with fellow patients about their worries and fears after receiving an organ transplant or the endless unknowns while waiting for a life-changing transplant have led me to try and inspire transplant patients all around the world to stay positive, have hope, push their boundaries, dream bigger and live their lives to the max."
