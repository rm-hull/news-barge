The cockpit voice recorder from a business jet that went down in Florida last week, with the loss of all nine people on board, has been recovered by investigators.

The National Transportation Safety Board said in a statement posted to social media that the device was found at the site where the Bahamas-bound Learjet hit the ground and burst into flames on Nov. 1.

The jet had plummeted from the sky amid severe weather. If data can be extracted from the recorder, it could offer crucial details about what the pilots said to each other as they encountered turbulence, ice formed on the wings and control of the aircraft was lost.

![A drone image shows aftermath of deadly Florida plane crash that killed nine in Greenacres, Florida](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/10/00/Florida_Small_Plane_Crash__9999.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

It was not immediately known whether the device had been damaged or working in the first place. The NTSB said the recorder was being evaluated at a lab in Washington. Photographs posted online by the agency showed the recorder's orange-painted metal shell, and it appeared to be in decent shape, aviation safety expert Jeff Guzzetti told The Associated Press.

“It's very plausible that any info on the recorder survived,” Guzzetti said. “If they can recover it, and it's from the accident flight, that will be a huge help to investigators.”

He noted that when the NTSB recovered the cockpit voice recorder from a medical transport plane that came down in Philadelphia in January 2025, with the loss of seven lives, the device turned out likely not to have been functioning for several years.

Witnesses captured dramatic video as the business jet plunged nose-first into the ground. Air traffic control transmissions recorded the disturbing moments as the pilots realized their fate. But the cockpit voice recorder could shed light on other aspects of the crash, including why the Learjet appeared to steer around a large storm cell only to head directly into a rapidly developing smaller one.

![In this photo released by the National Transportation Safety Board, an investigator holds the cockpit voice recorder recovered after the crash of a Learjet 60 in Greenacres](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/10/08/download.-11..jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Investigators said pieces of a key tail part called the elevator, which controls the plane's pitch, were found more than a mile from the main crater. That confirmed the plane started to break apart before impact. The co-pilot told air traffic controllers the plane was being thrown up and down more than 700 feet (213 meters), and she asked them for a better route through the area.

Generally, the NTSB takes 12 to 18 months to complete a crash investigation, though complicated cases can take longer. The NTSB's roughly 400 workers are pursuing more than 1,200 active investigations.

Investigators will review maintenance records and any history of problems with the plane.

They will also interview officials at Upper Jets, the company that operated the flight, as well as air traffic controllers and airport staff in Austin, Texas, where the plane departed.
