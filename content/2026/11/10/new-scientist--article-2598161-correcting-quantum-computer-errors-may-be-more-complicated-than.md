Quantum computers will not become useful until researchers perfect methods for correcting errors that these devices naturally make. Quantum error-correction (QEC) techniques have advanced rapidly in recent years, but several studies now suggest that the landscape of errors they must tackle is unexpectedly complex, which poses new challenges for making quantum computers practical.

The idea of QEC dates back to the very inception of quantum computing and the first investigations of how quantum computers could be put to work. At that time, no one had built a quantum computer, so researchers relied on mathematical theory to work out how well errors could be corrected, and which computations this error correction would allow the computers to reliably complete. Now, quantum computers do exist and data about their errors is fast accumulating. Increasingly, researchers are finding there are some types of errors that the earlier investigations had overlooked, and they could threaten quantum computers’ progress.
