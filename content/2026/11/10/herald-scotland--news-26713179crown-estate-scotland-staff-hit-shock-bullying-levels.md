Almost 30 per cent of staff at a Scottish Government public body have experienced "bullying or harassment", according to the results of internal research leaked to The Herald.

Results of the latest Crown Estate Scotland staff survey, carried out in 2025, found that 29% of employees reported such behaviour to researchers.

The figure was 20 percentage points higher than the benchmark set for levels of bullying and harassment in the public sector and was revealed in an analysis of the findings under a section headed "Inclusion and Respect".

The organisation, which is led by chief executive Ronan O'Hara, was told that "regular check-ins" with staff should be carried out to encourage trust, while "clear expectations of behaviour" and "accessible reporting routes" should be provided to strengthen "psychological safety".

Staff were able to give feedback on the survey at an event held following the survey's results and it is understood they were able to contribute to the discussion anonymously via a notice board.

One public servant commented: "29% is a shocking figure for bullying and harassment reported."

Another said: "Elephant in the room: Bullying and harassment is 29% is a huge figure that must be faced into and fixed. What will be done in this area?"

It is not known if any complaints of bullying or harassment were reported officially by staff to the organisation in 2025, with the survey reporting anonymous experiences to assess the organisation's workplace culture.

The survey also found 74% feel respected by colleagues (12 points under the benchmark) and that 52% feel safe to raise concerns.

The developments come after The Herald revealed last month that the board of CES was hit by a series of staff grievances over the last three years.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21692935.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Ronan O'Hara, chief executive of Crown Estate Scotland, pictured giving evidence on Scotwind to MSPs last Thursday. *(Image: Newsquest)*

A freedom of information (FOI) request by The Herald showed that nine grievances against the Crown Estate Scotland's governing body were made over the last three years.

The organisation declined to go into detail about the nature of the grievances from staff about the board.

The board of Crown Estate Scotland has experienced a number of changes to its membership in recent years including the departure of its former chair Euan McVicar, who was appointed chair in June 2023.

Cabinet secretary for climate action and rural affairs Gillian Martin announced the appointment of board member Michael Stewart as the new chair of the board in July this year for a three year period. The part-time appointment attracts a remuneration of £400 per day for a time commitment of 104 days per year.

Mr Stewart became a board member in November 2025 and had been an interim co-chair since April 2026 along with Ailsa Raeburn.

Established in 2017, CES, which has around 100 employees, manages land and property owned by the Crown and is a self-financing Statutory Public Corporation accountable to the Scottish Ministers and to the Scottish Parliament.

It is responsible for managing a diverse portfolio of land and property - called the Scottish Crown Estate - including seabed, coastline, commercial property and rural estates.

Income generated from leases, such as aquaculture, farming, and offshore wind, from the Scottish Crown Estate are used to run the organisation with profits transferred to the Scottish Consolidated Fund for public spending and capital profits are reinvested in the Estate.

It's portfolio includes Scotland's seabed out to 12 nautical miles, about half of the foreshore, around 37,000 hectares of rural estates, salmon fishing rights, and rights to naturally-occurring gold and silver.

The organisation, which is separate from Crown Estate in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, and is a public corporation, plays a central role in managing offshore renewable energy leases, including major programmes like ScotWind, which leased portions of the seabed to energy companies for the installation of offshore wind farms.

In September the public spending watchdog Audit Scotland said the Scottish Government must be more "transparent" over how it will use £755 million generated by the ScotWind project.

The Auditor General Stephen Boyle said: “Crown Estate Scotland and the Scottish Government took a high risk and reward approach to the ScotWind leasing round.

“Whether that decision represents value for money will be determined in the coming years. If developers sign long-term leases before their option periods end, then annual payments will continue to benefit Scotland for the next 60 years.

“However, if they give up their options, the capped option pricing model will result in limited wider economic benefits and substantially reduced public revenues."

Mr O'Hara along with Alistair Milloy, director of finance and business services at CES and Gillian Morrison, head of offshore wind development energy and infrastructure, gave evidence last Thursday to Holyrood's public audit committee on the Auditor General's Scotwind report.

Mr O'Hara told MSPs ScotWind had been designed to "deliver long term value".

Responding to the 2025 staff survey results a spokesperson for Crown Estate Scotland told The Herald: “Crown Estate Scotland takes any allegations of this type extremely seriously. This is why we have responded to concerns raised in the staff survey 2025 by improving processes and procedures to strengthen our working environment.

"These actions are designed to ensure that all members of staff feel supported to come forward and report any behaviour that makes them feel uncomfortable.

"The aim of the changes is to continue to encourage every member of staff to work proactively to ensure as safe, respectful and supportive a working environment as possible for everyone at the organisation.”
