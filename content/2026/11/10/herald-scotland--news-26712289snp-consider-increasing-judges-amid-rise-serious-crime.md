Ministers are considering increasing the number of judges amid warnings of a rise in "serious criminal business" and the prospect that victims of serious crime could face a four-year wait for justice unless action is taken.

Justice Secretary Neil Gray is assessing the situation amid warnings that Scotland does not have a large enough judiciary to deal with a rise in the number of High Court sittings which are expected to more than double.

Scotland currently has 36 judges who are known as senators of the College of Justice. The Lord President is the most senior judge in Scotland and the Lord Justice Clerk is the second most senior judge. The role of the Lord President includes regulatory responsibilities.

"Conversations are ongoing with the Lord President and the court service on what staffing will be required for the increased number of High Court sittings that are coming in January. Should those resources be required, that will be part of the budget consideration," Mr Gray told MSPs on Holyrood's criminal justice committee last week.

John Logue, Crown Agent and chief executive of the Crown Office and Procurator Fiscal Service (COPFS), stated that "the scale of the challenge" facing his service "is significant".

He told MSPs in a letter published last Friday that from October 2026 to March 2027, COPFS "expects to indict more than twice the usual number of potential High Court cases. For context, that means an increase from approximately 110 cases to around 240 each month."

Malcolm Graham, chief executive of the Scottish Courts and Tribunal Service, revealed to the criminal justice committee earlier this year that according to modelling "victims might be waiting up for to four years in High Court cases if we do not intervene and do something differently from the time that they report their crime to the time that the case is resolved" as he called for increased capacity and wider changes to the service.

![Victims of crime face long waits for justice](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21690708.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Victims of crime face long waits for justice *(Image: Dominic Lipinski)*

Ahead of appearing at the committee in early September Mr Graham told MSPs that while a criminal trial backlog of cases from the Covid pandemic had cleared new pressures were being put on the system including an "underlying growth in serious criminal business".

He told MSPs that High Court and sheriff solemn scheduled trials remained at more than twice their 2019-20 levels.

"The present challenge is therefore not a residual pandemic backlog - it is a new normal of substantially higher serious criminal demand entering the system.

"High Court business has increased significantly in both volume and complexity. Current forecasts provided by the Crown Office and Procurator Fiscal Service (COPFS) indicate that High Court indictments could exceed 2,700 in 2026-27, compared with 935 in 2023-24.

"No single factor explains this increase. It reflects underlying growth in serious criminal business, legislative and legal developments, significant growth in serious and historical offences reaching court, and an increasingly demanding case mix, including complex, lengthy and multi-accused trials.

"It also reflects sustained and welcome efforts to improve the investigation and prosecution of violence against women and girls and historical sexual offending."

He went on: "SCTS is responding to that increase in demand by expanding High Court trial provision to 26 courts per day from January 2027, ten more than the pre-pandemic position.

"That additional capacity is being managed by flexing capacity in the current system, realising the benefits of recent reforms we have implemented in relation to summary criminal business.

"The increased capacity will be essential to manage the substantially higher volume of serious criminal business.

"However, the scale of the forecast increase means that, even with this provision in place, SCTS criminal court modelling estimates that the average wait between pleading diet and an evidence-led trial could rise from around 50 weeks currently to 135 weeks by 2028-29, compared with 22 weeks before the pandemic.

"The modelling indicates that scheduled trial numbers and waiting periods would then stabilise from around March 2028 but would remain substantially above their pre-pandemic levels.

"People involved in the most serious criminal cases could therefore be waiting around two and a half years for trial from the pleading diet, even after this substantial increase in capacity.

"The resulting uncertainty and demands on victims, witnesses, accused people and their families would be fundamentally at odds with the collective commitment to a trauma-informed and victim-centred justice system."

Former Lord Advocate Dorothy Bain KC will take up a role as a judge in January replacing Lord Matthews who is retiring.
