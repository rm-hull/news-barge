Businesses across York and North Yorkshire are being encouraged to work together to tackle climate risks, improve resilience and reduce costs. But there is another side to the debate: how much are businesses already paying to meet the growing demands of climate and environmental policies?

An event taking place in York next month will bring together business leaders, policymakers and sustainability specialists to explore how organisations can prepare for climate-related disruption, make better use of resources and learn from businesses in Japan.

Organised by the Meaningful Business Network, the event will focus on collaboration, energy efficiency, resource sharing and the circular economy.

However, for many smaller businesses, the transition towards lower-carbon operations can bring significant upfront costs. Investment in equipment, vehicles, buildings, energy systems and changes to supply chains can all place additional demands on already stretched budgets.

## Who pays for the climate transition?

There is an important distinction between helping businesses prepare for climate-related disruption and requiring them to make changes to meet environmental policy objectives.

Preparing for flooding, drought, extreme heat and supply-chain disruption can be a matter of protecting a business against identifiable risks. However, environmental requirements and the transition towards lower-carbon operations can involve additional expenditure.

For smaller companies, the cost of replacing equipment, improving buildings, changing transport arrangements or meeting new supply-chain expectations can be difficult to absorb.

While some investments may deliver savings over time, the initial financial burden can fall disproportionately on smaller businesses that have less access to capital and fewer resources to manage regulatory changes.

This raises a wider question about whether the costs of achieving environmental objectives are being properly recognised when policies are developed.

## Learning from Japan

The York event will explore how businesses in Japan prepare for climate-related challenges and other disruptions.

Research by Dr Marco Sakai, an ecological economist at the University of York, compares how businesses in the UK and Japan approach climate resilience.

Japanese organisations have extensive experience of preparing for natural disasters, including earthquakes and tsunamis. The research will examine what UK businesses can learn from their approach to cooperation, preparedness and sharing costs.

> **Dr Marco Sakai, ecological economist at the University of York:**
>
> There are practical lessons that businesses in the UK can learn from Japan, particularly about working together to prepare for challenges and share costs. But it’s not a one-way exchange. Japanese businesses can also learn from what UK companies are doing around climate mitigation and energy efficiency. There is a real opportunity here to share ideas and learn from each other.

## Can collaboration really reduce costs?

The event will also hear from Reusefully, the lead partner behind ReYorkshire, a project funded through the York and North Yorkshire Combined Authority’s Carbon Negative Challenge Fund.

ReYorkshire is examining how materials from industrial, commercial, construction and agricultural activities can be reused rather than discarded, potentially turning waste from one organisation into a resource for another.

The approach could offer practical financial benefits, particularly where businesses can reduce disposal costs or obtain materials more economically through reuse.

But collaboration alone cannot remove the costs associated with environmental requirements or the investment needed to change business operations.

For smaller companies, the question is whether support and practical opportunities will be sufficient to make the transition financially sustainable.

## Where is the balance?

There is considerable disagreement about the extent to which climate change should drive government policy, how quickly businesses should be expected to adapt, and who should bear the financial costs.

While supporters argue that environmental measures are necessary to reduce emissions and prepare for future risks, critics question their economic impact. They point to higher operating costs, additional regulatory requirements and the expense of replacing equipment, vehicles and infrastructure.

For smaller businesses in particular, the concern is that policies intended to deliver long-term environmental benefits can impose immediate and substantial costs, potentially affecting competitiveness, investment and jobs.

That debate raises an important question for the organisers of the York event: when discussing how businesses can cut costs through climate action, will they also address the costs that climate-related policies themselves can impose?

Businesses are being encouraged to invest, adapt and change. The question is whether policymakers are giving sufficient consideration to the financial consequences, and whether the promised savings and benefits will outweigh the costs.
