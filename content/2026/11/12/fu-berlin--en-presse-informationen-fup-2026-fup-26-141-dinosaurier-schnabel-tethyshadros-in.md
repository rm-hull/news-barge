# Duck-Billed Dinosaur Had a Bird-Like Beak Hidden Among Its “Spikes”

## Researchers discover unusual mouth structure of the Italian dinosaur Tethyshadros insularis thanks to state-of-the-art technology

№ 141/2026 from Nov 12, 2026

Paleontologists have made a breakthrough that challenges conventional wisdom concerning one of the most unusual spiked mouth structures found among hadrosauroids, revealing that the Italian dinosaur *Tethyshadros insularis*had a significantly larger, more complex beak than previously thought, with an internal structure that remained concealed until now. A new study published in* Royal Society Open Science* has led to a reevaluation of previously held assumptions about the well-known fossil. Scientists from Elettra Sincrotrone Trieste (Italy), University College London (United Kingdom), and Freie Universität Berlin (Germany) participated in the project, which was led by researchers from the University of Bologna (Italy).

The international research team utilized high-resolution X-ray micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) at the TomoLab facility of Elettra Sincrotrone Trieste to image the internal bone architecture of three *Tethyshadros* specimens from the famous Villaggio del Pescatore fossil site near Trieste, including the holotype “Antonio.” This technology allowed them to explore the exceptionally delicate fossils without damaging them, including canals only fractions of a millimeter across that are completely invisible from the outside of the bone.

The scans revealed that the apparent “spikes” were not visible externally in life, but formed the internal bony scaffold supporting a much larger, deeper keratinous beak richly supplied by blood vessels and sensory tissues, structurally reminiscent of modern bird bills. Digital three-dimensional reconstructions uncovered a complex neurovascular system, which nourished a rapidly growing keratinous sheath and supported tactile organs.

## A Multipurpose Tool

The discovery offers new insights into what the dinosaur’s life may have looked like. The beak of *Tethyshadros* potentially carried out several jobs at once, acting as a feeding structure, a sensitive tactile organ, and through its rich blood supply, a heat exchanger. “What looked like a bizarre toothed or spiked beak may actually be the bony footprint of a much more complex living organ,” notes lead author Marco Muscioni from the University of Bologna. Filippo Maria Rotatori from the Paleontology Division at Freie Universität Berlin adds, “At first, I didn’t know what to expect when we put the study together, yet as I worked with Marco and the others, it became clear that this neurovascular system had an exceptionally important physiological function.”

The complex beak fulfilled multiple physiological roles: it compensated for heavy mechanical wear from processing abrasive, grit-laden vegetation, acted as a tactile sensory organ during foraging, and functioned as a thermal radiator to dissipate body heat before returning cooled blood to the neurocranium. This makes it key to understanding the evolutionary success of these dinosaurs.

Once the scientists were able to look inside the bone, the projections began to make sense as part of a network carrying blood vessels and possibly sensory tissues. The study also reevaluated specimen SC 57026 (“Secundus”), establishing that fully grown *Tethyshadros* * insularis* individuals reached 5.2 to 5.9 meters in length.

## Gaining New Scientific Findings from Well-Known Fossils

The study clearly illustrates how modern imaging techniques can be harnessed in paleontology to uncover anatomical information that would otherwise remain concealed if only conventional methods were used, even in fossils that are known to scientists.

“*Tethyshadros* continues to surprise us,” says senior author Federico Fanti from the University of Bologna. “The Villaggio del Pescatore fossils are among the most complete dinosaurs ever found in Europe, but new technologies are showing that even specimens we have studied for years can still preserve completely unexpected information.”

The study thus opens up new perspectives in the anatomy and evolution of herbivorous dinosaurs and shows how modern research methods can transform our understanding of even long-extinct creatures.
