Police forces and the National Crime Agency need to improve their oversight of retrospective facial recognition, according to a new report from His Majesty’s Inspectorate of Constabulary and Fire & Rescue Services (HMICFRS).

The inspection warns that safeguards and oversight are inconsistent, with concerns about data retention, potential algorithmic bias and whether investigators fully understand the limitations of the technology.

The findings have prompted questions about its use in North Yorkshire. The Harrogate Informer contacted North Yorkshire Police to ask whether the force uses retrospective facial recognition, what safeguards it has in place and whether it intends to review its procedures following the report.

The force did not respond to the questions by the time of publication.

## What is retrospective facial recognition?

Retrospective facial recognition involves comparing an image obtained after an incident with images already held by police, such as custody photographs.

Images can come from a range of sources, including CCTV, dashcam footage and social media. The technology searches existing images to identify potential matches that may help an investigation.

It is different from live facial recognition, which scans people in real time and compares their faces against a watchlist.

HMICFRS examined retrospective facial recognition in its inspection. The report did not examine live facial recognition or operator-initiated facial recognition.

## Concerns over safeguards and accountability

The inspection found that police forces have taken steps to address potential algorithmic bias, but more needs to be done to reduce the risk of wrongful arrests.

It also found that legislation has not kept pace with the development of the technology. There is currently no comprehensive statutory framework governing its use, making it harder for forces to understand the lawful basis for using it and the safeguards they must apply.

The report raised concerns that some custody images which should have been deleted under existing retention rules remain available for facial recognition searches.

Investigators do not always receive sufficient training or support to understand the technology’s capabilities and limitations. Some less experienced investigators did not understand that a potential match must be corroborated through further enquiries.

The inspection also found differences in the maturity of governance arrangements between forces. Some have established policies and safeguards, while others rely on limited national guidance.

HMICFRS made 16 recommendations, including calls for improved national and local governance, clearer national standards, consistent procedures, stronger oversight and supervision, and better guidance and training for investigators.

## Facial recognition already being used in North Yorkshire

North Yorkshire Police has introduced live facial recognition in York, with its first planned deployment in the city centre on 23 September 2026.

That technology is separate from the retrospective facial recognition examined in the HMICFRS report. Live facial recognition scans people in real time, whereas retrospective facial recognition searches images after an incident against existing police-held images.

Before the York deployment, Deputy Mayor for Policing, Fire and Crime Jo Coles said she had sought assurances from North Yorkshire Police about the use of live facial recognition, including data protection, oversight, deployment criteria and the risk of the technology being used beyond its intended purpose.

## Deputy Mayor says inspection findings will be considered

Jo Coles said the findings of HMICFRS reports are taken seriously and that the force’s delivery against inspection recommendations is scrutinised through regular public meetings.

> **Jo Coles, Deputy Mayor for Policing, Fire and Crime:**
>
> Keeping people safe and maintaining public trust in policing must always be the priority.
>
> Policing powers and new technology has an important role to play, but must always be lawful, proportionate and accountable.
>
> I have sought significant assurances about the use of Live Facial Recognition technology in North Yorkshire; those assurances have been published and processes put in place to ensure new technology is used appropriately and in the interests of public safety.
>
> We take the findings of all HMICFRS inspection reports seriously and will consider any findings carefully. Delivery by North Yorkshire Police on all inspection reports is also scrutinised in regular public meetings.

Her response refers specifically to live facial recognition. The HMICFRS inspection, however, examined the separate use of retrospective facial recognition.

## North Yorkshire Police did not respond to questions

The Harrogate Informer contacted North Yorkshire Police ahead of publication, asking whether the force uses retrospective facial recognition and, if so, how the technology is used.

The force was also asked what safeguards are in place to prevent inaccurate identification or misuse, and whether it would review its procedures in light of the HMICFRS findings.

North Yorkshire Police did not provide a response to any of the questions by the time of publication.

The absence of a response means it has not been possible to establish from the force whether retrospective facial recognition is currently used by North Yorkshire Police or what specific safeguards it may have in place.

The HMICFRS report identifies concerns about the consistency of oversight and safeguards across policing nationally. It does not identify North Yorkshire Police as a force where inspectors found specific failings in the use of retrospective facial recognition.

## What is retrospective facial recognition?

Retrospective facial recognition is a technology used by police to help identify people from images captured after an incident has taken place.

For example, if CCTV captures an image of someone suspected of committing a crime, police can submit that image to facial recognition software. The system compares the face against photographs already held in police databases, such as custody images, and identifies potential matches.

The technology can help investigators establish the identity of an unknown suspect, but a computer-generated match is not proof that someone has committed an offence. Potential matches must be checked and supported by further enquiries before police make decisions about an individual.

This is different from live facial recognition, which scans faces in real time as people pass cameras and compares them against a watchlist. The HMICFRS inspection examined retrospective facial recognition, not live deployments.
