A therapeutic vibrating bed could help protect against Alzheimer’s disease by triggering a remarkable process in the brain, according to a new study.

Scientists at the University of Oulu and Oulu University Hospital in Finland found that gentle, rhythmic full-body vibrations – paired with a temporary drop in blood pressure – can activate the brain's internal self-cleaning system while a person is awake.

The peer-reviewed findings, published in the journal *Nature Communications*, point to a new route in tackling neurodegenerative disorders before irreversible damage sets in.

Normally, the brain flushes out harmful proteins through its glymphatic network while we sleep. As we nod off, blood pressure drops and blood vessels gently pulse, pumping cerebrospinal fluid through neural tissue like a rinse cycle.

Professor Vesa Kiviniemi, head of the Functional Neuroimaging Research Group at the University of Oulu, said the research shows this vital process can now be artificially stimulated during the day.

![In a breakthrough study, scientists have found a new way to potentially fend off Alzheimer’s disease](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/20/13/42/iStock-1194461946.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“Alzheimer's disease and many other diseases that cause dementia are characterised by the gradual accumulation of harmful proteins in the brain over decades,” Prof Kiviniemi said.

“Now that we can safely enhance the brain's own clearance system, which gradually becomes less effective with age, while people are still healthy, it may be possible in future to prevent these diseases, or at least slow their progression.”

Dementia affects nearly one million people across the UK, with Alzheimer’s accounting for the vast majority of diagnoses, according to the NHS.

The disease develops silently over decades as misfolded proteins clump into stubborn plaques that strangle healthy brain cells.

![Just under a million people in the UK have dementia](https://static.independent.co.uk/2025/06/03/14/37/iStock-187272969.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

To see whether this build-up could be prevented, the Finnish team tested a two-pronged approach on 29 healthy adult volunteers resting on a specialised vibration platform.

Participants received a mild, short-acting medication that temporarily lowered blood pressure by dilating blood vessels, mimicking the natural vascular state of falling asleep. At the same time, the bed delivered rhythmic, whole-body vibrations.

Using advanced MRI scanners alongside wearable brain monitors, researchers tracked immediate shifts in fluid circulation.

Brain imaging confirmed that the proteins had been dislodged from neural tissue and carried directly into the large veins at the back of the head.

Johanna Tuunanen, the study's lead researcher and a doctoral candidate at the university, said the intervention produced results that “largely resembled the physiological changes we have previously observed during sleep”.

“The results suggest that harmful proteins were cleared from the brain more efficiently than usual,” Ms Tuunanen said.

![The vibrations trigger the brain's self-cleaning system, preventing the buildup of harmful proteins linked to Alzheimer’s](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/01/14/13/15/iStock-2198730527.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Targeting these proteins while they are still floating as soluble particles could prove decisive for preventive medicine.

“For the first time, we obtained evidence that brain-derived proteins passed directly into the large veins at the back of the brain, something that has not previously been observed even in animal models,” Prof Kiviniemi said.

Because the trial involved a modest cohort of 29 healthy adults, the research team cautioned that more clinical work is required to establish how long the cleansing effect persists and determine optimal treatment schedules.

Follow-up trials are expected to investigate whether regular vibration therapy can delay or halt memory decline in patients showing early markers of cognitive impairment.
