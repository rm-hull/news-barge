The TV landscape has changed dramatically this past year. New art televisions now look like matte-finish paintings on the wall. RGB televisions use red, green, and blue lights for accurate color. But OLED televisions still reign supreme for deep and rich blacks. They can control individual pixels or turn them off completely for an inky, cinematic look.

Fortunately, probably because of this intense competition, many OLED televisions are more affordable than ever. After testing many of the best models this year, I narrowed down my favorites to the ones that truly deserve a place on your TV stand.

Why Choose an OLED in the First Place?

An OLED television is different from LCD displays like LED and micro-RGB TVs. OLED (organic light-emitting diode) uses special organic material that lights up when fed electricity. That means each individual pixel can be turned completely on and off independently.

For movies and shows, that means dark scenes look more realistic and show extra shadow detail. In practice, when Star-Lord flops onto the screen in the intro of *Guardians of the Galaxy Vol. 2*, you can see more details in his dark gray suit—folds, ripples, and belts.

What Size TV Should I Get?

A good rule of thumb? Choose your OLED size based on the size of the room and how far away you will sit from the screen.

Larger sizes are more expensive—doubly so for OLED models. While an 85-inch OLED screen will look spectacular, it will cost you, and will be really hard to move around and install.

For most larger living rooms or group viewing areas, 65 inches is the standard. Anytime we quote a price or link to a retailer below, assume we are linking to the 65-inch version of the TV (unless we specifically call out another size).

Are RGB TVs Better Than OLED?

I've tested a lot of RGB TVs in 2026, and put them through the wringer against OLED models, often in the same room. In my results, OLED TVs still generally have the upper hand because individual pixels can be dimmed dark gray or turned off completely. Dark scenes in movies like *The Batman* look more realistic on an OLED than an RGB TV. However, I expect RGB TV tech to improve quickly. By next year, it might finally trump OLED.

A Sony OLED only makes sense for gaming, especially on a Sony Playstation 5. The Sony Bravia 8 II runs at a common 120-Hz refresh rate, but works seamlessly with the PS5 in ways other TVs don't. It can automatically enable features like HDR tone mapping (for a more colorful picture) and low-latency gaming mode (for a slight advantage in quick controller nudges).

Of course, the Bravia 8 II is excellent outside of gaming too. For movie night, we found that 4K and even older 1080p HD movies and shows looked crisp and colorful, with deep blacks. Off-angle viewing is also exceptional, helping those sitting off to the side in your entertainment room to still enjoy the experience without any weird dimming or glare. It also runs Google TV OS, one of the easiest operating systems to use. Google Gemini in particular is great at searching for content—prompts like “Show me the best psychological thrillers from the '90s” actually worked.

One downside is ports—this TV only has two HDMI 2.1 ports. Most premium models these days have four. Viewing in especially bright rooms can be challenging, but that is a common problem with many OLED TVs.

With deep and rich blacks, a speedy operating system, and exceptional gaming features like a 165-Hz refresh rate, it’s one of my favorite premium OLED TVs.

Sure, the Samsung S95H is the better overall pick, but the G5 surprised and delighted me with things like its lightning-fast processor, operating system, and size options (it goes all the way up to a 97-inch model that costs $25,000).

## OLED Alternatives

For picture quality, OLED TVs still reign supreme because they can control individual pixels and turn them off completely. However, over the last year, art TVs and RGB TVs have started to take more and more real estate on store shelves. Some brands, like Hisense and TCL, have a totally OLED-free TV lineup for 2026, switching focus to RGB and mini-LED. If you're curious about the competition, I’ve listed a few other TV types to consider.

**An art TV: Samsung The Frame Pro (2026)** I absolutely loved this premium art television. I mounted it on the wall in my office for a couple of months, showcasing shipwrecks and masterpiece paintings daily. The matte finish and anti-glare tech make this television able to display more like a piece of wall art in a museum than a shiny, reflective black box. The Pro version in particular offers far better picture quality for TV shows and movies than other art TVs on the market.**An RGB TV: LG Micro RGB Evo 75-inch** My top pick for an RGB television, the LG Micro RGB Evo is highly customizable in terms of picture quality. You can tweak just about every color and brightness setting to make sure dark scenes look textured and clear and colors look realistic, not oversaturated. It's unfortunately only available in larger sizes: 75, 86, and 100-inches.**A projector: AWOL Vision Aetherion Max**  
If you want to skip the rectangular box and TV stand altogether, consider a high-end, ultra-short-throw projector like the AWOL Vision Aetherion Max. While I do recommend using a projector screen, I’ve found some high-end projectors like this one can even outclass OLED in terms of color richness and black depth.
