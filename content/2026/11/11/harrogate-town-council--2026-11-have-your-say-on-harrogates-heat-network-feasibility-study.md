The Council has commissioned specialist engineering consultancy Buro Happold to undertake the Harrogate Heat Network Feasibility Study, looking initially at three priority residential areas in Bilton, Fairfax and Jennyfield. We have now narrowed down our area of study to the Fairfax priority area only.

The study is assessing whether heat networks could be technically, financially and environmentally viable and, importantly, whether they could bring benefits to local communities.

Initial technical work has now been completed and the project is moving into an important phase of community engagement. The current focus is on Fairfax, where residents are being invited to find out more, ask questions and tell the project team what they think.

## What is a heat network?

A heat network distributes heat from a shared centralised source, sometimes called an energy centre, to a number of different customers through a network of pipes. These customers could include homes, schools, hospitals, community buildings, shops and offices.

Heat networks vary considerably in size, from systems serving a small group of neighbouring buildings to large networks supplying whole areas. Rather than each connected building having to generate all of its own heat, heat is produced centrally and distributed through the network.

A network can make use of different sources of low-carbon heat. This could include heat produced efficiently using electricity and heat pumps, as well as heat recovered from sources that might otherwise be wasted. This flexibility means that the sources supplying a network can potentially change over time as technology and local opportunities develop.

Heat networks are already well established. There are more than 14,000 heat networks in the UK, serving around 500,000 households and currently providing around 3% of UK heat consumption. The national ambition is for heat networks to provide around 20% of heat consumption by 2050.

## What would a heat network mean for my home?

A home would be connected to the wider network through underground pipes.

Instead of a conventional boiler, a compact Heat Interface Unit (HIU) or heat exchanger transfers heat from the network into the property’s heating and hot water system. Radiators and hot water can therefore continue to operate in much the same way as a conventional central heating system.

Residents would remain in control of the heating within their home through their thermostat and heating controls, and individual properties would have their own heat meter.

Potential benefits being explored include more predictable heating costs, reduced reliance on individual boilers, access to lower-carbon sources of heat and the ability for a network to incorporate new heat sources in future without individual households having to replace their heating equipment.

However, the purpose of the current work is to test the feasibility and potential benefits of a network in Harrogate. **No decision has been made to build a heat network.**

## Why is Harrogate Town Council carrying out the study?

The Council successfully secured £39,943 through the Great British Energy Community Fund, supported by the North East and Yorkshire Net Zero Hub, to undertake this early-stage feasibility work.

The original project proposal set out an aim to examine whether low-carbon district heating networks could support priority residential areas in Harrogate, with a particular focus on creating positive benefits for households experiencing fuel poverty.

Buro Happold was appointed following a public procurement process to undertake the specialist technical study.

The work includes:

* identifying and engaging with stakeholders and communities;
* assessing demand for heat;
* identifying and assessing potential sources of heat;
* considering potential network routes and locations for energy centres;
* developing high-level costs and technical, economic and environmental modelling;
* analysing potential risks and benefits;
* considering possible community ownership models;
* identifying potential sources of funding for the construction and operation of a network; and
* recommending what, if anything, should happen next.

The study is therefore about more than identifying whether a heat network is technically possible. It is also considering whether a network could be financially viable, how it might operate, how it could benefit the communities it serves and whether there could be an appropriate model for community ownership.

## Where is the Council looking?

The feasibility study has focused initially on three priority residential areas in Bilton, Fairfax and Jennyfield.

The original feasibility work considered approximately 2,350 households across the three areas, while examining whether viable networks could potentially extend beyond their initial boundaries and benefit a greater number of households and community buildings.

In Fairfax, the current work is exploring a potential network that could connect Harrogate District Hospital, social housing and around 80% of homes across the study area, extending from the St Andrews estate towards Walworth Avenue in the north-east and Stonefall Drive in the south-east.

## What happens next?

Feedback from residents will form part of the wider feasibility assessment being undertaken by Buro Happold. The completed study will help the Council understand the technical, economic, environmental and community case for heat networks in Harrogate and identify recommended next steps.

Community involvement is an important part of that process. Any future heat network would need to respond to local needs and have the support of the community it is intended to serve.
