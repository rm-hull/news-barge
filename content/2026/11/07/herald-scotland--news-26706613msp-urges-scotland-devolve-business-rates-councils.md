The Scottish Government has been urged to devolve all non-domestic rates to the new regional councils as part of its overhaul of Scotland's local authorities.

The call from SNP MSP Alex Kerr comes as analysis by BiGGAR Economics suggests new onshore wind farms could generate tens of million of pounds for local communities every year.

Mr Kerr, the MSP for Hamilton, Larkhall and Stonehouse and the SNP's national secretary, said handing the cash raised over to local government would "unleash Scotland's energy and support economic growth across the nation".

Currently, non-domestic rates are set nationally by the Scottish Government and billed and collected by councils.

The income is pooled at a Scottish level and then redistributed back to local authorities. The Scottish Government guarantees each council a combined sum of rates and general revenue grant, so a council's funding does not rise or fall with what is collected in its area.

Mr Kerr says that creates a "lack of clear incentive" for local leaders to drive growth, because the benefits do not return to their communities.

![John Swinney apologises for the 'unacceptable' wait the footballer endured.](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/20167004.jpg?type=mds-article-620)John Swinney announced changes to local government in his Programme for Government *(Image: Jeff J Mitchell/Getty Images)*

John Swinney has set out plans to scrap Scotland's existing 32 councils and replace them with six to 10 regional super authorities, alongside 120 to 160 community authorities.

The First Minister has said the reform will see local government gain new powers.

In England, councils keep 50% of business rates growth.

In their briefing, BiGGAR estimates that the 7.8GW of new onshore wind needed to reach the Scottish Government's 20GW target would pay about £78 million in business rates a year, roughly 2% of the £3.2 billion collected in 2024-25.

The figure assumes each new wind farm pays £10,000 per megawatt a year, close to the £10,292 average paid by six recent projects it examined.

Under its proposal, rates from projects not yet operating would be ring-fenced for the host council, existing rates would be untouched, and no council would receive less than it does now.

BiGGAR says that means there would be no cost to the Scottish Government, and that the rates from a single turbine would, effectively, be enough to pay the equivalent of a full-time teacher's salary.

The briefing argues the reasons for pooling rates do not apply to wind.

Redistribution is normally progressive because economic activity clusters in cities, but BiGGAR says each £1 million paid in rates supports 920 operational jobs across the Scottish economy and only 10 in onshore wind.

Because wind farms are built in rural areas, it says the current system does "the exact opposite of what it intended".

It adds that wind farms on Contracts for Difference have a stable, index-linked income for 20 years, which should make their rates more predictable.

Highland Council would gain most. It is home to about six times more of the planned new wind farms than its share of Scotland’s population would suggest.

The Western Isles, Moray, Argyll and Bute and Dumfries and Galloway would also benefit, each with more than five times their population share.

South Lanarkshire, Mr Kerr's home council area, is 28% above. Glasgow, Edinburgh, Dundee and Aberdeen would receive none of the new wind rates.

OnPath Energy, whose Mill Rig wind farm in South Lanarkshire is expected, according to the company, to pay around £18 million in business rates over its lifetime, has thrown its weight behind the call.

![Robin Winstanley of OnPath Energy, which is backing calls for councils to retain the business rates generated by new onshore wind farms](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21592078.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Robin Winstanley of OnPath Energy, which is backing calls for councils to retain the business rates generated by new onshore wind farms *(Image: Supplied)*

Robin Winstanley, sustainability and community director at the renewables firm, said: "As a developer and investor in Scotland we take great care to ensure we deliver projects which build wealth locally through benefit funds and supply chain development, recognising Scotland needs infrastructure which improves the communities which host it.

"This is why we have joined the call for the tax revenue our projects create to be retained locally.

"This will create a much clearer link between hosting energy infrastructure and the long-term benefits it can bring, improving local confidence, community support and the regional growth rural Scotland needs."

Colin Palmer, director of policy for onshore and offshore delivery at Scottish Renewables, said it would allow communities hosting renewable energy projects to "see a direct, lasting share of the economic value they create".

He added: "Allowing councils to retain business rates from new onshore wind projects would strengthen the link between hosting infrastructure and sharing in its economic potential, alongside the significant community benefit funds developers already provide.

"By applying this to new projects above an agreed baseline, the Scottish Government could protect existing council funding without increasing businesses' bills. We urge ministers to work with industry to consider this opportunity through the public service reform programme."

![SNP MSP Alex Kerr](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21682368.jpg?type=mds-article-620)SNP MSP Alex Kerr called for the devolution of NDR *(Image: Supplied)*

Mr Kerr said devolving non-domestic rates could be a "transformative move".

He told The Herald: "The public service reform agenda being pursued by the Scottish Government is radical in its scope but that change cannot simply be about redrawing administrative lines on a map.

"Encouraging economic growth and supporting our businesses has to be at the heart of how we redesign our public services — without that, economic growth we cannot support the public services our country relies on.

"The devolution of non-domestic rates would be a transformative move that would put the incentive for generating economic growth and attracting investment that will directly benefit the people in our local communities."

During the Holyrood debate on the Non-Domestic Rates (Scotland) Bill in 2019, Labour, Conservative and Green MSPs on Holyrood's Local Government and Communities Committee backed an amendment from Green MSP Andy Wightman to hand control of business rates to councils.

However, business groups rallied against it, and MSPs voted to remove it at stage three. The Scottish Government argued that the national system gave certainty to both councils and ratepayers.

When approached for comment, the Scottish Government pointed to a recent answer to a written question by Deputy First Minister Jenny Gilruth.

She said the income was "retained in full by Local Authorities in Scotland" and that distribution was "carried forward under a needs-based formula, including population and deprivation indicators, which is kept under review".

Ms Gilruth added: "There is no current assessment of removing any aspect of NDRI from the distribution arrangements agreed with local government. As engagement continues on local governance reform, one aspect that will be open to consideration is future tax and funding models."

Last week, at a roundtable with council leaders, the Scottish Government and Cosla agreed to "further joint work on which powers should be exercised at a regional level and which best sit at the most local level".

In a statement released after the meeting, Mr Swinney said: "Our commitment to ambitious reform is about making public services work better and empowering local communities. It is crucial to driving economic growth and making sure Scotland reaches its full potential.

"My view is that can only mean more power being given to local government — it cannot and will not mean powers being given to the Scottish Government from local government. This process will strengthen local government and local decision making."

There has been some criticism from councillors over the speed of the reforms, with the Scottish Government looking to secure a "shared direction" with Cosla and local government by December.

Last week, writing in The Herald, Councillor Bill Heddle, Cosla's vice-president, called for fiscal reform, including "the overdue reform" of council tax, funding guarantees and further powers for local authorities. He said these must be "considered from the outset, not after structures and governance have been designed".
