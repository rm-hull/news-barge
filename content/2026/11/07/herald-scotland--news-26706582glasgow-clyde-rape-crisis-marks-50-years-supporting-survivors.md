From three determined women and a telephone in a borrowed room to Scotland’s oldest and largest rape crisis centre, Glasgow & Clyde Rape Crisis has spent five decades supporting survivors of sexual violence. As it marks its 50th anniversary, those who helped shape the organisation reflect on how far it has come, and how much remains unchanged.

Nearly 50 years after helping establish Glasgow & Clyde Rape Crisis, Rosemary Whyte still finds it difficult to accept that some of the attitudes she set out to challenge remain.

She recalls a recent conversation in which someone suggested that girls brought rape upon themselves because of how they dressed.

“I was gobsmacked that even after all this time, after all this progress, those views still exist,” she says.

“When we started the helpline all those years ago, that was one of the things we really wanted to challenge. I thought we had succeeded, but after hearing that, we clearly, sadly, have not.”

It takes her back to Glasgow in the early 1970s, when she was searching for women who shared her determination to tackle sexual violence.

“Glasgow at that time was a desert for feminism,” she recalls. “They called it women’s liberation at that time.”

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21680508.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Glasgow & Clyde Rape Crisis co-founder Rosemary Whyte with her four-month-old baby in 1977, shortly after the centre first opened its doors. *(Image: Supplied)*

After almost a year of searching, an advertisement in feminist magazine Spare Rib led her to a gathering organised by Sandy Ritchie-Urquhart.

Rosemary arrived expecting a room full of women, only to discover that just one other person was coming.

“I thought to myself, one? Just one.”

That third woman was DJ Maggie Cockburn, who died in 1988. Together, they would become the founders of Glasgow & Clyde Rape Crisis, soon joined by Jan Macleod.

“This army of determined women, with no experience but a willingness to try and do our best,” Rosemary recalls.

There was little money, no established service to follow and considerable resistance to their work.

When they tried leaving publicity cards in shops and cafés, many refused to display them because of one word.

“Rape wasn’t talked about. You couldn’t use that word,” Rosemary says.

Even her mother repeatedly asked her to change the organisation’s name. But the women refused to make the subject more palatable.

Their first real breakthrough came when a man in Holland Street offered them a spare room, allowing them to pay rent when they could afford it.

Armed with a notebook, a telephone and an answering machine, they opened their doors.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21680529.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The first telephone and answering machine used by Glasgow & Clyde Rape Crisis in 1977, when a small group of women launched a helpline that would become a lifeline for survivors of sexual violence. *(Image: Supplied)*

For Jan, the scale of what they had taken on soon became apparent.

“I didn’t really have much understanding of what the work would involve, but more so how it would impact me personally, and for such a long time,” she says.

Women would leave their homes to call from telephone boxes, with queues eventually forming as word spread about the helpline.

Some struggled even to recognise what had happened to them.

Jan remembers women initially ticking ‘no’ when asked on forms whether they had been raped, before crossing it out and changing their answer to ‘yes’.

Beside the question asking who had committed the assault, many wrote ‘husband’.

At a time when rape within marriage was rarely acknowledged, the centre was giving women space to understand and speak about their experiences.

“We all had spirit, an attitude that we should just get on with it, and do what was needed – do our best,” Jan says.

That determination continued through generations of volunteers, including Isabelle Kerr, who joined in the 1980s after accompanying a friend interested in the women’s movement.

“I thought I’d be helpful making cups of tea or something similar,” she recalls.

Instead, she became part of a growing service which, at the time, operated three days a week with unpaid volunteers and limited resources.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21680723.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The first Glasgow & Clyde Rape Crisis office in Holland Street, Glasgow, where a small group of determined women began supporting survivors of sexual violence in 1977. *(Image: Supplied)*

Isabelle later returned as Centre Manager, helping oversee its development into a more structured organisation, with a board of directors and services spanning six local authorities.

But half a century after those first calls, the demand for support remains overwhelming.

In February, 182 survivors were waiting for help, with waits reaching 226 days. Some waiting lists have since closed, while the organisation warns further cuts could follow without additional funding.

The wider figures are equally stark. Scotland recorded 16,430 sexual crimes in 2025-26, the highest number since comparable records began in 1971. Recorded rape and attempted rape also reached record levels.

For Claudia Macdonald-Bruce, director of Glasgow & Clyde Rape Crisis, the anniversary is therefore bittersweet.

She views it as an opportunity to celebrate five decades of progress, but also to confront the challenges facing survivors today.

“We are seeing the consequences of a system buckling under pressure. Women are coming to us carrying trauma, fear, shame and uncertainty, and some are then being told that they have to wait months for the specialist support they need,” she says.

Fifty years after a small group of women picked up a telephone and began listening, their work is far from finished.
