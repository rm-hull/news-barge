After a lifetime spent looking down at the Smoke on the Water from the Highway to Hell, I finally got round to visiting a Scottish folk club. And so, I've pitched up at Edinburgh's Royal Terrace, that douce reliquary of Auld Reekie's ramrod rectitude and rustic grandeur, where the first-time visitor can feel underdressed without a top hat and a silver cane.

The Edinburgh Folk Club gathers every fortnight on this ornamental New Town boulevard at number 14, the permanent home of the Ukrainian Community Centre, where it sits amid boutique hotels and candlewick restaurants of the kind that civic panjandrums might favour for discreet trysts conducted in long johns and petticoats.

This folk club has all the staples of how I've fondly imagined such a place to be. There are corduroy trousers and man-buns; Arran sweaters and speckled beards, but it will become an uplifting night in which I've rarely felt more at ease with being Scottish and where the cares of the world evaporate for a while on the love songs and laments of Scotland's lands and seas.

I'm here for a performance by Iona Fyfe, the gifted young Aberdeenshire singer and songwriter who combines a punishing international concert schedule with her role as Aberdeen University's youngest-ever rector. Ms Fyfe is also a stalwart advocate for the Scots language or, as it's described by this evening's chair, 'the Scots leid', or the 'Lallans' as it's probably more commonly known, and a tongue forever honoured by Robert Louis Stevenson: “Dear to my heart as the peat reek, Auld as Tantallon …”

I'd spent some hours with Ms Fyfe a week or so previously, but felt I needed to hear her sing to a live audience. I'm glad that I did, for she's been gifted a beautiful voice which dips and soars with the tragedies and sorrows of the lands that formed her. She's also an accomplished pianist and before we're even halfway through the first song of her 12, I'm actually singing the chorus and tapping my feet along with the rest of them.

In a folk club. In Edinburgh. In that New Town.

I've wanted to interview Ms Fyfe for some time and not simply for her status as one of Scotland's most garlanded musicians with a clutch of industry awards and baubles and television appearances. She's not yet reached her 30th birthday, but seems to have ripped the fun out of three full lifetimes. Her political and cultural views, vividly expressed on social media, often attract the mob's censure to the extent that, only once having previously met her, I've found myself cheering her on for never backing down, never retreating even when she's espousing views with which I fervently disagree.

A few months ago, she'd stoutly defended Nicola Sturgeon and been met with a fusillade of abuse which soon became dark with ugly comments about her appearance and her singing ability. While older and more experienced public figures and media actors loftily flounce off Twitter/X, usually denouncing it as “a hell-scape”, she admits to “loving” it, describing the platform as “a necessary evil at times, but a great leveller and fully representative of a mature, liberal democracy”.

She's able to walk in other people's shoes and to modify some of her previously held positions. Her delivery is peppered with the cadences and sounds of her native north-east: fa' fower, fowk, ain, baith. It's not contrived but something rich and real that I first encountered in Aberdeen taverns when I went north as a teenager for the football.

We both know that this interview will upset some of those in our own tribes whose rules and boundaries extend to governing who you ought to be talking to and who you ought not to be. Hers is the 'get it right round youze' attitude I can get on board with.

Ms Fyfe recounts her recent experience when, in her role as Aberdeen University rector, she'd faced criticism for visiting a stall run by pro-life activists.

“I do think it's really important to be seen to be talking to everyone, and listening to everyone,” she says, “including those with whom you profoundly disagree. The pro-life club, like all other student bodies, have QR codes where you can follow their activity on Instagram or whatever.

“I've got a separate Rector Instagram, where I follow all the societies and networks, all of the organising folks. So I followed them because I didn't want to be accused of excluding them. Yet, within 10 minutes of me following them, I've got an email from some students voicing their disgust at me following them, saying that I was endorsing them.

“I think some people who don't know anything about me, are yet keen to categorise me. Some people think of me as like a little Sturgeonite baby who agrees with everything she says. But actually, there's a long history of me calling out the party. I know what it's like to be betrayed by someone who says that they're doing one thing and then they end up doing another. It wasn't blind loyalty.”

She says too that she'll always remain grateful to Alex Salmond for what he did for the cause of Scottish independence and it's clear that he was a major factor in her political awakening. “Two things can be right at the same time, yeah?” She also expresses a measure of regret for having voted for Humza Yousaf rather than Kate Forbes in the 2023 SNP leadership contest.

“I think one of my regrets, maybe as a young party activist, is being too quick to support Humza because it was perhaps a popular thing within my age group. I think Kate Forbes was so capable and she speaks so eloquently. I think people my age group do have concerns about, you know, abortion rights and all that, but she said unequivocally that she would never roll back on that.

“I really do feel sad about the way she was treated. I wonder what would have happened over the last few years if she had won that leadership race, I think there would have been less tumultuousness. I think that hopefully there would have been that broad church. I think I think she would have been able to reach out to people and also see people who were on swithering about independence, even people who were confirmed unionists would have been inclined to vote yes.”

Her journey into Scottish traditional music wasn't paved with favours and advantages. “I didn't wake up one day and think I'm going to be a professional folk singer and make money, and pay £6000 for a visa every three years to the US. My mum would tell me at school that she didn't know if they'd have enough money to put me through university.

“It was just my mum and me as she and my dad broke up when I was five, and there was a bit of homelessness and staying with my grandmother in an intergenerational household. Lots of people don't know this about me. Some people are like: 'Oh, you went to the Gordon Schools in Huntly' which they've confused with Gordonstoun or Robert Gordon's College.

“They hear words and then build your character from it. They then add that she went to the Royal Conservatoire and had an elitist education from the cradle. But I'm a working-class artist. I got frustrated when Darren McGarvey, this male from the West of Scotland who's cornered the market for being a working-class artist, described me as a middle-class Scots language activist simply because we were getting a lot of traction for the Scots Language Bill. I was like: 'You don't know me'. What made it more disappointing is that I really respect him and his work.”

“I think if people want to debate your actual views and ask for the argument for independence, well, that's great. But when people are just, like, calling you a fat minx. I'm like, 'okay, that's not great' or when they say you can't sing. I know I can sing. I have the degree from the Conservatoire saying I can sing. I know categorically that I've done the training and that that's what I do. It's maybe not to your taste.

“I struggle to understand sometimes why a lot of men in the industry can go unhindered for having the same views as me. But there are also the people who attended the Conservatoire with me who aren't that political. What's the point of signing up for a carefully-curated image of what trad music is now? I'm thinking: 'where's the protest in this? Where's the folk concept in this'.

“I'll go to civic protests where you very rarely see the fellow folk singers that I went to university with. Are they just too afraid? Can't they cope with the backlash?”

She's a fervent Scottish nationalist and political campaigner who's lobbied Holyrood for the Scots Language Act and against sexual harassment in the music industry.

On social media, you'll often see Ms Fyfe posting short clips of her singing her way across the globe. Here she is on a three-month tour of the US, or from a small venue in Western Australia. It's hardly glamorous either. She pays her own way and will occasionally reach out for some funding from those who consider it important to have a talented young Scottish artist at the top of her game, promoting the Scottish language, culture and music.

She recalls a four-week tour of Western Australia in 2023, performing in small halls. “It was a really weird time because they were having this referendum on more representation for indigenous voices in government [which was voted down]. It was eye-opening to see how wild Australian politics was. I didn't enjoy my time there. I found some of it to be very misogynist and very racist. I've been to Eastern Australia, which is much nicer, but I wouldn't go back.

“Yet, all my life, I was told, 'Oh, America. Don't go'. But I love it there. You meet the most insanely nice people from all parts of the spectrum and they're so proud of their different traditions.”

This young singer, who's been performing and winning awards since she was barely into her teens, seems the picture of health and mental wellbeing. Occasionally, though, on social media she's hinted at struggles.

“I think it's important to say that what people see on Instagram about the makeup, the hair, and all the nice things is so not real. Last Sunday and Monday, I was completely done in with a migraine. I have a neurologist and physio issues. I go to the Centre for Integrative care for complex holistic pain management.

“When someone sees a young person who's busy doing their stuff, they'll assume you're too young to be that sick, or you're too pretty to be that sick, or too busy. I was diagnosed with fibromyalgia when I was 21. My knees started dislocating when I was seven. I spent most of my time in secondary school on crutches because they didn't know if I would grow out of the dislocations.

“Replacement is easy. What they did with me was take my knee out and then chiselled away at it. I was on crutches for three months. I actually flew to New Orleans for a showcase event on crutches, but it was amazing because I got so much industry attention at that as everyone was like, 'Oh, there's a Scottish ballad singer here and she's on crutches,' and everyone came to my showcase.

“I've struggled with bad chronic illness for probably my entire adult life. Some days I literally cry because I think that so much opportunity has been lost because I don't have that endless amount of energy that like my peers have. They can be out to two in the morning, drinking, playing tunes, and then they can be up doing their admin the next day. I'm like, whoa, I can't do any of that. But thank God for the NHS. I get like these Botox injections into my neck, and I go to the dental hospital, and they look at my jaw, and I've never ever had any reason to think that this is awful, or that we need to change this. I've always just been so grateful.

“I remember at the time when I had this surgery and I was really unwell. I couldn't walk and didn't know that my knee would get this big. People were posting on Twitter, being like, 'Did Humza Yousaf, the Health Secretary, boost her up the list.'

"And I'm like, 'Are you even for real? That's not how it works. That would be a massive scandal. That's not what happened.”

We discuss the travails of BBC Scotland and how it might develop a new vision following the axing of so much of its Scottish-generated material. Ms Fyfe believes that the Scotland channel had an opportunity to provide sustained support for authentic Scottish culture, language and regional identity which it largely passed up.

“In France, for example, there are so many stipulations around public broadcast that say 50% of it has to be either French-born musicians or somebody singing in Breton or whatever. In Wales, they are committed to supporting home-grown talent. We don't seem to have that here, and whatever we do tend to have in terms of broadcasts is framed around innovation versus tradition.

“We've arrived at a sorry place where there's no room for Ian Anderson's great late-night show. A lot of great late-night music programming that showcased emerging Scottish artists has gone.

“A few years ago, Gary West's Pipeline got axed. Lots of people from all musical traditions listened to that. And my favourite show was, of course, Robbie Shepherd doing Take the Floor. I listened to that all the time. It was the only Doric voice in the whole bloody corporation.

“Robbie wasn't just speaking full-bodied Doric all the time. He was speaking English, but with the vernacular of the north east.

“I'm not sure about the future of the BBC in Scotland. Was the Scotland channel set up to fail? You need to listen to Martin Geissler talking to Stuart Cosgrove about it on Scotcast. Mention of the kenspeckle and ubiquitous Mr Cosgrove brings us to the unlikely saviours of the Scots language: him and his long-standing china, Tam Cowan.

“Off the Ball is one of my all-time favourite shows,” says Ms Fyfe. “You never know what you'll get, but they're so natural. They're speaking in their own accents. That, in my opinion, is the last show on BBC Scotland featuring a Scots language, whether that be Glaswegian or whatever. It's actually the last show on BBC Radio Scotland actually to support both languages … a football show. I would say it's my favourite cultural show on BBC Radio Scotland, slightly ahead of Take the Floor and Travelling Folk.”

That should make Tam and Stuart's week.
