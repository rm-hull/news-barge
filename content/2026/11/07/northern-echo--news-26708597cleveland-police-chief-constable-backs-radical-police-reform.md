Cleveland Police chief Victoria Fuller has backed calls for radical reform to tackle increasingly complex crime.

Victoria Fuller joined Metropolitan Police Commissioner Sir Mark Rowley, Greater Manchester chief constable Sir Stephen Watson, Gloucestershire temporary chief constable Maggie Blyth and National Police Chiefs’ Council chair Gavin Stephens at Friday’s (November 6) reform event in Manchester.

The leaders debated whether England and Wales’s 43-force system is still fit for purpose.

Fuller said reform should make policing more effective, not simply redraw organisational boundaries.

Chief Constables Fuller and Blyth wrote in The Telegraph earlier this week that modern crime has outgrown the structures used to fight it.

They said organised crime groups, fraudsters and exploiters operate across force borders, while police funding and structures remain rooted in a time when crime was largely local.

They said their respective forces face similar pressures despite their different sizes and locations.

The article pointed to the response to the fatal A66 crash in August, in which seven people died, including two Cleveland Police officers.

The chiefs said the crash demanded a major deployment of resources, putting extra pressure on the force as everyday policing continued.

Cleveland Police arrested more than 50 people linked to serious and organised crime in the following weeks, with help from other agencies.

The chiefs said smaller forces struggle to fund digital forensics, cybercrime, covert surveillance and organised crime teams, while larger forces can spread the cost.

They said sharing specialist services, technology and buying could cut costs without weakening neighbourhood policing.

Fuller said Cleveland’s experience showed what extra specialist support and resources could achieve.

She said a single North East police force “may make sense”, arguing that Cleveland had stepped up its fight against organised crime with help from neighbouring forces and the National Crime Agency.

Met chief Mark Rowley said the proposals would strengthen local policing, not weaken it.

“The building block for the work we want to do is bottom up. It’s about strong local policing,” he said.

“The reason is, crime gets more complicated, so you need more specialist skills to deal with it.”

But local leaders and police and crime commissioners have criticised the proposals.

North East Mayor Kim McGuinness issued a warning over larger police forces, saying that creating a small number of very large forces could reduce local accountability.

She said: “If you move to a handful of very big forces you will risk losing the ability for local people to have their say

“Yes, some issues rightly need a national approach, but we have to protect the principle of local accountability – that means local targets, local control of budgets and a chief constable held to account by their residents.
