The First Minister is facing mounting pressure to overhaul Scotland’s ethics watchdogs after North Ayrshire Council became the fourth authority to demand action over the system which critics say is impeding the human right to freedom of speech.

The council has instructed its chief executive to write to John Swinney calling for an independent, comprehensive review of the functions, operations and statutory powers of the Ethical Standards Commissioner and the Standards Commission for Scotland.

The move follows growing concern among councillors about the way the standards regime operates and its potential impact on their ability to scrutinise decisions made by local authorities.

Councillor Matthew McLean, who brought forward the motion, said it was intended to ensure councils had the right arrangements in place to carry out their responsibilities.

He said: “It's to ensure that councils have got the best sort of setup so that they can properly scrutinise and undertake their role.”

He added: “It highlights recent cases where there's been disputes and arguments back and forward that are still ongoing.”

The motion originally proposed by Mr McLean was amended before being passed by the council.

The final version agreed by councillors expressed serious concerns that the application of the current code of conduct for councillors raised wider questions about procedural fairness, democratic process and accountability, as well as the ability of elected representatives to exercise legitimate scrutiny of local government.

It instructed the chief executive to write to the First Minister calling for a comprehensive review, including consideration of structural reform or replacement of the existing arrangements where justified by evidence and in the spirit of public service reform.

The council also agreed to ask its leader to raise the concerns at a future meeting of Convention of Scottish Local Authorities leaders and, if appropriate, support calls from other local authorities for a collective review and structural reform.

The latest intervention follows controversy surrounding Glasgow Labour councillor Fiona Higgins, who successfully challenged the Standards Commission in the Court of Session over its refusal to accept seven documents she wanted to use in a misconduct hearing.

Ms Higgins had faced scrutiny over social media comments about proposed cuts to teaching posts at Glasgow City Council. She had obtained internal council emails through freedom of information requests and used them to challenge the handling of the proposals.

The commission had refused to allow seven documents she submitted to be considered at the hearing.

In August, Lord Sandison ruled that the watchdog had acted unlawfully by preventing the material from being used.

In his judgment, he said: “By refusing to allow the productions in issue to be used at the substantive hearing, the commission was not allowing the petitioner to be properly heard. It had shut its ears to her proposed submissions and in so doing acted unlawfully.”

The Standards Commission has said it intends to appeal the decision, arguing that it could have wider implications for the way it conducts hearings.

The case has become a focal point for wider concerns about the balance between protecting council employees from unacceptable conduct and allowing elected representatives to challenge decisions and question public officials.

Other local authorities have also raised concerns about the framework. Dumfries and Galloway Council and East Renfrewshire Council have called for an independent review, while councillors in Edinburgh voted to express no confidence in the Standards Commission.

North Ayrshire's decision adds to the pressure for the Scottish Government to examine whether the current arrangements are fit for purpose.

Ms Higgins said of the development: "A fourth unanimous, cross-party council motion should be a wake-up call for the Scottish Government. Concern about Scotland’s standards system now reaches well beyond any individual case.

"The public loses when councillors are afraid to challenge those in power. Elected representatives must be able to ask difficult questions and receive a fair hearing when complaints are made against them.

"Bodies that demand accountability from councillors must accept it themselves. Ministers should now commission an independent review with a clear deadline and hear directly from those who have experienced the system.

"Four councils have spoken. The Scottish Government needs to act."

Mr McLean's original motion had set out more detailed proposals for changes to the system, including stronger protections for councillors' freedom of speech and political expression, greater rights for those facing complaints and safeguards against vexatious, malicious or politically motivated allegations.

It also called for consideration of whether councillors should have protections comparable, where appropriate, to those afforded to parliamentarians when carrying out their democratic responsibilities.

Those detailed proposals were part of the original motion and were not all reproduced as specific requirements in the final wording carried by North Ayrshire Council.= Under Scotland's councillors' code of conduct, elected members are required to follow rules governing their behaviour and dealings with council staff and the public.

Complaints are investigated by the Ethical Standards Commissioner, who can refer cases to the Standards Commission for a hearing. The Commission can impose sanctions, including censure and suspension, where a breach is established.

The system is intended to uphold public confidence in local government and ensure councillors are held to account for their conduct.

But critics argue that the process must also protect the ability of elected representatives to speak out on matters of public interest, challenge senior officials and properly defend themselves when complaints are made.

A Standards Commission spokesman said: "Councillors are entitled, within their debates, to raise their concerns and to discuss the importance of being able to scrutinise their organisation's performance and the actions of council officers. The Standards Commission was pleased to note that North Ayrshire Council acknowledged, in its motion, the need to protect council employees from unacceptable conduct.

"The Standards Commission believes that it is possible for all elected councillors to carry out their vital scrutiny role without breaching the councillor's code of conduct.

"The code states that councillors must treat everyone with courtesy and respect, and that they must not undermine any individual employee or group of employees, or raise concerns about their performance, conduct or capability in public.

"The Standards Commission maintains that any councillor can scrutinise effectively without breaching the code, and indeed the evidence shows the vast majority are able to do so.

"The Standards Commission would, of course, be happy to contribute to any future review into Scotland's ethical standards framework and the work of both the Ethical Standards Commissioner and the Standards Commission."

**A Scottish Government spokesperson said:** “The Ethical Standards Commissioner and Standards Commission for Scotland operate independently of Scottish Ministers and it would not be appropriate to comment on individual cases or ongoing legal proceedings.

"Local Government reform is an opportunity to ensure that the standards regime functions well and meets the needs of the people of Scotland through reform."
