A Scottish council is facing criticism from teaching unions over possible timetabling changes that could lead to an increase in virtual learning for pupils.

Argyll and Bute Council has just closed a survey of parents and carers examining the “secondary school day and timetable structure”, and which raises the prospect of “developing additional online learning opportunities.”

Scotland’s largest teaching union says online learning offers a poorer experience for young people, and that all pupils should have the right to expect face-to-face teaching. A spokesperson for the local authority told The Herald that it would be “premature to make any assumptions” about future plans.

The introduction to council’s the survey referenced a 2025 independent review which recommended that the council “plan collectively for a reshaping of Secondary Timetabling models” that could increase choices for pupils through “full alignment” of secondary school timetables. The authors of the report add that this would “provide the greatest opportunity to access a wider range of courses through maximising the use of current and emerging technology and facilitating partnership arrangements.” The recommendation is categorised under the dual headings of “Agile” and “Maximise technology.”

The report also said that the council should pilot “more shared senior phase courses and digital delivery models”.

When presented to councillors, officials’ recommendation stated: “Note that Officers will now take forward the development of recommendations of the report.”

A number of other councils in Scotland have introduced forms of timetable alignment, with some using this to expand the use of virtual learning across the authority. In urban and suburban areas, common timetable structures may also be used to enable pupils to study certain subjects by travelling to a school other than their own.

In its parent and carer survey, Argyll and Bute Council stated that it is “considering the introduction of a common school day and timetable structure across all secondary schools”. It adds that this “could allow schools to work together more effectively and offer more subjects and pathways online, particularly in the senior phase (S4-S6).”

Those completing the survey were asked if they would support “introducing a common timetable structure” across all high schools in the region “in order to expand learning opportunities for pupils.” The next question simply asked to what extent they “agree that increasing opportunities to access a broader range of courses is important”, with responses on a scale from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree’.

Participants were also asked which “potential benefits” of the plan are important to them, with options including “greater subject choice”, “access to specialist teachers”, “more flexible pathways”, “improved equity across schools”, and “better preparation for employment and further study.”

Question 9 asked if the “proposed increase in learning opportunities” is enough to justify “minor changes to existing school routines”, although the word “minor” is not defined and no examples of such changes are provided.

In addition, the survey included questions dealing explicitly with increased online learning. One asked if parents and carers would support “pupils accessing additional course options including learning remotely through digital links with other schools in order to widen subject choice.”

Another question asked whether “developing additional online learning opportunities in order to widen subject choice could lead to greater levels of pupil engagement?”

Responding to the details of the survey, a spokesperson for Scotland’s largest teaching union, the EIS, argued that an expansion of online learning would exacerbate problems facing school pupils:

“The EIS is clear that remote and online learning will further entrench inequity, and compound any existing attendance and engagement problems. Online learning is an incomparably poorer educational experience for young people than face-to-face learning with a qualified subject teacher. High-quality learning and teaching is founded on human interaction, rich communication and real-time individual support. Learning on-screen cannot compare.”

“National Qualifications have not been designed for online delivery and local authorities are risking a two-tier exam system split between those candidates who learn in class with a teacher and those who have to learn on a screen. We already know that the final years of school are the most stressful for young people. They need human connection and in-person support, not the isolated and alienating experience of learning online.”

“The EIS is absolutely clear that schools must be resourced sufficiently to allow for all learners, whatever their aptitudes, to excel by being taught in-person by qualified subject teachers across a broad range of subjects and levels and that school timetabling decisions should be underpinned by a sound educational rationale.

“All of our young people, no matter in which part of the country they live, should have the right to expect the same high-quality educational experience, delivered in class by dedicated, professional teachers.”

Speaking to The Herald, a spokesperson for the Scottish Secondary Teachers Association (SSTA) also raised concerns, noting that the sort of common timetabling arrangements being explored by Argyll and Bute Council “constrain” the ability of headteachers to make decisions about their schools. The spokesperson also questioned whether the plans are driven by a desire to expand choices for young people, or a need to make financial savings, and noted that the key issue behind the plans seems to be “an inability to recruit and retain specialist subject teachers.”

The SNP manifesto for the recent Holyrood election stated that the party is committed to “empowering headteachers to have a greater say in the education that their schools provides.”

A spokesperson for the Scottish Government said:

“All pupils are entitled to a full-time education, and schools should provide personalised support that maximises the attendance and engagement of pupils. While individual timetable arrangements are for councils, we would expect any changes to timetabling to be taken forward in consultation with communities and communicated well in advance of implementation.

“The Scottish Government has committed to empowering school leaders. This is backed with investment through the Scottish Attainment Challenge and Pupil Equity Funding, enabling headteachers to target it for maximum impact. We’re also supporting professional learning programmes - including Into and In Headship and the Columba 1400 Head Teachers' Leadership Academies.”

In response to a number of questions about the potential changes to timetabling in the region, a spokesperson for Argyll and Bute Council said:

“The independent review of the Council’s Education Service was considered by the Council in November 2025. Since then, the development of the recommendations in the report have been taken forward. That work includes looking at how greater alignment of timetables could increase subject choice and learning opportunities for young people.

“That work is ongoing, includes engagement within the Education Service and it would be premature to make any assumptions about what may come forward in due course.”
