Scotland’s hotels sector is always a hive of activity on the news front.

And a major development for a well-known portfolio has been eye-catching.

The Crerar hotels portfolio, including nine properties in Scotland, has been put up for sale less than four years after it was acquired by private equity player Blantyre Capital and partner Fairtree Hotel Investments.

When I covered this story on October 21, there had already been interest from domestic and international buyers, including hotel operators, private equity players and family offices.

The Crerar hotels business, which now employs about 450 people in Scotland and 500 in total including its hotel in the Lake District, was bought by Blantyre Capital and Fairtree Hotel Investments in a deal announced in March 2023.

Veteran Scottish hotelier Paddy Crerar, who built the business, said then that the sale of the group had come “far earlier than thought” as he described the acquisition by Blantyre Capital and Fairtree Hotel Investments as “cracking”.

Property agent Savills, which highlighted Scotland’s “global appeal”, has been appointed by Blantyre Capital to market the portfolio for sale.

Asked on October 21 about interest so far in the properties, Savills told The Herald: “Early indications of interest are from a mix of domestic and international buyers, including hotel operators, private equity and family offices. Scotland has global appeal and with [circa] 40 million people globally claiming ancestry to Scotland, there is exponential interest in acquiring hotels in the region.”

The Crerar hotels portfolio comprises 589 rooms across the 10 hotels, which range from 42 to 100 rooms, and includes landmark properties such as The Glencoe Inn and Loch Fyne Hotel & Spa at Inveraray.

Among the other Crerar properties on the market are Oban Bay Hotel, Isle of Mull Hotel & Spa at Craignure, Balmoral Arms at Ballater on Royal Deeside and Golf View Hotel & Spa in Nairn, as well as Thainstone House in Inverurie.

Also in the portfolio are Daffodil Hotel & Spa in the Lake District and Dunkeld House Hotel and Fonab Castle in Perthshire, which were added as a result of the acquisition by Blantyre Capital and Fairtree Hotel Investments.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21612603.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Fonab Castle in Perthshire is among the properties put up for sale. *(Image: Crerar Hotels)*

Savills declared that the Crerar hotels portfolio “represents a significant presence within the premium end of the hotel market, with its Scottish properties accounting for 14% of luxury rooms in the Scotland north regional submarket”.

It added that the opportunity is “underpinned by the strong performance of Scotland’s hotel sector”.

And it flagged its research showing revenue per available room (RevPAR) in the Scottish hotel sector had increased by 60% since 2019, the third-highest increase in Europe.

Savills added that, in the 12 months to April 2026, Scotland’s RevPAR reached £100, with growth since 2019 double the level recorded in England.

Luxury hotels have continued to outperform other hotel classes, supported by average annual growth in the average daily rate (ADR) of 10% since 2019, Savills noted.

Steven Fyfe, director of hotel capital markets at Savills, observed that “Scotland’s luxury hotels continue to benefit from their appeal to high-spending international visitors, particularly those from the US, Middle East and growing inbound markets from East Asia”.

He added: “The Crerar hotels portfolio represents a rare opportunity to acquire a substantial portfolio of luxury country house hotels in some of the UK’s most sought-after destinations. The portfolio combines high-quality assets, an established operating platform and a robust income stream with clear potential to drive further performance through targeted investment.”

Another of my big stories published last month was an exclusive interview with Baillie Gifford chief executive Tim Campbell.

The chief executive of the Edinburgh investment house, which manages around £200 billion of assets, said he “probably didn't expect the stress” of the role "to be what it is”, while declaring himself “very lucky” to be leading the business.

He added that “it's a stress you kind of just had to embrace” and underlined the importance of his family in enabling him to cope with a “fairly full-on job”.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21634092.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Baillie Gifford chief executive Tim Campbell. *(Image: Gordon Terris)*

Mr Campbell, who became chief executive of Baillie Gifford in April last year, also emphasised his belief that the absence of external shareholders in the investment partnership had enabled it to implement a recent reduction in the workforce “in a more human way”.

He reflected on the huge growth in the business since he joined in 1999.

And he underlined successes in winning clients in markets around the globe, including the US and Japan, with Baillie Gifford now selling its products in 42 countries.

Mr Campbell also highlighted the scale of Baillie Gifford’s private market investment activity.

Asked if the chief executive job had been what he expected, Mr Campbell replied: “I'm not sure if it is what I expected or not. I probably didn't expect the level of…the stress of it to be what it is, but it's a stress you kind of just had to embrace.

“I guess I'm lucky. I've got a nice wife and nice kids, and we can go for a walk in the evening and that sort of helps. You can have the debrief.

“But seriously, I think that stuff is pretty important in terms of just being able to cope with what is a fairly full-on job. I think without that it would be difficult.”

Mr Campbell added: “I've been here for 27 years, so I've got an enormous affection for the firm. I think, whether I like it or not, I've ended up in this role at a time where you've got enormous amounts of industry change, where the performance for some of our strategies isn't as good as we'd like it to be, but also where we can see a huge amount of growth opportunity.

“So trying to manage all that is a pretty energising job to have for a firm that I know well and feel a great loyalty to.

“Also, at a time when I think the industry as a whole hasn't always covered itself in glory, so I think what we do, the way we go about the investment task, the fact that we are really long term, we're looking for these great transformational businesses. We are a firm that tries to do the right thing for the right reasons in the right way …

“So I'm very lucky that I get to play a part in that.”

Asked if he would have imagined Baillie Gifford would be the scale it is now when he joined in 1999, Mr Campbell replied: “So I joined in September 1999 and actually joined the UK desk, which, in terms of assets I guess, was probably the biggest desk in Baillie Gifford at the time.

“When I joined there were 250 people and about £16 billion under management. So you contrast that with…1,600 people and about £200 billion now, so there has been an awful lot of growth.”
