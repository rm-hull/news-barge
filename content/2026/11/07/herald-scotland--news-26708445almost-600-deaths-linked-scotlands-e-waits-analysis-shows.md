Almost 600 excess deaths have been linked to lengthy waits in Scotland’s accident and emergency departments in the first eight months of this year, according to new analysis by the Scottish Liberal Democrats.

The party has accused the SNP Government of presiding over a “lethal” crisis in emergency care, with its calculations suggesting the number of deaths associated with waits of more than 12 hours has risen by more than 5,300% compared with a decade ago.

The analysis estimates that 597 additional deaths were associated with prolonged A&E waits between January and August 2026, compared with just 11 during the same period in 2016.

The figures represent an estimated number of excess deaths associated with lengthy waits, rather than a confirmed count of patients whose deaths were directly caused by delays.

The Scottish Liberal Democrats calculated the figures using a standardised mortality ratio used by the Royal College of Emergency Medicine (RCEM), which estimates one additional death for every 72 patients who spend between eight and 12 hours in an emergency department before admission.

The party applied the measure to the number of patients waiting more than 12 hours in Scottish A&E departments.

Its findings suggest a 5,327% increase in estimated deaths associated with prolonged waits over the past decade.

The analysis also examined summer waiting times, estimating that 185 excess deaths were associated with 12-hour waits during summer 2026, compared with none in the equivalent period in 2016.

The findings come amid continued pressure on Scotland's emergency departments, with official figures showing thousands of patients continue to face lengthy delays.

A total of 7,071 patients spent more than 12 hours in A&E during August, representing 4.8% of attendances, according to Public Health Scotland.

Just 66.7% of patients were seen and subsequently admitted, transferred or discharged within four hours, against the Scottish Government's target of 95%.

The Royal College of Emergency Medicine has repeatedly warned of the risks posed by overcrowding and lengthy waits in emergency departments.

However, its estimates are based on statistical associations between delays and mortality, rather than establishing that individual deaths were directly caused by waiting times.

Scottish Liberal Democrat health spokesperson Adam Harley said the latest figures highlighted the urgent need to tackle pressures across the NHS and social care system.

He said: “With hundreds of deaths a year now associated with long waits, the crisis in our A&E departments is quite literally lethal.

“The problems in Scotland’s healthcare system have exploded during the SNP’s time in charge.

“If things are to change, we must urgently fix the underlying crisis in social care.

“Too many hospital beds are occupied by people who can’t get the care package they need to return home. It’s driving up waits at A&E and leaving ambulances stacking up outside.

“Ministers must finally listen to our plans to boost pay and conditions for care workers and attract more people into the profession, helping us free up life-saving space in A&Es.”

The party argues that difficulties discharging medically fit patients into appropriate social care are contributing to delays throughout hospitals, leaving fewer beds available for people requiring emergency admission.

It is calling for improved pay and working conditions for care staff to help address recruitment pressures and increase capacity in the sector.

A Scottish government spokesman said: “Excessively long waits are clearly unacceptable and ministers are laser-focused on tackling the pressures facing our NHS and our exceptional workforce.

“The figures cited are not official statistics so we are unable to comment on how they were calculated. Published statistics show that around 97 per cent of all hospital discharges happen without delay, but clearly no one should have to wait longer in hospital than they need to.

“Whilst this situation is not unique to Scotland with all other UK nations experiencing similar pressures, we are taking action by expanding Hospital at Home services and implementing 'discharge without delay' in all health boards this winter, which will see people discharged within three days of admission if clinically appropriate.

“Ministers have been open about the need for reform of social care, but it is important to be clear the statutory responsibility for delivery of services lies with local authorities. Change is needed, but this must be done jointly with the NHS, the Scottish Government, local government and the third sector.”
