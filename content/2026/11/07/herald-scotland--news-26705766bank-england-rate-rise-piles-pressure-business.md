Just when businesses might have hoped that one of the biggest economic headaches of recent years was finally receding, along comes another reminder that the path back to something resembling normality was never going to be straightforward.

The Bank of England’s decision to raise interest rates from 3.75% to 4% was the defining business event of the week. It was the first increase in more than three years, prompted by renewed inflationary pressures associated largely with higher energy prices and the continuing conflict in the Middle East.

More worrying for businesses and households was the warning from Governor Andrew Bailey that, although this should not necessarily be seen as the start of a long sequence of increases, neither is it likely to be the last unless the situation in the Middle East is resolved.

The Bank expects inflation to peak at around 5% next year, with household energy bills among the main sources of upward pressure. It estimates inflation will not return to its 2% target until the third quarter of 2028.

That represents an unwelcome change in direction. Companies have spent years adjusting to sharply higher costs, while households have endured a prolonged squeeze on their finances. Any expectation that lower borrowing costs might finally offer some relief has now been called into question.

For businesses, the implications go well beyond the headline interest rate. Borrowing becomes more expensive, investment decisions become harder to justify, and consumers have less money to spend. At the same time, companies' costs keep rising.

The Bank highlighted that many firms have so far been able to absorb higher energy costs through their profit margins, or because they protected themselves against price increases in advance. But it warned those buffers would prove temporary if the energy shock persists, with higher costs eventually feeding through to consumers.

What makes the timing particularly frustrating is that this has been a week in which Scottish businesses have supplied of evidence of their willingness to invest and grow.

A group of veteran whisky entrepreneurs has launched a new £16 million Scottish spirits group, while Intelligent Water has secured £3.5m of funding with the aim of more than doubling turnover over the next five years. Glasgow Prestwick Airport is exploring the prospect of a new route to China, and surveying business Shepherd has been pushing ahead with its expansion in London.

JD Wetherspoon, meanwhile, is preparing to open a new 485-seat bar in Edinburgh in January, another substantial investment and one that will bring new jobs with it.

Even where the numbers have been good, however, the warnings have not been far behind. Graham’s The Family Dairy reported a surge in profits but also highlighted the prospect of rising food prices.

There are home-grown uncertainties too. Five months after the Scottish Government promised a “rapid” review of business rates, that process has still not begun. For companies trying to make investment decisions, another unresolved cost is hardly welcome.

Energy policy presents its own complications. The UK Government has introduced legislation to end new exploration licences at the same time as events overseas serve a reminder of how exposed businesses and households remain to movements in global energy markets.

None of this suggests Scottish businesses have lost their appetite for growth. The company news of the past few days suggests many are still prepared to invest and expand.

Businesses have shown plenty of resilience in recent years, but companies cannot control wars, oil prices or interest rates. Governments can't control many of those things either, but policymakers do shape the conditions businesses face at home. They should be wary of adding costs and uncertainty when companies are already dealing with forces well beyond their control.

The danger is not necessarily that businesses suddenly stop investing. It is that marginal decisions begin to change: an expansion postponed, another employee not recruited, or an investment that no longer produces an adequate return.

Taken individually, those decisions rarely make headlines. Added together, they matter enormously for economic growth.
