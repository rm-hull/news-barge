Unpaid carers are missing potentially life-saving NHS treatment because Scotland’s care system cannot provide anyone to look after their loved ones, a major report has found.

This year’s State of Caring report reveals blood tests, x-rays and biopsies were being missed because of the toll of caring for a loved one.

More than 1,600 people were surveyed as part of the Carers Scotland report, with half stating they had cancelled or postponed a medical appointment, test or treatment because of their caring responsibilities.

That figure has increased from 41% in 2023, with the SNP-led Scottish Government now facing pressure to set out a timeline to meet its manifesto commitment to provide annual health checks for carers.

More than a quarter (27%) of carers surveyed had cancelled or postponed an investigation such as a blood test, x-ray, scan or ultrasound, while 22% had missed screening appointments including breast and cervical screening.

Some carers had put off even more serious medical care, with 9% cancelling or postponing surgery.

Kirsten Hogg, director of Carers Scotland, said many unpaid carers were not choosing to neglect their own health but were being prevented from accessing healthcare by the demands of caring and a lack of replacement support.

Almost half (49%) of those who postponed or cancelled healthcare said replacement care was not available, while 43% said services were not flexible enough to accommodate their caring situations.

Ahead of this year’s Holyrood election, the SNP pledged to introduce annual health checks for unpaid carers, but Carers Scotland is now demanding ministers spell out when the promise will be delivered.

The charity also wants sufficient replacement care to ensure carers are actually able to attend appointments, warning that a lack of support is leaving some unable to look after their own health.

Almost a third (29%) reported poor physical health and more than a third (35%) saying their mental health was poor.

Despite this, 85% said nobody in the NHS had asked them about the impact their caring role was having on their own health.

Carers responding to the survey described putting potentially serious symptoms aside because they could not leave the person they cared for.

One said they had avoided visiting their GP after noticing a change in their breast because caring had to take priority. A subsequent mammogram was clear.

Another said they believed they had fractured an ankle but had not sought medical help because there was nobody else available to provide care.

Kirsten Hogg said: “These findings show that many carers are forced to put their own health needs after those of the person they care for.

“Carers are missing not only routine appointments, but diagnostic tests, screening appointments and even surgery, raising serious concerns about unmet health needs within the caring population.”

The pressures were particularly acute among carers facing financial hardship, with 72% of those struggling to make ends meet saying they had cancelled or postponed a medical appointment.

Ms Hogg warned those carers were often dealing with a combination of poverty, poor health, caring responsibilities and a lack of replacement care, leaving those with some of the greatest needs facing the biggest barriers to support.

She said: “Without action to improve replacement care, increase the flexibility of health services and address the financial pressures facing carers, these inequalities are likely to widen further and contribute to poorer health outcomes over the longer term.”

The State of Caring in Scotland 2026 report also raises wider concerns about the support available to unpaid carers, who provide care that would otherwise have to be delivered by public services.

Scotland has an estimated 722,000 unpaid carers, with the care they provide estimated to be worth £18.3 billion a year.

The Scottish Government has been asked for comment.
