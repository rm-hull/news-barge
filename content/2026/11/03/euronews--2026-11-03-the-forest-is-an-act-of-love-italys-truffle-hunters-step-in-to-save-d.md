## Reforestation can help lower soil temperatures, retain moisture and reduce erosion in woodlands where climate change is shrinking truffle harvests.

In the hills around Alba, home to the world’s most famous white truffles, hunters known as 'trifolau' traditionally begin their season on 1 October. But increasingly, they are confronting a challenge above ground: climate change.

Months of heat and drought have raised concerns about the future of Italy's prized white truffle, a fungus that depends on cool, moist soils and a delicate relationship with tree roots.

“The entire month of October was affected by the lack of rain in July and August and the high temperatures, so it was characterised by poor production and poor truffle shelf life,” says Antonio Degiacomi, president of Italy’s National Centre for Truffle Studies.

According to Degiacomi, dry and compacted soils have also produced smaller truffles this season.

The difficult harvest comes as Alba and the surrounding Langhe hills welcome visitors for the International White Truffle Fair, one of Italy’s best-known food events, which opened on 10 October and runs until 6 December.

## A changing climate, a changing truffle harvest

For Axel Iberti, president of the fair, the season began slowly, with smaller **truffles** emerging after a hot, dry summer. More recent rains have improved conditions, helping larger specimens appear.

Despite tighter supplies, demand remains strong. Prices at Alba’s truffle market are running about 20 to 30 per cent higher than last year, Iberti says, while hotels and other accommodations have largely sold out during the fair period.

The concerns extend beyond this year’s crop.

“This year certainly stands out from a trend we all know has been going on for five or six years now,” Degiacomi says, citing **hotter summers** and increasingly erratic rainfall patterns.

Truffle hunter Tino Marolo says uncertainty has always been part of the hunt.

“You certainly come home empty-handed more often than you do with a truffle,” he says. “It’s really the thrill of saying: I don’t know what I’m going to find.”

## From Italy to Croatia, truffle-producing regions see the same warning signs

Similar concerns are emerging elsewhere in Europe’s truffle-producing regions, suggesting a challenge extending beyond any single forest or region.

Across the Adriatic Sea in **Croatia**, truffle hunters and scientists describe many of the same challenges, from shrinking harvests to declining habitat suitability.

In oak forests outside Zagreb, truffle hunter Petar Alebic says years of drought have made the prized fungi harder to find.

“Each year for the past seven or eight years has been worse than the last,” Alebic says. “Due to the drought, unfortunately, truffles are becoming smaller and scarcer.”

Alebic estimates this year’s harvest is about 30 per cent below normal and increasingly supplements his income with truffle-hunting tours for tourists.

Scientists studying truffles in Croatia report similar findings. Croatia is home to about a dozen truffle species, according to mycologist Armin Mesic of the Rudjer Boskovic Institute. The most prized, the white truffle, can fetch as much as €4,000 per kilogram.

“**Drought**, a lack of rainfall and high temperatures have a significant impact on both the quantity and quality of truffles,” Mesic says.

“Quantities are declining, as is the geographical area where white truffles grow,” he adds.

## Forests are part of the solution to failing truffle harvests

Yet experts in Italy’s Piedmont region, where Alba is located, reject the idea that the white truffle is doomed.

“I don’t think we’re facing extinction; we’re facing a challenge,” Degiacomi says.

Researchers are studying ways to help truffle habitats adapt, including improved forest management and experimental irrigation during prolonged droughts.

“We’re trying to do what nature does, and do it artificially,” Degiacomi says.

In Alba, the response increasingly centres on restoring and protecting forests.

Iberti has expanded the fair’s focus beyond tourism to include environmental education and **conservation** efforts.

“The truffle is the sum, but it’s also the vector,” Iberti says. “People come to Alba and the Langhe to discover everything the territory has to offer.”

Working with municipalities, truffle hunters and landowners, communities have begun creating new public truffle grounds and planting trees that could eventually support truffle growth.

“The forest is an act of love and faith for the next two or three generations,” Iberti says.

Advocates say reforestation can help lower soil temperatures, retain moisture and reduce erosion, although it can take more than a decade before newly planted trees become suitable hosts for truffles.

Researchers are also investigating the role of **soil microorganisms** and looking for tree varieties better suited to hotter and drier conditions.

Attempts to cultivate the prized white truffle continue, but reliable commercial production remains elusive. Unlike black truffles, which can be cultivated, white truffles have largely resisted large-scale production.

As autumn progresses, the size of this year’s harvest will become clearer, along with its effect on prices and supplies reaching restaurants and markets.

Whether future generations will continue to enjoy the **thrill of the hunt** may depend on a mix of science, conservation and adaptation to a warmer climate.
