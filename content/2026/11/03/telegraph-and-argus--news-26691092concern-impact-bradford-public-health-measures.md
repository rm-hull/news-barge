Programmes to improve public health in some areas of Bradford have been compared to “putting a band aid on a gunshot wound.”

A council committee has been told that until more is done to tackle underlying issues of poverty and deprivation, schemes to tackle obesity, poor health and smoking will only have a limited impact.

Members of the Bradford West Area Committee were given an update on public health measures in the constituency at a meeting on Thursday, when officers were questioned about why there were still so many health issues in the area.

A report to the panel gave updates on work to introduce healthy eating schemes, encourage exercise, improve dental health and reduce smoking rates.

But members questioned how successful these schemes have been in an area that still suffers from poorer outcomes that most of Bradford and England.

A recent report into the constituency shows Bradford West has higher rates of deaths related to respiratory issues, infant mortality and obesity at Year 6.

20 per cent of adults in Bradford West smoke, compared to the 16 per cent average across the district.

At the same time 48.4 per cent of children in the constituency are classed as living in a “low income” family, and the health inequalities facing residents of Bradford West have long been seen as one of the biggest problems.

A report sent to members ahead of Thursday’s meeting said: “Bradford district continues to experience significant health challenges when compared to national averages, including lower healthy life expectancy, higher levels of long-term illness, obesity, smoking-related harm, physical inactivity, alcohol-related harm and health inequalities linked to deprivation.

“Wider determinants such as employment, education, housing, poverty and environmental conditions continue to influence health outcomes across communities.”

## Councillor questions what is being done

Chair of the committee Councillor Ishtiaq Ahmed (Heaton and Frizinghall, Bradford Independent), said: “Health inequalities have existed in Bradford for decades, and we have had successive strategies, but the gap still remains stubbornly wide.

“At what point do we stop commissioning new initiatives and examine why all these approaches have failed to deliver a sustained reduction in inequalities?

"What has public health learned from years of limited progress?

"What are we doing to ensure that in five years’ time we are not hearing about the same inequalities again?”

![Councillor Ishtiaq Ahmed](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/20865204.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Councillor Ishtiaq Ahmed *(Image: Bradford Council)*

## 'Bradford is a very deprived area'

Public Health officer Mikhail Foster, said: “When you talk about health inequalities you have to start the conversation with the fact that, unfortunately, Bradford is a very deprived area.

“Deprivation is insidious and underlies a number of different issues.

"Childhood obesity, smoking, cardiovascular issues, in some respect these can all be tied to deprivation.”

Cllr Ahmed replied: “Are our public health measures reducing the gap or managing the gap?”

Mr Foster said: “With smoking prevalence, we have seen a continuous decline in smoking rates over the last 10 years.

“We’re providing a protective factor for a number of issues – tackling issues at the point of symptoms being recognised.

“But without tackling underlying factors they won’t lead to a massive reduction.

“It is like putting a band aid on a gunshot wound.

“This work is a necessary part of drawing down rates, but we need to also be tackling other determinants of health.

“Our current interventions are providing a protective factor, but without tackling unemployment, poor housing, which is linked to respiratory issues, people feeling unsafe in communities – this is where the issues lie.”

## Services 'not sufficient to tackle underlying issues'

Councillor Mohammed Ali Islam (Manningham, Bradford Independents) said: “A lot of issues in my ward I hear again and again. We some strategies to tackle them, but no definitive outcomes.”

Mr Foster gave an example of a smoker who accessed the council’s stop smoking services.

He said: “The key reason they smoke is that they are depressed about their living situation.

“Even if they come to our stop smoking service, the underlying problem that has pushed them to smoke in the first place will still exist.

“The services available are a necessary part of our health approach, but they are not sufficient to tackle the underlying issues.”

The committee noted the report and voted to “express concern that persistent health inequalities remain.”

They asked officers to prepare a detailed report into why previous approaches have not narrowed the gap in health outcomes.
