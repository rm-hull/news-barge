What is being decided in the midterms and why do they matter?

David Smith

The US Congress has two chambers: the House of Representatives and theSenate. Both must approve federal legislation before it can become law and both play important roles in overseeing the executive branch.

The House is elected every two years, meaning all 435 voting seats are contested at every congressional election. The Senate has 100 members serving staggered six-year terms, so roughly a third of its seats are up for election every two years.

This year’s ballot includes:

All 435 seats in the House of Representatives. Republicans are defending a narrow majority, with Democrats needing a net gain of four seats to take control.

35 seats in the Senate. Thirty-three seats are up in regular elections, along with two special elections in Florida and Ohio to fill the remainder of terms that were vacated early. Republicans currently hold 53 seats, against 47 held by Democrats and independents who caucus with them. Democrats need to gain four seats to secure a majority.

39 governorships. Voters will choose governors in 36 states and three US territories.

Thousands of state legislative seats, as well as local offices, judicial contests and ballot measures on issues including taxes, abortion rights and voting rules.

The elections for Congress will determine which party controls Congress for the final two years of Trump’s second term. The governorships and state legislative contests will shape policymaking closer to home, from education and reproductive rights to taxation and the administration of elections.

The midterms have a long history of punishing the occupant of the White House. The president’s party has lost House seats in 18 of the past 20 midterm elections, a 90% rate of loss that stretches back to 1946.

Speaking to reporters after casting his ballot in Michigan, Republican Senate candidate Mike Rogers denounced what he called “vicious” Democratic attacks on Haley Stevens, the former Democratic primary candidate who abandoned her party to endorse him in the eleventh hour before the midterms.

“Obviously I’m disappointed in how vicious they’ve been,” Rogers told reporters assembled in a parking lot “But I think that speaks to who the modern Democrat party is.”

Rogers continued: “Here’s somebody who said ‘I voted Democrat all the way down the list, except for one person who I think is too extreme for our state.’ That’s country over party. They ought to celebrate her courage and encourage her to continue being a Democrat rather than trying to throw her out of the party.”

“I’ve never seen such viciousness online.”

Stevens, the erstwhile challenger of Democratic nominee Abdul El-Sayed, broke with her party to endorse Rogers over the weekend, after a renewed public break with El-Sayed over an issue that fiercely divided them in their primary race: US support for Israel.

The line to vote at Burbank Early Childhood School in Columbus, Ohio grew long as hundreds of people filed into the polling site to vote throughout the morning.

At the start of this day, if I have one suggestion to those who are deciding how to vote today, vote for the person who can actually make your life better. Vote for the person who you believe in your heart of hearts is actually going to make your family’s life better.

This election, especially for the role of governor, comes down to who’s going to allow your family to live a better life.

He added, “Our work begins tomorrow morning”.

Two days of torrential downpours gave way to a brighter morning in south Florida, where voters took advantage of the brief respite to show up at soggy polling stations in what officials said were encouraging numbers.

At Ramblewood elementary school in the Fort Lauderdale suburb of Coral Springs, Curt Tiefenbrun, president of the local Democratic club and a water district candidate, dodged an early shower to greet a “receptive” line of voters when the polls opened at 7am.

“We had a pretty good turnout this morning before everybody had to go to work. They’re very enthusiastic as they accept our voter guide, and we’ve seen a lot of Democrats come out. We’re really happy with that,” he said.

Sunday it rained for hours and people were coming out to vote. All of us were standing out there wearing our ponchos, handing out guides. It was fun to see everybody come out and really participate.

Florida election officials released early voting figures on Tuesday showing registered Republicans in the state returned over 250,000 more ballots than Democrats - 1.49 million to 1.21m. Democrats are hoping in-person turnout on Tuesday, together with support from hundreds of thousands of independent voters and Republicans disillusioned with their party, will help them overcome the deficit.

Tiefenbrun said Democratic turnout in early voting was “extremely encouraging” compared to what he saw in 2024 and 2022. “We’re hoping we can stay above the fray, so to speak, on this final day, election day,” he said.

Polls are set to begin opening in Alaska, which is hosting the competitive Senate race between Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan and Mary Peltola, his Democratic challenger.

The race has been complicated by revelations that Peltola’s campaign allegedly recruited another man named Dan Sullivan to join the race, hoping to peel ballots away from the incumbent. While the tactic wasn’t clearly illegal, it’s prompted calls from the Trump administration to toss out the results if Sullivan doesn’t win out, and to investigate Peltola’s campaign for potential criminal violations.

A federal judge recently denied a Department of Justice request to block the race’s certification, and the state of Alaska – itself investigating Peltola – assured the court that ballots were designed to minimize confusion. But we’ll learn over the coming days just how many votes the imposter Sullivan will receive – and if it’s enough to sway the outcome of a crucial contest.

Trump tells Fox he'll slash aid to Israel by end of this year

In an interview with Fox & Friends this morning, in which he made a meandering final pitch to Americans voting in the 2026 midterm election, Trump said that he had agreed with Benjamin Netanyahu to cut off $4 billion of US military aid to Israel.”

“Bibi Netanyahu and I have made a deal,” Trump said. “We give Isreal $4 billion a year, and we made a deal months ago that we’re stopping. And they’re fine with it, because, you know, they’ve become a very successful country. They don’t need it. And we won’t be paying anymore to Israel.”

Trump said that the deal came at the insistence of Netanyahu, whose political future is in doubt following an Israeli election that failed to deliver the longtime prime minister a clear majority.

US ties to Israel, the country’s conduct in Gaza, and the conflict with Iran have become major issues in the 2026 midterm election.

A leading abortion rights group has endorsed Troy Jackson, the Democratic Senate candidate for Maine, while attacking the record of his Republican opponent, Senator Susan Collins.

Mini Timmaraju, president of the advocacy group Reproductive Freedom for All, hailed Jackson - who was formerly an opponent of abortion - for “evolving” on the issue at a last-minute canvassing event in Scarborough. At the same time, she attacked Collins for trying to depict herself as “pro-choice”, saying she had voted to confirm the conservative US Supreme Court justice, Samuel Alito, who was the author of the 2022 court ruling that overturned the historic Roe versus Wade decision that guaranteed the right to an abortion. Jackson has repeatedly criticised Collins’ 2018 vote to confirm another conservative justice, Brett Kavanaugh, who subsequently voted to overturn Roe v Wade.

Timmaraju said Jackson’s election was vital to counteract a Trump administration plan to be “hand-off on abortion post-midterms” that she said would make it easer to dismantle state protections.

“We know the Trump administration has plans for a few weeks from now to reverse on abortion, to make it harder to get,” she said. “If we do not put Troy Jackson in the Senate, we won’t have that protection in Maine, where so many Mainers get access to abortion through the mail, through Telehealth.

“There are so many ways to ban abortion, by going around the states, and that is what the Trump administration is extremely interested in.”

Doug Emerson, a self-described activist, parked his car, festooned with slogans protesting the Trump administration’s policies, outside the office of Senator Susan Collins in Portland, Maine’s biggest city.

Emerson said he wanted to bring the attention of Maine voters to what he described as Collins’ tacit collusion with Donald Trump’s most extreme actions, despite her image as a moderate, independent-minded Republican cultivated over five terms as the state’s senator.

“We can’t change things unless we alter the demographics of the current Congress,” said Emerson. “I’m not sure Mainers are paying close enough attention.”

Astronauts For America, an alliance of more than 100 former Nasa explorers, has written an election day open letter to US voters reminding them of the importance of their vote in defending constitutional principles against threats to democracy.

“Federal authority is not a license for any president or federal agency to take over state election administration,” the letter, also signed by ther non-partisan, pro-democracy groups National Security Leaders for America and Leadership Now, said.

“Regardless of political party or personal beliefs, every American should be able to trust that elections are conducted according to the law, every lawful vote is counted, and the results are determined through established legal procedures - not political pressure or the arbitrary exercise of power.”

Astronauts For America, which includes astronauts from almost every generation of US spaceflight from Apollo to the space shuttle, recently completed a nationwide tour of college campuses urging young and first-time voters to ensure their voices are heard.
