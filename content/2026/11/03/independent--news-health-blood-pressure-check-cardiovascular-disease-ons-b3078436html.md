A cardiologist has urged people to do three simple health checks as over 110,000 deaths last year were reported as “avoidable”.

In England last year, 110,804 deaths were from causes considered preventable or treatable through effective public health measures or timely healthcare, according to the latest figures from the Office for National Statistics (ONS).

In Wales, a further 8,011 deaths were classified as avoidable.

Diseases of the circulatory system, including conditions affecting the heart and blood vessels, were the second leading cause of avoidable mortality in both England and Wales, behind cancers.

Of the 110,804 avoidable deaths in England in 2025, 64.1 per cent (71,057) could be attributed to conditions considered preventable, and 35.9 per cent (39,747) could be attributed to conditions considered treatable.

The data also revealed that men living in England’s most deprived areas were almost four times more likely to die from avoidable causes than those in the wealthiest areas.

Dr Francesco Lo Monaco, Harley Street-based cardiologist and founder of the National Longevity Clinic, says the figures underline the importance of identifying health risks before they develop into something more serious.

“One of the biggest misconceptions I encounter is that if you feel well, you must be healthy,” he said.

“Unfortunately, that isn’t always the case. High blood pressure, raised cholesterol and early metabolic problems can develop for years without producing obvious symptoms.

“By the time somebody experiences a significant health event, the underlying risk factors may have been present for a considerable period. We need to change the mindset from treating illness when it happens to identifying risk much earlier.”

## **Blood pressure**

High blood pressure often has no obvious symptoms but can increase the risk of heart attacks, strokes and kidney disease.

Dr Lo Monaco said: “A blood pressure check is one of the simplest and most important things people can do for their health. You can feel completely normal while your blood pressure is persistently elevated. Knowing your numbers means you can take appropriate action.”

![A blood pressure check is a simple but important test to do](https://static.independent.co.uk/2021/01/06/17/iStock-1177942982.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

## **Cholesterol, Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], HbA1c, Glucose and Fasting Insulin**

A full lipid profile measures different types of cholesterol and fats in the blood. Raised LDL cholesterol can contribute to fatty deposits building up inside arteries.

“Cholesterol is particularly important because it isn’t something you can judge by looking at someone. You can be slim, exercise regularly and feel healthy, but still have raised cholesterol. Family history also plays an important role,” the cardiologist said.

An HbA1c blood test measures average blood glucose levels over approximately two to three months and can help identify prediabetes and type 2 diabetes.

He added: “Metabolic health is a huge part of preventative medicine. People often think diabetes only affects those who are overweight, but that’s not the whole picture. Family history, age and genetics also matter. Identifying raised blood sugar early gives people an opportunity to address their risk.”

## **Cardiovascular fitness**

A VO2 max assessment measures how efficiently the body uses oxygen during exercise, providing an indication of cardiorespiratory fitness.

Dr Lo Manoco said: “Cardiovascular fitness is an important part of longevity. We shouldn’t just be interested in how much someone weighs, but how well their heart and lungs support them during physical activity. Maintaining fitness and muscle strength are important for healthier ageing.”

## **Don’t wait until something goes wrong**

Dr Lo Monaco says preventative healthcare should not be reserved for people who are already experiencing symptoms or approaching retirement.

“Longevity isn’t simply about trying to live to 100. It’s about protecting your health and maintaining your physical and mental capabilities for as long as possible.

“The earlier we understand our health risks, the more opportunity we have to do something constructive about them.”
