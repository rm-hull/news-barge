In a very cold courtroom in Bradford city centre sit 42 disparate people.

A lone police officer, in a hi-vis duty vest, stands by the doors. Others are positioned outside.

Everyone present is invested in the events playing out: this is a trial for conspiracy to murder, and the individuals gathered represent all aspects of the proceedings.

The learned judge. His clerk. An usher. A phalanx of barristers in robes and wigs. Sharp-suited detectives. Members of the public. The Press.

And the defendants, all seated in a secure dock alongside security staff.

Other interested parties are watching via a video link.

Through the windows, grey November skies can be seen hanging over Bradford.

In Bradford Crown Court 2, myriad people, all with a different focus on what is being outlined by the judge, suppress shivers in chilly temperatures.

This, then, is the dispassionate finale to a lengthy trial that began in July, was paused in August, re-started after allegations of jury tampering, and is now winding to its close.

And, after the jury was discharged, Bradford’s most senior judge is delivering an equally lengthy judgement that is set to conclude as late as Friday this week.

It is an unusual scenario that many professionals in the courtroom have not previously experienced.

During a “standard” trial, evidence is presented, witnesses give their recollections, questions are asked, cross-examination takes place – and all in front of a jury.

At the end of that process, prosecution and defence barristers give closing speeches designed to outline what has been said and to remind the jury of key matters relevant to their deliberations.

Then the judge sums up before the jury retires to consider its verdict(s).

Here, in this trial, there is no jury.

In delivering his judgement His Honour Judge Jonathan Rose is outlining the case and the evidence which is, in essence, an exercise in summing up without jurors being present.

After discharging the jury, Judge Rose ordered that he would continue without one, with him alone deciding on the verdicts.

Those verdicts will be handed down when the entire judgement has been read.

After many weeks during which summer turned to autumn, the trial is reaching its conclusion.

Mohammed Waqqas Khan, 27, of Folkestone Street, Bradford, Rahees Khan, 42, of no fixed abode but formerly of Granton Street, Bradford, Hanzalah Saladin, 27, of no fixed abode, Bradford, Adam Takolia, 29, of no fixed abode, Bradford, and a fifth man who cannot be named for legal reasons, all deny conspiracy to murder Mohammed Zayad Khan and his nephew Mohammed Usamah Khan on May 11 last year.

In addition, they all deny conspiracy to cause grievous bodily harm to both alleged victims.

Rahees Khan and Alice Dobson, 30, a former Police Community Support Officer (PCSO) of Nether Hall Road, Baildon, both deny perverting the course of justice by accessing confidential material related to the attacks, which Dobson allegedly passed on to Rahees Khan and others.
