Does Scotland's record youth participation figure owe more to better counting than to more young people finding jobs or places?

The headline rate is up to 93.6%, the highest since Skills Development Scotland began the Annual Participation Measure and up from 90.4% ten years ago.

But the share not taking part has barely moved in a decade, from 4.0% to 3.7%. There are 8,729 young people aged 16 to 19 in Scotland still not in education, employment, or training.

That is only 181 fewer Neets than last year, even though there are 5,431 more young people in the group.

So why is the headline going up? Because there is a third group. The "unconfirmed". The young people who are not on the Scottish Government's radar. The young people that we knew existed but whose circumstances we did not know.

This year's stats simply reflect that, thanks to data from the benefits system and from the tax office, Skills Development Scotland has got better at tracking them down. This year's report has this group going from 5.6% in 2016 to 2.66%.

Better data is not to be sniffed at, but let us be realistic about what that means.

When these teenagers are finally located, most are actually already working. They get shifted into the "participating" column, the headline number jumps.

But nobody won a new job. The government did not create opportunity.

So the record high is real, but a lot of it comes from better counting.

It is also worth scrutinising exactly what some of the definitions used in the statistics mean.

Each young person is counted by what they were doing for most of the year. So someone out of work for five months and in a job for seven counts as taking part — a short spell without a job or course does not show up.

The important numbers, though — the ones worth properly crunching — are on those left out.

In the most deprived fifth of neighbourhoods, 6.7% are not participating, against 1.2% in the least deprived.

That fifth holds 22.5% of the cohort and 40% of all non-participants.

Young women are ahead of young men in education by more than ten points, and men make up three in five of those unemployed and seeking work.

The map follows the same lines. Clackmannanshire has the lowest participation at 90.9%, down from 92.0%. Dundee has the highest non-participation rate at 6.7%, up from 6.2%. Fife is the lowest health board area at 92.4%, and Kirkcaldy is the lowest-ranked constituency at 90.4%.

The deprived-area numbers are starkest in the north-east. In Aberdeen City just 83.8% of young people in the poorest fifth of neighbourhoods are participating, against 90% in Glasgow.

Glasgow has 1,020 young people not participating and another 840 unconfirmed, from a cohort of 23,045.

On age, participation is 99.2% at 16, 95.3% at 17, 91.6% at 18 and 88.1% at 19. Of the 56,594 19-year-olds, 6,711 (11.9%) are either not participating or unconfirmed.

Crucially, and bizarrely, the Scottish numbers stop there.

Across the UK, the ONS puts the Neet rate at 10.7% for 18 to 20-year-olds but around 16% to 18% for those aged 21 to 24.

There is no geographical breakdown, so we do not, as far as I am aware, know what the Scotland-specific stats are for older Neets.

It stops at 19, just as, we know, participation begins to drop.

"The absence of a measure beyond age 19 creates a risk that health-related economic inactivity and longer-term disengagement are not fully captured and may therefore remain hidden, even where headline participation rates appear strong," the Scottish Health Equity Research Unit wrote in a report earlier this year.

All of this comes ahead of the publication of former Labour minister Alan Milburn's review of 18 to 24-year-old Neets for the UK Government.

Across the UK there is an estimated 1,012,000 Neets aged 16 to 24. This is the first time the number has exceeded one million since 2013. And it brings with it an eye-watering cost.

In 2024/25, for every £1 the Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) spent on employment support for young people, around £25 was spent on benefits for this age group.

Which is what drives Milburn's argument that money should move from welfare payments to employment support.

I understand that Scottish Government ministers met with Milburn last week. They are not under any illusion about the state of the problem. If there are lessons to be learned in his report, good ideas to be adopted, then they are open to taking them.

A record high is a good headline, but 8,729 young people is the number ministers should be judged on.
