*(Image: Gavin Mackenzie)*

Gavin Mackenzie is hard to reach these days. That is not just because of the ongoing strike action at the University of Edinburgh, where he works, but also because of his job.

As programme director for the Moray House School of Education and Sport’s MSc in Outdoor and Environmental Education, he does much of his instruction in the field. Given the nature of the programme, “field” should be read literally. Learning happens in the classroom, but as importantly it also takes place in the Cairngorms, in the forest, on the water, and across Scotland’s famous natural landscapes.

Mr Mackenzie is currently preparing to depart for a residential with the current cohort in November. These courses, he said, provide students and academics with opportunities to explore key questions around contemporary education, including how to address the “polycrises” facing the world: an “intersection of social, political and economic factors such as climate change, biodiversity loss and physical and mental health crises”.

Tackling these questions in nature and out of the classroom creates “powerful, critically engaged learning communities”, he said, and the courses bring together a wide range of disciplines to build better educators.

How many more there will be, however, is a burning question.

The Outdoor and Environmental Education master’s is one of several programmes tipped for closure next year by the university, part of a list of 90 programmes that will be “taught-out” of the College of Arts, Humanities, and Social Sciences (CAHSS). The other Moray House programmes on the list are the MSc in Inclusive Education; Postgraduate Diploma in Inclusive Education for Deaf Learners; Postgraduate Diploma in Inclusive Education for Visually Impaired Learners; MSc in Learning in Communities; and the MEd in Leadership in Learning.

University leadership has been adamant since the cuts were first announced that these changes affect programmes, not the course offerings or teaching on which the degree pathways are built.

Staff at Moray House, however, worry that losing the programmes will require cutting teaching and learning, given the difficulty of incorporating the highly specialised nature of these subjects into more general existing programme curricula.

Beyond this, they worry about the knock-on effects that even a partial loss of expertise in these disciplines may have on Scotland’s already strained public education system.

In response to questions about whether the programme closure at Moray House will likely lead to a loss of teaching, Prof Sam Fawkner, Head of School for Moray House, said programme closures were part of an “evidence-based” academic portfolio review to ensure the university’s offering is “aligned with student demand, a constantly evolving job market” and can be run sustainably.

It is “critical” to protect the university’s “world-leading reputation and excellence in research and teaching”, he added, which “requires conscious and sometimes difficult choices.”

“We will continue to explore opportunities to embed inclusive education and environmental and sustainability education in our wider academic portfolio in the context of our strategic priorities and the longer-term sustainability of the School.

“We remain committed to communicating any changes to our academic provision directly with our students, staff, external partners and wider community.”

![Students on courses](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21657495.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Outdoor education courses take students on residential learning opportunities to train them to work with young people in outdoor learning. *(Image: Gavin Mackenzie)*

Many specialist components, such as fieldwork and practical elements core to degrees like outdoor education, are more expensive than other programmes in the school, and the university has said it is difficult to cut costs further without closing the programme altogether.

The university has said it has not made any decisions about closing specific courses.

On the ground, however, staff at Moray House worry there is no practical way to separate programme closures from cuts to teaching.

And to Mr Mackenzie, the closures send a bigger message to the world than the need for fiscal responsibility.

“When the world is literally on fire, the University of Edinburgh is choosing to close programmes orientated towards equipping educators and community leaders to reshape education at a time of climate crisis.”

He also pointed to the Scottish Government’s Schools (Residential Outdoor Education) (Scotland) Act 2026, which passed with cross-party support and guarantees a residential outdoor learning experience for all pupils in Scotland as an illustration of the public commitment to outdoor education as a valuable resource.

Beyond his own programme, he worries how Scottish schools will be affected by the loss of specialist inclusive education programmes, at a time when public schools have reported annual increases in the number of pupils with additional support needs (ASN), alongside a simultaneous decline in the number of ASN specialists to support them.

Staff said courses in areas including ADHD, dyslexia, multilingual learners, and work with children’s services have been affected, and argued that initial teacher education could not replace specialist preparation in specialist fields.

This is a chief concern for Yvonne Foley, Professor of Literacies and Language Education at Moray House.

“You cannot expect a PGDE programme to be the only route for, or to carry the weight of, building specialists.

“That's not its aim, and that's not the job that it does. And so, schools end up suffering from a provision gap because we're not producing enough specialists in those specific areas.”

One or two courses, she said, cannot replace the specialist expertise that a dedicated programme offers, or the professional relationships that it builds over years.

“We work directly at the coalface with schools, and that severe lack of educated staff or lack of resources in the sector means that the pipeline to a qualified teacher is no longer there.”

Reflecting on the scale of programme closures at the university, Prof Foley questioned whether it should spark a wider conversation, inside and outside the university sector, about the purpose of higher education.

“I've been here a long time, and I have seen rationalisation of courses and programmes before, where some have been blended or removed. But I've never seen cuts on this scale. There is a lack of dialogue and debate nationally around what education is for, what university is for, and what it is that we want to achieve?”

Rachel O’Neill leads the deaf education pathway and previously directed the MSc in Inclusive Education. She agrees that universities have a civic “responsibility” for the people of Scotland.

“It's a long tradition at Moray House of caring about social justice, and I'm lucky in the way that the deaf education pathway has been safe. But it was always connected with many other things going on in the school, and those are now going.”

“We're all connected with wider communities, which feeds our research, and we're very connected with policy. Sometimes we help pass laws or amendments.

She said it is difficult to interpret the programme closures as anything other than a value statement, a message that this type of specialist teaching isn't valued enough to be a priority during a financial challenge.

![Students on courses](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21657496.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Outdoor and specialist education courses at the University of Edinburgh are set to be taught out. Staff question where the provision will go. *(Image: Gavin Mackenzie)*

When the university first announced the programme closures, however, a spokesperson made the exact opposite point.

“Our focus is on ensuring that our teaching, research and wider contribution remain relevant, sustainable and responsive to future needs, while seeking financial stability.

“The changes are not a judgement on the value of the disciplines, professions or communities represented by individual programmes.

“Our commitment to education and sport remains strong. Our contribution extends far beyond individual programmes. We will continue to make an important contribution through teaching, research, professional learning, knowledge exchange and partnerships.”

Taken together, the university’s communications and the concerns borne by Moray House staff offer a clear window into one of the key dilemmas at the core of the campus conflict: staff with lived experience in at-risk specialities and decision-makers cannot see a way to a shared future.

For Mr Mackenzie, the next steps will be difficult to reverse later.

“Small but high-quality programmes, rooted in community and addressing social and environmental justice, have been integral to policy development and research.

“They have been world-leading, societally relevant and central to the civic responsibilities of the University. When you cut a programme, you also erode the rich ecosystem of research, scholarship, and policy engagement that is the hallmark of these programmes.

“This is not a financial crisis but a moral crisis and a clear articulation of what senior leadership in the University of Edinburgh values.”
