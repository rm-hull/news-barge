Ministers have launched a radical overhaul to merge Scotland's 14 health boards into two mega-authorities without producing a business case, internal Scottish Government papers seen by The Herald reveal.

Civil servants have formally scored the risk of the reform triggering delays, spiralling costs, and operational chaos at more than two-and-a-half times their acceptable tolerance level.

The documents, prepared for the inaugural meeting of the NHS Simplification Programme Board on October 22, lay bare how ministers intend to push through what officials admit is one of the biggest reorganisation exercises since devolution to create "some of the largest public bodies in the UK".

They show core programme objectives still marked "unconfirmed", an Outline Business Case that will not be completed until the end of March 2027, and a legal route that officials were still scrambling to establish weeks after the decision was taken.

The Scottish Cabinet decided on August 27 to simplify the NHS by reducing the number of territorial boards, with John Swinney announcing the plan in his Programme for Government in September.

![John Swinney meets patient Stephanie Tacy during a visit to Queen Elizabeth University Hospital](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/20151892.jpg?type=mds-article-620)John Swinney announced the reform plans in his Programme for Government *(Image: Robert Perry/Getty)*

Under the blueprint, one new board will cover the west of the country and the other the east, with separate arrangements still to be confirmed for Shetland, Orkney, and the Western Isles.

However, health service insiders who have spoken to The Herald on the condition of anonymity say there is little evidence that redrawing regional boundaries is the most cost-effective way to improve frontline patient care.

They have also questioned why the government has not come forward with any estimate of how much the transition will cost.

A formal risk assessment, dated October 20, sets out the danger that the reform is delivered inconsistently, leading to delays, higher costs, organisational instability, and a "failure to realise its intended benefits".

Officials score that risk at 200, calculated by multiplying an impact rating of 50 by a likelihood rating of four. The target is to bring that score down to 75 by June 2028.

The boxes on the risk register for controls in place and actions planned are left entirely blank, although control confidence is described as "reasonable".

What will alarm MSPs is that the Outline Business Case — the document that tests whether the overhaul is viable, what options exist, what it will cost, and how long savings will take to appear — is not due in final draft until March 31, 2027.

A procurement exercise to hire external consultants to draw up the case is only now under way, with a first draft not expected until the end of the year.

By choosing the two-board model months before hiring consultants to look at the options, insiders fear the government is trying to justify a decision it has already made.

SNP ministers were previously carpeted by Holyrood's Finance and Public Administration Committee for attempting to push through the creation of the National Care Service (NCS) without an advance business case.

The committee warned ministers in 2022 that they "should not bring forward primary legislation unless it is based on a full and proper business case".

However, the route chosen by ministers allows them to largely bypass parliamentary scrutiny. As The Herald reported in September, the merger will be carried out using secondary legislation.

Under the National Health Service (Scotland) Act 1978, ministers have existing powers to change boundaries, dissolve existing authorities, and establish new ones. The legislation does, however, require multiple territorial boards.

A single national health board would have required primary legislation, triggering committee evidence sessions, amendments by MSPs, and final chamber votes. Settling on two boards allows ministers to use statutory instruments made under existing powers, giving MSPs significantly less oversight.

Included in the papers is an October 12 submission to Angela Constance, the Cabinet Secretary for Health and Care, which admits officials are in the process of obtaining legal advice on the secondary legislation route.

The submission also lays bare the private anxieties of NHS leadership, summarising feedback from meetings with health board chairs and chief executives.

Officials warned that while health chiefs agreed the status quo was unsustainable, they feared the reorganisation would "create uncertainty which could cause issues with the system," especially as hospitals enter severe winter pressures.

Board chiefs also pleaded with ministers for regular updates to prevent a damaging "information vacuum" from opening up.

The papers also disclose that the Public Bodies (Joint Working) Act 2014 will continue to apply.

That legislation joins up NHS community health services with council-run social care, combining budgets and staff so patients do not fall through the cracks between hospitals and care in the community.

Because it remains in force, the two new mega-boards will be legally required to negotiate fresh integration schemes with all 32 local councils and set up new joint authorities.

This is where the plan runs into its hardest questions, according to NHS insiders.

They struggle to see how superimposing two remote, national-scale bodies simplifies local delivery.

Every partnership also legally requires voting NHS health board members to sit alongside local councillors. Sources warn that two regional boards will struggle to supply enough senior leaders to cover dozens of local authority areas without being spread dangerously thin.

The speed of the reform is also a concern.

Under the "Summary of Programme Current Assumptions", the briefing states that "ministers expect this transition to be carried out as quickly as possible".

Yet officials warn: "There must be sufficient time to engage with and consult stakeholders. It would be safer to have some kind of phased transition from the status quo to the new boards being fully in place and empowered, rather than a big bang."

However, one insider said this was also a question of legality. The law fixes which body is responsible for what, so new arrangements cannot simply be built in the background while the existing boards carry on.

At some point responsibility has to be transferred, and doing so across the whole NHS at once risks disruption. They believe a realistic date for the new system to be fully in place is 2028 or 2029, particularly if changes to legislation are needed.

The documents also highlight the difficulty ministers will face in making savings.

While the programme's core objective is to reduce duplicated management and support functions, the First Minister has insisted there will be no compulsory redundancies.

The top jobs, however, will be overhauled. The chair and chief executive posts at the two new boards will be openly recruited, accompanied by a review of executive pay, with officials noting the roles involve a "different level of complexity and challenge to any current chair or chief executive".

A Scottish Government spokesperson said: "NHS Board Reform aims have been clearly confirmed by Ministers and are set out in our Programme for Government.

"Reform seeks to reduce unwarranted variation, to deliver more care in communities, to remove organisational boundaries and bureaucratic barriers to improve care and access to services for patients, and to create a better working environment for staff.

"As with any change programme at scale, there will be risk registers and other programme documentation established and developed over time.

"Detailed proposals, supported by a full business case, will be published following engagement with a range of stakeholders including NHS Boards, staff representatives, trade unions, local government, third sector partners and the public."
