**Today’s Object of the Week is a poignant reminder of how war could reach from a distant battlefield directly into a County Durham family home.**

There is something particularly poignant about a letter which talks cheerfully of Christmas when we know that its writer would never make it home.

Captain Ian Turnbull was just 23 and serving with the 8th Battalion, Durham Light Infantry, when he wrote to his family from Egypt during the Second World War.

Addressed simply “Dear Ma, Pa, and all”, the letter is filled not with grand thoughts of war but with the everyday things that mattered to a young man a long way from home.

He tells his family about four days’ leave in Alexandria, friends and fellow servicemen, a Persian carpet and news of his sisters Betty and Jean.

Betty had written to him from Officer Cadet Training Unit and Ian was hoping that she would succeed in gaining her commission.

Jean, meanwhile, had joined the Auxiliary Territorial Service, prompting some brotherly teasing.

But towards the end of his letter Ian’s thoughts increasingly turned towards home.

“I’ve just about had enough of this ‘amphibious’ existence, and if I had wings no homing pigeon would beat me!” he wrote.

And then comes a line which is difficult to read with hindsight: “Next Xmas folks and then what about a party. - I haven’t decided whether I shall fly home or sail.”

He signed off by wishing everyone a Merry Christmas.

Ian did not come home.

During fighting at El Daba on November 1 and 2, 1942, following the Second Battle of El Alamein, he was killed in action.

The Turnbull family initially received a telegram informing them that their son and brother had been reported missing.

A further letter would confirm his death.

Taken together, the correspondence tells the story of war at its most painfully personal: a young man looking forward to Christmas at home and a family receiving the news that meant there would instead be an empty chair.

But Ian’s story was also part of a remarkable family commitment to service.

Every Turnbull sibling served during the Second World War.

His sister Elizabeth ‘Betty’ Turnbull had enlisted in the ATS just days before the outbreak of war, beginning at Catterick Camp with the 1st Durham ATS Company.

She later became a Physical Training Sergeant at Neville’s Cross and, after initially being unsuccessful at Officer Cadet training, was commissioned as a Subaltern in 1942.

She served with Mixed Heavy Anti-Aircraft Batteries and was trained to work with predictors and 3.7-inch guns.

Betty also took part in Adventure Through Service, a travelling Ministry of Information exhibition devised to challenge prejudices about the ATS and encourage women to join.

Jean Turnbull also entered the ATS, as Ian’s letter records.

It is that shared service which makes the Turnbull family story especially fitting as Remembrance Sunday approaches.

Remembrance inevitably recalls those who went away to fight, but the Turnbulls also show how deeply military service could run through a single County Durham family, touching sons, daughters, brothers, sisters and parents.

For Ian’s family, its cost was devastating.

Around 3,000 Durham Light Infantry soldiers lost their lives during the Second World War.

More than 80 years later, Ian’s words still carry the hopes of a 23-year-old who expected another Christmas, another party and, eventually, a journey home.
