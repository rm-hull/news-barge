Plans have gone in to place a striking contemporary sculpture by world renowned artist Sir Antony Gormley on the West Front of York Minster.

We reported last week that Sir Antony and the Minster were working together to bring two of his sculptures to the cathedral.

They would be two body-like structures called *Meet*and* Close*.* Meet* would go on the West Front of the Minster, in a niche opposite the statue of Queen Elizabeth II which was installed in 2022.

![](https://wp.theyorkmix.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/antony-gormley-sculpture-york-minster-2.webp)

The planning application reveals how the new sculpture would look.

If approved *Meet* would stay on the West Front for a temporary three-year period starting in March 2028.

The application marks the next step following the earlier announcement that York Minster would be collaborating with Sir Antony to mark its 1,400th anniversary celebrations.

The Turner Prize-winning sculptor is most famous for creating the monumental Angel of the North in Gateshead and Another Place on Crosby Beach.

![](https://wp.theyorkmix.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/antony-gormley-sculpture-york-minster-1.webp)

This is how he describes the *Meet*sculpture in a design statement: “On the outside of the Minster, I would like to make a work called* Meet* for the empty double niche on the west façade.

“This work will be made of two stacks of mutually supporting cast-iron blocks evoking two slightly over-life-size bodies that come together to form a single structure…

“I want to make the relationship between the two forms, one horizontal and one vertical, and two materials, limestone and iron, meaningful.

![](https://wp.theyorkmix.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/antony-gormley-sculpture-york-minster-7.jpg)

“Bringing works that celebrate togetherness is a necessary antidote to the divisiveness of our present time… Bringing iron, the fundamental material of the Industrial Age, into a sacred context made of Permian limestone, is purposeful. Sculpture uses matter to affect the mind.

“For me, this project is a precious and welcome opportunity to make a new work for a collectively made structure, a place that has provided the space for human imagination for millennia.”

Historical surveys suggest the northern niche chosen for Sir Antony’s work may have sat entirely empty ever since the 14th century, when construction was interrupted by the Black Death.

The cast iron sculpture will weigh about 1,700kg, or 1.7 tonnes.

![](https://wp.theyorkmix.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/antony-gormley-sculpture-york-minster-6.webp)

The artwork is designed to weather naturally outdoors, gaining an earthy patina over time. To protect the cathedral’s delicate medieval fabric from rust run-off, the iron will be treated with protective sealants.

A close-up survey has revealed that the decorative stonework and plinth inside the niche have badly decayed over centuries of harsh weather, with cracking stones posing a potential safety hazard to people walking across the Duncombe Place forecourt below.

Installing the artwork means scaffolding can go up, allowing Minster craftspeople to carry out vital conservation repairs to the surrounding stone tracery and plinth capitals.

Plans also include permission to install discreet bird netting over the niche, but only if the resident city pigeon population decides to make the sculpture its new perch.

*Meet*is one of a pair of pieces planned for York Minster. A second sister sculpture titled* Close*, carved from limestone rather than iron, is slated to go on display inside the cathedral nave under a separate ecclesiastical application.

![](https://wp.theyorkmix.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/antony-gormley-sculpture-york-minster-5.webp)

Cathedrals have a growing track record of embracing contemporary masterworks, including Sir Antony’s celebrated sculpture *Doubt*, which was installed on the West Front of Wells Cathedral in 2021.

The York Minster planning application concludes: “*Meet* offers a significant opportunity to present new work by an internationally renowned British contemporary artist at York Minster.

“It is expected to attract public and media attention, bring new and returning visitors to the Minster, and encourage wider engagement with worship, prayer, events, heritage and visitor participation.”
