# Britain's benefits army bigger than actual army despite WW3 fears growing

## More people work for the Department for Work and Pensions than serve in the British Army, new figures show

Shocking new figures have revealed that more people are employed in the government department responsible for distributing benefits than serve in the army. Nearly 84,000 full-time equivalent staff work for the Department for Work and Pensions, while the army has 83,000 full-time personnel.

Defence Secretary Wes Streeting has cautioned that the UK must be prepared for war within 1,000 days, yet the Government has dismissed Conservative demands to boost defence spending by reducing the benefits budget, reports The Express. The welfare department also employs more staff than the Royal Navy and Royal Air Force combined, which together have 67,850 full-time personnel.

Helen Whately, Shadow Work and Pensions Secretary, remarked: "Britain has more people giving out benefits than we have serving in the Army. The first duty of every Government is defence, and the Conservatives will always put our defence first. Defending our country has to come before spending ever more on welfare.

"Labour have allowed for an ever-expanding Civil Service and an ever-growing welfare bill, as our defence goes unfunded. The Conservatives have a fully funded plan to spend 3% of GDP on defence by cutting the welfare bill and reprioritising spending elsewhere."

A government proposal to restrict access to disability benefits was shelved last year after backbench Labour MPs staged a rebellion. Work and pensions minister Sir Stephen Timms is conducting a review into alternative methods of reducing the benefits bill, though Prime Minister Andy Burnham has declined to commit to cost reductions before the next election.

Chancellor John Healey has pledged that the UK will allocate 3% of GDP to defence by 2030, however the specifics of how this target will be achieved won't be revealed until next year.

Next week, former Labour cabinet minister Alan Milburn will release the concluding report from his investigation into nearly a million young people not in education, employment or training, known as NEETs. The report is anticipated to feature reforms making benefit claims more difficult, while potentially increasing expenditure on education, training and mental health services.
