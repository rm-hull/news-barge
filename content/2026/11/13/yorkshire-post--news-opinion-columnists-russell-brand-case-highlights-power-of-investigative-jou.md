# Russell Brand case highlights power of investigative journalism: Daxa Patel

## Investigative journalism matters more than ever because, but for the Channel 4 documentary, a famous celebrity might never have been held to account. As a lawyer, what disturbs me most is not simply the criminal conduct of Russell Brand that has now been exposed, but the manner in which those bringing allegations were treated throughout the process.

The lines of attack deployed against victims, and the attempts to undermine their credibility, seemed at times less like robust legal representation and more like a profound absence of compassion and respect.

I doubt those women would ever have chosen to endure public scrutiny, cross-examination and personal attacks were it not for a determination to be heard. Speaking out about sexual abuse is rarely easy. Doing so against a wealthy and influential public figure requires extraordinary courage.

People in positions of power, whether celebrities or otherwise, can easily overlook the influence they wield. References in the judge's remarks to "laddish" behaviour, and the willingness of others to overlook or excuse what was taking place, reveal a culture in which unacceptable conduct becomes normalised. Silence is often mistaken for acceptance. It should never be.

Why is it that victims of abuse are so often left to fight for decades while those accused continue to enjoy wealth, admiration, and public support? The celebrity at the centre of this case continued to attract followers, endorsements, and attention long after serious concerns had been raised. The spectacle surrounding the trial itself was striking. Walking into court each day dressed as though attending a publicity event seemed to reinforce a sense of detachment from the gravity of the proceedings rather than demonstrating the personal transformation that supporters claimed had occurred in recent years.

Collectively, our tolerance of laddish behaviour and performative bravado represents a wider societal failure. Those who profit from promoting misogyny, sexual conquest and demeaning attitudes towards women cannot wash their hands of responsibility when such behaviour escalates. Abuse of power is not confined to those convicted. It flourishes in environments where influence is celebrated but accountability is absent.

Even now, there are voices on social media portraying this case as some form of establishment conspiracy or "stitch up". Such reactions should concern us all. They demonstrate how some are prepared to dismiss the experiences of victims in favour of preserving the reputation of someone they admire.

The reality is that abuse of power exists in every corner of society. Professional organisations, public institutions, and private workplaces are not immune. Too often warnings are ignored, concerns minimised and inappropriate behaviour excused as the individual involved is successful, profitable, or popular. Looking the other way remains one of the greatest enablers of misconduct.

A sentence of 10 years and six months reflects the legal consequences of criminal wrongdoing which will be reduced in accordance with the law. Yet the victims have already served a far longer sentence. They have carried the emotional burden of these experiences for decades. The effects of abuse stretch well beyond the immediate victim. They touch families, shape relationships, and leave scars that can endure for a lifetime. What should give us hope, however, is the courage shown by those women who refused to remain silent. In choosing to speak out, they have done more than secure justice for themselves. They have made it possible for others living with similar experiences to come forward, seek help and believe they will be heard. That may prove to be one of the most important legacies of this case.

They also remind us of the vital role played by investigative journalists, researchers, producers, and programme editors who work behind the scenes. Their work is often painstaking, unpopular, and risky, requiring months or even years of pursuing difficult stories in the face of legal threats, public criticism, and immense pressure. Yet without their persistence, these abuses of power might never become known. At its best, investigative journalism is not about celebrity, headlines, or ratings. It is about truth, accountability and giving a voice to those who would otherwise go unheard. In doing so, it performs a public service that lies at the heart of a healthy democracy: holding powerful people to account when others are unwilling or afraid to do so.

Justice is not achieved solely in courtrooms, as it begins when a victim finds the courage to speak, when others are prepared to listen and when journalists pursue difficult stories despite pressure, criticism, and risk. If there is any lesson to emerge from this case, it is that truth can still find its way into the light, however long it takes.

The women who came forward deserve our utmost respect as they have opened the doors for others to seek help and be heard. Equally, those who worked tirelessly to uncover the truth deserve our gratitude. Together, they are a reminder that abuse of power should never be beyond scrutiny and that, even after years, justice remains possible.

Daxa Patel is a leadership coach and solicitor.
