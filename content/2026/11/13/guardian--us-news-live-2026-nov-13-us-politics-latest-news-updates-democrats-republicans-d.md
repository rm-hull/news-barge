Bessent hints at discord between treasury and commerce departments over advance chip exports

Scott Bessent, the US treasury secretary, said today that “for months” he has been in favor of putting Megaspeed, the data company whose executive is an alleged Chinese spy, on a trade blacklist to stop Nvidia from exporting advanced chips to Beijing.

However, Bessent said this has been stalled because “commerce [department] has the pen”.

“You will have to ask them about their internal process,” the treasury secretary added in an interview with CNBC on Friday.

Earlier this week, Axios reported that Bessent allegedly confronted Howard Lutnick, the commerce secretary, about his failure to put Megaspeed on the trade blacklist in the White House Situation Room.

However, speaking to CNBC, Bessent refused to comment on the meeting.

“We don’t repeat conversations in the Situation Room,” he said.

Mamdani secures deal with Trump to delay new Snap rules for bodegas and corner stores around the country

Zohran Mamdani, the New York mayor, has struck a deal with Donald Trump to secure a 90-day delay on new rules for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (Snap), so that bodegas and corner stores across the country have time to comply, according to a source familiar.

The US agriculture department implemented the new guidelines, which retailers had to meet by 4 November, and require stores to stock at least seven varieties within each staple food category: dairy, grains, protein, and fruits or vegetables.

Retailers would also have to ensure that they offer perishable items (that require refrigeration after being opened) in three of the staple food categories.

Several advocates were concerned that the new rules would mean those relying on Snap would be unable to afford groceries at their only accessible store. According to the Food Action Research Center, bodegas, convenience stores and small grocery stores comprise roughly 70% of Snap retailers.

According to the source, the reprieve came about through Mamdani’s direct negotiations with the president. The New York mayor also sent a letter to Brooke Rollins, the agriculture secretary, earlier this month to ask requesting an extension. More than 100 bodega owners have reached out to Mamdani’s office with concerns about the new rule. According to the New York Department of City Planning, there are more than 10,000 bodegas and corner stores across the five boroughs.

Analysis: Chuck Schumer’s challenger for party control underscores Democrats’ ideological rift

George Chidi

US senator Chris Van Hollen’s challenge to Chuck Schumer’s leadership of Senate Democrats presents an ideological and biographical contrast to the party as it takes control of Congress in two months.

Though only about eight years younger than Schumer, Van Hollen is seen as a more progressive senator who has drawn attention for his opposition to the Trump administration’s abusive immigration practices – visiting his constituent Kilmar Ábrego García after an unlawful deportation to El Salvador – as well as opposition to federal corruption and US support for the Israeli government’s war in Gaza.

Long a supporter of a two-state solution, Van Hollen visited the Rafah crossing between Egypt and Gaza in January 2024 to observe aid shipments, declaring the assistance process “broken”. He subsequently voted for a resolution proposed by US senator Bernie Sanders to apply the human rights provisions of the Foreign Assistance Act to US military aid to Israel. The resolution failed 72-11, with most Democrats also opposing the measure.

Van Hollen authored a report in November 2025 which described the war as a “plan to ethnically cleanse Gaza of Palestinians”, and that “America is complicit.” He has worked to end arms shipments to Israel and opposes American security agreements with Israel in the National Defense Authorization Act.

Van Hollen has pursued legislation to ban federal officials from owning or promoting cryptocurrency, pointedly highlighting the president’s billion-dollar gains.

US Senate race in Alaska on knife's edge as Democrats eye ninth pick-up

The votes are still being counted in the nail-biting US Senate race in Alaska, where Democrat Mary Peltola, a former congresswoman, is leading the Republican incumbent, Dan Sullivan, by 5,800 votes.

As of now, 88% of the ballots have been counted. But since Alaska uses a ranked-choice voting system, if no candidate reaches 50% of the vote, the weakest of the four on the ballot will be eliminated and their votes will be redistributed to their next choice and then the overall number will be recounted.

The bright blue map that emerged on election night in the US was unthinkable two years ago. When Donald Trump won the 2024 presidential election, many Democratic voters curled up in bed and doomscrolled, fatigued and fearful about the direction of the country. Any desire to fight back was dampened by the enormity of the loss.

But the mood gradually shifted over the following two years, culminating in a generational shift on election day: Democrats flipped five governors’ seats and took control of the House and Senate by wide margins. Voters were angry about the rising cost of living, the war in Iran, masked immigration agents in their neighborhoods and datacenters rising in their backyards.

But anger alone didn’t boost turnout. That required years of building coalitions, developing local campaigns and expanding movement infrastructure that turned frustration into organized action – and, eventually, votes.

Now, these same organizers are determining where to go next. The goal was never about any one party or politician. To continue to build power, movements must diversify their tactics, make clear demands, put pressure on the newly elected and keep building community-based networks, movement experts said. And they must be unrelenting.

“Elections themselves don’t achieve what it is that people want,” said Keeanga-Yamahtta Taylor, an African American studies professor at Princeton University and the author of From #BlackLivesMatter to Black Liberation. “They set the table for the possibility of change.”
