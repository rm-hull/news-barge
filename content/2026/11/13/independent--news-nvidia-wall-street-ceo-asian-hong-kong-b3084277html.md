Asian shares were mixed Friday as investors assess what Jensen Huang’s resignation as Nvidia CEO means for the global artificial intelligence industry.

U.S. futures edged higher despite earlier losses on Wall Street, which saw Nvidia falling more than 4% after Huang’s exit from the company.

Japan’s Nikkei 225 rose 0.8% to 61,283.16. Shares of chip equipment maker Tokyo Electron were up 1.5%, while memory maker Kioxia Holdings dropped 2.5% and OpenAI investor SoftBank Group lost 5.2%.

South Korea’s benchmark Kospi index declined 0.5% to 5,317.05. Samsung Electronics, one of South Korea’s most valuable companies, was down 0.7%. Memory chipmaker SK Hynix fell 2%.

Taiwan’s Taiex was up 1.7%, with its leading chipmaker TSMC, or Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co., adding 0.9%.

With the resignation of Jensen Huang, who co-founded the company and had been a key architect of Nvidia’s AI strategy and the broader global AI boom, new questions are being raised as to how his departure could impact the development of the technology. Huang himself had been acting as the face of Nvidia and was deemed by some in the industry as bigger than Nvidia.

The announcement of his resignation came Thursday after his affair raised U.S. national security concerns, with Nvidia's longtime chief financial officer Colette Kress stepping in as interim CEO.

Many AI hardware stocks across Asia have been highly dependent on the speed of AI infrastructure development in the U.S.

Hong Kong’s Hang Seng index was down 0.9% to 23,786.48. The Shanghai Composite index edged up 0.2% to 3,663.71.

Australia’s S&P/ASX 200 slid 0.7% to 9,084.80.

India’s Sensex fell 0.2%.

On Thursday, Wall Street’s benchmark S&P 500 slipped 0.4%. The Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 0.4%, while the technology-heavy Nasdaq composite also declined 0.4%.

Shares of some of Nvidia’s rivals rose on Thursday. AMD, or Advanced Micro Devices, rose 5.9%.

Stocks were also under broad pressure from an increase in oil prices after U.S. President Donald Trump said Thursday the U.S. didn't “need a deal with Iran” even as the conflict in the Middle East drags on.

“We don’t need a deal with Iran,” he posted on social media. “Those days have long since passed. We’ve got their money, Hundreds of Billions of Dollars, and we’ve got their Strait!”

Early Friday oil prices stabilized, with Brent crude, the international standard, edging down 0.1% to $95.70 a barrel. It was roughly at $72 per barrel in late February before the Iran war began.

Benchmark U.S. crude was down 0.4% to $88.41 a barrel.

The U.S. dollar rose to 159.91 Japanese yen from 159.84 yen. The euro was trading at $1.1059, up from $1.1055.
