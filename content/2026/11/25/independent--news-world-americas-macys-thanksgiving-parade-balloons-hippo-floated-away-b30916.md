One of the stars of this year's Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade should be easy to spot: a giant blue hippo with a goofy smile.

The giant inflatable is among 100 set to float through the city, weather permitting, for the landmark 100th parade. Craftsmen painstakingly recreated it by hand using archival photographs and video, as the original balloon vanished in 1931.

Alongside its starring role in the store's holiday window displays this year, the long-lost hippo takes its rightful place in a star-studded lineup featuring musical performers such as Megan Moroney, Teddy Swims, Queen Latifah, Keke Palmer, JoJo, and Jimmy Fallon & The Roots.

The festivities will also feature a tribute to Dolly Parton led by Megan Hilty, alongside special appearances by Melissa Joan Hart, Jack McBrayer, Janet McTeer, and Holly Robinson Peete, leading up to the arrival of Santa Claus. In a shift from tradition, nine Broadway productions will merge for a 10-minute medley rather than separate full-length show numbers.

![The Macy's annual Thanksgiving Day parade passes down Broadway in New York, Nov. 27, 1930](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/05/Macy%27s_Thanksgiving_Parade_at_1__72578.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

"It’s all about celebrating the tradition of the parade," says Brendan Kennedy, director of creative production at Macy’s Studios. "It’s got so much weight behind it, and we’re just trying to inflate it with some air and fly it down the street."

The event could face weather challenges, with AccuWeather forecasting rain later in the day and winds ranging between 12 to 25 mph (19 to 40 kph). Under safety rules, large balloons must remain grounded if sustained winds exceed 23 mph (37 kph) or gusts top 34 mph (55 kph). Such conditions have forced the parade balloons to stay on the ground only once before, in 1971.

## Bluey, Mickey Mouse and Underdog

Will Coss, vice president and executive producer of Macy’s Studios, aimed to feature every decade in this centennial edition. The lineup alongside Blue Hippo includes character balloons of Bluey, Petula Pig, Hello Kitty, Snoopy's alter ego Joe Cool, Mickey Mouse, Underdog, and a Minion riding a banana. A 101-foot-long (31-meter-long) Doodlebug caterpillar and 1,000 clowns will also process along the streets.

"The parade every year is a snapshot. It’s a time capsule of that specific year. And, in this case, this is going to be a representation of our larger history," he says.

Launched in 1924, the parade originally served to promote the holiday window displays — which that year featured 27 fairy tales performed by marionettes — at Macy's Herald Square flagship store.

Puppeteer and illustrator Tony Sarg designed those early windows, floats, and character costumes, and Macy's borrowed live animals from the Central Park Zoo for the initial parades. In subsequent years, organizers even brought in live tigers and lions.

![A giant turkey float squeezes between buildings as the 31st annual Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade moves down Broadway near 37th Street in New York, Nov. 28, 1957](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/18/America_25__Objects_Thanksgiving_Turkey_21124.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

By 1927, Macy's phased out the live animals after young spectators grew frightened. Sarg — believed to have drawn inspiration from Indonesian shadow puppets — substituted the real animals with air-filled balloons. The following year, he partnered with Goodyear Tire & Rubber Company to build balloons that could be filled with helium.

"With each year, the balloons got a little bit bigger and more sophisticated. And in many years, they would take a character and then the next year they would repaint the balloon to be another character," says Darin Johnson, founder and CEO of the American Theater for Puppetry Arts.

A dedicated float on Thursday will pay homage to Sarg, who guided the parade's creative direction through 1941 and is widely regarded as the father of modern American puppetry.

## The balloons actually floated away

During the early decades, the helium-filled balloons were not packed up and stored for the next year. Instead, organizers released them into the sky with tagged envelopes promising a finder’s fee to anyone returning them to Macy's.

Blue Hippo was among those set loose 95 years ago and never returned, prompting Kennedy to dig into newspaper archives searching for clues.

His team uncovered black-and-white video footage from 1931 showing the hippo flying past the newly completed Empire State Building, along with accounts indicating it was likely lost over the sea. All archived images were black and white, leaving the actual color open to interpretation.

![Workers inflate the Joe Cool and Woodstock balloon during Macy's Balloon Inflation 2026 on November 25, 2026 in New York City](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/18/2306276197..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

"What we decided was that, of course, it was the color of the sky because how else do you lose a flying hippo?" he says. His team added age spots for the 2026 version: "It’s had some time out there, and we’re really excited to bring it back."

The recreated balloon will also honor Fiona, the beloved Cincinnati Zoo hippo who died this month after ingesting a knit hat. Handlers walking Blue Hippo will wear purple patches showing Fiona’s face inside a heart alongside the words "Celebrating Fiona."

## Building a balloon uses old and new technology

Modern technology shapes today's balloon development, converting initial sketches into 3D digital renderings and physical models using digital printing.

Technology is widely integrated: converted lawn mowers under the floats have given way to modern self-driving electric vehicles, and this year’s "Harry Potter" entry features an animatronic Sorting Hat. Nevertheless, traditional methods endure.

"The art of building a balloon is still very much a sacred art for our Macy’s and Macy’s Studios team. Every piece of fabric is cut and sealed together by hand. Every balloon that you see is assembled, painted, and brought to life by hand," says Coss, who heads a team of 75 full-time staff members.

The parade has marched every year — except from 1942 to 1944 when balloon materials were donated to the World War II effort — inspiring generations of spectators. "These giant balloons turn you into a child," Kennedy says. "I feel like we’re all kind of kids for a day."

For the 100th parade, Johnson will be participating on the street as a handler for a recreation of one of the earliest character balloons, Felix the Cat.

![Inflation workers work with a Buzz Lightyear balloon on the eve of the 100th Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade on Manhattan's Upper West Side in New York City, US, November 25, 2026](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/18/2026-11-25T162752Z_1675551319_RC22VOA2VS86_RTRMADP_3_USA-THANKSGIVING-BALLOONS.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

"It will be a dream come true," he says.

Thursday also marks a milestone anniversary for another Thanksgiving TV tradition: the National Dog Show, turning 25 this year. NBC broadcasts the canine competition immediately following the parade.

The network originally decided in 2002 to broadcast highlights from the Kennel Club of Philadelphia’s annual competition as something of a lark, rebranding it as the National Dog Show. Strong viewer ratings surprised NBC executives, turning the broadcast into a holiday programming staple.

"It’s been quite a 25-year journey, playing a role in establishing a showcase for dogs that has changed the Thanksgiving Day viewing habits of millions of American families," said dog expert David Frei. He co-hosts the telecast alongside actor John O’Hurley, best known for playing the eccentric J. Peterman on "Seinfeld," and NBC Sports analyst Mary Carillo.
