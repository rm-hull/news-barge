Audio from seabirds fitted with mini recording devices and GPS trackers has been used by researchers to show that marine life can record encounters with vessels that are otherwise difficult to track.

Many large vessels are now legally required to carry automatic identification systems broadcasting their identity and location at regular intervals. Although this has allowed interactions between animals and fisheries to be studied at scale, there are enormous gaps in coverage.

In fact, recent research suggests that three-quarters of industrial fishing vessels don’t use the technology, says Jonathan Rutter at the University of Oxford. This is partly because regulations on the types of vessels that are required to share data vary by country. Large satellite ‘blind spots’ also contribute to the problem, he says, and so does subterfuge for the purpose of illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing (IUU).

Rutter says that being caught as bycatch in fishing is one of the greatest risks to seabird populations, but if fishing vessels are only tracked “with conventional methods, such footprints of risk may be strikingly incomplete”.

The team analysed audio collected from devices attached to seven northern gannets (*Morus bassanus*) and found that one third of the audio detections couldn’t be matched with vessel tracking data – mostly off the UK coast.

Overall, 12 per cent of the vessel detections made by the four critically endangered Balearic shearwaters (*Puffinus mauretanicus*) included in the study couldn’t be matched to public data. But given the high vessel density where they were – off the Catalan coast – the researchers estimated that up to 20 per cent of the vessels detected by the four birds weren’t broadcasting their identity.

Tammy Davies at BirdLife International – a global partnership of non-governmental organisations – who wasn’t involved in the study, says the research could “give us a more complete picture of the risks seabirds face at sea” and “ultimately, this could contribute to better assessments of bycatch risk and help identify fisheries and areas where conservation measures are most needed”.

Peter Ejbye-Ernst at the Netherlands Institute for the Study of Crime and Law Enforcement (NSCR), describes the study as “fascinating” from a green criminology point of view, too.

![A northern gannet ](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI_321993699.jpg?w=840)

“What this study is somewhat proposing is that we can also look outside of humans for witnesses too,” he says, which overcomes a key barrier in observing crime at sea.

In terms of its applications for cracking down on IUU, he says: “There’s a fine line between trying to understand something better and document it and then trying to control it and surveil it.”

There are caveats, as the study has a small sample size and records both false-negatives and false-positives. It also gives very little information on the type of activity vessels were carrying out. But, while the research isn’t a solution to the problem of IUU, it is “part of the toolbox” for understanding what is happening at sea, says Ejbye-Ernst.

The study didn’t differentiate between the size of vessels, but Rutter and his colleagues say this would be a good area for future research.

“Animal-borne audio recorders could potentially be used to investigate other ecological interactions and human activities that are difficult to observe directly, particularly in the marine environment,” says team member Ana Bertoldi Carneiro, also at BirdLife International.

Hugo Tagholm at the charity Oceana says it is shocking that so many of the interactions were with unseen vessels, but he is concerned about attaching devices to seabirds on a broader scale to track this.

“Seabirds are already grappling with the harm caused by overfishing and climate breakdown, we don’t want to put them at even greater risk from vessel operators acting illegally,” he says.

“What we need is mandatory remote electronic monitoring across all fishing vessels, clear and effective laws and catch limits to protect and restore our seas, and truly deterrent sanctions for those breaking the law.”
