Foreign professionals moving to Germany and seeking a residence permit for self-employment face a bizarre catch-22: the visa requires adequate health insurance, but the legal duty to support themselves locks them out of the public health system.

This can leave incoming qualified professionals from outside the EU in a tough spot where they are forced to pay for extremely expensive private insurance for themselves and their family, or else potentially give up on their dream of moving to the country altogether.

Santosh Pandey, an Indian national and solicitor of England and Wales, discovered of this maddening barrier to entry while making his own preparations to establish himself and his business in Germany. He has since directly petitioned the German government to address the issue, and is waiting for a response.

Pandey told The Local how the challenge nearly derailed his plans to move to Germany, and detailed the only viable solution he found for himself.

**Germany has a 'shortage of workers its prepared to insure'**

Pandey's efforts to move to Germany began with three requests.

In order to establish his legal practice in Germany advising on English law, he contacted regional German bar associations to seek admission as a foreign practitioner.

Pandey said that he received immediate replies from both the Munich and Düsseldorf bar associations which included application forms and instructions for how to apply.

"The profession, in other words, said yes before lunch," Pandey told The Local in an email.

But he knew that to apply for a residence permit for self-employment he'd also need to secure German health insurance. So he simultaneously wrote a letter to the country's largest public health insurance provider, Techniker Krankenkasse (TK), to request to enrol. This is where the trouble began.

In its response to Pandey, TK rejected his request for public health insurance and noted that for people coming from non-European countries or who are privately insured, enrolling in a statutory coverage in Germany is only possible if they get insurance through regular employment in Germany, or through unemployment insurance (*Arbeitslosengeld*) or as an academic student under the age of 30.

"Germany does not have a shortage of skilled workers. It has a shortage of skilled workers it is prepared to insure," Pandey added.

**A catch-22 in Germany's Social Code**

As Pandey would come to understand, he had stumbled on a sort of bureaucratic catch-22 in Germany's Social Code (SGB V), which effectively blocks qualified professionals from easily or affordably joining the German insurance system.

To receive a visa for self-employment under the Residence Act, an applicant must demonstrate adequate health cover without relying on public funds. For this statutory health insurance is explicitly recognised as adequate by definition.

Yet conversely, provisions in Germany's Social Code prevent non-EU nationals from entering statutory insurance if their visa requires them to prove that they are financially independent (as a visa for self-employment does).

In effect, Germany's self-employment visa requires health insurance that meets statutory standards, but the nature of the visa also prevents many applicants for gaining statutory coverage.

In Pandey's words: "The permit requires insurance; the requirement itself bars the public insurer."

If he were coming to Germany as an employee, or as someone receiving benefits he could enter public insurance. "Arriving as someone who pays for myself, I am out," he said.

**Private insurance at a premium**

Having adequate health insurance is mandatory for everyone living in Germany. So for those who cannot access public health insurance coverage, the only alternative is private insurance.

Yet here too many individuals are turned away, especially those with pre-existing or chronic health conditions.

**EXPLAINED: Can you switch from private to public health insurance in Germany?**

The one option that is open to everyone is the state-regulated basic tariff (*Basistarif*), which private insurers are legally mandated to offer without medical exclusions.

But this tariff is charged at the statutory maximum rate, regardless of actual earnings — costing up to €1,226.44 per month in 2026 for health and long-term care.

Pandey, who plans to move to Germany with his wife, also needs to ensure adequate coverage for her. Currently, his spouse would be freely insured as his dependent under the public system. But if he were insured via the *Basistarif* this would cost an additional €1,226.44 per month – putting the couple's total monthly health insurance bill over €2,500.

Complicating the picture even further, Santosh notes that brokers he contacted confirmed that private insurers would not process his application until he was registered in Germany.

"I am asked to buy something nobody will sell me yet," Pandey said.

The Local contacted the Federal Ministry of the Interior (BMI) and the Federal Ministry of Health (BMG) to ask if the authorities were aware of the issue. The BMI did not immediately respond.

A spokesperson for the Health Ministry confirmed simply that "as soon as the residence or habitual legal abode is in Germany, there is an opportunity to access private health insurance or – if applicable – statutory health insurance".

**The end-all solution: An edit to one sentence of legal code**

Unwilling to give up on his plan to move to Germany, Pandey contacted numerous authorities, including the Ministry of Labour, and even went as far as petitioning the health and labour committees in the Bundestag.

Pandey told The Local that the problem could be fixed with a "three word amendment" to one line in Germany's Social Code.

"Parliament already knows about this," Pandey said, adding that the Bundestag addressed the exact dilemma in 2023, but only for non-EU academic researchers.

That amendment allowed incoming academics to voluntarily join the statutory health system within three months of arrival, waiving any prior insurance history.

This was also confirmed to The Local by a spokesperson for the Ministry of Health, who added that "an expansion of this right is not planned" and noted that researchers "are generally only in Germany for a limited time" and therefore "constitute a different group of people than self-employed individuals".

Pandey argues that extending the same provision to self-employed professionals would be a net positive for immigrants, the insurance system and immigration authorities alike: "The [insurance] funds gain members who pay in full. The visa officer gains a document he can file."

"The *Basistarif* returns to the role it was designed for, as a safety net rather than a toll gate," he added.

As of November 9th, Pandey said he filed his suggested amendment as a formal petition, which the Bundestag must now examine and answer in writing.

**Possible workarounds**

In the meantime, Pandey has found another solution for himself, that basically amounts to living in the United Kingdom for at least one year on a public insurance plan there.

This works because people coming from other public insurance systems in Europe are allowed to transfer into the German system on arrival.

TK confirmed to Pandey in writing that if he were to spend 12 months on NHS insurance in the UK, he could then access statutory coverage on arrival in Germany.

Fortunately for Pandey, who is already entitled to work in England and Wales, this option is open to him. For many other non-EU nationals, however, moving to England may not be an option.

"The lawyer coming from London gets in, and the same lawyer coming from Delhi does not," Pandey said.
