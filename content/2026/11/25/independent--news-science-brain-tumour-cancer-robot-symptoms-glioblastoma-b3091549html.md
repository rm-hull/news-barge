A minuscule robot, small enough to travel through the bloodstream, has been created by scientists to carry medicines to the brain.

The nanorobotic system, called a BrainBot, has been shown to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB), the protective layer of cells that keeps infections, germs and other harmful cells out of the brain. The same barrier can also sometimes prevent medicines from reaching the organ.

Experts said that this is one of the “biggest challenges in treating brain diseases”.

The researchers behind the work found that the miniature bots can deliver five times more medication into the brain than would usually be expected.

Their first assessment focused on whether the technology could help treat brain tumours, specifically a type of brain cancer called glioblastoma.

But they say it could one day be used to treat other neurological conditions such as Parkinson's disease.

![The technology could one day be used to treat other neurological conditions such as Parkinson’s disease](https://static.independent.co.uk/2025/10/01/13/01121731-f91c29b3-61e2-4034-8503-721d205ed7d6.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The bots have been described as balloon-like in structure and are injected into the bloodstream.

Specialised DNA molecules called aptamers help the BrainBots recognise and bind to cells lining the BBB.

Near-infrared light directed to the brain helps the bots use “light-activated propulsion” to transport across the barrier.

The cells ferry the BrainBots into the brain, where they can release the “payload” of medication to help treat brain tumours.

The bots are made of biocompatible and biodegradable materials and are 250 nanometres in size, or hundreds of times smaller than the width of a human hair.

Experts from the California Institute of Technology (Caltech) describe in the journal Science Robotics how BrainBot is “designed to actively traverse the BBB through on-demand propulsion coupled with biological targeting”.

The approach has been demonstrated in mice, with hopes for trials in humans in the future.

The authors describe how BrainBots loaded with a type of chemotherapy were able to achieve “enhanced brain delivery” in mice with glioblastoma.

This in turn suppressed tumour growth and can “markedly reduce systemic toxicity” linked to chemotherapy compared with usual chemotherapy administration, they said.

## Symptoms of a brain tumour

NHS

The symptoms of a brain tumour vary depending on the exact part of the brain affected.

Common symptoms include:

* headaches
* seizures (fits)
* persistently feeling sick (nausea), being sick (vomiting) and drowsiness
* mental or behavioural changes, such as memory problems or changes in personality
* progressive weakness or paralysis on one side of the body
* vision or speech problems

Wei Gao, professor of medical engineering at Caltech, said: “We showed that our BrainBots can carry medication through the bloodstream and help deliver it across the blood–brain barrier, one of the biggest challenges in treating brain diseases.

“Because the BrainBots are so small and the blood circulation is so fast, we initially were not sure how much a method based on nanoscale propulsion would increase the passage of drugs through the BBB; we are thrilled to see it worked so well.

“Our new nanorobot is not limited to brain delivery, but we are trying to address one of the biggest problems in medicine— crossing the blood–brain barrier — as a starting point.

“We targeted cancer, but there are a lot of neurological diseases, such as Parkinson’s, for example, that we hope could benefit from this technology.”

Commenting, Dr Karen Noble, director of research and policy at the charity Brain Tumour Research, said: “The blood-brain barrier prevents many potentially effective drugs from reaching brain tumours and finding ways to overcome this could transform treatment and improve outcomes.

“But scientific progress requires sustained investment.

“That’s why we continue to call for an increase in the national investment in brain tumours to at least £45 million a year to ensure that Government can meet its ambitious survival targets set out in the National Cancer Plan.”

Roslyn Bill, professor of biotechnology at Aston University, said: “This approach combines biological recognition with active propulsion to help transport drugs across the blood-brain barrier, harnessing biology and engineering to overcome one of the major limitations of drug delivery.

“The results in mice are encouraging, and although there is still a considerable way to go before we know whether this approach will translate into patients, it illustrates the enormous potential of interdisciplinary science.”
