## Amid lingering suspicion, Brussels wants to get a "fuller picture" of Irish-made alumina sales to Russia through a new reporting mechanism.

The European Union is seeking additional clarity on sales of Irish-made alumina to Russia, as suspicion remains about their possible link with Moscow's war machine.

Traded as a white powder, alumina is the raw material used to make aluminium, a lightweight metal often found on the battlefield in Ukraine.

Despite public pressure from Kyiv and the Baltic countries, Brussels did not include an export ban on alumina in **the 22nd sanctions package**, still under negotiation.

Instead, the European Commission has proposed a reporting mechanism to improve surveillance on alumina shipments bound to Russia.

"The idea of the mechanism is that we get a fuller picture of what the trade flows are," Siobhan McGarry, the Commission's sanctions spokesperson, said on Wednesday in response to a question from Euronews. "It's not clear to us."

The mechanism will compel member states to report every three months on the amount, value, transport method, date and place of loading and discharge, contract type, importer and, crucially, final destination of alumina sold to Russia.

When asked whether the Commission reserved the right to sanction these exports if the trade data raised alarm, McGarry urged caution.

"The purpose of this reporting mechanism is to identify just that: where is this alumina ending up? And then we will look at what further measures we may or may not need to take," she said.

"We're not quite there yet."

The issue came to prominence earlier this year after a media consortium published an investigation tracing the links between Aughinish Alumina, a sprawling refinery in western Ireland, and Russia's military complex.

It detailed how Irish-made alumina is being sent to Russian smelters owned by the plant's parent company, United Company Rusal, which in turn sells the metal to a trader that supplies aluminium to sanctioned defence manufacturers.

The weapons churned out by these manufacturers have been deployed to kill Ukrainian civilians and bombard civilian infrastructure, the investigation said.

Separately, Swedish authorities concluded that Rusal remained under the effective control of Oleg Deripaska, a sanctioned Russian oligarch with close ties to the Kremlin.

The revelations **triggered a scandal** in Ireland, just as the country was prepared to take over the EU Council's six-month presidency.

Ukraine's top leadership asked Dublin to cease alumina sales to Russia immediately. The Irish government warned that sanctions could decimate hundreds of local jobs and disrupt a strategic supply chain for Europe. As the public outcry continued, Dublin launched a probe to corroborate the media claims.

The probe **ended up in a grey area**: it found "no evidence" to either confirm or refute the relation between Irish-made alumina and Russian weapons. The inconclusive findings were then passed on to Brussels "for its consideration".

At the same time, Aughinish said it had taken the allegations with "the utmost seriousness" and promised to establish a new framework of "independent, third-party verification" to enhance surveillance of alumina flows into Russian smelters.

Still, doubts continued to linger.

On Wednesday, the Commission made it clear that further answers were needed.

"One thing that was clear from what we saw over the summer when it came to Aughinish is that no one had the complete picture. And so this is an opportunity for member states and for the Commission to see what's taking place here," McGarry said.

"Alumina is a critical raw material, and we do have industries in Europe that rely on alumina, so we need to see whether we have the right balance."

In a statement, Ireland's Department of Enterprise, Tourism and Employment said it was "aware" of the reporting mechanism but declined to comment until final adoption.

"As Presidency, we stand ready to work with the Commission and all member states to secure the adoption of an ambitious package," a spokesperson said.

Aughinish did not immediately reply to a request for comment.

On Tuesday, Vladyslav Vlasiuk, Ukraine's sanctions commissioner, told Euronews **he was "disappointed"** that an export ban on alumina had not been proposed, calling the decision a "clear example" of political interests obstructing a "rightful measure".

Brussels aims to adopt the new sanctions before the winter break.
