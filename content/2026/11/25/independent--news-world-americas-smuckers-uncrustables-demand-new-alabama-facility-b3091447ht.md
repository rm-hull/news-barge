Business is booming for Uncrustables, but J.M. Smucker has found itself in a jam trying to keep up.

The Ohio-based company says demand is outpacing production, making it harder to keep the popular sandwiches on freezer shelves.

Uncrustables recently became the second-biggest brand in the entire freezer aisle over a 13-week period, CEO Mark Smucker said Tuesday. Sales jumped 15 percent in the latest quarter, with the brand also hitting record highs for quarterly sales and production.

But all that popularity has created a problem: Smucker can't make the sandwiches fast enough.

"Demand is exceeding our available supply and we are now capacity constrained," Smucker said in the company’s latest earnings update.

![Smucker is expanding its Alabama factory, with the next phase expected to open in late fiscal 2027 to boost Uncrustables production](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/13/2167836416..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

To help keep up, Smucker is expanding its manufacturing facility in McCalla, Alabama. The second phase of the plant is expected to open toward the end of fiscal 2027.

The first phase of the Alabama facility opened in November 2024. It's the third dedicated Uncrustables bakery Smucker has opened, following its original 2013 location in Scottsville, Kentucky, and another in Longmont, Colorado, in 2019. At 900,000 square feet, the Alabama plant is also the largest of the three.

Uncrustables got their start in 1996 when two friends began making the crustless sandwiches in Fergus Falls, Minnesota. Smucker bought the business in 1998, then spent years refining the recipe and figuring out how to produce the sandwiches on a much larger scale. They hit grocery store shelves in 2000 and have since become a lunchbox favorite.

Now, the company is looking to keep shoppers interested with protein-packed options and limited-time flavors. Despite already bringing in more than $1 billion a year, Smucker says fewer than half of consumers have tried Uncrustables, leaving plenty of room for the brand to grow.

“We are well positioned to drive continued growth across more households and occasions,” Smucker said.

Uncrustables' popularity has also inspired copycats. In October 2025, Smucker sued Trader Joe's over its own frozen peanut butter and strawberry jam sandwiches, claiming the grocery chain had copied the brand's signature look.

The lawsuit cited similarities including the round shape, crimped edges and blue packaging, as well as a picture of a sandwich with a bite taken out of it.

Smucker claimed the look led shoppers to think the products were connected to Uncrustables, and accused Trader Joe's of trademark infringement.
