Mojave the tortoise, the oldest animal at San Francisco Zoo and Gardens, has died at the age of 79.

​The desert reptile was one of the most well known animals at the 100-acre zoo, where he lived for almost 30 years.

​Zoo officials say that they chose to euthanize Mojave on November 19, after a CT scan carried out earlier this year determined that he had developed an unspecified spinal condition. In the months following the scan, Mojave’s condition was closely monitored.

​“When he began showing signs that the condition was impacting his quality of life, we made the very difficult decision to say goodbye,” officials wrote in a statement. “While we will deeply miss Mojave, we could not be more grateful for the impact he made here at the ARC and across the Zoo.”

​Bridget Israel, an assistant curator at the conservation center where Mojave lived, revealed more about the tortoise’s health struggle to SFGATE. She said that teams gathered once or twice a month to assess Mojave’s condition, but that his health worsened in the week leading up to his death.

![Mojave (pictured), a desert tortoise, has died at San Francisco Zoo at the age of 79](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/12/39/Screenshot-2026-11-25-at-12-30-19.png?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&trim=139%2C3%2C24%2C0&auto=webp)

​“His appetite and mobility acutely decreased, and he wasn’t giving us much resistance on the manipulation of his limbs, which was a clear indication to us that his condition had progressed past the point of management,” Israel said.

​Mojave resided at the Koret Animal Rescue and Conservation Center and lived what the zoo describes as a “prolific life” serving as an “ambassador” for the embattled desert tortoise species - which is the official state reptile for both California and Nevada.

However, despite their popularity, desert tortoise populations are in jeopardy and are listed as threatened under the U.S. Endangered Species Act. The California Fish and Game Commission also marked the reptiles as being endangered in 2024.

​Desert tortoises are a keystone species - a phrase noting that an organism keeps ecosystems in balance through behaviors such as controlling prey populations or modifying natural habitats.

![The tortoise was euthanized following a CT scan earlier this year, which detected that he had a spinal condition](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/12/18/Screenshot-2026-11-25-at-12-31-06.png?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&trim=176%2C2%2C0%2C0&auto=webp)

Mojave’s death comes just months after his 79th birthday, which San Francisco Zoo says was celebrated on May 17. He was treated to a selection of strawberries, dandelion flowers and vegetables arranged to spell out “Happy Birthday.”

​At the time, he was the second oldest desert tortoise living under human care in California. The species typically lives longer in captivity than in the wild, where tortoises generally live for between 30 and 50 years, according to the International Fund for Animal Welfare.

​In death, Mojave has been succeeded as the zoo’s oldest animal by 61-year-old Cactus, another tortoise, San Francisco Zoo registrar Ingrid Russell told SFGATE.
