## The European Commission wants to keep monitoring Montenegro after it joins the EU, extending a new anti-backsliding tool in the draft accession treaty alongside a 10-year probation period, despite some member states questioning its added value.

The European Commission proposed extending a new tool, designed to prevent candidates from backsliding on their commitments as they prepare to join, so that it continues to apply even after Montenegro joins the EU.

Montenegro, the candidate furthest along on the road to membership, is the testing ground for tougher safeguards meant to avoid another Viktor Orbán, the Hungarian politician whose government has repeatedly clashed with Brussels over the rule of law.

Last week, member states broadly endorsed a draft position on the new safeguards drawn up by Ireland, which holds the rotating presidency of the EU Council. That cleared the way to begin work on Montenegro's accession treaty.

On that basis, the Commission tabled a first draft of the relevant treaty provisions, known as Chapter 35, which national representatives discussed on Tuesday. The text contains a significant novelty: a post-accession monitoring mechanism.

Under the original safeguards proposal, the Commission would monitor a country in the gap between the end of accession talks and its actual entry into the bloc. The aim is to stop newcomers from slowing down or reversing reforms once they have secured a political deal to close negotiations but are not yet members.

The Commission would check that the country is implementing and upholding all the commitments it made during the talks, report regularly to the European Parliament and the Council, and propose corrective action where necessary. As a last resort, it could even postpone the country's entry.

During the discussions, several member states favourable to stronger safeguards, such as Germany, France and the Netherlands, suggested extending this monitoring beyond accession. The Irish presidency left the idea out of its draft position but kept the question open.

Other countries, most notably members of the Friends of the Western Balkans group, questioned the added value, as EU member states are already scrutinised through instruments such as the rule of law reports and infringement proceedings.

They asked how the monitoring would work, which areas would need extra attention after accession and how to avoid duplicating existing tools.

The Commission, by contrast, considers it natural that the new safeguards come with monitoring of how they are applied. Still, it did not put forth such a mechanism in its original proposal.

"In light of the period during which certain safeguard clauses will remain available after accession, the EU considers that a provision on post-accession monitoring should also be included in the Accession Treaty," reads the draft text, seen by Euronews.

Particularly relevant is the so-called institutional safeguard. It would place a newcomer on a sort of probation, during which violations of EU values and rules would be swiftly addressed, including through the suspension of voting rights for the most serious offences.

The Commission's draft confirms the shortening of the probation period from 15 to 10 years, extendable by five more, as Ireland proposed. But it did not follow the Irish compromise on how the measure would be decided.

The original proposal relied on reverse qualified majority voting: a Commission proposal to trigger the safeguard would pass unless a qualified majority of member states voted against it.

The Friends of the Western Balkans pushed back, arguing this gives the Commission too much power. As a compromise, Ireland proposed ordinary qualified majority voting, under which countries would have to approve the trigger rather than block it.

The Commission, however, put reverse qualified majority voting back on the table. The Irish presidency will now take over the discussion on Chapter 35 to seek a compromise.

"I am not sure the Commission draft helped the discussion much," an EU diplomat told Euronews, asking to remain anonymous. "There is a risk that the member states will form trenches, but we need to proceed with Montenegro's accession treaty."
