# Global ocean iodine won't offset future air pollution, study warns

Posted on Wednesday 25 November 2026

![Arctic ice melting](https://www.york.ac.uk/media/news-and-events/pressreleases/2026/arctic-1000.jpg)

The world’s oceans will not play the role of scrubbing harmful ozone pollution from the atmosphere as the planet warms in the way that was expected, new research suggests.

Scientists had previously assumed that rising emissions of iodine gas from seawater would increasingly help break down ground-level ozone - a toxic air pollutant and greenhouse gas - over the coming century.

A study by scientists at the University of York, in collaboration with the University of East Anglia, suggests this will not be the case, and that iodine's global air-cleaning and cooling effects will actually stall, and weaken slightly, by the year 2100.

Whilst the global iodine emissions stabilise, the study showed the opposite for the Arctic ocean, where iodine emissions are predicted to surge by 143% by the end of the century.

### **Climate models**

When ozone in the lower atmosphere reacts with iodide in seawater, it releases iodine gas into the air. Once airborne, iodine destroys ozone and helps form tiny aerosol particles that seed clouds and reflect sunlight.

Using updated climate models under a high-emissions scenario, researchers found that iodine currently accounts for nearly 11% of global ozone destruction, which is slightly higher than earlier estimates.

Scientists projected that iodine emissions would surge over the century and continue to break down these harmful ozone gases further, but the new simulations show global iodine emissions will dip by 3% by 2100, nudging its share of ozone destruction down to 10.2%.

### **Surface pollution**

Professor Lucy Carpenter, from the University of York’s Wolfson Atmospheric Chemistry Laboratories, said: “Warmer sea temperatures alter solubility, diffusion and chemistry at the ocean’s surface, slowing down the release of iodine into the air.

“Losing this anticipated surge is not good news for air quality and climate efforts, because it would have been a helpful buffer to offset some of the damage to the atmosphere caused by human activity.”

The impact of the findings could mean more surface pollution in the future which can damage human health, harm crops and trap heat. Iodine breaks down the pollutants, but with less of it around than expected, more of the harmful ozone will remain in the lower atmosphere.

It also means that with fewer iodine particles, there is less potential for cloud formation that can reflect sunlight back into space, and so scientists predict the climate could warm slightly faster than previously thought.

### **Arctic changes**

Professor Mat Evans, from the University York’s Wolfson Atmospheric Chemistry Laboratories, said: "We thought a warming world would trigger more iodine, which would help create brighter, denser clouds that bounce extra sunlight back into space, but if we now don’t have this surge we lose some of this natural cooling system."

The researchers note, however, that whilst ozone near the ground is harmful, the ozone layer higher up protects the Earth from ultraviolet radiation, and so stable iodine emissions can reduce the risk of extra iodine being lofted upward and depleting the protective ozone layer.

The study, published in the journal *Science Advances,* also showed changes in the Arctic, where melting sea ice is altering how the ocean and atmosphere interact.  As sea ice retreats, vast areas of open seawater are exposed to the air for the first time, resulting in Arctic ocean iodine emissions increasing by 143%.

Dr Ryan Pound, who completed the work as part of his postdoctoral studies at the University of York, and now at the University of Reading, said: “This spike is projected to strip away a significant amount of springtime surface ozone across the Arctic Circle, which can help clear local air pollution, but with the risk of triggering unpredictable cloud cover and heat-trapping over an already fragile polar region.

“These projections, however, also carry uncertainties, particularly regarding what happens to iodine once it lands on Arctic snow and ice, and so it is important that future climate models capture ocean-ice interactions to more accurately predict both regional air quality and how clouds form over the planet.”
