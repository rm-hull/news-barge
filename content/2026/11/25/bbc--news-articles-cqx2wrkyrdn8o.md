The fire was one of Hong Kong's deadliest in decades

**It was a sunny Wednesday afternoon on 26 November last year, and Mrs Lau was attending a dance class at an activity centre close to her home.**

At around 14:45 she heard the faint sounds of fire engine sirens - but didn't think much of it.

It was only a while later, when she started receiving calls from a friend, that she found out that Wang Fuk Court - the eight-block apartment complex she had called home for the past 40 years - was ablaze. And her husband - a wheelchair user - was trapped inside their home.

"I grabbed my things quickly and ran down without even putting my shoes on properly," Mrs Lau, who wanted to be identified only by her surname, told the BBC.

When she got there, the building had been cordoned off by police.

She called her 74-year-old husband but he told her not to worry - he hadn't heard an alarm so it was probably a small fire, he said.

Mrs Lau could only watch helplessly as the blaze tore through Wang Fuk Court at a peak speed of about 10m (33ft) per second - an "unprecedented" rate, a final report released by an independent committee in relation to the deadly blaze that killed 168 people found.

It was a tragedy that was entirely preventable but happened as a result of a series of human errors, fire safety failures, alleged fraud and suspected corruption, said the report.

Lau Man Kwong took care of his grandson before his death (blurred at source)

"It was ferocious… I saw the fire jump [from one building to another] - then a huge fireball burst. It was truly hell on Earth. How could anyone possibly escape?" said Mrs Lau.

She tried calling her husband again. This time, there was no answer.

The body of Lau Man Kwong was eventually found by rescuers - he had locked himself inside the toilet.

"He was wearing his slippers, sitting on the toilet and clutching his identity card," said his wife, who added that she escaped death "by the narrowest margin".

"I would have held onto my husband and died with him.

"He was such a good person. I just don't understand why he had to die in such a terrible way."

Mr Lau and his wife moved into Wang Fuk Court in 1983 - the year the building was completed.

She recalls an idyllic life - a family with three children who were able to grow up close to the sea and spend weekends playing at a nearby park.

"All three of my children adored my husband… He could be very stern, and I was the only woman who could make him laugh."

## No way out

In 2024, mandated renovations began in Wang Fuk Court.

In Hong Kong, all buildings over 30 years old have to undergo an inspection - and any necessary renovations as a result.

Like many building complexes in Hong Kong, Wang Fuk Court had a management committee that oversaw things like building repairs. As many as 57 construction companies submitted bids. But the company that won, Prestige Construction and Engineering (PC&E), submitted the most expensive bid at HK$336m ($42.8m; £32.4m).

Residents, many of whom were over 65, had to bear the cost. The hefty bills triggered protests but eventually, the contract was signed. But once the renovations began in July 2024, residents were unhappy.

Among the complaints was that workers constantly smoked on site, dropping cigarette butts everywhere.

The 1,154 page report suggested the fire was caused by a lit cigarette.

There were also complaints about most of Wang Fuk Court's eight buildings having their windows taped over with foam boards to stop debris falling in - blocking daylight or any sort of view at all, for months.

All eight buildings were also covered in a protective mesh netting and bamboo scaffolding.

The report also found that after typhoons damaged the original scaffolding nets in 2025, cheaper non-fire-retardant replacements were chosen - something that was done as a "cost-saving measure at the expense of the safety of the residents".

Residents had filed nearly 140 complaints to six government departments, including the fire services and the buildings departments, in and after 2024, but these were largely ignored, the report went on to say.

It also revealed that a staff member of the estate's property manager, ISS, had turned off the power supply to the fire alarms in all eight buildings - which is why no alarms rang on the day itself.

ISS later told the BBC it had "reviewed and reinforced its procedures [and] staff training" in Hong Kong, adding that it takes the findings of the report "very seriously".

But for those who did manage to flee their homes - the emergency staircase turned out to be another death trap. Windows were removed from some fire escape stairwells to create temporary openings for workers - letting in smoke and cutting off vital escape routes.

Dozens, including Christy's husband, died in such stairwells.

![On the right there is a bespectacled woman looks far into the distance while holding a boy. A young girl stands besides them. Only the backs of the children can be seen. ](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/438a/live/3b9f9890-e8ac-11f1-869a-f96fd5660210.jpg)

Christy's daughter celebrated her ninth birthday the day before the fire

Christy, who wanted to be identified only by her first name, says her husband was at home alone when the fire broke out. She only found out about it when her friends sent her messages about it. By the time she made it to the apartment complex, it was already cordoned off.

She found out later that he had died of smoke inhalation in the stairwell, something she says that is at least of some consolation to her.

"My husband's body would have been burnt and he would have been in more pain if he was at home. At the very least… his suffering was minimal."

Christy says her family had just celebrated their eldest child's ninth birthday the day before - they had saved some leftover cake in the fridge to eat the next day. Their father never got to eat it.

Both children have been deeply affected by the loss of their father.

Her youngest child, a seven-year-old boy, had "covered his ears and yelled" when he was first told what had happened to his father.

He now often comforts his mother, saying he will take care of her as he is the only man in the family.

"He hides his emotions. A seven-year-old child should not behave this way, and I feel heartbroken," says Christy, who has herself been diagnosed with PTSD and depression.

Her daughter has "extreme thoughts" she says, and channels her emotions through art.

She turned 10 on Wednesday, but this year, there was no birthday celebration in sight.

Her birthday, she says, will now instead be a day to commemorate her father.

## Bid-rigging and rubber stamps

Wang Fuk Court's problems date back to before the renovations began.

The management company hired to oversee and supervise the project, Will Power, conspired with PC&E to defraud residents in Wang Fuk Court, according to the city's Independent Commission Against Corruption (ICAC).

Although PC&E was found to have breached safety requirements and violated building department rules for multiple previous projects, homeowners at Wang Fuk Court were told that they had a clean record.

The ICAC alleges that this was part of a lucrative scheme, with Will Power's director Wong Hap-yin, along with other individuals, laundering over $5m in suspected proceeds from the tendering process from Wang Fuk Court, among other housing projects.

Once PC&E cinched the contract, Will Power's registered inspector - an individual meant to oversee the renovations - merely rubber-stamped the documents, without having actually conducted any inspections personally, the report added.

Will Power has been since liquidated and PC&E could not be reached for a response.

Both companies - Will Power and PC&E - and seven individuals have been charged with 25 counts of offences including including manslaughter, conspiracy to defraud, attempting to pervert the course of public justice and tax evasion.

Four of them are still in custody but none of the cases have gone to trial yet.

The report said both companies involved were the "main culprits responsible".

But it also directed heavy criticism at authorities, saying that "no government department took effective ownership of various fire safety issues and complaints about fire hazards fell into a vacuum".

One expert, Prof John Burns of Hong Kong University, had called the event the "largest governance crisis" that authorities have faced since 2020 - a reference to the controversial National Security Law that was passed in that year.

On the day the report was released, almost one year after the fire, Hong Kong's housing chief Winnie Ho resigned and the city's top leader John Lee apologised for the first time, promising that a "large number" of reforms would be rolled out.

## A shell of a home

Five months after the fire, Mrs Lau was allowed into her flat for the first time to collect any personal belongings that might have remained.

In her first visit, she was given just three hours to retrieve 40 years' worth of belongings.

Pictures taken from the visit show a home that has been utterly burned out - with every surface blackened and soot covering the floor.

"I almost fainted when I saw what had become of my home. I don't understand how the home I had built, such a warm and beautiful home, could end up like this," said Mrs Lau.

![The metal frame of a wheelchair stands in a flat strewn with ashes](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/624/cpsprodpb/4102/live/60913a80-e8ae-11f1-869a-f96fd5660210.jpg)

The frame of Mr Lau's wheelchair was one of the few things left in their burnt home

Her children, who came with her, described the house as a "war zone". Among the rubble, they found broken pieces of a piggy bank that Mr Lau had dutifully filled with coins every day for the past 20 years.

"Everyone in our family knew it was his favourite possession. The piggy bank died with him," said Mrs Lau.

Mrs Lau and her daughter have since rented a place nearby to live - and she is worried about how she is going to afford a new house.

The government has said it will spend $867m (£663m) to acquire affected flats - but Mrs Lau says it is not nearly enough to buy a similarly sized flat in the area.

Still, her family was in a better position than many others, she said.

"Should I say that we are fortunate?

"I lost my husband but many families lost multiple family members... Babies less than a year old died. "

Stories have emerged of some of the other victims - a six-month-old who died alongside her 68-year-old grandmother when the fire broke out.

The bodies of an Indonesian domestic helper and the elderly person she worked for were found huddled together.

![Mrs Lau, in a pink shirt, holds a piggy bank. There are two more piggy banks on the shelf](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/4baf/live/401c9860-e8b0-11f1-869a-f96fd5660210.jpg)

Mrs Lau's son has since bought new piggy banks in memory of his father

As for Christy, she says she is planning to file a lawsuit against those responsible for the fire - but acknowledges that even if she wins, she "can't [get] my husband's life back".

"I share things that happen in our daily life, as if he still existed," she says. "I often write to him, 'I miss you so much. Are you doing well on the other side?'"

It's a similar story for Mrs Lau, who says she can't go anywhere without seeing her husband.

"Every day, something reminds me of him," she said.

"I still find it very hard to accept that my husband is gone, my home is gone, and so many of my neighbours are gone…. This place was my roots. I never imagined it would also mark an ending for me."
