Sustained high blood sugar levels are linked to worse memory and brain function in mid-life, a new 30-year study has found.

Nearly 3,000 young and healthy adults who developed type 2 diabetes - a condition that affects peoples’ ability to regulate blood sugar - had signs of structural changes in their brain, researchers with the American Heart Association said Wednesday.

The findings suggest managing blood sugar levels during young adulthood (ages 18-30) may be beneficial to preserve brain health later in life.

Most healthy adults are recommended to screen for diabetes risk starting at age 35, according to the Mayo Clinic.

“Diabetes is known to affect cognitive function, but cognitive impairment due to diabetes was once believed to happen mostly during childhood among people with type 1 diabetes or after age 65 in people with type 2 diabetes,” lead author and University of California, San Francisco neuroepidemiologist Regina Paradela explained in a statement.

![The findings suggest managing blood sugar levels during young adulthood may be beneficial to preserve brain health later in life](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/20/30/GettyImages-2243967741.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

That’s why this connection between raised blood sugar levels and poorer cognitive performance is “very important,” she noted.

“This suggests that changes in glucose metabolism may be relevant to brain health earlier in adulthood than we typically think about,” Paradela said.

## Decades of data

Researchers analyzed data from the federal Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults study, which enrolled more than 5,100 Black and white adults aged 18-30 from 1985 to 1986.

The study participants underwent one cognitive test at the 30-year mark that examined memory, processing speed, executive function, verbal fluency and overall cognition.

More than 600 participants had MRI imaging that revealed the brain changes.

Blood sugar levels were measured at the seven-year mark and again at 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 years.

![More than 600 participants of the study had MRIs and all participants had a cognitive test, the researchers said. The data was collected as a part of a federal study](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/20/16/GettyImages-1230058373.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

## The blood-brain connection

Healthy blood sugar levels are between 70-99 milligrams of sugar per deciliter of blood (mg/dL), according to the Cleveland Clinic.

Levels above 126 mg/dL typically mean you have diabetes.

The brain needs blood sugar to function, using the sugar - also known as glucose - as it’s main source of energy.

But both high and low levels of blood sugar can damage blood vessels that send blood to the brain, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention says.

“When your brain receives too little blood, brain cells can die. This can cause problems with memory and thinking and eventually can lead to vascular dementia,” the agency says.
