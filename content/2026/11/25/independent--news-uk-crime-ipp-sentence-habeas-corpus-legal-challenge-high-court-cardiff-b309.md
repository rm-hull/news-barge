Prisoners trapped on indefinite jail terms have been “forgotten by the state” and left emaciated, suicidal and disturbed after decades without hope, a court has heard.

One prisoner serving an Imprisonment for Public Protection (IPP) jail term sewed his own mouth shut with with wire, cut off parts of his ear and carries a suicide note with him after his mental heath deteriorated after more than 20 years in prison, despite receiving a minimum tariff of just 23 months.

Wayne Williams is among four IPP prisoners making a bid for freedom after the United Nations ruled their detention is arbitrary and unlawful by international standards.

In an application for habeas corpus challenging their continued detention, heard by two High Court judges at Cardiff on Wednesday, lawyers for the prisoners said if the court fails to intervene “this human misery will continue to intensify”.

Alun Jones KC, for the prisoners, said the fallout from the long-abandoned IPP sentencing regime is a miscarriage of justice on a par with Hillsborough, the infected blood and Post Office scandals.

“This is a unique problem in English criminal law,” he told the court. “Nothing like this has ever arisen before, such a statutory mess involving so many people, trapping people in what everybody recognises is a fundamentally unjust position.”

![Clockwise from top left: Wayne Williams, Leroy Douglas, Abdullahi Suleman, Shaun Lloyd and Josh Mcrae](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/08/04/10/12/IPPNEWSPLITFIVE.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

He told the court all four prisoners had resorted to self-harm or made attempts on their own life as they languished on the open-ended jail terms.

Mr Williams, now 38, watched the proceedings from HMP Wymott, in Lancashire, as the court was told he had been monitored under suicide prevention measures 56 times. He was jailed in 2006 after he attempted to injure a police officer in a fight, aged 17.

“The sentence feels like standing in a queue for twenty years – all the stress of a long queue, and no end to it, ever,” he said in written submissions to the court. “It is like playing a game that I am losing.”

Abdullahi Suleman, who has served almost 19 years for a laptop robbery, had been in segregation for almost a year before he was transferred to a secure hospital. He was found by his lawyer in his cell partially naked, emaciated and gaunt with ligatures from torn bed sheets around his wrists, the court heard.

The suspension of his parole reviews while he is hospital also makes his detention unlawful, his lawyers argue.

Leroy Douglas, 44, served just under 20 years for street robbery of a mobile phone in 36 different prisons before he was finally released on parole last month. His original minimum tariff was just two and half years.

Describing the toll of the uncertain jail term, he said: “Not knowing when, or whether, I will ever be released breaks you into a million pieces. I have spent years in some of the most volatile environments imaginable. There have been times when I have harmed myself, and times when I have felt suicidal.”

![IPP prisoner Wayne Williams, pictured with his late grandmother, has served more than 20 years in prison for a 23 month jail term](https://static.independent.co.uk/2024/06/21/15/newFile-5.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Father-of-two Shaun Lloyd, 39, who was recently hauled back to prison for the fifth time having served more than 13 years for street robbery, said he feels at the end of his tether.

“I do not know how much longer I can keep my strength up,” the court was told.

A fifth IPP prisoner, Josh Mcrae, was also found to be arbitrarily detained in the landmark legal opinion issued by the UN Working Group for Arbitrary Detention this summer. Sadly, he died in his cell at HMP Long Lartin in September 2024.

Mr Jones, aided by Dr Muin Boase, argued the four prisoners are among “many hundreds of victims of what is recognised publicly and politically as a scandal and a disgrace to British justice”.

Mr Jones described the disparity between their original minimum tariff and the time they have served as “astonishing and disproportionate”, adding: “They have been detained out of all proportion to their offences and their guilt.”

He said the IPP regime was equivalent to The Gulag Archipelago - referring to the famous books on Soviet labour camps.

The court heard there is a significant “circularity” problem for prisoners whose mental health deteriorates as a result of the hopeless jail term, which then adds to their risk when assessed for release by the Parole Board.

The sentence has been linked increased rates of self harm and more than 90 prisoners have taken their own lives, the court heard. The UN special rapporteur on torture has previously warned the punishment may amount to “psychological torture”.

“Unless the court here intervenes there’s going to be nothing to come to the help of these otherwise helpless victims of an unjust system,” Mr Jones told the court. “Otherwise in ten years time they will be back here again and it will be said by the secretary of state they are even more of a risk now because of what has happened in the intervening years, so this court has a duty to intervene.”

![Justice secretary Alex Norris has said he will bring legislation to end the injustice of IPP sentences](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/08/11/21/2289000698..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

He also accused the justice secretary, Alex Norris, of being “Janus-faced” for publicly describing the sentence as a “grotesque spectacle” and a “deep injustice” while defending the legal action.

Mr Norris has pledged to bring forward legislation to end the injustice of the IPP sentence before the end of Parliament. However, Mr Jones said these promises are “written in water”, noting that governments can fall and legislative priorities can change.

William Hays, for Mr Norris, argued the prisoners have been detained under a court order and can be legally held until they pass the parole board’s release test.

“The detention is lawful and the application for habeas corpus falls to be dismissed,” he told the court.

Under questioning from Lord Justice Stuart-Smith, he admitted that the parole board would have to consider the a person’s mental ill-health or risk - even it was caused by the sentence itself.

Lord Justice Stuart-Smith and Mr Justice Kimblin reserved judgment.
