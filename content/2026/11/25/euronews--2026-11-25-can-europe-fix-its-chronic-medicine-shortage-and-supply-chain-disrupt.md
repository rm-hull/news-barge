## The European Parliament just approved new legislation to strengthen the production of essential treatments and cut risks linked to limited suppliers. But will it be enough?

Approved today by the European Parliament, the Critical Medicines Act aims to boost supply, industry innovation, and combat antimicrobial resistance.

Initially proposed by the EU Commission in March 2025, the legislation focuses in particular on essential drugs, whose shortage might cause serious harm to patients, including antibiotics, insulin and treatments for chronic diseases.

## Why is Europe's drug supply under threat?

One of the main reasons is the continent's dependence on a limited number of suppliers, particularly outside the EU, for essential medicines and their active ingredients.

Even if some medicines are manufactured in Europe, they still depend on substances produced, for example, in India or in China, tells Euronews Jorge Batista, pharmacist and senior advisor at The Pharmaceutical Group of the European Union (PGEU).

He adds that about three-quarters of production sites for chemically active pharmaceutical ingredients are actually outside the EU.

When a manufacturer faces production problems, or demand suddenly increases, the entire supply chain can be disrupted. And as countries often compete to buy the same medicines, smaller markets can struggle to secure supplies.

## How will the Act strengthen the EU's pharmaceutical industry?

Factories producing critical medicines or their active ingredients could receive financial support and faster administrative procedures to expand their production.

Second, governments will have to consider supply reliability when purchasing medicines, rather than focusing mainly on the lowest price.

Europe is heavily dependent on a small number of non-EU suppliers, and public buyers will generally have to favour European manufacturing. The Act also makes it easier for countries to buy medicines together, pooling their purchasing power to improve access, particularly in smaller markets.

“Small countries may need too few packs to attract several suppliers on their own", says Batista. "We see already that the demand is quite low. So, by pulling orders, by bringing together different countries, for example, small countries can increase the number of orders, and they can offer manufacturers, let's say, a larger, more predictable market."

## Which drugs are currently running short?

Medicines currently facing supply shortages are Vaxigrip, a seasonal flu vaccine; Azactam, an antibiotic for serious bacterial infections; ifosfamide, used in chemotherapy; extended-release quetiapine, prescribed for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder; and Creon, which helps people with pancreatic enzyme insufficiency digest food.

But the Critical Medicines Act is not an emergency measure that will immediately restock pharmacies.

Building new factories, diversifying suppliers and changing public procurement practices will take time, and the law cannot guarantee that every medicine will always be available, or that prices for patients will fall.

Its success will depend on whether governments will actually use these new tools, whether manufacturers will invest in additional production, and whether countries will cooperate when supplies are limited.

## **Is Europe on the road to extinction?**

Also in today's episode, we take a look at the demographic crisis affecting the continent.

Europe is running out of babies, and the clock is ticking. It’s more than a demographic shift. It’s a challenge that could reshape Europe’s economy, workforce and future.

“This is extremely difficult for the balance of public budgets of the governments, which have to allocate more and more funding for elderly care for healthcare, but also for keeping basic infrastructure running.", Tomas Sobotka, Deputy Director, Vienna Institute of Demography, tells Euronews.

"It will squeeze the amount of money available for investments. It will also squeeze the amount of money available for younger people, for supporting families and for allocating money to other generations and other needs than the needs of the elderly.

## Where are some of the worst birth rates in Europe?

In some cases, birth rates are dropping to wartime levels.

Deaths outnumbered births in France last year, with the lowest level of newborns since 1942: 645,000. It doesn't go much better in Germany, with only 654,000 babies in 2026, the lowest number on record since data collection began in 1946.

And then there’s Italy, where births have fallen for 17 straight years, dropping 38% since 2008, to 356,000 in 2025.

Spain saw a similar decline (- 24% between 2015 and 2025), and last year’s tiny 1% growth won’t make up for it.

## What are governments doing?

Parental leave is great, but it doesn’t pay for prams. That’s why some countries are going straight for people’s wallets.

In Hungary, mothers with at least three children are exempt from employment income tax for life, even after their children have grown up. Poland is taking a similar approach. Families receive about €2,200 a year for each child under 18. And in Malta, families with a combined annual income below €30,000 can receive up to €1,400 per child annually, plus an annual supplement of around €900.

But according to Anna Matysiak, President of the European Association for Population Studies, these measures are not likely to be a silver bullet.

“Researchers consistently say that these policies are rather unlikely to bring fertility back to the levels of around two children per woman, the level we ideally would like to have."

"I still think Scandinavian countries are doing a good job, with public childcare combined with parental leave. They also have universal financial support for families. These countries are still a model which many countries in Europe are trying to follow.”
