The U.S. Army detonated about 9,500 pounds of degraded lead azide, a sensitive explosive, in bunkers at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant on Wednesday, with U.S. officials casting the operation as a massive success.

At about 9 a.m. CST, a loud boom was heard in Middletown, sending a large plume of smoke rising above the plant as nearby residents sheltered in place.

Afterward, Army officials said all of the lead had been detonated and that efforts to mitigate the fallout had been tremendously effective.

“We’re finding a 0.0 detection level of lead in the atmosphere,” an Army spokesperson said during a press conference. Another official said it had gone “100 percent as planned.”

Still, some locals expressed a sense of unease, and state and local officials criticized the operation amid fears of health and environmental impacts. There is no safe level of lead exposure, and children and pregnant women are especially vulnerable. Army maps reviewed by *The New YorkTimes* showed a bungled blast could result in toxic lead particles proliferating over 150 square miles of farmland.

![The US Army detonated a degraded stockpile of lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa, on Wednesday. US officials cast the operation as a success](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/15/2026-11-25T153211Z_1522438814_RC23VOA1K5LS_RTRMADP_3_IOWA-ARMY-DETONATION.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The explosion was carried out because inspectors found the 68 drums of material — the nation's entire stockpile of lead azide — had degraded to the point that it could explode on its own at any time, leaving a controlled detonation as the only viable choice.

"Inaction is no longer an option," an Army general said in a statement.

Ahead of the blast, the Federal Emergency Management Agency had recommended that residents of Middletown, Iowa — a tiny town of several hundred people near the Illinois border — and others in a five-mile zone of the plant relocate on Tuesday, and offered them hotel stays.

Middletown residents scrambled to prepare their homes, with some boarding up windows. Those in surrounding towns, and even states, took precautions as well, upending Thanksgiving plans.

Illinois health officials urged people to remain indoors up to two days after detonation.

About an hour before the explosion Wednesday morning, area residents received a cellphone alert from FEMA warning that "EVACUATION IS NO LONGER SAFE OR ADVISED." The alert said residents should shelter in place immediately.

The drums were detonated at about 9 a.m. CST, rattling nearby windows and sending dogs barking. Journalists in HAZMAT suits documented explosion while situated about a half-mile away, capturing smoke rising over the treeline.

![Ahead of the blast, Middletown residents scrambled to prepare their homes, with some boarding up windows and many sheltering indoors](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/15/58/AP26329549129026.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

U.S. officials said they took “unprecedented” precautions to limit the fallout.

In order to absorb thermal energy and weigh down airborne particles, water tanks, gel blocks and foam were applied — with the foam likely having been responsible for the plume of smoke, an Army spokesperson said. The Environmental Protection Agency also deployed teams to sample air and soil.

"We are prepared to take the most comprehensive measures available to ensure that any risk to the communities, families, and residents are mitigated, and any disruptions are minimized," EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin told CNN after the blast.

Upon receiving an all-clear announcement, residents have been instructed to wipe down "high-contact exterior surfaces," such as patios, window sills, play equipment to remove any dust.

![About an hour before a scheduled blast, Iowa residents received a cellphone alert from FEMA warning that ‘EVACUATION IS NO LONGER SAFE OR ADVISED’](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/14/Iowa_Army_Lead_Detonation__6738.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Despite the precautions, some state and local officials have criticized the Army's decision, while demanding answers.

Senator-elect Josh Turek wrote on X that the materials should have "never have been allowed to degrade to this point."

"Everyday Iowans are entitled to know how Middletown came to this situation, and learn why the administration believes this is the best path forward," Governor-elect Rob Sand wrote on X ahead of the controlled explosion.

“There is obviously more testing to do that will provide certainty over time, but for now, federal officials have reported that lead dispersal should be limited, Iowans are safe, and damage to property is limited,” Sand wrote afterwards.

Public health organizations, including the American Lung Association, had urged military officials to consider delaying the detonation over concerns about the spread of lead dust. Lead can damage the brain, kidneys and nervous system, and even small amounts can harm a child's development and a pregnancy.

Some locals also expressed a sense of unease after the materials were exploded.

"It's kind of unsettling, you know — it's up there now," an Iowa resident told *The New York Times*. "It's like, it finally happened, and now it's up there, and now you just wait and see."

A woman who runs a daycare about 20 miles from the site said she intends to keep her kids inside all day.

Others, meanwhile, said they hardly noticed the detonation.

“It was nothing,” a Burlington, Iowa, resident told *The Associated Press*. “It seemed like government put fear in a lot of people over nothing.”

![Journalists in HAZMAT suits documented the events from about a half-mile away from the plant](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/16/2306840926..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The blast was postponed several times before a federal appeals court cleared the way for Wednesday’s operation. Army officials said they had to act immediately before colder weather raised the risk of an "uncontrolled detonation."

The lead azide stockpile had been sitting in Middletown since 2009, when the Army transported the materials there from across the nation. Some of the material was already more than 60 years old when it was moved.

“We were handed a problem frankly that started in 2009,” a spokesman for the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives said Wednesday. He applauded the Trump administration for taking action.

The U.S. military has spent years trying to replace lead azide — a toxic compound that has been used to detonate munitions since World War I — with a less hazardous alternative.
