Pedro Sánchez has won praise from the left for standing up to Donald Trump and promoting progressive causes

**Speaking in a wrestling ring on the island of Tenerife as he campaigned ahead of Sunday's general election, Spain's Socialist Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez cautioned those listening about the spread of right-wing governments.**

"What good has President [Donald] Trump done the United States and, by extension, the world - was he a change or a step backwards?" he asked supporters, before invoking the administrations of Brazil's former leader, Jair Bolsonaro, and Argentinian President Javier Milei.

"If we don't want that for the world, why would we want it for our own country?" said Sánchez.

He issued his warning as Spain's conservative People's Party (PP) leads most of the polls and appears poised to form a governing coalition with the hard-right Vox. The election comes amid a deeply polarised political atmosphere in which both left and right have warned about their opponents' ability to undermine democracy.

In a Europe dominated by right-leaning governments, Sánchez has gained a reputation as the continent's most outspoken and uncompromising left-wing prime minister.

His strident rhetoric defending feminism, immigration and green energy, along with his condemnation of Israel's military action in Gaza and verbal clashes with Trump, has fostered this image.

Having overseen consistent economic growth in recent years, his stock is high among the international left.

Yet in Spain, Sánchez has become a hate figure for the right, who see him as an autocrat with little regard for the separation of state powers, obsessed with clinging on to power and surrounded by a corrupt cabal.

Alberto Núñez Feijóo's right-wing People's Party (PP) is currently ahead in the polls

In a bullring in Valencia, the same day that Sánchez was staging his rally, PP leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo told supporters that the last few years of Socialist-led government had been "insulting and even humiliating for a lot of people". He added: "We know them, they are capable of anything."

A couple of days earlier, Ignacio Garriga, spokesman for Vox, had gone further, describing Sánchez as "a very dangerous psychopath".

Both the PP and Vox have cast doubt on the neutrality of postal votes and votes cast by Spaniards abroad ahead of the election, suggesting that government manipulation could sway them in its favour.

"This is a campaign that is overwhelmingly ideological," said Cristina Monge, a political scientist at the Complutense University. "That means that it is not so much about voters rewarding or punishing a government's performance or the proposals being made, but about belonging to one or other ideological bloc."

Much of the hostility to Sánchez stems from his reliance on hard-left and separatist parties.

Ahead of the 2023 general election, he faced fierce criticism from the right for his reliance on the pro-independence EH Bildu coalition, which had links to the defunct Basque armed group ETA.

After that vote, he was able to form a new administration with the support of another separatist party, Together for Catalonia (Junts), in exchange for an amnesty for all Catalans facing legal action for their role in a failed 2017 bid for independence.

At the time, Núñez Feijóo accused Sánchez of being "a prime minister who has bought his investiture in exchange for the judicial impunity of his partners".

Guillermo Fernández Vázquez, a political scientist at Carlos III University, said those parliamentary partnerships "fed the rhetoric of the right and far right, which was that Pedro Sánchez is an amoral person without scruples – someone whose word can't be trusted and who just wants to remain in power".

This legislation has been one of turmoil for Sánchez, during which he has been unable to push a single budget through parliament.

In addition, the prime minister has been corralled by a litany of judicial investigations. He has acknowledged errors of judgement in trusting two close former associates in the Socialist Party, José Luis Ábalos, who has been given a 24-year-jail sentence for corruption-related crimes, and Santos Cerdán, who is due to go on trial on similar charges.

By contrast, he has defended his former attorney general, Álvaro García Ortiz, who was barred from public office for revealing confidential information, and his brother, David, who was barred from office for nine years after being found guilty of irregularities in taking on a post as a regional arts director.

In the most controversial case, Sánchez's wife, Begoña Gómez, is due to go on trial for influence peddling.

She denies wrongdoing and Sánchez has said the cases involving García Ortiz, his brother and wife are "nasty politics, not corruption", blaming them on efforts by his political opponents to use the judiciary to destabilise his government.

Politicians on the right draw no such distinction between the various cases, and Vox leader Santiago Abascal describes the government as "a criminal mafia".

María Pérez, a young office worker who has no political allegiance, says she is going to vote for the PP, partly because of her concerns about the country's housing crisis and the rail system, which has seen two major tragedies this year, but above all due to the judicial investigations.

"I think that Sánchez has lost credibility with all the corruption cases," she says. "It seems that everyone in government is involved… so I'm not sure I want them to continue governing. I believe it's time for a change."

Santiago Abascal and his far-right Vox party could enter government for the first time after Sunday's election

Allegations that Sánchez has co-opted state institutions, such as the CIS national research centre, which publishes opinion polls, and national broadcaster RTVE, have only compounded the right's view of him as a populist who has scant regard for the rule of law.

However, with the far right seemingly set to enter government for the first time in Spain's democratic era, claims of demagoguery cut both ways.

Politicians on the left say that coalition administrations formed by the PP and Vox in four of Spain's 17 regions are a blueprint for socially regressive policies they plan to implement on a national level after the election.

In all four of those regions, Vox has negotiated the introduction of "national priority", which seeks to give Spaniards priority access to public services and subsidies ahead of foreigners.

The Castilla y León region has also seen culture-related controversies driven by Vox. Most recently, a play about left-wing poet and dramatist Federico García Lorca, who was killed by right-wing rebels during the 1936-39 civil war, was cancelled in the region's capital, Valladolid, apparently at the behest of the Vox-controlled local culture ministry.

Last month the same department published new guidelines governing the awarding of subsidies for films, stating that projects involving what it called "ideological indoctrination" would not be eligible. Spain's culture minister, Ernest Urtasun, described this as "an intolerable attempt at censorship".

Susana Fuertes, a schoolteacher who will vote for the left on Sunday, said such moves are a sinister throwback to the dictatorship of Francisco Franco.

"These political messages take us back to the Franco era by talking about getting rid of basic civil rights regarding women, gender equality and immigration," she said. "All of this has been said in such an aggressive tone, with insults and lack of respect for people who don't share the same ideas."

Critics of Núñez Feijóo argue that he has already shown signs of taking on Vox's agenda by hardening his rhetoric on immigration and promising to roll back a law allowing euthanasia and eliminate the equality ministry, which manages matters related to gender, sexuality and ethnicity.

Spain's deep left-right schism has also been visible beyond the political arena.

Hooded anti-fascist youths have clashed with police on a number of occasions on the campaign trail, such as when they demonstrated against an appearance by Vox's Abascal in A Coruña.

An arguably more visible phenomenon has been far-right youths. In one incident, several dozen of them raised their arms in the fascist salute and sang the pro-Franco anthem *Cara al Sol* (Facing the sun) while facing off against housing protesters in Madrid.

A 2025 CIS poll found that around one in five under-25s had a positive view of the dictatorship.
