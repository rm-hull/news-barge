**Domestic energy prices are set to soar in January under the regulator Ofgem's new price cap, pushing up bills for millions of households across England, Scotland and Wales.**

With household budgets already under pressure, finding ways to reduce gas and electricity costs has become increasingly important. But how much difference can simple changes really make?

The BBC spoke to three people about what they're doing to keep their energy costs down.

## 'We power down at peak times'

![Kelly Anne Harvey](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/53a4/live/eed87fe0-e74d-11f1-88dc-f746e0cde621.jpg)

Kelly Anne Harvey uses electric blankets and portable oil radiators in some rooms to save on the central heating

Like many people, Kelly Anne Harvey, a building surveyor from Cleveleys near Blackpool, is worried about her energy bill going up next year.

The 44-year-old, who is married with a five-year-old son, has a combined household income of £60,000.

She currently pays about £154 a month for gas and electricity on a standard variable rate with Octopus, but is looking for a fixed-rate deal to offset the rise.

The family fell into arrears on their energy bills a few years ago and Kelly says she won't let it happen again.

To control her costs she pays by direct debit, which spreads her payments and is typically £138 cheaper a year than paying on receipt of a bill.

The family uses electric blankets and portable oil radiators in some rooms so they don't have to turn the central heating on as often.

They also take part in Octopus's energy saving scheme, which rewards customers who use less electricity at peak times, such as early evenings, with a free hour of energy at the weekend.

"On Sunday we'll turn on the dishwasher, tumble drier, washing machine and maybe even charge our electric cars," says Kelly.

## 'We've turned the heating down by 2.5C'

![Stacey and her kids](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/800/cpsprodpb/4be2/live/fe282b50-e827-11f1-8d9d-c156c75bcdb5.jpg)

Stacey says she keeps the curtains closed "to keep the heat in" and her daughters wear thermal clothing

Stacey, 40, a single mother from Bristol, says dealing with rising energy costs has felt like a "battle" recently and she's worried about 2027.

The office worker, whose daughters are aged 10 and 12, is on a fixed-term tracker tariff with E.On Next, which charges rates set below the quarterly Ofgem price cap. She expects to pay £1,800 for energy this year, up £400 on last year.

Stacey, who is part of the Changing Realities project at Glasgow University, which documents life for people on low incomes, has tried a number of things to control her energy costs.

She's turned her central heating down from 22C to 19.5C, bought electric blankets and pays her bills by direct debit.

She also leaves her curtains shut to "keep the heat in", while her daughters wear thermal clothing if it gets chilly in the house. The family is also using less hot water.

"I've got girls and they're growing up and they want to spend loads of time in the shower [but] it's just not possible."

## 'I order heating oil in bulk'

![Bradley Knowles](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/50b3/live/2388b890-e762-11f1-b407-33329b1ec67a.jpg)

Around 1.5 million households in the UK rely on heating oil for heating and hot water, and their costs have already soared in the past year due to disruption in global oil markets.

"When I first moved here two years ago you could get a litre of heating oil for 50-60p," says Bradley Knowles, 32, who lives in a barn conversion with his husband, mother and sister in rural Lincolnshire.

"At the height of the Iran war it went up to £1.20 or £1.30 and now it's stabilised at around £1."

To cut costs, the finance worker orders oil in bulk, buying 1,000-1,500 litres, preferably in the summer months when prices are lower. He also tries to order a few weeks in advance to avoid expensive next-day deliveries.

Bradley uses comparison sites to see which local suppliers offer the cheapest oil and puts money aside each month so he can always afford his orders.

Oil-powered boilers may take time to heat up, but Bradley advises against leaving them on all day as that can be very expensive.

Instead, he uses an electric tank to store his warm water and tops up the heating in parts of the property with electric radiators.
