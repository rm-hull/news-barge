## The Quantum Act is the EU’s latest bid in the race to deploy the next cutting-edge technology and stop it falling behind the US and China.

The EU on Wednesday unveiled its plans to prepare Europe for the quantum age, including driving €50 billion of investment in the technology, in a bid to position the block for the next technological revolution after largely missing out on the global AI race.

Quantum technology harnesses the laws of quantum mechanics, where particles can exist in more than one place at time, to deliver huge advances in computing power, communication security and sensing equipment. It also raises significant cybersecurity risks given its ability to rapidly accelerate computer processing power.

The European Commission's proposed Quantum Act aims to position the EU as a leader in the next tech upheaval and to protect itself from post-quantum cyberthreats. It's also an attempt to regain the continent's technological sovereignty after years of reliance on American Big Tech firms and Chinese critical supply chains.

The EU executive pitched the legislation as an ‘enabling framework’, similar to those under the Chips Acts 1 & 2, which sought to drive up Europe’s domestic chip manufacturing base.

The Commission hopes it can take advantage of Europe’s strengths on quantum — with one in three quantum companies based in the EU and making up 44% of all quantum patents — to position itself as a global quantum leader by 2030.

“With the Quantum Act, we are putting in place the key elements to turn our world-class quantum research into industrial capacity, strengthen critical supply chains and prepare for the cyber risks linked to quantum computing,” Henna Virkkunen, the EU's commissioner for technology, said on Wednesday.

“Europe has already built strong foundations in quantum; now we must turn them into industrial scale, resilient supply chains and secure deployment,” she added.

The Commission expects the global quantum market to exceed €155 billion by 2040. Quantum computing alone could generate $1.3 -2.7 trillion (€1.18 - 2.46 trillion) in economic value for companies by 2035, according to consulting firm McKinsey.

The Quantum Act focuses on streamlining current efforts across the EU to build a holistic strategy to help companies scale, supported by European public and private investment.

Specialistic hubs and competence and acceleration centres are foreseen for each member state. These would provide technical support to small and medium-sized enterprises, while serving as an incubator for new projects and technologies to make their way to market.

## Quantum cyber threats

Quantum poses unprecedented cybersecurity risks. Quantum computers can break current encryption methods in mere hours, compared to millions of years for normal computers, putting global data privacy in danger.

For years now, industry leaders and experts have been pushing governments and businesses to upgrade all their online systems with post-quantum cybersecurity tools to protect themselves from cyberattacks.

The day that the first quantum computer will go online is referred to as Q-Day, potentially giving attackers the ability to carry out cloud-based cyberattacks at far greater scale than today.

The Quantum Act would require EU member states to transition to post-quantum cryptography by 2030 for high-risk areas like critical infrastructure, and 2035 for all other systems.

AI has already ushered in a new age of cybersecurity incidents this year. Attackers have used AI-powered tools to bring down critical infrastructure and hit governments and private organisations, such as this month’s attack on the Commission’s digital infrastructure by alleged Chinese hackers.

## Sovereign Tech Equity Fund, but you can call me STEF

Alongside the Quantum Act, the Commission proposed the creation of a new investment tool, known as the Sovereign Tech Equity Fund (STEF), which aims to drive up to €50 billion of investment in quantum and other technologies like AI that are critical to European technological sovereignty.

Europe already invests more public money in quantum than the US and is not far behind China. Annual public investment in the EU is expected to rise to €8.6 billion this year, compared to €13.6 billion in China and €5.3 billion in the US.

However, it's in private investment that Europe lags behind, attracting just €1.1 billion compared to €7 billion in the US last year, according to the Commission. With STEF, the Commission aims to bridge this gap.

The fund is essentially seed capital, with the Commission aiming to attract up to €6 in private funding for every €1 of public money invested from private investors, member states and the European Investment Bank. The European executive said it wants STEF to be operational as soon as possible, by 2028 at the latest when the next EU long-term budget takes effect.

It's also designed to work hand-in-hand with the European Competitiveness Fund, set to become operational in January 2028, which aims to streamline EU industrial and research support by bringing existing programmes under a single framework, with a total budget of €234.3 billion.

## Supply chains and sovereignty

A crucial aspect of the Commission’s quantum proposal includes measures to strengthen risk monitoring, along with procurement rules to protect critical systems from exploitation.

Supply chains would be monitored by the Commission and member states to identify critical suppliers and components in order to reduce risks.

However, access to procurement would remain tied to rules established by the World Trade Organisation, allowing for non-EU entities to take part as long as they have a European controlled presence and follow EU rules, rather than ensuring every component is ‘made in Europe’.

The Quantum Act also aims to reduce Europe’s reliance on critical components, with nine of 47 components identified currently having no EU supplier at all.

The legislation must now go to the European Parliament for review before being ratified by the European Council.

## Quantum industry reaction

The European Quantum Industry Consortium (QuIC), which represents more than 240 organisations, welcomed the proposal, calling the Quantum Act a "defining opportunity for Europe to translate its scientific and technological excellence into industrial leadership.”

“It is essential that the expertise, ambitions and needs of our industry are reflected in this legislative framework,” Dr. Thomas Strohm, President of QuIC, said in a statement.

The organisation, however, said procurement volumes should be added to the draft proposal, including €300–500 million of commitments within six months of entry into force, and €7.7–12.3 billion over five years, across all national and European public buyers.

QuIC also called for STEF to include a dedicated share of investment for quantum tech that would be open to scale-ups and start-ups in order to keep pace with the US and China.

The Business Software Alliance (BSA) reacted by urging the EU to accelerate quantum adoption, investment and innovation while remaining open to partnering with foreign firms.

“The goal should be quantum leadership, not quantum barriers; European capability and international openness reinforce each other rather than compete,” Hadrien Valembois, the BSA's director of policy, said in a statement.

Outside of Europe, IBM’s head of government and regulatory affairs, Jean Marc Leclerc, warned that the Quantum Act shouldn’t rule out non-European companies for procurement.

“The quantum industry opportunity extends beyond any single company, country, or region — and Europe should not have to choose between building its own capabilities or accessing the best available quantum technology from around the world,” Leclerc told Euronews.

He added that as the proposal develops, it should “move Europe’s already thriving and open quantum ecosystem forward," including by "reinforcing efforts that will scale adoption [and] investment."

Similarly, Matteo Quattrocchi, public policy director at Cisco, told Euronews that the proposal "will need to ensure Europe can collaborate with the international partners, governments, and companies leading the quantum revolution." He suggested that would be crucial to giving Europe "the reach it needs to scale in quantum".
