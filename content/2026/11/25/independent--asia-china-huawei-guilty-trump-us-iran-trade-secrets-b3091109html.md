Huawei has been found guilty of conspiring to steal U.S. trade secrets, violating sanctions, and misleading banks about its business dealings in Iran and North Korea in a landmark case.

One of China’s largest technology companies was convicted on 10 of 12 counts following an 11-week trial but was acquitted of the more serious charges of racketeering conspiracy and obstruction of justice.

The case marks a rare criminal prosecution of a foreign corporation in the United States. Prosecutors first announced charges against Huawei in 2019 and subsequently expanded the allegations.

Huawei’s defense team argued that prosecutors had already decided the company was a criminal organization and built their case around that assumption, maintaining that the firm's success was driven by its own innovation.

Brian Heberlig, an attorney for Huawei, said the company would appeal the verdict, adding that its acquittal on the racketeering conspiracy charge “confirms that these allegations lack factual basis and vindicates our consistent position over the past eight years”.

He said acquittal on racketeering charges was a “profound rejection” of the government's narrative that it was corrupt from top to bottom and had a corporate culture of cheating, stealing and lying.

U.S. Attorney Joseph Nocella said in a statement: “Few crimes are as serious as Huawei’s decades-long pattern of endangering U.S. national security, violating the sanctity of U.S. commerce, and impeding the enforcement of U.S. laws.”

![Huawei attorney Brian Heberlig said the company would appeal the convictions but saw the racketeering conspiracy acquittal as vindication](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/22/EEUU-HUAWEI_89342.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The Justice Department said Huawei and its subsidiaries helped the Iranian government develop its core internet infrastructure and expand access to mobile networks across the country.

Huawei also helped the regime in Tehran and Zaeim, a component of Iran’s intelligence apparatus, monitor and censor users’ access to the internet, the department said.

It added that testimony established that Huawei had supplied surveillance cameras that enabled Iranian authorities to monitor protest activity.

“For decades, Huawei and three of its subsidiaries conducted their business in the United States on the basis of lies and theft,” U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche said in a statement.

“Thanks to the work of the FBI and our federal prosecutors, those who engaged in these criminal activities will be held accountable.”

Huawei’s defense attorney, however, maintained that it was through hard work that the company had expanded its operations to 170 countries and grown its workforce to more than 200,000 employees.

The company was acquitted on charges of racketeering conspiracy, a charge alleging the company had acted as a criminal enterprise.

The charge typically is used in organized crime and drug cases, but prosecutors used it to accuse Huawei of operating in an illicit fashion to steal intellectual property from American companies.

The case against Huawei began in 2019 after the dramatic arrest of senior executive Meng Wanzhou in Canada in 2018.

![Closing arguments of the Huawei Technologies trial at Brooklyn Federal Court](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/04/2026-11-17T190341Z_434839394_RC2NPOA3TJ6G_RTRMADP_3_USA-CHINA-HUAWEI.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The company hasn't been allowed to sell equipment to U.S. carriers for years because of security concerns. It has also been blacklisted by other Western allies, including Canada and Britain.

Wanzhou, Huawei's chief financial officer and daughter of the company's founder, was held for three years in Canada before she was released and returned to China through a deal with U.S. prosecutors.

Wanzhou was secretly indicted on U.S. charges that she misled an HSBC banker about Huawei's business in Iran, allowing the company to move dollars in and out of Iran in violation of American sanctions.

Wanzhou did not testify at trial, though an HSBC banker she met with in 2013 did testify.

During the trial, jurors were shown a video that prosecutors said captured a Huawei employee slipping a component of a T-Mobile testing robot into his backpack. They also heard testimony about a Huawei engineer sneaking into a Fujitsu booth at a trade show to photograph a device.

The victim companies included Cisco Systems, Motorola Solutions, T-Mobile, Quintel USA and Fujitsu, according to court records.

The defense argued that HSBC and the other banks knew about Huawei's business in Iran and were competing for Huawei's business.
