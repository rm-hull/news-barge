Young Republicans in Texas gathered at a brewery this week to work out where their party goes next, three weeks after midterm results that devastated Republicans nationwide.

The Monday meeting was arranged by the Tarrant County Young Republicans, a local group confronting a national reckoning at close quarters. Their previous leader resigned shortly before the midterms because she couldn't bring herself to vote for some of her party's candidates.

Their county, once a conservative stronghold, then flipped blue.

“Everyone has someone to blame, and that finger is never pointed at ourself,” said Derrick Wilson, who heads the Texas Young Republican Federation, a coalition that includes the Tarrant County chapter. “So we're going to have to get past that and figure out, what did we do wrong?”

In Texas, Democrats took the U.S. Senate race and the governor's office, their first statewide victories in more than three decades. No consensus has emerged on the way forward, but several people at this week's meeting had similar ideas.

![White House briefing room screens display AI-modified videos of Hakeem Jeffries (D-NY) and Chuck Schumer (D-NY) that were shared on social media by Trump](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/17/29/GettyImages-2238632780.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Some are bored with “owning the libs” and put off by meme-driven political messaging that they see as flippant in the face of real challenges.

Many felt President Donald Trump didn't follow through on his promises. Others offered their party leaders similar advice: “grow up,” “take themselves seriously” and “be humble.”

## Some Republicans saw no progress on affordability

The event opened with someone introduced by Wilson as a “survivor" of the midterms. State Appeals Court Judge Brian Walker, a Republican who won reelection, gave a withering assessment of how the party had stumbled down the wrong path with Trump-style belligerent politics led by the “loudest” and “craziest” voices.

Robert Coe, a mustachioed 29-year-old in a black cowboy hat, murmured in agreement as Walker spoke. Coe has voted for Trump three times, and now gives the Republican Party an approval of 4 out of 10 after seeing little progress on affordability.

“Me and millions of other Americans are just tired of that, and we’re not seeing any viable solution to it," he said. “It’s almost as if the people in power don’t even want to try and solve it.”

Coe still voted for Republicans in the midterms, and he was the one who wanted party leaders to “grow up,” He has been put off by “the high school level name-calling, the infighting on an almost childish level."

![Robert Coe, center front, joins others as they listen to a guest speaker during a meeting organized by the Tarrant County Young Republicans at the Martin House Brewing Company in Fort Worth, Texas](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/17/45/AP26328606354498.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Citing Trump's screeds against talk show hosts, Coe said “there is no decency.”

Andrew Gochis, a 29-year-old coach and teacher who has worked in politics, was another attendee who nodded through Walker's remarks. Rather than voting a straight Republican ticket, he backed a mix of candidates.

He said Republicans had conflated being respectful and being weak. The party has to “do better," he said, at standing on their principles of deportations or opposition to abortion while also treating people such as immigrants or women who get abortions as “a human being that has value.”

## Leaders say they're in ‘listening mode’

Others had a far more extreme view.

“The Republican Party needs to get meaner,” said Stephen Nesbitt, a 26-year-old former U.S. Marine. “We need to get stricter, we need to triple down.”

He listed non-Christians, immigrants and communists as the “enemy.” His view meshes with some who believe the Make America Great Again movement didn't go far enough, but he was an outlier among those interviewed at Monday's meeting.

Wilson, the chair of the state organization, wants the party to “return to its roots" of “fiscal and social conservatism.” He and the organization are currently in “listening mode” to be better prepared ahead of 2028, a strategy that he felt was missed before the midterms.

![State Appeals Court Judge Brian Walker addresses attendees at the meeting](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/17/17/AP26328606347386.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“Young people wanted to buy homes and start families,” he said, “then they look on the news and they see Trump say, ‘I don’t want housing prices to go down, I want them to go up.’”

Trevor Carr, 25, attended the meeting for the first time because he saw the midterm elections as a repudiation of the party's old guard and he wanted to have a role in its future.

He didn't vote in the midterms because he doubted Trump was going to fulfil his promises. He wants to see a party less focused on loyalty to one man and one that clings more closely to an ideologically sound platform.

While Carr said Trump's approach was once helpful for shaking up the system 10 years ago, "it’s served its purpose, and it's time to move on to be a serious political movement.”
