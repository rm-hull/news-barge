# Young must feel safe to report bullying: Dr Elizabeth Nassem

## The recent Anti-Bullying Week which took place between November 16 and 20 reminds us that the biggest challenge is not identifying bullying. It is creating environments in which children feel safe enough to speak about it.

Despite decades of research, countless interventions, awareness campaigns and a legal requirement for schools to have anti-bullying policies, many children still suffer in silence. The question is: why?

The common assumption is that children don’t speak up because they don’t recognise bullying when they see it. In reality, many young people understand exactly what is happening. What stops them speaking out is the social cost of doing so.

Bullying thrives in silence because reporting it can feel risky.

![Dr Elizabeth Nassem shares her expert insight](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOjM4ZjQ1MGM2LTMxOWEtNDI2MC04N2IxLTZiNDg3NDdkOWRjNDpjY2MwZTgwMS0wMmE5LTRmZTYtOGMwYy04NGFlMTdmOGNjYzc=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)


One reason is that bullying has become normalised. Teasing, name-calling and ‘banter’ are often dismissed as harmless, making it difficult for children to know when behaviour has crossed a line. Perpetrators can avoid accountability by claiming they were ‘only joking’.

More importantly, the children doing the bullying are not always the obvious villains.

Many are popular, admired and socially influential. They may be surrounded by friends, well-connected within peer groups and even viewed positively by teachers. Challenging them can feel intimidating.

Speaking out against them can carry the risk of exclusion, ridicule or becoming a target yourself.

Children know this. Witnesses know it too. Many remain silent because they fear what might happen next.

Children often tell me that reporting bullying can make them feel even more vulnerable, particularly if they believe those responsible will target them afterwards.

Some young people feel their concerns are minimised or dismissed, making them less likely to seek help in future.

Many young people remain reluctant to report appearance-based comments or behaviour because they fear social consequences from their peers.

The reality is that bullying is often far more complicated than adults would like it to be.

Schools naturally want to identify a victim and a perpetrator, but real-life situations are rarely that straightforward. Bullying can occur within friendships and be dismissed as a ‘falling out’. Some forms of bullying and harassment are subtle, making them easier to dismiss and harder for young people to challenge. Long-term victims sometimes retaliate, only to find that their response attracts more attention than the behaviour that provoked it.

When children feel they won’t be understood, they become less likely to seek help. For some, there is also shame. Others worry they will be labelled a ‘snitch’ by their peers. Some have experienced bullying for so long that they simply stop believing things can change. The result is that too many children are left to manage the problem alone.

Young people need a greater role in shaping how schools understand and respond to bullying. Asking pupils about their experiences, involving them in anti-bullying policies and creating regular opportunities for honest discussion can provide insights adults might otherwise miss.

Schools should also pay close attention to what happens after bullying is reported. It is not enough to intervene once and move on. Teachers need to follow up with all those involved to ensure the behaviour has stopped and that victims are not punished socially for speaking out.

Parents have an important role too. Regular conversations about friendships, relationships and school experiences can help identify concerns before they become crises. Changes in behaviour, withdrawal from social activities or increasing reluctance to attend school can all be warning signs that something is wrong.

Ultimately, bullying persists when children feel they have more to lose by speaking up than by staying silent.

If we genuinely want children to ask for help, we must create environments in which they feel safe, supported and believed when they do. Talking about it and being open about the issues that surround it is the first step.

Only then will we begin to break bullying’s greatest ally: silence.

Dr Elizabeth Nassem is a lecturer in psychology at the University of Bradford
