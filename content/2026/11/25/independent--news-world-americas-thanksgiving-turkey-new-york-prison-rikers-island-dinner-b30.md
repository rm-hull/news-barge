Kitchen staff at New York City's Rikers Island jail will dish out around 10,000 Thanksgiving lunches on Thursday.

The holiday operation at the more than 400-acre complex began in earnest earlier this week, with batches of turkey roasts loaded into tall ovens lined up in a cavernous, industrial-grade kitchen.

Getting more than 6,000 pounds of turkey cooked in time for Thanksgiving at one of the nation's largest jails takes a few days' head start and plenty of ovens.

![Workers at New York City’s notorious Rikers Island jail are cooking thousands of pounds of turkey to serve to inmates for Thanksgiving](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/05/Thankgiving_NYC_Jail_27862.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Julet Thompson Pitt, a senior cook, switched gears to Thanksgiving preparations Monday morning as soon as the day's lunches had been finished and sent off to housing units across the complex. The kitchen staff turns out more than 20,000 meals per day.

“It’s always a joyous time for us, as you can see, everyone puts the effort in to get it done,” she said as she launched into a flurry of activity.

Working her way down a line of 10-pound boneless roasts, Thompson Pitt generously slathered each one with a dark marinade of spices, including dried sage, thyme, garlic and onion powder mixed with browning sauce.

The seasoned meat slabs are then loaded on tray carts and placed in a fridge for about a day to soak up the flavor before going into an oven, she said. She opened one of the tall ovens to reveal a batch of finished roasts, their skins lightly browned after about three hours of cooking.

![Lans Reid, a kitchen employee on Rikers Island, uses a knife chained to a prep table to prepare for the Thanksgiving meal on Rikers Island](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/10/download..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The turkeys are then set out to cool before being transferred back to a walk-in fridge to await slicing. On the other side of the ovens, just past a row of massive stainless steel caldrons, another set of cooks used deli-style meat slicers to carve the turkey into tender slabs, taking care to set aside any scraps and juices in a large bin nearby.

“That’s what we use to make the gravy,” Thompson Pitt said. “There’s no turkey without a gravy.” The essential sauce is one of the few things that will be cooked on the holiday itself, she said. The turkey and other meal components will mostly be finished by Wednesday, as many of the staffers will be off on Thanksgiving.

Each inmate will be apportioned 4 ounces of meat on Thursday – roughly a slice or two, according to dietitian Christinne Caylan-Linstromberg. They'll also get a serving of familiar Thanksgiving fixings – green beans, yams, stuffing and cranberry sauce – as well as a slice of carrot cake for dessert.

Options exist for those with other dietary needs, including a plant-based protein for vegetarians and a kosher pound cake, she added. Some might object to inmates being given such niceties, especially at Rikers, a jail plagued for years by violence, overcrowding and mismanagement.

Last week, Mayor Zohran Mamdani announced a sweeping plan to shutter the antiquated jail by 2032 – 100 years after it first opened – as the city is all but certain to blow past a legal requirement to close Rikers and replace it with four smaller, more modern jails by 2027.

![New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani has announced plans to shut the prison by 2032](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/10/2306103968..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The jail currently houses about 6,700 people, most of them awaiting trial or serving out shorter sentences.

But Capt. Marc Allen Lorquet, a veteran corrections officer who oversees kitchen operations, says a familiar meal – along with time watching some of the day's football games on TV – goes a long way in maintaining order in the unruly lockup.

“Thanksgiving is like the one day on the calendar they look forward to,” he said. “It’s one of the easier days to work because everybody gets what they’re supposed to. So securitywise, it’s definitely a benefit to have.” Jessus Bailey, an inmate who works in the kitchen, said he didn't expect a holiday meal as he awaits trial on multiple counts of robbery.

“This is a new experience for me,” he said. “I’m definitely gonna enjoy this meal. This is my first time away from my family.”

Like other inmates on kitchen detail, the 44-year-old focuses on cleaning duties and doesn't do any cooking, which is reserved for staff. “It’s pretty hard work, but I enjoy it. I get to learn different things,” Bailey said. “I learned how to work a buffer. I learned how to properly clean the kettles and really sanitize a kitchen.”

Thompson Pitt, meanwhile, will be with family for the holiday. But the native of Jamaica, who has worked at Rikers for 11 years, won't be cooking, and her mom certainly won't be serving turkey, she said.

“All this turkey?” Thompson Pitt said. “I’m done looking at turkey.”
