**Nearly 40,000 women and men in York and North Yorkshire have been victims of domestic violence over the last five years, according to the region's deputy mayor.**

Jo Coles, Labour's deputy mayor for policing, fire and crime, gave that figure as she answered questions during a BBC Radio York interview.

She also spoke about county lines drug dealing, antisocial behaviour, fallout from last year's Langdale Moor wildfire and plans to turn the former RAF Linton-on-Ouse into a centre for more than 1,000 asylum seekers.

![A blue line](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/0a1a/live/383e9170-6247-11f0-b1b9-2b94c1f6d9f3.jpg)

## Tide turning 'slowly' on domestic violence reporting

Coles, answering a question from a domestic violence victim about the level of support available, said it was "devastating" to hear people's stories of abuse.

She said over the last five years, 28,000 women and nearly 11,000 men across York and North Yorkshire had reported an incident of domestic violence.

"I am a local councillor in the City of York, I hear the stories all the time," she explained.

"The numbers are really high. Most importantly, I am determined we do better on all violence against women and girls.

"There is a lot of support out there. We commission IDAS, the independent domestic abuse service. They do an incredible job, supporting thousands of women every year."

Coles acknowledged that "for too long" people had not reported incidents, but data showed victims were now more confident to do so.

"That is a tide that is turning, slowly - and probably too slowly," she added.

## Fire crews 'couldn't have dealt' with moor blaze alone

Coles said "a huge amount" had been learned in the aftermath of the Langdale Moor wildfire last year which an investigation found was likely to have been caused by someone cooking using a campfire or gas burner and went on to burn for weeks.

Working in "partnership" with other public bodies, but also with volunteers and the local community, was "the only way" to deal with large wildfires, she said.

"The farmers, landowners and gamekeepers of the North Yorkshire Moors did an outstanding job and there is no way the fire and rescue service could have dealt with that fire without the support of those partners.

"That's the lesson the mayor and I took to the prime minister for the wildfires summit that happened a couple of months ago: that areas like ours need proper resourcing to be able to deal with these incidents," Coles said.

## No answer over minister visiting Linton-on-Ouse

It has been proposed that an accommodation centre to house up to 1,200 asylum seekers could be built at the former RAF station in Linton-on-Ouse.

Coles was asked whether government ministers had explained their reasons for not visiting the village yet, despite repeated invitations from residents and councillors.

She answered: "No, and we have made multiple representations into the Home Office on behalf of residents, particularly on public safety concerns.

"The mayor has been making similar representations to the Home Office and the Ministry of Defence. We really want this site for housing."

The Home Office has previously said no final decision had been made on the site's future.

## Don't suffer antisocial behaviour in silence

Answering a question about a rise in crime and antisocial behaviour in the York village of Heslington, Coles urged people to report incidents to North Yorkshire Police.

Parish councillor Charlie Stewart described locals being harassed by gangs of youths speeding through the village on e-bikes.

Stewart praised the way the police had handled the problems, but asked whether the response could be sustained in the long-term.

Coles said: "If anyone is experiencing antisocial behaviour, anywhere in the county, please make sure it's being reported to the local neighbourhood policing team.

"If they are reported early, the police can intervene. But, most importantly, if we need to get partners together to deal with a longer-term problem, we can do that as well."

## County lines crackdown

Coles said that "over the last couple of years", 32 so-called county lines drugs operations had been shut down.

"That is as a result of some amazing work the police officers have been doing," she said.

County lines gangs are generally based in cities and urban areas, and use dedicated phone numbers - the line - to sell drugs in smaller towns and rural areas.

Coles added: "These organised criminal gangs move around all the time.

"Drugs are trafficked into our county, probably using some of the most vulnerable young people in our communities.

"This stuff is devastating for the individuals who take it, but it is also devastating for the young people who get exploited into carrying those drugs."
