Teenage girls have said they routinely see jokes about rape brushed aside, amid warnings that trivialising such remarks makes it harder to confront more damaging behaviour.

Data from a new Girlguiding survey reveals that most girls aged 11 to 16 feel catcalling (71 per cent), playground "banter" (72 per cent) and sexist jokes (68 per cent) are routinely dismissed as "not a big deal".

Among teenage girls aged 15 and 16, more than two-thirds (67 per cent) also reported that jokes concerning rape were downplayed, with 432 of the 646 respondents saying that such comments were either somewhat or completely ignored.

The findings follow public concern over resurfaced archive footage, including a 2007 television broadcast showing now-convicted rapist Russell Brand joking about rape, and TV presenter Jonathan Ross making a quip about comedian David Walliams and 15-year-olds several years prior.

Both clips sparked criticism, leading Mr Ross to say that he recognised "how deeply shocking it is to revisit footage of a joke delivered by me at the Comedy Awards in 2004, in which I make light of the notion of Walliams going home with a 15-year-old".

*Great British Bake Off* host Noel Fielding, who appeared alongside Brand in the 2007 clip, added: "Looking back at some of the jokes I made alongside him, I was appalled. They were horrible and inappropriate, and I very much regret them."

Mr Walliams has faced accusations of sexual misconduct, including grooming teenage girls as young as 15, and abusing his position to pursue women. He denies any wrongdoing.

Earlier this month, Brand – who was accused of abusing his celebrity status to prey on women – was jailed for 10-and-a-half years for raping one woman and sexually assaulting three others.

Girlguiding chief executive Felicity Oswald said: “Girls are telling us that misogyny has become so normal it’s brushed off as ‘not a big deal’. When catcalling, sexist jokes and so-called ‘banter’ go unchallenged, girls learn to put up with it.

“Over time, that tells everyone disrespect towards girls and women is acceptable and makes it harder to call out more harmful behaviour when it happens.

“It’s no surprise everyday misogyny is holding girls back and impacting their confidence and that women entering the workplace report sexist behaviours too.

“Girls are clear about what needs to change. They want sexist attitudes challenged and education on consent, respect and healthy relationships taught from a younger age, including in primary schools. This is vital to preventing harmful attitudes from taking hold.”

The survey also found four in 10 girls (40 per cent) want people in positions of power to do more to challenge sexist jokes, language and attitudes.

Just over a third (37 per cent) said that schools should have regular lessons on consent, respect and healthy relationships starting at a younger age and 33 per cent said more must be done to help men and boys understand the impact of their words and actions on girls and women.

Meanwhile, separate research from the White Ribbon charity, which aims to engage men and boys to prevent violence against women and girls, found more than three quarters of women (78 per cent) agreed that sexist jokes contribute to such violence being taken less seriously.

While eight in 10 men said they believed sexist comments were likely to be repeated if nobody challenged them, more than half (57 per cent) of men who had heard sexist comments said they felt unsure about how to respond.

Lynne Elliot, chief executive of White Ribbon UK, said: “Challenging something doesn’t have to mean starting an argument. It can be as simple as changing the tone of a conversation or having a quiet word afterwards.

“All of us can make a positive and very real difference, and we want everyone to feel they have the tools to do that.”
