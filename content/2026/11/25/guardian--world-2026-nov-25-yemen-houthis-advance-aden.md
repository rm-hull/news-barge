Hussein fled his home six years ago after it fell under Houthi control, moving as far south as he could to the port city of Aden, where there was a measure of safety.

The Saudi-backed Yemeni government had made the city its temporary capital; the hundreds of kilometres between it and the Houthis were well fortified.

Today, Hussein is once again considering the painful decision of leaving home. In September, the Iran-aligned Houthis swept aside Yemeni government forces, and now have set their sights on Aden. Houthi fighters are amassing to the north and east of the city, and have begun encircling the government-controlled territory.

![Displaced Yemeni children and adults queue for food aid being unloaded from a truck](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/2f7b3e53201f01a9642b7b2b653a88e00a34e175/0_0_1280_720/master/1280.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

“I fled my home in Sana’a and came here. I don’t think the Houthis plan to take over Aden right now, but if they get closer, I will have no choice but to flee again to Marib or Hadhramaut,” said Hussein, who was speaking under a pseudonym.

Hussein works as a schoolteacher and was formerly a member of the Islah party, an Islamist political party aligned with Saudi Arabia that is in opposition to the Houthi movement. He fears that if the Houthis take control of Aden he could face persecution for his former affiliation.

“The Houthis don’t believe in freedom, they just want to hear one voice, the one they like,” he said.

Hussein is one of many residents and organisations nervously watching the Houthi advance. The militia is still about 50 miles (80km) north of Aden, but local people fear government forces may not be able to resist another Houthi assault, having seen the dramatic collapse of government forces and allied militias in September.

![Houthi fighters walk along a desert road in Yemen, carrying rifles](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/8bfbc7d112f25345de4339e06345ff713bb23e7c/0_0_3652_2066/master/3652.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

Aden has been the seat of the Saudi-backed government of Yemen since 2015. If the Houthis took the city, it would be a huge blow for the government, and a massive failure for the Saudi-led coalition which has for years fought against the Houthis.

The 12-year-long civil war would tilt dramatically in the Houthis’ favour and be a boon for Iran, which has long invested military and political support in the insurgent movement.

The Yemeni government has pulled back many of its fighters to help defend Aden and the surrounding areas, announcing last week that fighters were redeployed from areas along the south-western coast towards the port city.

Saudi Arabia has tried to halt the Houthi advance with a renewed bombing campaign, carrying out hundreds of airstrikes in Yemen since September, killing and wounding 822 civilians, including 181 children, according to statistics from the independent Yemen Data Project.

Houthi authorities have alleged that Saudi Arabia has carried out 5,500 air and missile attacks since July.

![A rescuer in red overalls walks through the rubble of a building destroyed by a Saudi strike in Hodeida.](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/c185c35e96b7698791fe9074d3943e0c212a9d02/0_0_6016_4010/master/6016.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

The Houthis have not announced any intention to take Aden, but have instead been amassing troops north of Kirish, and in al-Bayda, areas which, if taken, would cut off the northern and eastern roads to Aden.

“The buildups in al-Bayda could threaten Aden’s eastern road links through Abyan and Saudi supply lines into the city,” said Mohammed al-Basha, the founder of the US-based risk advisory Basha Report.

“The Houthis appear to be testing how well Aden’s defences will hold. If they collapse, the Houthis could try to seize the city. If government forces hold, the Houthis could try to lay siege to it,” al-Basha added.

![National Security Forces soldiers man a checkpoint in Aden, Yemen, beside a military truck.](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/7434b4fbccf98988d1a2318df68439680892f349/0_0_4096_3072/master/4096.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

Houthi forces have begun striking Aden and its surrounding areas, rupturing the relative safety the city has offered for years. Residents have watched anxiously as explosions grow nearer, and displaced people from surrounding areas have started to arrive in greater numbers.

“Since last week, we’ve started following the news every hour. When I get busy, I tell my daughter to follow it and update me,” said Rami, a resident of Aden who spoke under a pseudonym.

The 53-year-old bitterly recalled his experience during the four-month battle for Aden in 2015, when he was stuck in the crossfire between the Houthis and Yemeni government forces. He, like other people in Aden, is trying to make his own calculation of when, or if, the Houthis will arrive.

International organisations are also making contingency plans in case of a Houthi takeover of the city.

![A displaced woman carries an aid box while men unload boxes from a truck at the camp.](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/f7704ee553e3b12951bc08fed943e3d6f79d9869/0_0_6240_4160/master/6240.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

“We were informed by management that they are issuing termination notices to all staff this week. If the Houthis don’t reach Aden, they will continue operations and renew our contracts; otherwise this is the end of the road,” an aid worker with an international NGO said.

Already, several NGOs were forced to shut down in the city of Taiz after sweeping Houthi gains.

Not all NGOs would leave in the case of a Houthi takeover, but some are unable to work in Houthi-controlled areas either due to fears for the safety of aid workers, or because the US Treasury designated the Houthis a foreign terrorist organisation in 2025.

Yemen has for years faced a severe humanitarian crisis, with about 18.7 million people facing acute food insecurity.

![Displaced Yemeni women hold young children on a hospital stretcher bed, awaiting care.](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/c43460bef18a903999702664e2882138a2b0b298/0_0_6240_4160/master/6240.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

Fighting has only exacerbated conditions, severing transport routes, pushing up the prices of goods and worsening the country’s displacement crisis – including more than 350,000 people displaced since August.

“I earn daily wages, and when there is no work, my six family members don’t get enough food,” said Ahmed, a restaurant worker in his 50s. “I don’t care about politics or wars, and I’m not involved in any of it. I’m just like millions of Yemenis struggling to provide basic necessities for their families.”

For many of Aden’s residents, the prospect of a Houthi takeover has shattered the sense of safety that they sought by coming to the government stronghold in the first place.

“I’m not stocking up on food because I won’t stay in Aden and wait for the battles to reach the city. It isn’t only me; my family is also ready to flee at any time … staying at home won’t be safe and we might get trapped,” Rami said.
