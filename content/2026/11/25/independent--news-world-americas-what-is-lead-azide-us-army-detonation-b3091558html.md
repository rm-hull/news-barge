The U.S. Army has carried out a controlled detonation of a hazardous, deteriorating stockpile of lead azide in Iowa, prompting concerns from local officials and scientists over potential health and environmental impacts.

Lead azide is a highly sensitive chemical compound composed of lead and nitrogen, usually taking the physical form of a colorless or white crystalline powder or needlelike crystals. Serving as an initiating explosive in military munitions, the substance has historically been utilized in detonators designed to set off secondary explosives such as TNT.

Extremely sensitive to shock, heat, impact, and friction, the chemical may explode when dry. Over prolonged periods and amid fluctuating temperatures, lead azide forms larger, highly unstable crystals that further heighten the danger of detonation. The decades-old supply stored in Iowa had begun crystallizing, creating a critical risk of spontaneous detonation.

![Debris flies into the air after the Army detonated several tons of highly-volatile, degraded lead azide on the grounds of the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant on November 25, 2026 in Middletown, Iowa](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/15/2306835116..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Once broken down, lead acts as a toxic heavy metal capable of damaging the brain, nervous system, kidneys, heart, and other organs, while also causing developmental damage. Given that lead exposure poses both immediate risks and longer-term environmental hazards, researchers and officials are questioning potential lead levels in surrounding air, land, and water sources that could impact residents, crops, cattle, and regional wildlife following the explosion.

Owing to its hazards, the military discontinued using lead azide in the 1980s. According to the Army, safety preparations ahead of Wednesday’s detonation included protective barriers around the blast zone to reduce the spread of the resulting plume, alongside ballistic gels designed to absorb lead dust.
