Kilmar Ábrego García, the Salvadoran national whose mistaken deportation turned him into a symbol of Donald Trump’s mass deportation efforts, has landed in Spain.

Ábrego García arrived in Madrid on Wednesday after leaving the United States of his own volition. Prior to his departure, Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) agents met him at the airport and removed his ankle monitor, according to We Are CASA, a Maryland-based advocacy group.

The interaction, the group noted, was “a powerful symbol that ends a chapter marked by illegal deportation, imprisonment, abuse at Cecot, retaliatory prosecution, and government surveillance.”

As he made his way through the Madrid airport, Ábrego García did not speak to reporters, instead raising his fist in the air as he climbed into a waiting van.

Trump administration approves Elon Musk’s plan for datacenters in space

Edward Helmore

US federal regulators approved a constellation of datacenters to process data in space today, saying that they would “support America’s booming super intelligence economy”.

The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) said in a statement that regulators had approved 1m Starmind orbital satellites, or compute modules, built by Elon Musk’s SpaceX. It is the first such authorization in US history.

The FCC said that the authorization “promises to bring the human race closer to a Kardashev II civilization – one that can harness the full power of the sun”.

“America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence,” said the FCC chair, Brendan Carr.

Boosters of the plan have argued that datacenters in space are essential as the AI industry grows and their cost on Earth on the power grid and water supply are high.

“By directly harnessing near-constant solar power with little operating or maintenance costs, these satellites will achieve transformative cost and energy efficiency while significantly reducing the environmental impact associated with terrestrial datacenters,” SpaceX had argued to the FCC.

But critics say the technology for such space datacenters is still being developed, and their deployment is a long way off.

Under the license approved today, SpaceX must begin deployment no later than November 2033, deploy 10% of ⁠the maximum number – or 100,000 – no later than November 2035 and deploy 50% by November 2038, ​the FCC said.

'I feel free of everything evil': Kilmar Abrego speaks to reporters just hours after arriving in Spain

Shrai Popat

Kilmar Abrego Garcia told reporters on Wednesday that he feels “free of everything evil that has followed me up until this point”.

“I’m happy this time because this time I’m leaving with my freedom, not like before when they took me out inhumanely,” Abrego Garcia said on a press call just hours after arriving in Madrid, Spain.

He added: “The government did did not decide where my story continues. My family and I decide our future.”

Abrego Garcia, a Salvadoran immigrant who had been living in Maryland, was deported by the Trump administration to Cecot last, the country’s notorious anti-terrorism mega-prison, even though a court had barred his removal to El Salvador because of gang threats against his family.

“I’m glad that the Trump administration did not succeed at what they wanted to do,” said Senator Chris Van Hollen, the Maryland Democrat who traveled to El Salvador to visit Abrego Garcia in April last year. “They wanted to leave him there to rot for the rest of his life. Instead, we saw our judicial system work alongside a strong legal team to produce this outcome, where Kilmar Abrego Garcia is able to chart his own future.”

Van Hollen, who is vying to become Senate majority leader when Democrats regain control of Congress next year, has made Abrego Garcia’s case and opposition to the administration’s sweeping deportation crackdown central to his vision for the party.

“Abrego Garcia’s journey is an example of our Constitution working despite the attacks of the Trump administration,” Van Hollen added. He said he had spoken with the Spanish ambassador to the United States, Ángeles Moreno Bau, and briefed her on “all the wrongs that have been committed by the Trump administration against Kilmar Abrego Garcia”, adding that he was pleased Abrego Garcia was now in Spain and able to continue telling his story”.

Abrego Garcia, who is married to a US citizen and shares a young son with her, was returned to the United States following a supreme court ruling. The administration has since sought to deport him again, exploring transfers to several African countries.

“Instead of flying ICE Air, he flew United Airlines,” said Simon Sandoval-Moshenberg, Abrego Garcia’s co-counsel.“Instead of being in shackles, he was in economy plus. And there’s really no other way to see this other than Mr Abrego Garcia’s decision to hold strong throughout the last two years of this process.”

Sandoval-Moshenberg said his legal team would eventually be able to disclose Abrego Garcia’s next destination after Spain, but added that a pathway exists for him to obtain permanent residency in the United States in the near future.

“This is a short-term absence from the US,” Sandoval-Moshenberg said.

While Abrego Garcia did not take questions on Wednesday, he underscored the need to “keep fighting”.

“I’m happy. My family’s with me. There’s a tremendous community with me,” he said.

The BBC has lodged an urgent appeal to have Donald Trump’s $10bn defamation lawsuit against it thrown out, after internal surprise at a judge’s decision to allow the case to go to trial.

The corporation is currently heading to court next year over a Panorama documentary that spliced together two parts of Trump’s speech made before the 6 January insurrection at the US Capitol.

While the broadcaster has apologised over the edit, which featured in the 2024 programme Trump: A Second Chance, it has always denied that the film defamed Trump. It has also said it was never broadcast in Florida, where Trump’s legal action is being heard.

The federal judge Jeffrey T Kuntz, a Trump appointee, ruled earlier this month that the lawsuit could proceed, denying the BBC’s attempt to have the case thrown out. However, the BBC has now appealed to a higher court, the United States court of appeals for the 11th circuit, restating that the Florida court has no jurisdiction to hear the case.

In a legal submission seen by the Guardian, the BBC’s lawyers state that the documentary “never aired in the United States”. It argues that Kuntz found the Florida court held jurisdiction over the BBC “despite the complete lack of evidence that anyone accessed it there, as both Florida law and this circuit’s precedent require.”

The move is a sign of the BBC’s desperation to have the case dismissed, given the time, expense and political headache it is causing, while it is also making huge cuts at home and holding talks with the UK government over its future funding.

“We are surprised and disappointed by the court’s decision that this case should be heard in Florida,” said a BBC spokesperson. “As we have made clear from the start, the BBC broadcast this documentary in the UK – and UK only. We did not show it in Florida, nor did any other broadcaster.

US inflation hits highest since May after prices climb 3.8% over the last year

Lauren Aratani

Consumer prices rose an annualized 3.8% in October, according to new inflation data released today, the highest since May and the last inflation reading before a crucial US Federal Reserve board meeting next month.

Prices rose 0.3% from September to October amid soaring energy prices, according to the Personal Consumption Expenditure index, the Fed’s preferred inflation measure from the commerce department’s Bureau of Economic Analysis.

The data comes after the Bureau of Labor Statistics separately reported prices climbed 0.4% from September to October, higher than economists had anticipated.

Energy prices, including gas prices at the pump, have cooled, going from a high of $4.41 a gallon in early October to $3.61 a gallon by mid-November, according to AAA.

In early December, the Fed will hold the last of its interest rate-setting meetings for the year, and officials are widely expected to hike interest rates for the second time this year. The Fed has set a target inflation rate of 2%, a level not seen since February 2021. Higher interest rates make it more expensive to borrow money, slowing price increases and raising the price of loans like mortgages and student debt.

Though officials held rates steady at the last board meeting in late October, minutes from the meeting showed that some expressed concern about inflation’s stickiness.

“A few of them warned that further delay risked allowing high inflation to become entrenched or necessitating a larger and more disruptive tightening later,” the minutes said.

The move would come at a tumultuous time for the US economy. A new survey from the Conference Board released on Tuesday showed consumer confidence had ticked up in November to its highest level since January 2025, probably driven by the shake-up from the midterm elections.
