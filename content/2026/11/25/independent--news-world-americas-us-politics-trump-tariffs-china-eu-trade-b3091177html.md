The Trump administration is expected to announce plans soon to raise tariffs on China, the European Union, and 14 other economies, according to two people familiar with the matter.

The U.S. Trade Representative's Office is expected to release the results of a months-long tariff investigation into industrial subsidies by the 16 economies in the coming days, according to people who were not authorized to comment publicly and spoke on condition of anonymity.

They stressed that the timing of the tariff rollout could still change. The White House and USTR did not respond to requests for comment.

![Auto workers rally against President Donald Trump's proposed tariffs on auto imports and the impact on auto makers and their surrounding communities, during a news conference on Capitol Hill](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/06/12/48/GettyImages-1001877180.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Plans for the new tariffs are part of an effort to replace broader duties the administration set last year, which the Supreme Court later struck down. The plans were reported earlier on Tuesday by Bloomberg and The Wall Street Journal.

The tariffs respond to what the White House has called excessive manufacturing output in those countries, boosted by subsidies, that has flooded the market with lower-priced goods that undercut American companies.

The anticipated action is a second step in the administration’s efforts to replace the duties announced in April 2025, which the Supreme Court ruled unconstitutional in February. In July, the White House slapped 60 trading partners with levies of 10% to 12.5%, charging that they have inadequately enforced bans on goods produced by forced labor.

The anticipated trade action demonstrates that the Trump administration is still willing to impose import taxes despite a sweeping loss for the Republican Party in this month’s midterm elections, fueled largely by affordability concerns as inflation remains elevated.

![Secretary of Commerce Howard Lutnick holds a chart as President Donald Trump delivers remarks on tariffs in the Rose Garden at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., April 2, 2025](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/04/11/2026-11-02T110037Z_737185847_RC2XPDARWXOC_RTRMADP_3_USA-ELECTION-ECONOMY-VIBES.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

At the same time, the new duties could test how the Democrats will respond to tariffs imposed unilaterally by Trump. Even with majorities in both the House and Senate, Trump could veto any legislation that overturns or waters down the tariffs.

The excess industrial capacity probes were initiated under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, which allows the president to levy tariffs against nations that discriminate against U.S. companies or commerce.

The anticipated tariff levels were not immediately clear, but would be added to the 10% to 12.5% tariffs announced earlier this year.

Many countries, including China, protested those tariffs, which took effect just as the clock ran out on temporary levies Trump had imposed after the Supreme Court in February struck down sweeping “reciprocal” tariffs he levied on nearly every U.S. trade partner.

The expected move comes as Trump visited the Chinese tech hub of Shenzhen last week for his third face-to-face engagement with Chinese President Xi Jinping this year. Xi’s expected to travel to Doral, Florida, next month for the Group of 20 Summit, which Trump is hosting.

While Trump has moved to build a closer relationship with Xi, the yawning trade deficit remains a significant irritant in the U.S.-China relationship.

Massive capacity in a slew of Chinese industries, from autos to solar panels, cement and steel manufacturing, has drawn increased attention from Beijing’s trading partners in recent years.

Although China’s own leaders have prioritized rebalancing the economy, slowing domestic demand has prompted companies to expand into overseas markets. Surging exports pushed China’s trade surplus to a record of nearly $1.2 trillion last year.
