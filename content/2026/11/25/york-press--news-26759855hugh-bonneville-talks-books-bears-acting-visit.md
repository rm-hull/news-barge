## Hugh Bonneville charms Malton audience with tales of Paddington and spies

Acting legend Hugh Bonneville talks to a full house at the Wesley Centre in Malton *(Image: Nick Fletcher)*

'UNFORTUNATELY Paddington bear will not be joining us tonight', Mr Brown announces to an audience of all ages during a visit to Malton last week.

"It's the first question I always get asked," says Mr Brown, who of course is acting legend Hugh Bonneville, to a full house at the Wesley Centre as part of a whistle-stop tour of Yorkshire.

Were we disappointed? Well only for a second as the Peruvian bear's adoptive British dad owned the room with an evening of laughter, anecdotes and stories including his preference for jam over marmalade.

This was Hugh's first visit to Malton despite it being mentioned in the hugely successful period drama Downton Abbey.

Characters in the series, which is based near Ripon, visit the town to go to the fat market, although Hugh, who plays Lord Grantham, has no idea what that is.

But he was delighted to be in Malton and looking forward to exploring the town the following day.

For Friday evening though, Hugh is here to talk about his latest books and long-standing career as an actor.

Hugh's second children's book, Rory Sparkes And The Spy Who Loved Tea, follows on from last year's Rory Sparkes And The Elephant In The Room.

In this new adventure, Rory finds himself caught up in a mystery involving secret messages and some suspiciously popular tea.

Hugh's foray into fiction comes after the self-penned 2022 memoir Playing Under the Piano, which in turn inspired the Roy Sparkes' stories.

Growing up in Blackheath, London, the youngest of three, Hugh's parents have lived abroad for a period and had a dressing-up box filled with items from the travels, which he loved to dive into.

"I'd invited my pals round to be in my plays, they would be the servants while I would play the king or Sultan of somewhere.

"In turn, I stood on the football pitch between two white poles while a series of balls flew past me."

Hugh's career started on the stage with the National Theatre followed by the Royal Shakespeare Company.

He then went on to star in films including Tomorrow Never Dies, Notting Hill and Viceroy's House, along with TV roles in everything from The Gold to Twenty Twelve and W1A.

Hugh says writing the children's books was a continuation of his story-telling as an actor.

"I've been story telling all my life. I often think in the term of film scenes which influences how I write - what angle would the camera be at, would I be able to see everyone in the room.

"I was told to write what is in my head and ask 'what would a 10-year-old enjoy', not what the market place would like.

Both Rory Sparkes books are based on true incidents of when Hugh was growing up

Rory Sparkes and the Elephant in the Room, follows a schoolboy dreamer whose life is turned upside down by the arrival of the circus.

Hugh recalls: "When I was a child, the circus used to come to town and circus kids sometimes came to school on the elephant, which is about the most exotic mode of transport ever seen in south-east London."

While inspiration for his second novel came from Hugh's mother.

"I was at my publishers with a team of around six or seven in the room when they wanted to know what my next book was going to be about. I sort of froze because I hadn't really thought about it and looked around the room for ideas. I saw my memoir and recalled I had mentioned that my mum used to work for M16 later in life, so that was the spark - spies I said!"

Hugh admits he would be a 'pretty rubbish spy' as he can never remember where his keys are.

"I'd probably come or with 'I'm a spy' to people as well," he adds.

Hugh is now following in the footsteps of the much-loved children's author Michael Bond whose books the incredibly popular Paddington films are based and introducing a whole new generation to the lovable bear.

"I had met Michael and was very nervous when I got the first script as I was concerned a film may ruin the spirit of Paddington he had created, but I was laughing from the start and thought it's in good hands here.

"Indeed Michael appears in the first Paddington film. As Paddington is travelling through London he passes a cafe and Michael is seen raising a glass of wine to the bear."

Hugh says the appeal of Paddington to people of all ages is partially because we can all relate to him.

"We have all been a Paddington at some point in our lives, be that starting a new school or job or visiting a different country and we have all needed friendship from a stranger.

"Paddington is a great role model who always sees the best in other people, which we can all learn from."

Hugh has spoken out on the situation in the Middle East, calling for an end to humans suffering in Gaza, but has not desire to enter politics.

"Absolutely not," he says, " I certainly don't have the patience, but I do admire anyone who wants to become part of it."

Looking ahead, the story for the fourth Paddington film is in development while Hugh also stars in the upcoming British comedy Dog Person with Ryedale's own James Norton. In the new year, he is heading to Australia to return to the stage.

As children - and adults - queue to buy signed copies of Rory Sparkes And The Spy Who Loved Tea, along with a quick selfie, 'disappointment' at the absence of Paddington seems to be forgotten.

But if you do bump into the little bear, just don't tell him that Mr Brown prefers jam to marmalade.
