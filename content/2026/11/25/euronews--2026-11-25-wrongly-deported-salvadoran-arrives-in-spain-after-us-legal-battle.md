## The Salvadoran, deported "by mistake" to a prison in El Salvador in 2025, has brought an end to 20 months of litigation in the United States, with Madrid as the first stop in what his lawyers describe as a temporary stay abroad.

Kilmar Abrego García, a Salvadoran man whose wrongful deportation from the United States put him at the centre of a controversy over US President Donald Trump's immigration crackdown, arrived in Madrid on Wednesday.

His case became a focal point in the debate over Trump's mass deportation policy in 2025, raising concerns about migrants' rights to challenge deportation orders and the administration's compliance with court rulings.

Wearing sunglasses and a baseball cap, Abrego García made his way to a vehicle at Madrid airport without speaking to reporters gathered at the scene, according to AFP journalists.

"While details remain private, Kilmar is planning on hosting various international speaking engagements, with stops planned in different countries," We Are CASA, a US immigrant advocacy group which has been supporting him, said in a statement.

The Salvadoran ultimately left the United States voluntarily on a commercial flight bound for Spain.

"I am leaving happy, I am leaving with my freedom," he said in a statement released by We Are CASA.

The US Department of Homeland Security (DHS) confirmed his departure from the country in a statement, in which it labelled Abrego García a "trafficker, wife beater, gang member, and child predator."

"We are delighted to announce that Kilmar Abrego Garcia has self-deported from the United States to Spain," said Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, quoted in the statement.

His lawyers, for their part, welcomed the fact that their client had been able to leave "on his own terms" after his long-running dispute with the US immigration authorities.

One of his legal representatives, Simón Sandoval-Moshenberg, said the departure brought an end to a deportation campaign he described as "retaliation". He also stressed that the move to Spain is temporary and that Abrego García, who is married to a US citizen, hopes to return to the United States once Donald Trump leaves the White House.

## **Deported "by mistake" to Bukele's mega-prison**

His case began in March 2025, when federal agents arrested Abrego García in the street. Days later he was deported to El Salvador and sent to the Terrorism Confinement Centre (CECOT), a controversial maximum-security mega-prison built on the orders of President Nayib Bukele and intended to house alleged members of criminal gangs.

The deportation went ahead even though there was a court order expressly prohibiting his deportation to El Salvador, where he had received threats from armed groups before leaving the country. The US government itself later acknowledged that it had been "a mistake", although it initially resisted facilitating his return.

Back in the United States, Abrego García reported having suffered beatings, sleep deprivation and psychological torture during the almost three months he was held in CECOT.

The legal battle reached the US Supreme Court, which ordered the Trump administration to facilitate his return. The authorities eventually brought him back to the country, although they did so in the context of a criminal investigation that led to an indictment over his alleged involvement in a migrant-smuggling network.

However, a federal judge ultimately dismissed all the charges, ruling that the indictment was in fact motivated by a desire to retaliate against the Salvadoran for having opposed his deportation.

## **The Trump administration tried to deport him to Africa**

As the criminal case progressed, the US government continued trying to deport him from the country. The authorities considered sending him to Uganda, Ghana and Eswatini, but failed to secure agreement from those countries to receive him. Meanwhile, Abrego García applied for asylum to prevent his deportation.

The dispute became one of the most high-profile cases under Trump's immigration policy and highlighted the differences between the executive and judicial branches over the limits of presidential powers regarding deportations.

Throughout the process, the US president and several members of his administration publicly accused the Salvadoran of belonging to Mara Salvatrucha, also known as MS-13, as well as of human trafficking and assaulting his wife, all allegations that were echoed in the statement published on Tuesday by the DHS announcing his departure from the country.

However, these accusations were called into question by the courts. In fact, federal judge Paula Xinis, who oversaw part of the litigation in Maryland, went so far as to open a contempt-of-court inquiry against some of the prosecutors involved in the case for allegedly repeatedly disobeying her orders.

Now, with Madrid as his first stop, Abrego García plans to travel to other countries to take part in various events and speak about his experience, according to Sandoval-Moshenberg.
