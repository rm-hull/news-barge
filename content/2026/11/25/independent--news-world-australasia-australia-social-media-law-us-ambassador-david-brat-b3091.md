The US has criticised Australia’s proposed sweeping online safety legislation that requires social media companies to take steps to protect users from serious harm.

The US argued that the legislation’s vaguely defined rules and penalties of up to five per cent of global revenue could force American technology companies to censor lawful speech and impose disproportionate compliance costs on them.

The legislation, negotiated between prime minister Anthony Albanese’s Labor government and the Greens, would allow individuals to sue social media platforms if breaches of their duty of care cause harm.

It would also require platforms to let users opt out of algorithm-driven feeds, assess risks posed by addictive design features and establish a digital complaints ombudsman.

US ambassador David Brat suggested that Washington could take action over the bill, which is expected to pass the Senate.

In a social media post, he said freedom of expression was fundamental to a free society and that “protecting it is a US priority”.

“Today I met Minister for Communications Anika Wells and laid out the Trump Administration’s well-known objections to the Digital Duty of Care Legislation. Vaguely defined ‘harms’, backed by penalties of up to five per cent of global revenue, will push platforms to censor lawful speech,” he said in a statement shared on X.

“As Australia’s largest foreign investor, the US is concerned with regulations that impose disproportionate compliance burdens on American companies relative to competitors.”

The proposed reforms also include stronger privacy protections and expanded powers for Australia’s eSafety Commissioner to remove harmful content, including AI-generated sexualised images and material promoting violence, harassment and hatred against women.

“This is seismic reform, this ‌is urgent reform and this is reform we will deliver to Australians in parliament this week,” Ms Wells told reporters earlier.

“That will make it abundantly clear that this is not about disagreeing with feminism or about expressing an opinion,” she said. “It is about protecting children from content that promotes hatred of women, violence, coercion, intimidation, harassment, death.”

Greens communications spokeswoman Sarah Hanson-Young said: “Big tech will face big consequences when they fail to keep Australians safe online. Social media giants will pay billions of dollars for the harm they cause.

“Today the parliament is drawing a line in the sand. The era of big tech profiting off the harm of Australians and getting away with it is over.”

Ms Wells had previously said that US president Donald Trump’s Truth Social platform would fall under the new laws, while stressing that the government remained committed to “constructive dialogue” with Washington.

She reiterated that position after meeting Mr Brat on Tuesday, saying: “I think we would say there are points of differences, and we’re ironing them out.”

Mr Albanese said in parliament: “It’s about Australia ⁠taking action on the side of parents and children, not foreign-owned tech companies.

“World-leading reform and we are getting it done. Just like we have led the world when it comes to the ⁠under-16s social media ban. This is the next step.”
