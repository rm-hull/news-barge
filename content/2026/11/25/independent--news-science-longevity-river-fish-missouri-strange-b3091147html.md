A fish native to the Upper Missouri River in Montana lives up to 130 years, scientists have found in a new study which triples previous estimates of the species’ lifespan of around 40 years.

The blue sucker fish is native to 23 US states, including Minnesota and Montana, but until now a comprehensive life history for the species remained elusive.

Researchers from the University of Minnesota Duluth sought to examine the species’ population distribution and longevity of the fish using recently refined aging techniques.

They collected and processed samples of close to 50 fish from a population in Montana.

Scientists found that some of the blue sucker fish alive today hatched in the late 1800s, with many specimen caught in the study even carrying markers of nuclear bombs tested in the mid-to-late 1950s.

The findings rewrite our understanding of the species, whose lifespan is now known to be often greater than that of humans.

![Blue sucker fish](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/6/27/Blue-sucker-fish.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

In the study, scientists collected otoliths, or earstones, from inside the cranium of the fish.

These tiny, stone-like structures grow throughout the fish’s lifetime, forming a new layer each year.

Researchers thinly section the otoliths to determine the age of each individual specimen.

Data from the otoliths revealed that the blue sucker fish’s lifespan far exceed previous estimates.

In the fish previously thought to live to about 40 years, “we were seeing the population had ages up to 130 years old,” said Alec Lackmann, an author of the study published in the journal *Nature Communications*.

To confirm the finding a technique called “bomb radiocarbon dating” was used, in which scientists look for a specific marker in organisms that lived through a nuclear bomb explosion.

Thermonuclear bombs tested in the mid to late 1950s and early 1960s more than doubled the amount of radioactive form of carbon in the atmosphere.

This radiocarbon was absorbed by plants during photosynthesis and passed up the food chain into animals, humans, and marine life.

Scientists look for levels of this radiocarbon in a tooth, tree ring, or fish ear bone, and compare it against historical atmospheric or marine reference to determine when a tissue was formed.

![Blue sucker fish spotted in upper Missouri river](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/6/31/Blue-sucker-fish-spotted-in-upper-Missouri-river.avif?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

With a redefined lifespan of 130 years, the Montana species becomes the fourth centenarian sucker fish discovered, joining bigmouth buffalo, smallmouth buffalo, and black buffalo.

It is also one of the oldest-living vertebrates in North America, according to the latest study.

“To put a timeline on some of those older fish: the oldest one hatched around two years after Montana became a state, around the dawn of the automobile, like 20 years before the *Titanic* sank – it's just wild,” said Luke Holmquist, a Missouri River fisheries biologist.

The blue sucker fish seems to have an unusually slow-paced life history, taking years to reach its full size.

![Montana Fish, Wildlife & Parks personnel Maggie Wallace, Mike Schilz, and Zach Shattuck (left to right) collecting blue suckers](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/6/57/Montana-Fish-Wildlife-Parks-personnel-Maggie-Wallace-Mike-Schilz-and-Zach-Shattuck-(left-to-right)-c.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Scientists are yet to find the precise biological mechanisms that give the fish its exceptional longevity.

Even so, researchers have raised conservation concern for the species across much of its range.

In surveys over the last three decades, virtually few young blue sucker have been found coming into the population, suggesting that the population is under threat from habitat fragmentation caused by large dams.

The study found that a vast majority of the fish caught in the recent surveys belonged to generations born before the completion of major dams in the Missouri river.

Scientists call for future studies to assess the connectivity between spawning and rearing habitats on the blue sucker fish to help conserve the species.
