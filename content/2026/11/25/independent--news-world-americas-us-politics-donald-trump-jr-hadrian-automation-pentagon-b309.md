The Pentagon is reportedly weighing a $15 billion federal loan for Hadrian Automation, a defense manufacturing startup based in California -- and backed by a venture capital firm tied to Donald Trump Jr.

If finalized, the award would rank among the largest government loans in U.S. history and would be roughly double Hadrian’s entire valuation from a funding round this summer, *ProPublica* first reported on Tuesday. It would also be 10 times the size of the largest loan issued during the Pentagon’s recent lending expansion.

Records and interviews reviewed by *ProPublica* show the loan would fund the construction of a dozen “automated factories” in partnership with other defense and commercial companies to scale up production of munitions, drones and precision components.

The discussions come weeks before Democrats take control of both chambers of Congress, where party leaders have pledged to scrutinize federal contracts and awards benefiting firms connected to the Trump family.

Hadrian Automation has raised more than $1 billion in private capital in recent months. In August, the company announced a financing round featuring “major participation by 1789 Capital,” the venture capital firm Trump Jr. joined as a partner after President Donald Trump’s election victory in November 2024.

![The Pentagon is reportedly considering a $15 billion loan for a defense manufacturing startup partially owned by a venture capital firm connected to Donald Trump Jr](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/16/2294060524..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

In a LinkedIn post celebrating the August investment, 1789 Capital wrote: “This is just the beginning.”

A spokesperson for the firm told *ProPublica* it held a “minority stake” in Hadrian, without disclosing the exact percentage, and maintained the firm did not interfere in government contracting decisions.

“Our thesis is to invest in American companies that are rebuilding domestic industrial capacity and reducing dependence on China for critical supply chains that underpin our economy,” 1789 Capital said in a statement. “Hadrian embodies the opportunity we see to strengthen America’s productive capabilities while pursuing compelling returns for our investors.”

Trump Jr.’s attorney, Alan Garten, denied his client had any involvement with Hadrian’s operations or federal loan discussions.

“Don is a private citizen who has been building businesses and investing in companies since long before his father first ran for public office,” Garten told ProPublica. “He holds no government position and has never sought to influence government policies, regulations or decisions to advance or benefit his personal business interests.”

![If approved, the $15 billion award to California startup Hadrian Automation would be roughly double the company’s entire valuation and 10 times larger than any loan issued during the Pentagon’s recent lending spree](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/22/16/16/GettyImages-1445610650.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The Pentagon also denied that political connections factored into its lending decisions, while disputing the reported $15 billion figure and characterizing talks with Hadrian as being in “early stages.”

“Outside affiliations, investors, or political connections play absolutely no role in the Department’s funding decisions,” the Pentagon said in a statement, adding that multibillion-dollar deals with other companies had already been agreed to but not yet announced.

A White House official similarly told *ProPublica* that Hadrian initiated contact with the Pentagon and said, “It is at an extremely early stage.”

However, internal defense documents reviewed by *ProPublica* detailed specific loan terms under consideration, assessments of merits and risks, and references to the August funding round involving 1789 Capital, as well as the “White House and DoW leadership’s expressed need for advanced manufacturing.”

Campaign filings show Hadrian’s CEO donated nearly $500,000 to Republican groups in July.

![The proposed financing would fund the construction of a dozen AI- and robotics-powered factories to expand domestic production of munitions, drones and precision defense components](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/13/01/2026-10-30T032743Z_727797601_RC2CDOARUJZ2_RTRMADP_3_USA-ELECTION-FLORIDA.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The proposed loan would be issued through the Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital. Congress barred the office from backing purely military ventures when it established the unit under the Biden administration to shore up domestic supply chains against China.

While Pentagon documents note Hadrian’s proposed factories would focus on military munitions and components, they state the facilities could “pivot rapidly to commercial applications.”

Former defense officials told *ProPublica* that a $15 billion loan to a relatively untested contractor could violate long-standing safeguards for taxpayer funds. Because the government would finance the company’s expansion and award it defense contracts to pay back the loan, officials warned the arrangement would create an unhealthy interdependence and give Hadrian an unfair advantage over competitors.

“OSC is clearly a powerful tool to advance national security. But its ability to invest and access to long-term funding are tenuous,” a former defense official familiar with the unit told the publication. “Some senior leaders seemingly want to use it as a pot of money for deals that exceed its mandate.”

![Pentagon officials disputed the $15 billion figure and cast discussions with Hadrian Automation as preliminary, insisting that ‘outside affiliations, investors, or political connections’ played no role in funding decisions](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/19/36/GettyImages-689681.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Founded in 2020 by Australian-born entrepreneur Chris Power — who moved to the U.S. with $6,000 — Hadrian aims to solve skilled labor shortages in aerospace and defense manufacturing by pairing robotics with its proprietary artificial intelligence software, Opus.

Power told reporters that traditional defense contractors lacked sufficient trained workers at a March ribbon-cutting ceremony for a new submarine parts factory in Alabama.

“The number-one problem that the Navy’s identified is we can’t find enough skilled workers, so we have to automate our way out of this problem,” Power said. “We need to automate and have people running multiple robots in a new way of doing things versus replacing jobs.”

Hadrian, which employs an estimated few hundred workers and has recruited staff from SpaceX, Tesla, Anduril, Raytheon and Palantir, has opened new facilities in Arizona and Alabama this year and secured a Navy commitment of up to $900 million in March to build submarine components.

Despite its rapid fundraising, Hadrian has released minimal public data verifying the output of its automated factories. In congressional testimony last year, Power said only that the company had produced parts for “missile systems, fighter jets, spacecraft and naval vessels.”
