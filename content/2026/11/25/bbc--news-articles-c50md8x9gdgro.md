**The UK could be included in the EU's proposed scheme to protect car manufacturing in Europe without Britain having to impose import taxes on Chinese electric cars, under draft plans.**

The proposal has been drawn up by Ireland, which currently holds the EU's rotating presidency, and will be discussed by EU governments.

It comes after previous suggestions the EU would demand the UK align its tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles as a condition of joining the scheme.

Senior figures in the British government have been talking increasingly positively about the potential to join on terms London would be willing to sign up to, and are cautiously optimistic about this latest move.

The "Made in Europe" scheme, known formally as the Industrial Accelerator Act, aims to protect EU manufacturing with restrictions on goods from outside countries, with a particular focus on both China and the car industry.

The idea is potentially dangerous to the UK, as being outside the EU would mean being exposed to restrictions principally intended for others.

Germany and France had previously agreed a joint position under which UK involvement would have been conditional on matching EU import restrictions and tariffs on imports of Chinese electric cars and hybrids.

Alignment in this area with the EU would increase the price of increasingly popular Chinese cars in the UK, as well as raising the prospect of a potential Chinese retaliation with its own tariffs imposed on British-made cars.

## 'Real concern'

Now Ireland, as first reported by the Financial Times, external, is suggesting the UK could join Made in Europe without the need to match the EU's tariffs.

The argument made in favour of this is the UK and EU car industries are so heavily integrated and interdependent.

The proposal, which would also apply to Switzerland and countries in the European Economic Area (EEA) such as Norway, would depend on the non-EU countries granting the EU access to their subsidy and procurement schemes.

However, the idea is still at its early stages. It will first be discussed by EU ambassadors, before it is considered by competition ministers from the EU's member states at a meeting next month.

The proposal would also have to be accepted by the European Commission and the European Parliament.

Andy Burnham has previously pushed for UK inclusion in the scheme to avoid "collateral damage" to British industry.

Speaking at Prime Minister's Questions on Wednesday, he claimed the government had successfully "removed the threat" from the Made in Europe scheme as part of talks over the EU deals he agreed last week.

Pressed later whether he had overstated how advanced talks were on the EU side, the prime minister's spokesman said: "We continue to engage on these issues with Europe in a genuine spirit of cooperation."

A spokesperson for the Irish presidency said it had proposed a "pragmatic and balanced way forward" on the scheme, having "listened closely to all member states".
