Campaigners are calling for reform after a survey of hundreds of schools found “widespread non-inclusive practices” during acts of ‘religious observance’ in non-denominational schools.

Data gathered by HSS through FOI requests revealed that such activities are dominated by representatives of Christian faith and ideology, with a large minority of cases included pupils participating in acts of prayer and worship.

Some councils refused to release the information, with Glasgow City, South Lanarkshire, and West Dunbartonshire stating that it would cost too much to do so, and Midlothian arguing that the data does not exist.

Schools in Scotland are expected to provide opportunities for acts of religious observance during the school year. The Scottish Government says that such activity must be “sensitive to all individual spiritual needs and beliefs”, but critics say that in practice RO tends to prioritise a focus on Christian beliefs and rituals.

According to the most recent census data, a majority of Scots – and an even larger proportion of young people – state that they have no religion.

The survey report from HSS contains information covering nine local authorities: Aberdeenshire, Dundee City, East Dunbartonshire, East Lothian, Falkirk, Fife, Highland, and Scottish Borders.

Over the course of 2025, schools in these council areas carried out more than 2,200 acts of religious observance, with around two-thirds “led or co-led by a representative of the Christian faith.” Across 750 schools, just 16 activities were “led by representatives of a non-Christian belief group".

The report goes on to state that “four in ten activities (38%) asked pupils to affirm their belief in a God through prayer, worship, or hymns".

The survey uncovered significant variation between councils. In both Highland and Aberdeenshire, more than half of religious observance activities featured “acts of worship”, while in Fife and Dundee City the figure was below 10%.

In addition, the data gathered by HSS confirms that a proportion of pupils are being withdrawn from religious observance, with around one third of schools reporting that this had happened.

The Scottish Government recently updated guidance around religious observance in schools, introducing a right for children to opt in against their parents wishes, but refusing to allow them to opt out.

The Children and Young People’s Commissioner for Scotland has clearly stated that school pupils should be able to withdraw themselves from acts of religious observance if they wish.

Janine Hunt, CEO of Humanist Society Scotland: “This report proves conclusively that the stories about non-inclusive religious observance that we shared in our 2025 report *Preaching is Not Teaching* are not the exception but the norm. A significant majority of Religious Observance in Scotland's non-denominational schools is still delivered by Christian representatives, with almost 40% involving acts of worship. In the secular space of a non-denominational school, no pupil should be asked to affirm their belief in a God during Religious Observance.

“We are clear: faith communities can and should make a positive contribution to school life. In a climate of growing hostility towards minoritised groups, Religious Observance should provide opportunities to promote understanding of different belief systems. It was particularly shocking to discover how rarely Muslim, Sikh, Hindu, Jewish, and Pagan representatives are invited to lead Religious Observance.

“Schools also need to recognise that most children’s values and morals aren’t grounded in faith. The Scottish Government says Religious Observance should reflect the diversity of school communities. We have learned that it isn’t clear what opportunities non-religious pupils are being offered to reflect on their own values and beliefs. Children should be able to ‘see themselves’ in Religious Observance, and this won’t happen when activities are consistently delivered through the prism of Christianity.

“Finally, as the UN Committee on the Rights of the Child has repeatedly called for, Scottish school pupils should be able to opt themselves out of Religious Observance if they so wish. Not allowing them to do this - obliging them instead to rely on their parents or guardians to do so - clearly contravenes their right to be heard and freedom of belief under the UNCRC. Moreover, since the Scottish government's latest Religious Observance Act passed this year, we find ourselves in the ludicrous situation where children have the right to opt into religious observance against their parent's wishes, but not out. This is discrimination, pure and simple, against non-religious and minority religious children and families.

“Humanism is a worldview that centres freedom of thought and belief, secularism, and diversity. Our campaign to reform Religious Observance is central to who we are and we believe this report places beyond doubt the case for change.”

A spokesperson for the Scottish Government said: “National guidance is already clear that religious observance in schools should be sensitive to all individual spiritual needs and beliefs, whether these come from a faith or non-faith perspective.

“Religious observance in schools is not compulsory and the forthcoming changes under the Act will strengthen the rights of children and young people in Scotland. They will also bring legislation into line with existing guidance, building on our commitment to the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child.

“The statutory guidance to accompany implementation of the Act is currently under consultation – and the Humanist Society has been involved in the development of this draft guidance. It will emphasise the expectation that religious observance in non-denominational schools reflects the variety of faith and belief perspectives across Scotland.”
