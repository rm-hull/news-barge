While the autumn festival brings internationally acclaimed musicians to Cumnock, the work of The Cumnock Tryst extends throughout the year.

For its founder, Sir James MacMillan, the festival is about more than bringing world-class performers to his hometown. It is also about making classical music accessible to people who may never have considered it was for them.

MacMillan, who grew up in Cumnock and describes his family background as “very modest”, knows the pressures and perceptions that can make classical music feel elitist.

“There’s sometimes an expectation that you have to be a certain way, or dress a certain way, or know what the music is in advance,” he says. Even the etiquette surrounding classical concerts, such as knowing when to clap, can become a barrier.

“It can terrify some people,” he says.

Breaking down those barriers is particularly significant in Cumnock, which is more commonly associated with its history of coal mining and industry than with classical music.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/19052080.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Sir James MacMillan is the brains behind the festival *(Image: Cumncok Tryst)*

“Cumnock is, maybe it’s not the place you would expect an arts festival to be, but ... it’s the place where I became a musician, and I knew it would work,” MacMillan says.

He initially expected the festival to have a relatively modest reach. Instead, its first edition in 2014 was reviewed by The New York Times, and it has since attracted international press and regular supporters from the United States.

“It’s become a go-to festival, it’s one of the major festivals in Scotland’s calendar, and yet it’s still quite small,” he says. “It happens in small spaces, churches, village halls, schools, but there’s a lot going on.”

Over the years, the festival has welcomed acclaimed ensembles, including The Sixteen and Westminster Cathedral Choir, while violinist Nicola Benedetti is its patron. This year, the BBC Scottish Symphony Orchestra was among the festival’s high-profile performers.

For MacMillan, these concerts are among its “high points”, but the festival’s achievements take different forms. Alongside its professional performances, it creates opportunities for people in the local community.

One example is The Unbroken Thread, which brings together pupils from Hillside School, supporting children and young people with complex additional support needs, and adults at Riverside Centre. Throughout the year, participants work with professional musicians, including sessions with MacMillan himself at the keyboard, before sharing their work at the festival.

For Sylvia Dool, whose daughter Alyssa has been involved in the project since her time at Hillside School and has since moved on to Riverside Centre, music has always been a big part of family life. Finding a way for Alyssa to access it in the same way, however, was more difficult.

“To be able to access music, which for our family is a huge part of our family, it’s easily accessible for the rest of us. For Alyssa, it was much more difficult to find a way for her to be able to access music,” she says.

“To have this team come in and provide that for her and for her friends at Riverside is just phenomenal. And it’s brought so much joy to their lives, it’s brought joy to the community, and it’s brought joy to the parents to be able to give her that same experience that we have.”

She describes the music made by the participants as “the purest form of music”, adding: “It comes from the heart. It’s what’s inside them at that time, and it’s such an important form of expression, particularly when a lot of them struggle verbally to communicate. The music becomes a really true form of expression for them.”

Paul Brunton, an Ayrshire singer-songwriter who runs Rock 'n' Role Models, a company that encourages creativity through music and songwriting, has been working with the group since June.

“I just go in every week, sometimes it’s just me with the guitar, and we just work together. And I might have a keyboard player in with me, and it’s just to get them completely engaged with music. They love it,” he says.

Brunton recalls one participant who, he says, had never spoken or vocalised before working with him.

“I put her through the vocoder, and she heard her voice differently, and she started speaking.”

“I went back home after that session, and I just cried for about two hours, because it was just so emotional. We get a lot of that; we get these kinds of breakthroughs.”

For MacMillan, the sessions can reach people in ways that everyday activities may not.

“It means the world to them,” he says. “It reaches parts of them interiorly that maybe other activities, that everyday activities don’t.”

He says staff at the centres also notice changes in participants’ attitudes and behaviour.

“They can sense a delight when they’re involved in activities like that, playing drums and all the tech instruments and so on.”

“There’s something that happens in these sessions, these weekly sessions for the young and old, that can bring about change in their lives,” he says.

The festival’s community involvement also extends to its festival chorus, which welcomes anyone who wants to sing, without auditions.

“We just take whoever wants to sing, and they work at a very high level,” MacMillan says. He adds that being part of the chorus can affect not only singers’ social lives but also their “interior spiritual life”.

Maggie Broadley, an artist and volunteer with Cumnock Tryst who describes herself as an “enthusiastic amateur singer”, sings with the festival chorus.

“Music was a sort of refuge for me. Singing is so very important to me,” she says.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/19506927.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Composer Sir James MacMillan *(Image: Cumnock Tryst)*

“I don’t read music. It’s a bit of a challenge, but the feeling when your voice becomes part of the whole is wonderful.

“Voice, it’s such a personal instrument. It’s the thing that we’ve all been using together for millennia.”

She also sees the importance of that connection in the people she sings alongside.

“It’s the passion that comes through from all of them. And it’s that then being part of that and feeling connected.”

MacMillan says the festival has helped turn Cumnock into “a musical town, a centre for musical excellence in some ways”.

While the autumn events remain at the heart of its work, the Tryst now runs concerts throughout the year and has a presence in local schools and the wider community.

“It has fulfilled a lot of my aspirations, but we're going to build more and go in different directions.”

“We've got a reasonably new general manager who's very inspirational, he's a real genius, David Lee, and working with him and collaborating with him and planning ahead has given me even more of a vision of where the tracks could go.”

For MacMillan, the festival’s development is an ongoing process. “It’s got to be continually evolving, not staying still.”
