Farmers are being encouraged to plant more trees to protect livestock from extreme heat, after Yorkshire recorded its driest July in 155 years.

James Newhouse, who runs Meg’s Farm in the Yorkshire Dales, has already begun planting trees to cope with rising temperatures and has seen first-hand the benefits they can bring.

Mr Newhouse said: "Last summer, in the middle of the hot spell, when I went to check on the cows, they were all standing under the one tree we have, trying to get some shade.

"This really made me think about how we need to change our land for the future.

"Our animals are designed for the hills, the wet and the cold, not the heat.

"Hedges and trees provide cover from the sun and this is very important.

"Animals lose condition when the weather is like this and we need to help minimise that."

With support from the Yorkshire Dales Millennium Trust (YDMT), Mr Newhouse has planted 22 individual trees across his fields and more than 500 metres of hedgerow.

These trees, planted within working fields rather than as part of a larger woodland, provide essential shade and shelter for livestock while allowing the land to remain productive.

Hedgerows offer added value by supporting wildlife and helping to link habitats across the landscape.

Across the region, the conversation is growing about how trees can form part of a broader strategy to manage the impacts of climate change without taking valuable farmland out of use.

Trees not only moderate temperatures and offer shade but also protect against wind and other extreme conditions.

Their roots help maintain soil structure and improve water retention, although the benefits depend on careful selection of species, planting locations, and long-term management.

Woodland now covers around five per cent of the Yorkshire Dales National Park, up from 4.3 per cent thanks in part to YDMT and its partners, who have planted 1.8 million trees in the region.

Carol Douglas, from Yorkshire Dales Millennium Trust, said: "Trees are naturally resilient and I’m often surprised how they manage to survive despite the extremes in weather we are now experiencing.

"However, going forward it will be increasingly important to ensure a diverse range of appropriate species are planted within woodland creation and management schemes and that planting and maintenance procedures are meticulously undertaken to ensure young trees are given the best chance of growing healthy and strong."

For many farmers, the challenge lies in balancing food production with environmental improvements and business resilience.

Restoring hedgerows and planting individual trees offer a middle ground, supporting climate adaptation while maintaining grazing land.

Mr Newhouse said: "We plant a few more metres of hedgerow or trees every year.

"YDMT can help in so many ways, from advice and funding to helping get them planted.

"Having an organisation like YDMT supporting farmers will really start to make some big changes in the Dales, which is what nature and the farms need to help deal with climate change."

Projects like Meg’s Farm offer an example of how trees can be integrated into the agricultural landscape.

Ms Douglas said: "We would be happy to hear from any landowners interested in planting trees or who have woodlands they would like to manage.

"We can provide bespoke support, from supplying and planting a single landscape tree to creating larger agroforestry schemes looking at integrating trees across the whole farm.

"In certain settings, we may also be able to support the creation or restoration of hedgerows if they link with other woodlands or trees, and there are woodland management grants available for small woodlands too."

The charity stresses that successful planting involves more than just increasing tree numbers.

Selecting the right species, protecting saplings, and ensuring they become established are all critical, especially as climate conditions continue to change.

Further information about YDMT’s woodland programmes and support for landowners is available directly from the charity.
