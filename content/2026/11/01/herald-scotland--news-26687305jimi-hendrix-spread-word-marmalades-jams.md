THIS week’s Icon refers not to a tangy preserve but to a pop-rock (more pop) band, whose members had their origins in the east end of Glasgow but ended up, as so often, living in the Home Counties or Los Angeles. All roads lead out of Scotland.

Marmalade were originally called the Gaylords, a name about which in these Soviet-style times I am saying nothing. To hell with it, I will say something. The name came from a notorious post-war US street gang called the Chicago Gaylords. But you knew that.

What you might not know is that, in an undated interview on yon YouTube, keyboardist and guitarist William “Junior” Campbell revealed that their manager came up with the M name one morning after breakfast. “When he said The Marmalade we fell about. We sort of rolled around the floor for hours laughing.” Then they agreed it was great.

“What better than something that stares you in the face first thing in the morning when you get out of your bed?” Not The Plooks, then?

They were the first Scottish band to take top spot in the UK singles chart, a milestone they marked by wearing kilts on Top of yon Pops, with their cover of The Beatles’ Ob-La-Di, Ob-La-Da on New Year's Day 1969.

I say “cover” but it wasn’t a version of the Lennon-McCartney composition. It was the exact same song. Publisher Dick James offered it to them ahead of The Beatles’ White Album being released. Marmalade didn’t even know it was a Lennon-McCartney composition.

Any road, it sold millions worldwide. In the US, their one major hit was the yearning, less upbeat Reflections of My Life.

Apropos The Beatles, at times Marmalade’s music echoed that of the Fab Four, with beautiful harmonies, brass instruments and short, Harrison-esque guitar breaks. They were undeniably talented musicians and singers.

But they were ever dogged by an identity crisis musically. Bit soul, bit rock, bit singer-songwriter, some say even a bit prog, but I’m not having that: way too middle of the road.

**To be frank**

AT times, they went for a conventional big band sound. Sinatra aside, that doesn’t do it for me either. I can imagine my old mum nodding along to it in a way that she never did to Yes or Gentle Giant.

Bruce Eder, writing on the AllMusic website, compares Marmalade’s career to that of the Tremeloes, “who made incredibly well-crafted pop/rock but were never taken seriously”.

No shame on them, as most bands did it (and do it), but their intonation was American, with more references to Carolina than Castlemilk, and singing “tremp” instead of “tramp” (to do an American accent, just turn every “a” into an “e”).

Marmalade origins lie in 1961 Glasgow, when teenagers Pat Fairley, the aforementioned Campbell (aged 14) and Billy Johnston shared their love of rock and also roll. With guitar and bass covered, they experimented with various drummers, called themselves the Gaylords and played local clubs for little or no money.

After Bill Irving, from local Baillieston group the Cadillacs, took over from Johnston on bass, Thomas “Nippy” McAleese, from the Monarchs, took over on vocals and adopted the stage name Dean Ford, so the band’s name changed to Dean Ford & the Gaylords, in line with the prevailing mode, a la Cliff Richard & the Shadows.

Better gigs followed, and the band steadily built a reputation. In the early 1960s, however, Scottish groups couldn’t get record company executives to hear them, until the Merseyside sound exploded on the scene and, soon, “regional” bands were in.

Championed by Scottish music journalist Gordon Reid, Dean Ford & the Gaylords signed to EMI’s Columbia label in 1964 after auditions at Glasgow’s Locarno Ballroom and went on to record four singles, including a cover of 1963 Chubby Checker US hit Twenty Miles, which sold well locally but not Brit-wide.

**Tartan not special**

SO, despite Merseybeat, Scottish bands were still ignored, even when music polls made the Gaylords top band in Scotland, never mind their regularly supporting visiting English acts like the Hollies.

So, on returning from Germany in early 1965, the group holed up in Wimbledon. On the recommendation of the Tremeloes, who had played with them in Glasgow and already had two hit singles under their belt, the Gaylords were invited to join London agency Starlite Artistes, owned and managed by Peter Walsh.

With their EMI contract ended, Walsh got them signed to CBS as The Marmalade. Former Orpington postman Alan Whitehead became their new drummer and, by now, Graham Knight of the Vampires had replaced Johnston on bass and harmony vocals.

On 19 January 1967, the band made their debut at London’s Marquee Club, supporting – wait for it – Pink Floyd! Crivvens. Soon, they’d a twice-weekly residency at the club. They toured with the Who, Joe Cocker, Traffic, Gene Pitney, and the Tremeloes.

But their singles still bombed in the UK, despite – wait for it – Jimi Hendrix describing I See The Rain as the “best cut of 1967”.

At last, in 1968, Lovin’ Things, their most commercial sounding single yet, broke into the UK Top Ten, reaching number six and selling 300,000 copies.

The record company wanted more like this and so came Ob-La-Di, Ob-La-Da, securing the band’s niche as soft, bubblegum pop. Leaving CBS for Decca, they produced Reflections of My Life, which topped the British charts and became a Top Ten American single.

However, a following album, Reflections of the Marmalade, showcased the group’s increasingly confused identity: sort of soul, sort of singer-songwriter, a weak glimmer of soft prog.

This split personality arguably engendered the first splits in the band and, in succeeding years, too many changes occurred to chronicle here. In one incarnation, the NME said they’d become “the poor man’s Status Quo”.

**Sock it to them**

THEIR declining reputation only recovered when drummer Whitehead, after being kicked out, told the News of the World about all the debauchery with groupies. Apparently, one photie featured him wearing only a sock. And not on his foot.

In succeeding years, there were deaths, alcoholism, celebrity chauffeuring, music bars, lap dancing clubs, all the usual. A version of Marmalade is still doing the rounds but without any original members.

They made some great music, though, and were genuinely talented individuals (check out the late Dean Ford singing on the YouTube video mentioned earlier).

We leave the last word to a fan, writing on Facebook earlier this month: “The band stripped away all … pretences, expressing that true happiness came from writing honest songs and connecting with audiences on a genuine human level.”
