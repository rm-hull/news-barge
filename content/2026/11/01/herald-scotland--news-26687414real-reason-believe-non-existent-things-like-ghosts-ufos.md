*For Halloween weekend, our Writer at Large talks to the celebrated historian of the paranormal, Professor Malcolm Gaskill, about why we’re so obsessed with the dark side*

Malcolm Gaskill is his own perfect case study. Here’s a man trying to understand why the supernatural still plays such a significant role in our modern secular world, yet for an answer he need only look in the mirror.

Gaskill is one of the leading academic authorities on the history of the supernatural, magic and witchcraft. A history professor, his work is acclaimed internationally.

His latest book is Worlds of Fear and Wonder: Living with the Supernatural, Past and Present. It’s already been showered with praise by Lucy Worsley and Tom Holland.

The puzzle he wants to unravel is why the paranormal retains a hold over so many people in the 21st century. His own life provides an answer.

His family wasn’t religious when he was a child in the 1970s. Gaskill, now 59 and a classic Gen Xer, is an atheist, though not hardline.

He doesn’t believe in gods, ghosts or an afterlife. Indeed, he thinks that once we die “it’s just the end, blackness, like general anaesthetic forever”. Yet he’s fascinated by stories of ghosts and demons, and he’s rather superstitious. You certainly won’t find him walking under ladders.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21655252.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Professor Malcolm Gaskill *(Image: Supplied)*

It’s Gaskill’s contention that, in this godless world where religion is rapidly dying, we need the supernatural more than ever. In the past, both the church and the supernatural – healing charms or festivals with deep pagan roots meant to ensure that crops grew – made people feel safe.

The world was a confusing yet wondrous place, and belief in the otherworldly helped our ancestors navigate the turbulent tides of their lives.

Today, with the church playing little role in society, all that’s really left to add to that sense of safety and reassurance is the supernatural. It also provides some spiritual meaning, especially for non-believers.

And so we see sales of paranormal items booming tenfold in Britain. High street stores stock Ouija boards, and sales have risen by 300%. It’s been reported that UK spending at Halloween outstrips Christmas.

Dark tourism pulls in millions of pounds in Britain, with folk visiting haunted houses or UFO sites. Horror movies break the international trend in cinema which sees so many films flop. Produce a horror movie and you’re basically guaranteed a profit.

As in the past, Gaskill explains, the supernatural and paranormal are providing meaning for people today. Where there’s emptiness, the supernatural brings wonder; where there’s fear, it brings hope.

Gaskill says that people once reported seeing witches and vampires. Today, it’s UFOs and alien abductions. Instead of visiting their priest, they consult their horoscopes or pay for healers.

As faith declined, astrology stepped in: the UK market is around £150 million. Gone are the days of horoscopes in newspapers. Today, smart speakers read your stars.

*Astrology*

GEN Z and millennials are particularly drawn to astrology for mindfulness and dealing with anxiety. The global astrology app business is worth roughly $8 billion.

There’s been a rise in spiritual healing. Two-thirds of UK adults have used traditional or complimentary medicine. The online world is filled with “gurus” – often shysters – flogging the latest spiritual wellness gimmick.

Gaskill says that in this modern, technological world there’s a mass “compulsion to immerse ourselves” in the supernatural. These experiences “shouldn’t be relevant to our lives, but they really are”.

Meanwhile, just as in the past, stories of ghosts, witches, demons and monsters “still speak to our nightmares and unconscious selves”, and “exert a timeless power as a language for expressing trauma and unease”.

The modern world is a frightening place – just like the world of the past. When we’re scared, we need to release that fear. That’s why the Ancient Greeks invented shocking, blood-soaked – often supernatural – tragedies. They believed in “catharsis” – the notion that watching an upsetting drama purged the audience of negative emotions like fear.

There’s absolutely no difference between a human born today and a human born 500 or 5,000 years ago. We’re the same creature. And, says Gaskill, that means we remain – and probably always will remain – a species in which emotion dominates reason.

“The awkward truth is that for as long as we feel alone in the universe, in need of hope and reassurance, worlds of fear and wonder will remain part of who we are.”

The world today remains a “place of enchantment”, just as it was centuries ago, millennia ago. We just lie to ourselves and pretend that we’re rational and scientific. As Gaskill points out, a recent Gallup poll showed that 39% of Americans believe in ghosts. Don’t for a moment think Americans are more gullible than Brits, though. Here, the figure is between 34-38%.

Paganism is growing rapidly in Britain, as are many other “New Age” religions. “WitchTok” is huge on TikTok, with mostly young women talking about their attraction to movements like Wicca.

The Uncanny podcast – which features real people recounting what they claim are true cases of hauntings

– is one of the most successful shows in Britain. It got so big it transferred to TV. Uncanny reached the top of the BBC’s charts for on-demand audio programmes.

“We haven’t really changed that much,” Gaskill tells me. He sees a strong connection between the emotional lives of our ancestors – in eras like the witch hunts of the 1600s – and ourselves today. By trying to understand why they were so connected to the supernatural world, we might just make some sense of our lives too.

“There’s a common denominator between ourselves and our ancestors. We might not believe in witches, but we have the same anxieties and needs – joys, hopes and fears. Supernatural beliefs are embedded in those common feelings.”

*Ghosts*

HERE’S a good example. There are many ghost sightings of monks in Britain, Gaskill explains. These only started, however, after the dissolution of the monasteries by Henry VIII.

Monasteries were taken from the church and given to Henry’s henchmen. The society-wide guilt associated with this act of mass expropriation expressed itself in ghostly sightings of clerical figures.

Where do Americans often report seeing ghosts? At sites associated with national guilt: the burial grounds of indigenous people. “It’s classic Stephen King,” Gaskill adds.

Most haunted house stories are in some way inversions of the concept of the perfect home, he believes, like the Amityville Horror set in an idyllic American neighbourhood. It underscores our fear of domestic failure, and the often unspoken tensions within family life.

After Covid, sightings of ghosts rose dramatically in Britain – by at least threefold. The supernatural is a way of expressing very deep, subliminal fears which we often don’t want to acknowledge. It’s a psychological pressure valve.

There’s an “anthropological need” for the supernatural, Gaskill says. That’s why ghost stories are so similar across the world, and also across time. One of the first recorded ghosts stories came from the Roman writer Pliny around

100 AD. It featured a ghost in a cobwebbed old house clanking chains. You’d see the same in any haunted house movie today.

“I’m an atheist,” Gaskill says. “I don’t believe in the supernatural, but I’m drawn to it. It’s perfectly possible to be a rationalist and still have this supernatural inclination.”

That’s why, he says, the old adage “there are no atheists in foxholes” is so true. Within us all, there’s such a sense of wonder at the world, and such fear too, that everyone is attracted in some way to the supernatural, either through religious faith or curiosity about the paranormal.

“It’s hardwired into us, it’s a facet of being human. We’ve evolved this way.” For most of human history, Gaskill notes, the notion of atheism was utterly absurd. Until the 20th century basically everyone was a “believer”. From the perspective of human history, atheism “is a rather odd position”. Indeed, even today, across the globe, more people than not believe in some sort of deity. “Most people still live in a spiritual realm,” he adds.

Gaskill sees the supernatural as a “tool” which helps us deal emotionally with a complex, frightening world. In the past, life was precarious. If crops failed, you starved.

“So crafting corn dollies to ask spirits to help with the harvest made the average peasant feel much safer. Such practices gave ordinary people a sense of control over their lives.

Today, you can try to regain some control by reading your horoscope, or going to fortune tellers or faith healers, or buying “healing” crystals.

The market for psychics in Britain is growing by 5% annually. More than 100,000 Brits regularly pay for premium phone-based tarot readings. Gaskill also points to the boom in the belief in “Guardian Angels”.

*Spells*

THE sale of magic spells in Britain is rising. You can buy DIY spell kits to perform love spells, money spells, and protection spells. This is precisely the same kind of “magic” that would have had you burned at the stake in Scotland 400 years ago. These spells make the practitioner feel as if they have “power”. It’s rooted, Gaskill says, in the need to feel that you control your life.

As an aside, Gaskill explains that the greatest surges in witchcraft accusations in earlier centuries were always accompanied by a sense that society had become polarised – often between Catholics and Protestants. What could be more polarised than life in 2026?

Polarisation instills a sense of instability, and once again, it’s fear that makes us turn to the supernatural. In the past, that sometimes meant praying, or at other times burning the woman next door. The supernatural is rooted in “existential anxiety”.

New Age lifestyles are particularly big among Gen Z. As a university professor, Gaskill has seen an “explosion of anxiety among teenagers. Young people, particularly young women, are trying to take control of their lives through various New Age beliefs. That’s why ‘WitchTok’ on TikTok is so huge”.

He adds: “It’s not like an hierarchal church, it’s a democratised, horizontal community. It’s really ‘old wine in new bottles’.”

There’s an emptiness, he feels, to Richard Dawkins-style atheism, which attacks and savages anything that smacks of faith or the supernatural. To Gaskill, it’s a dogmatic belief, like a religion. “And I’m suspicious of dogma,” he adds.

Humans today confront the same fears as humans in the Stone Age: principally, our mortality. We need some way of making sense of death, Gaskill says. Some visit church, others believe in ghosts. “Every age lives in its own Age of Anxiety,” he adds.

Which takes us to AI – a technology that’s inherently “ghostly”. Indeed, there’s something of the Ouija board about artificial intelligence. Here’s a disembodied entity speaking directly to you. In some cases, people have fallen in love with AIs, or even suffered AI psychosis akin to possession.

To Gaskill, there are echoes of witchcraft in AI. “In folk tales, the devil introduces himself into people’s lives and is smooth, slick and overconfident. Yet he’s based on nothing but lies.”

AI is rather like Mephistopheles in Doctor Faustus, promising everything you desire. In folk tales, the devil panders to human fantasies, promising wealth or love or revenge.

“That’s what the algorithm is,” says Gaskill. Millions are today asking AI the most intimate questions about their lives. Yet in the end, it’s AI that is effectively shaping the user’s thoughts – harvesting them for data, the way Satan harvests souls. It’s hard to escape the supernatural symbolism.

Like the devil in witchcraft stories, AI is a “dark fantasy”. And think of witch hunts. Today, we may not burn people, but we’re certainly hunting and demonising people. Social media is one huge mob with pitchforks and torches turning this person into a folk devil today or that person into a pariah tomorrow.

*Witches*

WITCHES were the ultimate outsiders – often women considered too clever, individualistic, rebellious or weird – so they had to die. Today, we turn other outsiders into enemies, from sexual minorities to migrants.

The same base emotions which coursed through our ancestors, triggering supernatural experiences like belief in witches, run strong within us too. The more things change, the more they stay the same, right?

Where once our ancestors told of being abducted by fairies, today many claim they’ve been abducted by aliens.

In some historic cases it appears accused witches believed they really had met Satan. There’s a significant minority in America today who believe that their enemies – the Democrats – are part of a Satanic child-killing cult. It’s called the QAnon conspiracy.

Some witches were burned for publicly cursing an enemy. They would wish someone dead or tell someone they would regret crossing them. That person would then die – simply through coincidence – and it was off to the gallows. Today, social media is full of people wishing death on others. Not so long ago, poison pen letters did the work of social media.

One in three British adults today has wished someone dead, Gaskill adds – most with zero regret. “That takes you to the heart of witchcraft.” The church considered it “lethal malice towards others”. Today, just as in the past, “many people have very hostile feelings towards others”.

Much of this hate – whether historical or modern – is rooted in “projective identification”. The theory centres on “self-loathing. People hate themselves. They can’t bear that feeling, so they transfer it onto someone else”.

This theory also explains the scapegoating of witches in bygone times. Today, the jealous, lonely incel is the ultimate symbol of projective identification, Gaskill suggests.

It’s telling that the word we use for these hateful people online is supernatural: “the troll”, with their delusions of an almost magical ability to wound people on the other side of the planet, even though they’re isolated losers.

It is words which are used to hurt so often in the modern world. What did witches supposedly use to hurt others? Words, in the form of spells. So words still have a “quasi-magical power. They shouldn’t hurt but they do”.

In previous centuries, “when there was conflict, suspicion of witchery followed”. It’s similar to today’s culture wars, where conflict is followed by accusations that political enemies are trying to destroy society. Such claims – as with witchcraft allegations – are often accompanied by reports of sexual deviance. This trope particularly affects migrants.

*Fear*

IN our modern secular and capitalist world, it’s mostly through Halloween and horror films that we find the biggest expression of our need for a connection to the supernatural.

Halloween, although vastly commercialised, remains “an imaginative space”. Like a trip to the movies, Halloween stirs emotions, allowing us to experience a cathartic purge of fear and dread.

We literally play with fire at Halloween. We dress as the dead. We seek out terror. In a world where few of us seldom experience death directly any more – despite knowing that death awaits us all – festivals like Halloween “give us agency, alleviate anxiety, make us feel we’ve got our lives back on track. Rituals are important because they’re subliminal. We’re not always conscious of the good they do us”.

Festivals like Halloween also provide a sense of “continuity in an age of rapid change, when what we mostly experience is isolation”. So Halloween makes us feel more like our socially cohesive ancestors who had community and strong identities. Community is fading today, and identity increasingly fluid and questioned.

Covid stands out as a singularly supernatural time, with so many reports of ghosts. Gaskill says we should remember those empty motorways. Society itself seemed dead, with ghostly office blocks and shopping malls. Now think of one of the most successful horror movies of late, Backrooms, which turned simple urban landscapes, like abandoned stores, into places of horror.

Gaskill quotes Hilary Mantel, the writer, who said “superstitions unite and demarcate communities. A group of people praying together or telling each other ghost stories are engaged in an emotional bonding exercise of considerable social utility”. Worshipping together, or sharing spooky stories, helps forge strong groups.

TV is littered with shows where the public recount “real-life” hauntings. It’s not just the UK show Uncanny. There’s Haunted, Paranormal Witness, and My Ghost Story.

Different periods seem to latch on to different paranormal events. Alien invaders were a 1950s staple, due to the American “Reds Under the Bed” scare. In the 1970s, there was a flowering of conspiracy theories around the idea that aliens had built the pyramids or even created humans, underscoring public distrust of power following Watergate.

Today, ghosts are the big trend and that’s probably down to Covid and the sense that death came so close to us all. “Ghosts are like memories,” Gaskill says. Isolation also ramps up a sense of the supernatural. He points to the isolation experienced by early settlers in America who quickly turned to witch-hunting.

There’s also a feedback loop between the supernatural and culture. Think of Dennis Wheatley’s famous devil-worship novels and the intense interest in Satanism between the 1950s and 1980s. Much of this period, especially the 1970s with terrorism and war, was “very dark”. It seemed that evil was everywhere.

“The supernatural is like a smoky mirror, a dark reflection of the anxieties of each age,” Gaskill adds.

Just before the witch hunts in Scotland reached their height, a pamphlet called “News from Scotland” painted a picture of a nation plagued by devil worship.

*Angels*

DURING and after the First World War, spiritualism and interest in ghosts became a national obsession. Up to the outbreak of war, humanity seemed to be on an ever-upward trajectory of science, rationality and slowly improving living standards. Then millions were killed. It was enormously traumatic. On the first day of the Battle of the Somme, nearly 20,000 British soldiers died.

The catastrophe led to what Gaskill calls “the re-enchantment of Europe” as people turned to seances and mediums to contact their loved ones.

Soldiers on the battlefield began reporting visions – like the Angel of Mons – and turned to increasingly superstitious behaviour.

The belief that taking the third light from a match is bad luck stems from trench warfare and the fear that snipers would get a bead on whoever lit their cigarette last.

In the trenches of Ukraine, soldiers today have - understandably - embraced superstition, and religion.

The Enlightenment and the Industrial Revolution slowly led to a “disenchantment of the world”, Gaskill explains, and the end of crazes like witch-hunting. From 1914, the world went in reverse. Arthur Conan Doyle, creator of Sherlock Holmes, was at the centre of the spiritualist movement following the death of his son on the Western Front.

One of the most famous – or rather infamous – mediums of the interwar period was Scotland’s Helen Duncan. She was the last person convicted under the Witchcraft Act. However, the charge was that she pretended to have supernatural powers and so defrauded people.

Gaskill has met the children of dead soldiers whose families turned to Duncan to connect with their loved ones. “Thousands of people went to her seances,” he says. “It was an extraordinary manifestation of what can happen through intense emotion.”

He tells the story of the family of one airman, Van Dyke Fernald, who died in combat. His mother became obsessed with spiritualism. Even though her obsession tore the surviving family apart, “spiritualism gave his mother a space where she could manage a grief that was otherwise unmanageable”.

In a way, he suggests, spiritualism played a similar role to the war memorials all across Britain, providing a “focus” for loss. Today’s fascination with angels has similar psychological roots. Gaskill says the belief in guardian angels provides a sense of safety in a terrifying world. In a culture where people are desperate for attention “it makes you feel special or that you’ve been chosen”.

Gaskill notes that people who have experienced unhappy childhoods are often inclined towards believing in angels. “It’s very sad,” he adds.

After the Second World War, there was an explosion of horror movies in Britain. Gaskill’s favourite, The Dead of Night, was made in 1945. Although the war is never mentioned, the conflict hangs over the film.

Gaskill quotes the famous folklorist Marina Warner who said that people “believe” supernatural stories not despite their outlandishness but because of their outlandishness.

*Monsters*

HUMANS, after all, are irrational beings in a world which insists we behave rationally. That’s a contradictory way to live. Supernatural fiction – either in print or on film – “blurs the line” between rationality and irrationality. It speaks to us because it reflects our innate sense that the world is a confusing and frightening place about which we know little.

“Ghost stories excite our imaginations. They say something about ourselves, they take us out of rationality and materialism.”

We’re still very much in thrall to the superstitions of the Middle Ages which we’ve been told are stupid and ignorant, Gaskill says. “We’re addicted to those values, even though we’ve been taught to be embarrassed about them.”

Experiencing the supernatural – even through horror movies – “makes us feel like a different version of ourselves”. That different version is much closer to the emotional reality of who we really are when the accoutrements of civilisation are stripped away.

Monsters, says Gaskill, “are hardwired in us”. They’re a metaphor for our deepest anxieties. Mermaids and sea monsters could be seen as an expression of ancient people trying to make sense of sailors dying at sea. The novel Dracula was infamously interpreted as tapping into subconscious fears about the “foreign other” endangering British society.

The novel appeared at a time of migration by Jewish people from Eastern Europe.

The imagination likes to play with reality, Gaskill says, so monsters “bubble up from people’s inner fears”. Dealing with monsters in a fictional fashion means we can explore our fears safely. “We flirt with the flame,” as Gaskill puts it.

And, of course, we turn what we hate and fear into monsters. Many today routinely claim migrants are that ultimate modern monster: the sex offender. Gaskill invokes Sylvia Plath, as he refers to this practice as “the incalculable malice of the everyday”.

More than 50% of Americans see Satan, he says, “as a real spiritual being”.

Such beliefs become extremely dangerous when, as we often see today, the religious right literally demonises its enemies, saying that they are doing the Devil’s work.

That the most powerful democracy on Earth has so many people who believe Satan is real proves, says Gaskill, that we are not living in a “disenchanted world. This world is still extremely enchanted. Demons and spirits are real to many”.

It’s a myth, he believes, that modernity “chased away” the supernatural from our lives. In terms of our emotional inner lives, humans are the same now as they were in the 1600s or 6000 BC. In such a technological world, with religion fading so fast, the supernatural is, perhaps, “more relevant than ever”.

For generations, there have been claims that the supernatural would vanish from our lives. But it never has, and it probably never will, Gaskill adds, given the emotional make-up of humanity.

One of the biggest literary trends today is “romantasy” novels: often adult stories with some supernatural element. Gaskill says these are a hit with young women as they provide a sense of power in a deeply misogynistic world. Such books, with their supernatural storylines, answer a “psychological need”.

In the future, the supernatural – especially our anxieties around death – will increasingly meld with technology. Today, there’s talk of uploading human consciousness after death, or recreating a personality from online data. What would this be but a digital ghost?

Gaskill points out that “the rise of empiricism and materialism left a hole” in the spiritual heart of humankind. How strange, then, that science may now be on the verge of creating a modern ghost – the digital simulacrum of a once-living person.

“Our emotional needs certainly won’t be satisfied in the digital age,” Gaskill adds. “It’ll only get worse, and that will force us to look for new forms of spiritual comfort. We’ll definitely still be searching for proof that we’re not alone.”
