*Expect purges, infighting and an even more imperious president should Republicans be on the receiving end of an election thrashing on Tuesday writes Foreign Editor David Pratt*

Many Americans it seems are leaving nothing to chance – or even for that matter to divine intervention. That includes American born Pope Leo XIV who became the first sitting pontiff known to have cast a ballot in any political election at any time in history.

That ballot is of course the US midterm elections this Tuesday in which US citizen Leo - Robert Francis Prevost - like more than 43 million Americans, have already voted early in person or returned mail ballots before election day.

Leo and US president Donald Trump have not always seen eye to eye in the past. The war in Iran, US foreign policy and military intervention in places like Venezuela and the treatment of immigrants in America, are just a few of the issues over which they have clashed.

Trump of course has a predilection toward being at loggerheads with people, and Pope Leo is only one on a very long list.

But many political pundits are of the view that should things go badly for Trump and the Republicans on Tuesday - and polls suggest it will – then the president’s tendency toward feuding and lashing out will only escalate. The only question is who will find themselves in Trump’s crosshairs.

Over the past days as the election looms, the US press has been replete with reports that an increasingly unpredictable Trump, under pressure and angry with naysayers, will seek to exact revenge both at home and abroad should the election outcome fail to go his way.

According to several current and former White House officials cited last week by the New York Times (NYT), who were around between early 2017 and early 2021, the past few weeks have felt particularly reminiscent of Trump’s first term.

“That period was marked by short, explosive staff tenures and infighting…toward the end, he lashed out as his poll numbers sank, then stoked fear and outrage among his supporters with false claims that the 2020 election was rigged, to the point that a large group attacked the Capitol,” reminded Katie Rogers, a White House correspondent, who has covered both Trump terms.

But as Rogers also notes, the difference between that term and this one is that Trump returned to power with “a vengeance” and since then has ordered his Justice Department to carry out a retribution campaign against his perceived enemies.

*Major setbacks*

With current polls suggesting that Republicans are likely to lose control of the House of Representatives, and possibly even the Senate, the question then on many minds post Tuesday, is will a furious Trump react as he has in the past when faced with major setbacks?

In past times and conventional terms the kind of setback that Trump might face as a result of midterm losses, would make him into what Washington insiders sometimes refer to as a “lame duck” president.

In other words, one so politically hamstrung as to be unable to push through their agenda during the remaining two years in office. That though according to some on both sides of the aisle interviewed by Edward Luce the Financial Times (FT) US national editor, is not the role they see Trump forced into.

“He is more likely to turn into an angry duck,” says Chris Christie, former Republican governor of New Jersey and Trump’s two-time rival for the presidential nomination.

“My fear is not of specific things he will do - we could compile a list - but of the great Trumpian unknown,” Christie told the FT, echoing the fears of other Washington insiders.

All this of course very much depends on the outcome of this week’s ballot, but things certainly don’t look good for Trump and the Republicans.

As of yesterday according to RealClearPolling’s latest national congressional ballot average, Democrats still lead Republicans by 8.2 percentage points. Democrats lead by 51.4 percent to 43.2 percent.

Meanwhile a separate economist/ YouGov survey released earlier last Thursday found that 44 percent of registered voters expect Democrats to control the House of Representatives after the election, compared with 31 percent who expect Republicans to retain control.

As for the Senate, betting markets make the Democrats a 67 per cent favourite to flip the chamber, while Nate Silver, America’s most prominent quantitative election forecaster, gives Democrats a 76 percent chance.

The bottom line here is that almost all the polls are running in the Democrats favour and for that reason Trump these past days has reversed a decision on conducting campaign rallies.

On Friday just a day after announcing he would cancel a pair of rallies, administration officials announced that Trump will be headlining in person, the previously cancelled rallies in Kansas and Iowa ahead of Tuesday’s vote.

The move marks a sharp reversal after Trump told reporters on Thursday, “I don’t feel I have to go to Kansas.”

Iowa and Kansas are states that are normally friendly to the president’s Republican Party. Not since 1932, for instance, has Kansas elected a Democrat to a full term in the Senate.

But according to early polling data, voters in both states have trended more towards Democratic Senate candidates this campaign cycle.

*'The Tell'*

Trump’s reverse decision on campaigning in what are usually recognised as deeply red (Republican) states is “the tell” in terms of GOP concerns says some election observers.

“Parties generally focus on base mobilisation in the late stages. They believe that the conversion window for persuadable voters is largely closed, even though this is the time period of so-called closing arguments,” said Thomas F. Schaller Professor of Political Science at University of Maryland, Baltimore County.

“But there’s a base mobilisation when you think you’re going to win, and then there’s a base mobilisation when you know you’re going to lose. And I think the where of it is very telling, Where is Trump?” Schaller told The New Republic magazine a few days ago as Trump’s reverse decision became public.

Another state being seen as a key “swing” or “battleground” state is Georgia.

The southern state has been so competitive in recent federal election cycles that its three most recent Senate matchups went to runoffs.

Georgia is a major focus for Trump, and he has campaigned twice in Dalton, Georgia, in the past few weeks.

The state is a source of personal grievance for the president, primarily because it was where he disputed his preelection loss in 2020.

Should Republicans’ worst fears be realised in such states, then it’s almost certain that Trump will seek to apportion blame elsewhere despite the clear drop in his own personal popularity and polling.

Compared to other presidents leading their parties during competitive midterms, few see Trump holding himself accountable for Republican losses.

Everything and anyone other than Trump himself is already up for grabs to deflect from the potential losses to come from Tuesday’s vote..

At a recent campaigning rally in Michigan, Trump even turned to faulting voters should Republicans lose this week - a line he’s used before in his first term during the 2018 election when Democrats won the House.

“You will only have yourselves to blame and I don’t think there’s any coming back from that either,” Trump told the crowd in the Great Lakes state, home to one of the country’s closest Senate contests.

Along with apportioning blame will also come the inevitable list of individuals held responsible. Several administration officials, advisers and close allies have described the atmosphere within Trump's circle as a “toxic mix of jockeying for power and pre-emptive finger-pointing”. One Trump adviser said that no one would be safe from a purge in the worst-case scenario.

Some of the administration's most high-profile figures are believed to have their jobs at risk, with Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth and White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles said to be frequently discussed in Maga circles.

Hegseth already faces a bipartisan push for his ouster after the midterms having long faced criticism for waging a disastrous war against Iran. A Democrat win of the House could incentivise an ongoing effort to impeach Hegseth, potentially convincing more Republicans to get on board in a vote shortly after the elections.

*Impeach Hegseth*

Already some Republicans are gunning for Hegseth. GOP Rep. Thomas Massie has vowed to force a vote to impeach Hegseth when the House returns after the midterms in November if the secretary is still in office.

If Republicans lose the majority, Massie is increasingly confident that there will be the bipartisan votes needed for impeachment, which just requires a simple majority.

It’s not inconceivable either that Trump himself could fire his “secretary of war”. A number of analysts say that Trump’s request for a near-50 per cent increase in the US defence budget to $1.5tn - one of the few items on his legislative agenda - is unlikely to pass while Hegseth is running the Pentagon.

As one Democratic aide told CNN recently, Hegseth is “the lowest hanging fruit because of the animosity that seems to be both from Republican and Democratic senators and staff.” That view might also be taken by Trump even though he has defended Hegseth to date.

But it’s not only on the US domestic political landscape that Trump’s ire might be directed in the fallout of a Republican beating in the midterms.

As numerous analysts point out, even if a Democratic win allows the party to use hearings and investigations to make the president’s decision-making more transparent, the question is whether an electoral rebuke would result in more foreign policy restraint.

According to Dr Andrew Payne a Research Director at the international affairs think tank Chatham House, here “history offers a warning.”

After his 2006 midterm defeat, President George W Bush did not change course. Instead, he defied the mandate voters had given by escalating an unpopular war in the Middle East, explained Payne in a recent assessment.

“President Trump recently pledged not to attack Iran before the midterms, having previously expressed a desire to help his party in the elections. Whether he too will try to end the war through a dramatic escalation once the ballots are counted remains to be seen,” says Payne.

“But he would not be the first president to double down on an unpopular policy once unshackled from electoral constraints. The ‘lame duck’ phase of Trump’s presidency may therefore prove even more disruptive on the international stage,” the Chatham House analyst concludes.

Others share a similar view, insisting that rather than feeling constrained after a midterm battering, Trump will likely blame the Democrats for any domestic and international failures that occur in 2027 and 2028, which could make the last two years of his presidency more volatile.

To put this another way, Trump could well double down on foreign policy to gain the exposure he so obsessively seeks, making big play of interactions with global leaders, some not always immediately identifiable as US allies.

As so many of Trump’s massively ostentatious and vastly expensive vanity architectural projects like luxury ballroom, arches and monuments to himself have shown, one way or another he seems determined to leave his stamp on history. The question is the dangerous extent to which he is prepared to go to achieve this on the US or global stage.

*Embarrassment*

Those same architectural projects too are proving a source of irritation and embarrassment for some within his Republican ranks. Already there are signs that certain figures are starting to distance themselves from Trump and the potential damage he might do before the next presidential election.

The early waves of such dissent are evident in the likes of the seven House Republicans who voted to curb Trump’s Iran war powers and the votes against, or at least public declarations against, his tariff policies.

And so just as the recent election in Israel is set to impact both the domestic and global geopolitical landscape, so too is Tuesday’s US midterms.

Should the polls be accurate and prove disastrous for the Republicans, then there will be a growing scramble within the administration to throw others under the bus in an effort to save oneself.

If nothing else you can depend on Donald Trump to find fault with others for his own failings and after the midterms it’s almost certain that revenge and retribution will be on the cards. Trump does not take well to losing, and it could well get ugly as others both home and away pay the price for that loss.
