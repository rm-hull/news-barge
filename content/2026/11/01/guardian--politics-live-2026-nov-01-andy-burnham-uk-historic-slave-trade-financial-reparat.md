Healey also rules out reparations for Britain's slave trade

Chancellor of the Exchequer John Healey appearing on the BBC1 current affairs programme, Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg, in No 11 Downing Street, Westminster. Photograph: Jeff Overs/PA

At the end of his interview with Kuennsberg, Healey was also asked to comment on a potential apology for Britain’s near-250 years of perpetrating the slave trade and why the government should or shouldn’t pay reparations to enslaved people and their countries.

Sitting in the grand settings of Number 11 Downing St, Kuenssberg pointed out the lavish interiors:

“So many Commonwealth countries think that this country’s wealth, maybe the wealth in this building, this gilt chandelier, came off the back of slavery. So why is a government not willing to contemplate reparations?”

Healey replied saying the PM was open to “a discussion about an apology”, but “he doesn’t believe that reparations are the right approach”. He makes the same argument as Burnham that the current British people shouldn’t have to pay for past wrongdoings.

Considering a formal apology doesn’t mean accepting that British people now should accept the financial liability for any party and things that happened hundreds of years ago, in which they had no role.

For almost 250 years (1560s-1807), British ships trafficked more than three million enslaved African men, women and children in a state policy backed by the monarchy and parliament.

Many were taken to British-owned plantations in the Caribbean, which enslaved generations. Children were born into slavery, uprisings were put down with extreme brutality

There were still 800,000 enslaved people on British plantations when enslavement was finally abolished by parliament in 1833

U.K. politicians have traditionally focused on the country’s role in banning the slave trade in 1807 and outlawing slavery throughout the British Empire in 1833.

Abolishment was only achieved because the government agreed to “compensate” the enslavers. The government paid an enormous sum, the equivalent of billions of dollars these days, to the “owners” of enslaved people; this came from a huge loan that was only paid off in 2015.

Britain has never paid any compensation or reparations to the people who were enslaved, or their countries.

Truth telling and transparency is key to acknowledging Britain’s history of enslavement and the transatlantic slavery.

Some descendants of British people who profited from the slave trade have already apologised and offered compensation.

That includes The Guardian newspaper. In 2023, we apologised for the role our newspaper’s founders had in transatlantic enslavement, and launched a 10-year programme of restorative justice involving community engagement with descendant communities linked to the Guardian’s founders in Jamaica and the US.

For example, this is the case that has been made about the logic of slavery reparations – and how it’s not just about payment but addressing inequalities and engaging in good faith with descendants of enslaved people.

Britain trafficked enslaved African people for centuries; kings and queens were investors

David Conn

The Guardian has done extensive reporting on Britain’s centuries-long trafficking of enslaved African people, a policy that was supported by parliament and the monarchy from the 1560s-1807.

Britain’s trafficking operations – which included royal companies led by kings and, later, entrepreneurs in Bristol, Liverpool and other port cities, violently transported more than 3 million enslaved African men, women and children to colonial plantations in the Americas and Caribbean.

The Register of British Slave Traders, the product of a three-year project by a team of specialist historians, documents the investors in Britain’s near 250-year transatlantic slave trade.

It shows the relentless involvement of the British monarchy, parliament and financial and business elites; the role of the church in legitimising it; and how the investors’ wealth was woven deep into the country’s fabric and used to fund hospitals, schools, welfare organisations, the arts, science and culture.

Key elements include:

A series of British kings and queens were leaders and investors in the trafficking of enslaved African people;

More than 100 MPs were investors in slave-trading companies, including scores in the Tory and Whig parties – the forerunners of today’s Conservatives and Liberal Democrats;

Dozens of directors and original financiers of the Bank of England were investors in the slave trade;

More than 1,000 women invested in slave-trading companies;

Forty-five Church of England clergymen were investors in the slave-trading Royal African Company.

The publication of the register last month will provide further evidence for the growing global movement calling on European governments to acknowledge their perpetration of enslavement, issue a full, formal apology and engage positively with discussions for reparatory justice.

Successive British governments have refused to do so, arguing there is no duty to make reparations because enslavement and the slave trade were not against the law at the time.

Badenoch rejects apology idea, saying 'when will the demands stop?'

On the slave trade apology, Badenoch repeats what she wrote on X yesterday, dismissing the discussion as “naval gazing”.

She also claims that “We have apologised. Tony Blair apologised twice.”

I assume she’s referring to Blair’s statements in 2006 and 2007, around the 200th anniversary of the British abolition of the slave trade, where the then PM expressed “deep sorrow and regret”, calling the slave trade trade “profoundly shameful” and a “crime against humanity”.

These statements stop short of an actual apology though. Blair avoided this, just like King Charles did earlier this week, partly due to the concerns that an explicit, formal apology for past wrongdoing could open the door to legal and financial claims.

Badenoch tells Kuennsberg she believes the call is coming now because “many of these [Commonwealth] countries are struggling with the same issues that we’re struggling with. They’re not becoming richer. They don’t know what to do, so they want to ask Britain for more money.”

When Kuennsberg says that the government is just talking about an apology, and has ruled out financial reparations, Badenoch goes:

When will the demands stop? What about if they ask for it next year? How many apologies are needed? Everyone knows the transatlantic slave trade was wrong. Britain played a huge role in stopping it.

The problem with this endless talk about apologies is that it is a distraction. It then means that people start focusing on things that happen hundreds of years ago, instead of solving today’s problems.

Some historical context here – the historical Tory party were directly involved in and supported enslavement for nearly 200 years.

It was a state-backed operation that had the full support of parliament’s major parties. Tory MPs were investors in the slave trade; many owned plantations. The Tory governments also opposed and delayed the push for abolition as that campaign grew in the late 18th century.

Caribbean leaders welcome 'significant development' of apology move

Burnham’s consideration of a formal apology has been welcomed by leaders of Caribbean nations, but differences remain on reparations.

Barbados’ prime minister, Mia Mottley, welcomed Burnham’s acknowledgment of the “brutality of the trans-Atlantic slave trade, his openness to a formal apology and his offer of structured dialogue”.

She noted the different approach on reparations – Burnham has ruled this out entirely- but said:

Those differences should not prevent progress on health, education, tackling poverty and inequality, and fairer debt terms for vulnerable states.

Gaston Browne, the prime minister of Antigua and Barbuda called Burnham’s comments a “significant and welcome development”. He reiterated calls for reparation but said this could be addressed.

The Caribbean’s longstanding position on reparatory justice remains unchanged. Differences over financial compensation should be addressed through dialogue, but they need not prevent progress in areas where agreement is possible.

Burnham open to issuing apology over Britain's role in slave trade

Andy Burnham says the UK will listen to calls to issue a formal apology, but doesn’t believe that reparations is the “right answer”.

He argued yesterday in The Guardian that Britain needs to acknowledge its “complicated history” in order to protect the Commonwealth, the 56 nations that grew out of the British Empire.

He’s headed to Antigua and Babuda in coming days. King Charles has been in the Caribbean this week where he’s faced calls for Britain to apologise and pay reparations.

The King, whose forebears directly benefited from trafficking of enslaved people, admitted the “great suffering and injustices of the past” but did not issue an apology. It is UK government policy currently, to not apologise for the country’s role in the slave trade.

Burnham, introducing a potential change in his column yesterday, wrote:

The Britain I believe in is confident, decent and brave. We led the world in abolishing slavery – and we can show leadership again today.

For years, those members of the Commonwealth affected by the slave trade have asked for an honest conversation about the legacies of slavery.

By sticking our heads in the sand, we have damaged the ability of the Commonwealth to function.

He said his government is offering to begin a “structured dialogue” with those affected by slavery – but ruled out financial compensation for past wrongs.

But Burnham says that “British people today” cannot be held liable for historic wrongs, “in which they played no personal part”.

Instead he said he would launch a new drive to restructure debt for the poorest Commonwealth countries – that would be led by former PM Gordon Brown – that would help them repay debt under more favourable conditions.
