Nicola Sturgeon has described a phone call with Donald Trump as “mad in many respects”, warning that the US president’s "erratic" behaviour will only worsen on the world stage if voters punish Republicans in the upcoming midterm elections.

Speaking on the BBC's Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg, Ms Sturgeon shared details of the her conversation with the US President after his first election win but before his inauguration.

She told the programme: "When I had a conversation with him after his first election, but before he was inaugurated, the conversation was mad in many respects.

"But after the call ended, General Mike Flynn, who at the time was his national security adviser, telephoned my chief of staff to ask if the call had happened, and to get a readout of what was said, because Trump had made this call with no reference to any of his advisers."

She added: "That's just a small anecdote that I think illustrates a bigger picture of how completely different it is."

She described the exchange as ranking "amongst the most absurd of my entire time in office".

She said she felt she had to "say her piece" immediately and emphasised the need for the "longstanding" relationship between Scotland and the US to continue.

But she also condemned some of the rhetoric used during his presidential campaign and said she hoped policies like a Muslim ban would not be part of his administration. She also asked about his Scottish businesses.

"I doubt he heard a single word," she said.

The President then launched into a "monologue", according to the former first minister.

Paraphrasing the President, she said he asked whether Ms Sturgeon was aware he was Scottish on his mother's side, before describing Scotland as having a "mad obsession" with wind farms.

"Had I noticed what had happened to the US economy since his election?" she writes.

"No President had ever created such a strong economy and he wasn't even in office yet. His popularity ratings were soaring too. It was unprecedented. And his sons? Did I know he had the smartest sons any father had ever had? And so it went on."

Ms Sturgeon then said: "When the call ended, I wondered if I had just woken from a very bad acid dream."

A few minutes after that call, Ms Sturgeon's chief of staff, Liz Lloyd, received a call from President Trump's national security adviser, General Mike Flynn.

He was calling to "ask Liz if it was true that the President-elect had just spoken to the First Minister, and if so, could she tell him what had been discussed? It seemed that he had known nothing about it."

Ms Sturgeon was on the programme two days before the US midterm elections, which will decide whether Mr Trump's Republicans keep control of Congress.

She said world leaders struggled to know how to deal with the president: "Often he takes loyalty and indications of friendship as signs of weakness. So he ends up punishing his friends much more than he punishes his enemies. There's such an erraticism. The normal rulebook doesn't really apply."

She warned that if the Republicans lose both the House and the Senate, his behaviour could become "even less predictable" because his room for manoeuvre at home would be limited.

"It's likely that that erratic behaviour will play out on the international scale," she said. "If the Middle East flares up again, for example, then that will feed into inflation, interest rates, energy costs here."

Former Bank of England chief economist Andy Haldane, who appeared alongside her, agreed the president’s focus would shift abroad. “His energy will redirect internationally, geopolitically,” he said. “And that may well show up in high energy costs and interest rates.”

He said if Mr Trump is hemmed in at home, he could be forced to rein back the US budget deficit, which might ease some pressure on financial markets.

Admiral Sir Tony Radakin, the former Chief of the Defence Staff, who dealt with Mr Trump’s Pentagon, took a different view of the relationship. “There’s more froth, but the substance stays the same,” he said.

Mr Trump has been openly hostile to Ms Sturgeon. After a four-day trip to Scotland in July 2025, he told reporters on Air Force One that he praised John Swinney, but added: "I didn't have a lot of respect for the woman that preceded him. I thought she was terrible as a first minister of Scotland."

She replied on Instagram: "Feeling was mutual, Donnie. Forever proud to represent all the things that offend your view of the world."

He had called her a "woke extremist" when she resigned in 2023. As first minister, she stripped him of his status as a Scottish business ambassador.

Mr Trump goes into the vote with his poll ratings at a low. A new Emerson College poll found his approval at 37%, the lowest in the pollster’s surveys, and put the Democrats 10 points ahead of the Republicans on the generic congressional ballot.

Civiqs tracking, reported by our sister paper USA Today, shows his net approval is positive in only eight of the 50 states. It is negative in all six states with tight Senate races: Alaska, Michigan, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio and Texas. It is also negative in the reliably Republican states of Kentucky and South Carolina.
