Ministers have failed to replace almost every ferry earmarked for renewal under a 14-year-old Scottish Government plan — despite the final deadline passing last year.

The Herald can reveal that 11 of the 12 vessels identified for replacement in the 2013–2022 Ferries Plan put in place by the Scottish Government's Transport Scotland agency remain in CalMac’s permanent fleet, more than a decade after the programme was published.

The plan set out deadlines for replacing ageing vessels between 2016 and 2025. But only one of the 12 ships — the Hebridean Isles — has been withdrawn, leaving the overwhelming majority still in service despite the Government’s own timetable for renewal having expired.

The replacement programme has not delivered the wholesale renewal promised in the original plan with just two ferries coming online. Glen Sannox, one of the two ferry fiasco vessels being built at state-owned shipyard firm Ferguson Marine entered service in January 2025 as a replacement for 42-year-old Isle of Arran.

The Isle of Islay began carrying passengers in 2026 but was not earmarked to replace an ageing ferry. Neither arrival has resulted in the withdrawal of most of the ageing vessels identified for replacement.

Other replacement projects remain unfinished. The Loch Indaal is intended to replace the Hebridean Isles, while the Claymore is planned to take over the role of the Isle of Lewis. Neither has yet entered service.

The continuing reliance on ageing vessels has become a major issue for CalMac as it struggles to maintain reliable services across the network.

The failure has left Scotland’s ferry operator dependent on a fleet containing some of the oldest passenger vessels in the country, with 15 of its 33 permanent vessels having reached or passed the 30-year mark for replacement.

A Transport Scotland plan said vessels should be replaced when they reached the end of their working lives, which it estimated at around 30 years. CalMac’s own fleet policy recognises that planning for replacement should begin when ships reach 25.

CalMac now says that a "regular rhythm of new vessels at 18 month intervals beyond those in build will ensure the average of the fleet stays at a level which suits service resilience".

And its chief executive Duncan Mackison in a briefing to MPs has acknowledged that keeping older ships in service for longer is affecting the operator’s ability to run its network.

He said that the age of the fleet was having “a material impact on the conditions in which we operate because we are keeping older vessels for longer”.

He also said: “We need a new vessel every year to 18 months to maintain reliability.”

![Stephen Flynn](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21478629.jpg?type=mds-article-620)A letter has been sent to transport secretary Stephen Flynn *(Image: House of Commons/UK Parliament)*

Cumbrae's ferry committee and community council have written to transport secretary Stephen Flynn asking him to commission an external review of CalMac’s handling of its Clyde and Hebrides ferry contract, following disruption on the Largs–Cumbrae route on October 20 and October 27.

The move came a matter of months after T**he Herald revealed minsters scrapped a 20-year-old system of fines for ferry failure in Scotland’s new multi-billion-pound lifeline services** deal with**CalMac**.

The **Scottish Government** has been criticised over the move which comes despite repeated promises of tighter scrutiny, stronger performance measures and greater accountability after the ferry operator came under immense criticism over how it manages the service.

Angus Campbell, chairman of the Cumbrae ferry committee, said the failure to renew the fleet was ultimately the responsibility of the Scottish Government, which had failed to provide the investment needed to replace ageing vessels but that said the ageing fleet does not excuse CalMac from properly managing the network.

He said: “Transport Scotland published the plan in 2012 and sat in their hands and did nothing.” He added that CalMac had been “dealt a bad hand”, arguing that the operator had spent years lobbying behind the scenes for replacement vessels that have not arrived.

But he described CalMac's culture as “very siloed and very inward-looking”, that the current contract was supposed to be community and service-focused, but that the operator was becoming “less and less responsive to community needs”.

He said the experience on Cumbrae illustrated the frustration felt by communities when commitments made by the operator were not followed through.

It comes as the handover of the already long-delayed sister ferry to Glen Sannox, Glen Rosa has been pushed back again by the state owned shipyard company Ferguson Marine.

The ship was due to be handed over before the end of this year, but MSPs have been told handover date would be pushed to February to allow a new propeller to be fitted in January.

Among those still operating is the Isle of Cumbrae, which entered service in April 1977 and is now 49 years old, while the Isle of Mull, which entered service in 1988, is 38.

The original plan identified the Isle of Cumbrae and Hebridean Isles for replacement by 2016. A further group — including the Isle of Arran, Loch Linnhe, Loch Riddon and Isle of Mull — was supposed to be replaced by 2019.

The remaining six vessels were given a 2025 deadline. They were the Lord of the Isles, Loch Fyne, Loch Dunvegan, Caledonian Isles, Loch Tarbet and Loch Buie.

All six remain in the permanent fleet, with the Lord of the Isles now 37, the Loch Fyne and Loch Dunvegan 35, the Caledonian Isles 33, and the Loch Tarbet and Loch Buie 34.

Critics it underlines the scale of the gap between the Government’s promises and the reality facing island communities, many of which depend on ferries for access to work, education, healthcare and essential supplies.

Arrival of new ships has not translated into the withdrawal of most of the vessels identified in the original plan. In several cases, new vessels are intended to allow older ships to be redeployed or retained as resilience cover, rather than immediately removed from the fleet.

The scale of the problem has been particularly evident on routes where older vessels have little or no immediate replacement available.

The age of the fleet has been repeatedly raised as a factor behind the difficulties facing services across the network, with the operator having to balance repairs, maintenance, vessel availability and the limited number of ships available to provide cover when one breaks down.

The Scottish Government’s original plan recognised that replacing vessels was essential to maintaining the network. It also acknowledged that decisions on the timing of replacement would have to take account of the condition of individual ships and the operational requirements of the routes they served.

But the deadlines in the plan were intended to provide a timetable for action. The Government has since committed to further investment in new vessels, with several projects at different stages of construction and delivery. But the delays have left CalMac trying to maintain services with ships that were supposed to have been replaced years ago.

CalMac in an MPs briefing said that the operator faces several years of work to catch up with the investment needed. Mr Mackison said “for the next few years, there’s a lot of catch up. If we can maintain that reinvestment, things should stabilise.”

For islanders and coastal communities, however, the consequences of the delays are being felt now, through cancelled sailings, disrupted journeys and continuing uncertainty over the reliability of services on which they depend.

CalMac has pledged to improve ferry reliability after admitting that ageing vessels, technical faults and major repairs continued to affect services across Scotland’s west coast network.

David MacBrayne Ltd (DML), the parent company of CalMac, said its operations faced significant challenges during the financial year, with ships being taken out of service for repairs reducing the number available to cover routes and leaving less flexibility when services were disrupted.

![CalMac: ](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/17569602.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The continuing reliance on ageing vessels has become a major issue for CalMac

In its latest annual financial statement, the company said bad weather had also affected punctuality and cancellations, while it set out plans to strengthen the fleet and improve the information provided to passengers.

Mr Mackison said: “We recognise the challenges our communities have experienced, particularly as a result of fleet resilience issues, and we are committed to being open about both our progress and the areas where more work is needed.”

He added: “Securing CHFS3 [Clyde and Hebrides Ferry Service 3] was an important milestone, but we are clear that it marks the beginning of a new chapter rather than the end of a process.”

The accounts cover a period in which CalMac continued to operate Scotland’s west coast ferry network while dealing with problems involving vessels including MV Caledonian Isles, which required major steelwork repairs, and the introduction of new ships intended to increase capacity and reliability.

The new contract was directly awarded by the Scottish Government and will see CalMac continue operating the network for ten and a half years. The company said the length of the agreement gave it greater stability to plan future improvements, while the Scottish Government would retain strategic oversight and direction.

A CalMac spokesman said: "Everyone involved in delivering ferry services across the west coast is acutely aware of the need for continued investment in both ports and vessels to provide reliable, resilient services which meet community needs.

"We'll see an exceptional level of positive change in our fleet profile between 2025 and 2029, with six major and seven small vessels entering service during this time.

"There's also a commitment to keep an extra vessel in the fleet for resilience, the benefit of which was evidenced this summer when we were able to protect over 70,000 customer journeys through deploying a resilience vessel and network-wide reliability at it's highest level for several years.

"The new vessels will increase standardisation across the fleet, improving our flexibility in deployment and capability to respond to disruption.

"Work is underway to procure a replacement for MV Lord of the Isles, and design new vessels for Mull, alongside multiple port and harbour upgrade projects being delivered by our partners."  
  
A Transport Scotland spokesman said: “We launched our Islands Connectivity Plan (ICP) in May 2025, that replaced the Scottish Government’s Ferries Plan.

“As outlined in the ICP Vessels and Ports Plan we are increasing the size of the CalMac fleet, moving to introduce two new major vessels for the Little Minch routes and a resilience vessel for the major fleet, alongside progressing business cases and procurement for other vessels and ports projects.

“The arrival of thirteen new vessels to the CalMac fleet, which started with the MV Glen Sannox and was followed by the MV Isle of Islay, will improve the resilience of ferry services. The procurement for the MV Lord of the Isles’ replacement is also underway. Seven new small vessels are also under construction and will be arriving from summer 2027. In total, this means that this Government has invested to renew over a third of CalMac’s entire fleet.”
