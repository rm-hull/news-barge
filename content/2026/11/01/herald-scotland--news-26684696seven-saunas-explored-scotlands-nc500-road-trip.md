Seven saunas, one dramatic road trip. It should be no surprise that the saunas of the NC500 are in some of Scotland’s most stunning and wild locations: next to a bend in the river on one of Scotland’s most beautiful wilderness projects; looking out from the rising promontory of Dunnet Head; at a tiny harbour in a crumpled landscape of rock and water.

But what I learned on my travels is that some are also mental health lifelines to local communities and vital new spaces for bringing people together.

My pal Amber and I went as tourists, but often it seemed what was most interesting about the saunas was what they were doing for locals rather than the iconic driving route’s passing trade like us.

At a community sauna in Applecross, which was bringing in funds for a much-needed local sports pitch, volunteers talked of the “mental health crisis in the Highlands” and described how in a winter of darkness, it was “nice to have that space to sit by the fire”.

They spoke of the benefits of having a place that brings together people in an area where socialising often requires driving, that isn’t a pub.

At other saunas there was talk of the welcome heat of a shared space, in times when fuel for heating, particularly oil, is so expensive.

In one of the most sparsely populated parts of the UK, sauna-owner Brogan Rees, described how there are “very few social hubs in the area” and that, with the “local pub closed down”, she hoped her warm, cosy space would offer a hub for people to come together.

Our tour was done as part of a camping and electric vehicle (EV) drive of the route that came with its own stresses, usually involving range anxiety. Often, it seemed as if a sauna was just what the doctor ordered after some tense moment at a charging point, or a flooded campsite.

Be warned. Unlike the sauna-dense Central Belt, which I have also written about, an NC500 sauna tour requires planning: opening days are limited and two on our list were closed, including Fire and Steam at Gairloch which came highly recommended, when we passed.

### Alladale Wilderness Reserve sauna, Ardgay

Alladale's wood-fired sauna sits at a bend in the River Alladale on the wilderness reserve owned by Paul Lister, heir to the MFI fortune, who has restored woodland here and long talked of his ambition to reintroduce wolves.

Mostly, this sauna is used by tourists staying at the lodge itself, and a few are leaving as we arrive to find the box one of the hottest I have ever been in, with thermometers reading over 100C.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21651525.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Journalist Vicky Allan visiting the sauna at the Alladale Wilderness Reserve as part of a NC500 tour *(Image: Amber Parsons)*

The heat was a joy, as was the cooling dunk in the fast-running river ten minutes later.

Where Alladale wins most points is the view, looking up a glen, dotted by native tree restoration and more distant old Caledonian forest remnant. Here you might spot a golden eagle whilst taking your sweat; though don't sweat too much. There are no wolves yet at the door.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21651537.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Journalist Vicky Allan visiting the sauna at the Alladale Wilderness Reserve as part of a NC500 tour *(Image: Amber Parsons)*

### Seaview Sauna, Dunnet Bay

AJ and Jake McCall opened the first sauna on the stretch of coast along the north of Sutherland last year.

“We built it,” AJ says, “because I had quite a big career in charity and I didn’t want to do it anymore because it was stressful.”

They also thought that the one thing that was missing on this far north edge of Scotland was “a place to be warm and look after yourself a little bit”.

The most northerly sauna on the UK mainland sits atop Dunnet Head, on the private House of the Northern Gate estate, a baronial-style holiday mansion whose guests often book to use it. It is wood-fired, with most of the site pulled together or constructed by Jake.

![Journalist Vicky Allan visiting the Seaview Sauna at Dunnet Bay as part of a NC500 tour](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21651565.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Ally and Jake McCall at the Seaview Sauna at Dunnet Bay as part of a NC500 tour *(Image: Amber Parsons)*

“Everything apart from the stove and the wood inside the sauna,” she says, “is what we found locally. Jake is a Facebook Marketplace genius. The barrels are from a local brewery, so we just donated money to charity.”

AJ, who is Aufguss trained, offers guided sessions with fragrances, body scrubs, which she makes herself.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21651601.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Journalist Vicky Allan visiting the Seaview Sauna at Dunnet Bay as part of a NC500 tour *(Image: Amber Parsons)*

### Soul Solstice Sauna, Talmine Bay

“This sauna isn’t just a ‘do the NC500 and stop and have a wellness session’ project,” says Brogan Rees, “This is for my community.”

After a drenching night of rain in which we ditched the idea of camping for a bed and breakfast, this sauna perched above Talmine Bay, with a widescreen window view on one of Sutherland's most dramatic, island-dotted stretches of sea, made for the perfect spot for a dawn warm up.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21651650.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Journalist Vicky Allan and Brogan Rees owner of the Soul Solstice Sauna at Talmine Bay *(Image: Amber Parsons)*

No need for barrels or tubs – the silvery sea is right there, just a skip across the road and an ivory expanse of sand, its gentle swells beckoning. We bounced around for a while before heading in for some heat. This is also, it is said, one of the calmest swimming spots on the north coast. My own preference is always for sea with my sauna, and the dip was a treat.

Rees, who grew up in Melness and was already an avid cold-water swimmer, became a sauna convert after trying one in Yorkshire.

Soul Solstice, she believes offers an important new hub in an area, one of the most sparsely populated in the UK, which has experienced change since a tract of the land came under the ownership of billionaire Anders Povlsen’s Wildland company – as well as the promise of a spaceport and (not yet delivered) jobs.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21651677.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Journalist Vicky Allan and Brogan Rees owner of the Soul Solstice Sauna at Talmine Bay *(Image: Amber Parsons)*

“We have very few social hubs in the area right now. The local pub in Melness has closed, and while there is one in Tongue, driving isn't an option if you want to have a drink and socialise. We have our wonderful Kyle Centre, but that tends to attract an older generation.

“There are so many new faces moving into the area now, especially with everything Wildland is doing, but nowhere for everyone to naturally mix. My hope for the winter is that the sauna becomes a warm, inviting social hub where locals and newcomers can all gather, connect, and get to know one another.”

### Loyly sauna, Clachtoll Bay

Loyly, situated in a corner of a campsite at the back of white-sand Clachtoll Bay, is one designed for the tourists, not the locals. Perhaps because of the weather, but mostly because this wasn’t a sauna that had gone big on window panorama, it didn’t feel like it was about the grand view out over the dunes – but rather more about retreating into its cocoon.

However, what this sauna lacks in immediate surroundings is more than made up for in its cold dip opportunities This was our most invigoratingly ecstatic swim of the trip. The wind blast over the dunes made it feel like a risk to go in, but the tumbling waves, often a target for surfers, left us elated.

Following that, the sauna itself was comfortable and welcome – offering only cold shower outside, but access to hot shower facilities in the main caravan site block, for added warm up.

## Othala, Diabaig

At another wind-blown spot by the picturesque harbour at Diabaig is Othala, a sauna whose name, according to its founder Rebecca Edie, is the last rune of the runic alphabet, meaning both heritage and comfort.

Its floor to ceiling glass doors look out from the edge of this tiny village across a windswept Loch Torridon and it feels like a tiny cocoon of comfort against the rugged, looming landscape.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21651823.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Rebecca Edie, owner of Othala sauna in Diabaig *(Image: Amber Parsons)*

Long before she had this sauna built, Edie, a yoga teacher and shamanic practitioner, was the instigator of the Diabaig Dippers. The group began when four years ago, after a loony dook, Edie sent a message to their Whatsapp group saying, “Have I got to wait another 50 weeks before anyone will swim with me again?”

A couple of years later, after a visit to Watershed sauna in Findhorn, Edie concluded that one way of getting more people into the water might be to “make them hot”. She then enthused other villagers and friends so much that they came together and helped her build it.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21651846.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Othala sauna in Diabaig *(Image: Amber Parsons)*

“I think,” she says, “Othala has given a space where people can sit and have conversations with people in the community that they wouldn’t have sat and had time with previously – and also whilst mingling with tourists.”

For those who like a jump or a dive, I recommend, a high tide leap off the harbour wall. Check with a local for tide safety and be sure to acclimatise to the cold with a gradual dook beforehand. And, for after, a drink, or even a meal, at the local restaurant, the excellent Gille Brighde.

### Applecross Community Sauna, Applecross

There’s a sense of arrival when approaching the Applecross Community Sauna at its seaside perch, and particularly so when you have arrived, as we did, from the south west, via the 600-metre, hair-pinned climb of the Bealach na Ba.

That’s heightened when you learn that the sauna itself arrived via that challenging route. Tery McCowan recalls that her husband picked the sauna up in Fife last autumn and drove it all the way through wind, rain and wild weather. “He took a photograph of it at the top, looking down on the view.”

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21651783.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Kirsten Glover and Tery McCowan, volunteers at the Applecross Community Sauna *(Image: Amber Parsons)*

The sauna is also remarkable in being a community project devised by the Applecross Sports and Wellbeing Association, and one that is beginning to raise money for other facilities, and, they tell me, “transforming the community”. It was inspired by a visit some of the residents did to Norway.

“We get,” says Kirsten Glover, a sauna volunteer and charity PR, “such dark, long, cold winters. We felt it would be quite nice to lean into the sauna culture.”

Among other things, they hope to use the sauna funds to create a sports pitch. Sports and wellness facilities were something that the association had felt were missing from Applecross. “Especially,” says Glover, “the sports pitch – right now the kids don’t have access to a sports pitch at all.”

In a lot of places in Scotland, McCowan points out, meeting up takes place around a pub. “But sometimes it’s nice to have something that doesn’t involve drinking, a nice wee hub where people can feel healthy and come together.”

“There were times in January and February when it was just so dark and miserable that we were meeting up there with the fairy lights on and going into the sea when it was dark and stormy and we would never have done that without the sauna, and having that place to socialise.”

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21651812.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Applecross Community Sauna *(Image: Amber Parsons)*

The construction, a cute barrel with tiny chimney, offers a circular window on the gorgeous stretch of adjacent sea, and it’s possible to walk down over the stony shoreline for a quick cooling dip.

There are, they tell me, about 250 people in Applecross, and 100 of them have joined the sauna club, which offers half price sessions and events.

### Great Glen Sauna, Bridge of Oich

Due to EV charging problems, we arrived late at the Great Glen Sauna, gushing profuse apologies and jangling with stress. But it didn’t take long in this converted horsebox in a secluded spot by the quirkily-designed Bridge of Oich, to feel the tension dissipate.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21651878.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Sarah and Ben McPhee outside Great Glen Sauna at Bridge of Oich *(Image: Amber Parsons)*

What’s particularly charming about this sauna is the spot in the river, in the leafy shade of draping tree branches, where sauna guests can take a cold water dip. There’s nothing like cooling off with a crouch in the river.

Freelance designer Sarah McPhee, and her husband Ben, a senior data strategist, had been looking at what to do with their “skillsets and interests” and had a desire, she says, “to do something that brought people together and did something positive in the world, made people feel good.”

And that they certainly do – not only hosting the sauna at the site by their home, but taking the mobile horsebox to other locations, and catering for both tourists and locals.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21651886.jpg?type=mds-article-620)A dip in the river beside Great Glen Sauna at Bridge of Oich *(Image: Amber Parsons)*

“It's a fantastic way to experience the Highlands in all its beauty, grounding yourself within the local landscape,” she adds.

To make it affordable for the community they offer sauna passes, designed for regulars to make heat part of their regular rhythm and maximise the benefits sauna can bring.

“I think part of the reason I love the sauna,” she adds, “is that it gets me outside way more, especially in the winter when we do evening sessions. We’ve got all the fairy lights out and the stars are amazing. We’ve even had Northern Lights in the sky overhead."
