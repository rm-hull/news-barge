Keeping a golf course going is an expensive business. At Elmwood Golf Course in Fife, campaigners are fighting for more time to secure its future after annual running costs were put at as much as £300,000.

Scotland’s Rural College, which owns Elmwood Golf Course, has said it can no longer subsidise the operation because the course is no longer used for educational purposes, leaving the college unable to justify supporting the losses of a private venture from its charitable funds.

The clubhouse, catering operation and professional shop have already closed, while members have been asked whether they could take responsibility for running the course themselves. Without a viable alternative, operations could be progressively reduced towards closure.

![Business of Golf logo](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/20731906.jpg?type=mds-article-620) *(Image: Damian Shields)*

Elmwood Golf Course is an unusual case, not least because of its ownership and former role in education. But the financial dilemma underlying the dispute will sound familiar at golf clubs across Scotland: how do you cover the cost of maintaining a course and clubhouse without pushing membership fees beyond what golfers are willing, or able, to pay?

Documents seen by The Herald from another Scottish members' club offer a glimpse of that pressure long before a course reaches the point of possible closure.

The club remains financially stable and recorded a modest surplus in its latest accounts, but its committee has warned that subscriptions and ordinary trading income are not currently sufficient to cover recurring running costs without support from fundraising and donations. Members are now being asked to consider a significant rise in subscriptions.

Its predicament illustrates one of the uncomfortable realities facing the traditional golf club.

Courses cost money to maintain regardless of how many rounds are played. Clubhouses have to be heated, repaired and insured. Machinery eventually needs replacing. When those costs rise faster than ordinary income, there are only so many levers a club can pull: charge members more, attract more visitors, raise more money elsewhere, cut spending or generate additional commercial income.

Push subscriptions too far, however, and clubs risk losing the very members whose fees provide their most dependable source of income.

### Can Scotland's traditional golf model survive?

![Consultant Liam Greasley says golf club membership is increasingly regarded as a 'retail purchase', with fewer people willing to take on responsibility for running the business](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21653018.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Consultant Liam Greasley says golf club membership is increasingly regarded as a 'retail purchase', with fewer people willing to take on responsibility for running the business *(Image: Supplied)*

The tension between keeping membership affordable and generating enough income to run and invest in a golf club goes to the heart of a bigger question facing the game in Scotland.

This week, former PGA commercial director Liam Greasley argued that the traditional model of clubs owned and run by their members is set for a sharp decline, as rising costs, changing consumer expectations and years of under-investment push more clubs to consider outside capital and professional management.

Mr Greasley, who helped Scotscraig Golf Club secure a long-term operating agreement with The Ancient Links Golf Company, believes membership itself is changing: golfers increasingly see it as a purchase rather than accepting responsibility for helping to run the organisation. He argues that many clubs remain excellent places to play golf, but struggle to fund improvements and major maintenance while keeping fees at levels their members will accept.

### How LIV Golf's crisis could affect Scottish tournaments

![Adrian Meronk's Dunhill Links victory secured his DP World Tour status for 2027 as uncertainty mounted over LIV Golf](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21653218.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Adrian Meronk's Dunhill Links victory secured his DP World Tour status for 2027 as uncertainty mounted over LIV Golf *(Image: Getty Images)*

Finally, this week’s Around the Greens turns to the continuing turmoil surrounding LIV Golf, but with a distinctly Scottish question: what could the upheaval mean for two of the country’s biggest tournaments? LIV players have become a significant presence at both the Alfred Dunhill Links Championship and the Scottish Open, giving developments on the breakaway circuit a relevance that extends well beyond LIV itself.

The latest edition looks at where the uncertainty leaves those players and what the fallout could mean for the fields, and ultimately the pulling power, of two events that bring some of the world’s biggest golfing names to Scotland.
