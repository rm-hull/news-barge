## Guy Fawkes: 15 facts about York's most infamous son and Bonfire Night legend

Guy Fawkes

*Guy Fawkes: 15 facts about York's Bonfire Night legend, by DAVID WILSON*

AS a boy, I remember the excitement I used to feel when every year I went round to my friend Rob’s house on the evening of November 5th for Bonfire Night, which commemorates York's most infamous son, Guy Fawkes.

After tea, we all went out into the garden where Rob’s dad had already prepared a huge bonfire. On top of the fire was the guy, a figure made from discarded paper and old clothes.

The evening progressed with fireworks being let off and us kids eating sausages and baked potatoes around the bonfire. Happy days indeed! It was only a few years later when I learned about why we did it.

Most people have heard about the Gunpowder Plot of November 5, 1605 and the traditions surrounding Bonfire Night, but how many know the detailed story of Guy Fawkes, the man who was caught attempting to blow up King James I and the Houses of Parliament?

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/16513290.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Guy Fawkes

Nowadays, I live in the city where Guy Fawkes was born, and you can still go to the location where he lived out his early years. And there’s an inn named after him.

He was christened in the nearby church of St Michael-le-Belfry and was a pupil at St Peter’s School in Clifton.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21650675.jpg?type=mds-article-620)St Peter's School, York

But how was it that Guido Fawkes arguably became one of the most well-known ‘terrorists’ of Early Modern England?

Not that everyone would agree with that description, though. Guy Fawkes has been described as ‘the last man to enter Parliament with honest intentions.’ And AL Laishley penned an article in the November 1955 edition of Yorkshire Life Illustrated claiming that Guy Fawkes "showed a fine courage and a loyalty to his friends that drew reluctant admiration even from his [trial] judges".

Although Fawkes was born and spent his early childhood in York, the turning-point in his life came on the death of his father Edward. Guy left York with his mother to live at his stepfather’s new home in Scotton, near Knaresborough, and Guy adored his new stepfather, Dionysius Bainbridge.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21650673.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The Guy Fawkes Arms Scotton

Bainbridge was the squire of Scotton village and a Catholic. Guy made friends with Catholics and saw them at their worship. It’s thought that as a loyal friend he began to feel resentment for those who were responsible for their persecution. Guy also got to know Thomas Percy, a devout Catholic, who lived in Scotton; Percy was to become a key conspirator alongside Fawkes.

In 1591, Fawkes left Scotton and England to become a mercenary for a Spanish regiment. After Spain’s victory at the Siege of Calais, Guido Fawkes was promoted to the rank of alférez or sub-lieutenant. His military experience and contacts in both France and Spain brought him to the attention of insurgent English Catholics who persuaded him to join them in a scheme to rid English Roman Catholics of their persecutors.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21650671.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The Guy Fawkes Inn in York

In 1604, Fawkes was approached by Thomas Wintour, an English Catholic. Wintour and his associate Robert Catesby were plotting to assassinate King James I and place his nine-year-old daughter Elizabeth Stuart on the English throne.

With his knowledge of explosives, Fawkes was considered to be the ideal candidate for this project. Altogether, there were five initial conspirators. They were later joined by several others including Francis Tresham, Catesby’s cousin, who was eventually suspected of betraying his fellow conspirators.

The conspirators decided to load the undercroft beneath the House of Lords with 20 barrels of gunpowder. Fawkes discovered that some of the gunpowder had decayed, so another 36 barrels were added. The conspirators were forced to wait until Parliament opened. There’d been a delay since London had been beset by plague, and the usual July date for the opening of Parliament was postponed until November 5.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21650672.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Bonfire Night firework displays take place across York and Yorkshire - all thanks to the story of Guy Fawkes

Concerned that harm might come to the few Catholic MPs, one of the conspirators sent an anonymous letter to Lord Monteagle urging him to stay away from Parliament on November 5.

Monteagle showed the letter to the Earl of Salisbury who then passed it to the king. On November 4, after a thorough search of the buildings surrounding Parliament, Fawkes was caught red-handed among his 36 barrels holding a long match, and gave his name as John Johnson.

He wasn’t taken directly to a dungeon, but was brought to the King’s bedchamber to meet James I. Fawkes despised the king, expressing his wish to ‘blow you Scotch beggars back to your native mountains’. Fawkes initially resisted naming his fellow-conspirators but finally gave in after days of increasingly brutal torture.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21650676.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Memorial plaque to Guy Fawkes in Stonegate, York

Fawkes was condemned to be hanged, drawn and quartered, but on the day of his execution, he cheated the authorities by jumping from the scaffold and breaking his neck.

Days before Fawkes’ execution, MP Edward Montague proposed the Observance of 5th November Act. The law enshrined the November 5 as a National Day of Commemoration to celebrate the failure of the Gunpowder Plot. This Act was repealed in 1859, but the tradition of Bonfire Night has continued to the present day even if it’s being increasingly eclipsed by Halloween.

But Parliament hasn’t forgotten. Every year, before the official opening of Parliament, teams of guards inspect the cellars under the Palace of Westminster to make sure that nobody has placed explosives there. If successful in their search, the guards are given a glass of port wine and Parliament commences.
