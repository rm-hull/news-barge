From her base in Thurso on the north coast of Scotland, Amy Jones is investigating a technology that some believe could help solve the problem of environmentally-friendly energy generation.

Everyone will be familiar with wind turbines, thousands of which are now found across Scotland and around its coastline, and many will have seen some of the increasingly common solar arrays being erected in fields and industrial sites. But the devices in which Jones, a PhD researcher at the University of the Highlands and Islands (UHI), is interested are very different, and largely invisible.

“They're what we call wet renewables,” she explains.

“A lot of the industry is wind and solar. And offshore wind is relatively straightforward because we learnt a lot of lessons originally from its parents on land. So we sort of know, to an extent what it will look like in water, although obviously there are differences.

“But wet renewables, tidal and wave, are different, because getting something wet is really complicated, and then putting it underneath the surface is very different and that's all quite new and there's quite a lot of anxiety surrounding it because it doesn't have any on-land parents to replicate.”

Despite those data gaps, there has been increasing interest in tidal power because it offers the prospect of clean, carbon-free energy while solving perhaps the most obvious problem with wind turbines: what if the wind blow when needed?

“We don't know when the wind will blow, and what speed, and there are gusts that cause problems...but the tide is the tide, so as long as the moon is here, there will be energy.

We know exactly when that energy's going to come in. It shifts throughout the day, but it shifts in predictable phases, so twice a day, every day, you will get tide. This the difference with wind. There will always be a sun and there will always be a moon.”

Solar and tidal energy are cosmically reliable, but like other renewables, need to be deployed in appropriate locations. The ideal circumstances for solar may not exist in Scotland, but the sheer scale of the energy passing through the channels like the Pentland Firth makes them perfect for underwater power generation, and plans are already in motion to exploit this incredible, natural, and endlessly renewable energy resource.

As is so often the case, however, the situation is not that simple. As Jones explains, pulling all of this energy out of our coastal waters is not a consequence-free proposition, and we do not yet fully understand the repercussions that could follow.

“So air isn't very dense at all,” she says, waving her hand through the air.

“It's difficult to do that through water. Hence why swimming's much harder than walking.

“So, the density of power that you get when you're putting a turbine through water means that when it's spinning, you get much, much, much higher values.”

This is, of course, why the technology seems to promising, and why companies have sprung up seeking a way to make it commercially viable - but it also explains why the overall impact of a turbine is very different in water than it is in the air. Jones believes that the consequences could affect the wider marine eco-system in various different ways, impacting on all levels of the food chain and potentially altering the environment itself.

Top predator species, she says, use the rapid tidal flows we are seeking to tame as conveyer belts, allowing them to travel long distances while burning far fewer calories. The chances of them striking the turbines themselves are thought to be very low, but the presence – and noise – of those arrays could redirect animals towards other areas that put greater strain on their energy reserves.

“A lot of species also use tidal streams for foraging,” Jones adds, “and we're finding this out more and more.”

“They will just hold station in the water column and forage there. And we also see a lot of seabirds coming. There are quite a lot of what we call hydro-dynamic surface features.

“There are slicks, eddies, convergences where two waters are meeting one another, and they create some sort of visual clue. Birds use those. Two water bodies push upwards, meaning the fish will be available near the surface, and some of them will be holding stations because it's a tidal stream, so they dive in.

“Tidal stream energy is brilliant because there's loads of energy in there, but it's also a really popular travelling destination and a really popular foraging destination.

“So not only is there lots of opportunity to extract energy, there's just an awful lot ecologically going on there. It's not like the open ocean. It's a coastal habitat.”

![A woman in front of a lighthouse using a telescope to look out to sea.](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21681541.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Amy Jones says that underwater renewables have consequences for coastal and marine food chains *(Image: Amy Jones)*

For scientists like her, this area is known as the neritic zone: shallow coastal waters that are home to the majority of life in the seas. Between Caithness and Orkney, that area is also an energy-rich tidal stream habitat, and that crucial combination generates “an absolute feast” for all sorts of creatures.

“The tides are drawing the plankton up and pushing them along. Fish forage there, so seals forage there, so orca forage there, all those sorts of things.

“We don't know what taking the energy out of the system will do to the system as a whole. What is the threshold? Where can we continue to take energy out the system and it won't have too much of an impact on that food web, and where do we tip it over the edge and find that there isn't enough tidal movement anymore?

What’s more, the possible impacts of undersea renewables are not limited to biological aspects.

“So large sort of chunks of sediment move across during these really high energy periods, and then as the tide becomes slack, it drops out of the water column.

Jones is concerned that this process could be disrupted by an unsustainable increase in underwater renewables, causing new problems with sedimentation of the sea bed that would further impact on fragile food chains. At the most basic level, the technology would extract energy from an environment that depends upon it, and doing so will have a variety of consequences that we do not, at present, fully understand. We may not be able to see the impact of our actions below the surface of the waves, but Jones is adamant that we must not ignore it.

“It's not to say we shouldn't do it. We just need to think far more carefully.

“We've learned a lot of lessons from sticking a turbine up in the air. We've learned a few lessons from sticking them down in the water.

“But there's a lot more to know and there's a lot more to learn.”
