## Only York locals can pronounce this street name correctly. Can you?

Gillygate York in 1990

*York's Gillygate: The street many visitors pronounce wrongly by MAXINE GORDON*

YORK has many names and locations to confuse visitors and newcomers to the city.

Firstly, there are the bar walls, where you'll be disappointed if you turn up looking for a pint.

And many are left scratching their heads when looking for York 'Castle' – only to discover a grassy mound by a car park.

Next come the place names that only locals know how to pronounce.

Top of that list comes Gillygate.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/9384319.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Gillygate in York. Picture: Google Maps

Many a visitor – and new arrival to York – say this street name totally wrong.

I will put my hands up and admit that when I moved to the city almost 30 years ago one of the first corrections pointed out was that Gillygate is pronounced with a soft rather than a hard "G".

But where did the name come from?

Surprisingly, given the modern-look of the buildings, the street name of Gillygate dates back to the 16th century.

It was named after the church of St Giles, which was situated roughly on the site of the Salvation Army Citadel building.

The church was out of use by 1586 and demolished.

And the street has a history of misfortune over the decades and centuries which followed.

Being outside the safety of the city walls, Gillygate was virtually destroyed during the siege of York in 1644 and the bar walls have been on the wrong side of the road ever since.

The York Conservation Trust outlines the fascinating history of the street – and how it overcame countless challenges to its existence.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21677042.jpg?type=mds-article-620)This shows two trams passing on Gillygate towards the bottom of Lord Mayor's Walk. This image was taken around 1912, shortly after the electric trams were launched in York

It also reveals some little-known facts, including how Gillygate once lead to York’s Horse Fair: a market for horses and cattle dating from medieval times on a site now occupied by York Hospital.

There was money to be made from this trade and none other than the Archbishop of York was a beneficiary. He claimed the right to charge a toll for every animal brought to and leaving the fair – effectively imposing a double-tax on livestock.

Ahead of the Civil War siege of York in 1644, all the buildings in the moat against the walls were demolished as a defensive measure.

As York slowly recovered from the Civil War the area in front of the ramparts on Gillygate was repopulated by a range of buildings, the earliest surviving being nos 26-28 constructed in 1769 for the master builder Robert Clough.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21677041.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Advertisement-covered wall on the north side of Gillygate, April 1902. Picture: Explore York Libraries and Archives

By the 1930s Gillygate was at risk again, with talk of more demolition to create a wider inner ring road.

Gillygate’s demolition might well have gone ahead had it not been for the massive destruction of property during the Second World War.

Over the next two decades the city council saw the benefits of retaining what remained of York’s inner city, along with considering new, more popular ideas for the preservation of York’s unique character.

Today, Gillygate is a busy thoroughfare used by locals and visitors heading to and from the car parks, shops and cafes that line the street.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/19666940.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Gillygate is bustling with traffic and people today. Image: Newsquest

Its name is woven into centuries of York history, from medieval horse fairs and civil war destruction to battles over modern redevelopment.

Yet for all that history, many visitors still stumble over the same thing when they first arrive: remembering that Gillygate is pronounced with a soft "G" rather than a hard one.
