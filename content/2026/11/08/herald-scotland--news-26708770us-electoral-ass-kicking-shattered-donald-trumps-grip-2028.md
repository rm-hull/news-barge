*As the blame game for the midterms disaster plays out among Republicans, it’s already shaping the presidential contest in two years’ time writes Foreign Editor David Pratt*

It was all so very different barely a few weeks ago. Admittedly it wasn’t exactly business as usual within the Trump administration and Republican Party, for they knew fine well that the coming midterm elections posed a serious challenge,

This however did not stop a certain complacency from prevailing. In retrospect, it’s now obvious that many within the party’s ranks had for some time been tone deaf to the concerns of the American people. Last Tuesday that seeming indifference cost Trump and the GOP dearly at the ballot box.

“It was a Watergate-level repudiation… an ass kicking for the Republican party,” was how US senator Ted Cruz summed it up in the wake of the Democrats’ sweeping wins last week.

In fact such was the decimation of the Republicans, that Cruz himself might well have lost his seat were it not for that fact that he wasn’t on the ballot and not up for re-election until 2030. That though did not stop the Democrats from flipping that other Texas Senate seat belonging to Republican John Cornyn.

Democrat James Talarico won that race against Republican Ken Paxton, meaning Talarico will join Cruz as Texas's other senator when the new Congress is sworn in.

But long before that swearing in ceremony on January 3rd, 2027, there will be a lot more “ass-kicking” to come for the Republicans, this time though from inside their own ranks as the internal blame game for the midterm disaster plays out.

Put quite simply, what last week’s rout has done is completely shatter the expected trajectory of the 2028 Republican primary, transforming what was once a clear succession plan into a wide open chaotic civil war for the future of the GOP.

Waking up to those results last Wednesday, Republicans were faced with the stark realisation that the Democrats “blue wave” had effectively decoupled the MAGA movement from an automatic coronation of Trump's inner circle.

What comes now will effectively be a circular GOP firing squad picking each other off as the internal reckoning plays out.

The first salvo was fired by none other than Vice President JD Vance who placed the blame for the defeat squarely on the war with Iran, which President Trump began eight months ago.

“If you’d like to argue it was worth it, that losing the Senate for the next 6 years was worth it, then do so,” the vice president posted on social media. “But everyone knows why we lost.”

*Political failure*

Vance’s comments were a striking sign that he is willing to separate himself from the president as he considers his own political future - and he is far from the only one.

Already potential candidates are being forced to choose between doubling down on current administration policies or presenting a stark course correction.

While some observers say that Vance’s comments marked the start of the presidential campaign, historians may instead date the start to November 2nd, when Ron DeSantis, Florida’s Republican governor, accused unnamed fellow Republicans of “gaslighting” Americans by pretending the war had not raised costs of petrol and other commodities.

“Listen, we just got to be honest. Gas is higher. It hurts people, working-class people, and it's higher because of this conflict. That’s just the fact,” said DeSantis.

“If you can't admit that to voters, then you're gaslighting them, and they're not going to listen to you on other things,” he went on to warn.

By this breaking from party lines right around the midterms election day, DeSantis effectively drew a stark line between himself and the current administration. So where now does this leave Republicans?

The first clues in answering that all important question will come this Tuesday when the House Republican leadership election gets underway exactly one week after the midterms. In this high stakes battleground, many US political observers predict that what we will likely see is a contest between the old versus the new guard of the GOP.

In other words, it will effectively be a litmus test as to how closely GOP members perceive ties between their existing leadership team and the midterm battering and decide whether to chart a different course.

At the centre of this core leadership battle will be a head-to-head contest between House Majority Leader Steve Scalise and Judiciary Chair Jim Jordan, or as some have depicted it, the “establishment versus insurgency.”

Scalise is positioning himself as the institutionalist who can unify the mainstream wing of the party, while Jordan is rallying the hardline, populist factions demanding an aggressive shift in strategy after the election losses.

Against this backdrop of the House Republican leadership election, other seismic shifts are already underway. In the first move to shake up his inner circle since last week’s bruising defeat, Trump has announced that White House chief of staff Susie Wiles is being replaced by Stephen Miller, his longtime aide and architect of the administration’s hardline immigration policies.

“Stephen's been with me since the beginning. He gets it,” Trump told reporters on Friday, before writing on social media that Miller will become chief of staff Monday 9 November, the day before the House Republican leadership contest gets underway in earnest.

Known for his combative style and rhetorical pugnacity, Miller’s appointment signals that the president is looking for a loyalist to handle what are likely to be fraught relations with the Democratic-controlled Congress that will take office in January.

*'Blood on his hands'*

Democratic leaders on Capitol Hill were quick to disparage the move. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries - widely expected to become the new House speaker said: “Stephen Miller is a dangerous xenophobe and reckless extremist who has American blood on his hands. His presence in government should be eradicated like a malignant tumour. Fire him today.”

Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer meanwhile added: “Donald Trump learned nothing from his historic defeat. Stephen Miller is the architect of the most barbaric, inhumane policies of this administration. Trump couldn't have made a worse choice. Our Democratic majorities will fight him every step of the way.”

In other words, the new battlelines between the Republicans and Democrats are already being drawn and Trump’s message could not be clearer, signalling as it does that he is seeking a more confrontational approach to advancing the administration's agenda rather than looking for compromises.

Miller who personally drafted or edited nearly every executive order Trump has signed, is expected to push a maximalist approach to executive power now that Democrats control Congress. According to the Wall Street Journal (WSJ) citing associates of the president, an early test for Miller, is whether he can satisfy Trump’s desire to punish his perceived political enemies.

Miller’s appointment could also have a determining impact on the future of US Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth, who many see as vulnerable and a potential scapegoat for Trump over the war in Iran that proved so unpopular with voters in the midterms.

White House watchers say that Miller unlike Wiles, who had grown frustrated with Hegseth, would be more supportive of the Defence Secretary with whom he is also close socially.

As if to highlight that mutual support, soon after the announcement of Miller’s appointment Hegseth posted an image of himself with the new chief of staff, and a phrase from his nearly six minute social media video: “Stand. Fight. Defend the Republic.”

So far Trump too has indicated that he has “every intention” of keeping Hegseth, but that has done little to staunch speculation of a knives out campaign against the Pentagon chief. There have even been suggestions that the House could vote as soon as this week to impeach Hegseth.

House Republicans, among them Thomas Massie of Kentucky, the famously anti-Trump GOP lawmaker, told CNN he plans to trigger a vote to impeach Hegseth when Congress returns this week and multiple GOP sources said they expect it to pass.

Hegseth could of course choose to resign rather than face congressional probes and impeachment proceedings. But finding a candidate to replace him, particularly one that a Democratic-led Senate would confirm, could prove challenging, especially for new chief of staff Miller.

“Pete is focused on three things: survival, survival, and survival,” a US official was quoted by Politico magazine as saying “He wants to survive long enough where he can leave on his own terms... He wants to survive to preserve a political future.”

*'Shellacking'*

That question of the political future - and not just Hegseth’s - is understandably said to be the main talking point around Washington’s corridors of power right now post the Republicans’ midterm “shellacking”.

Inevitably speculation has also focused on what it might mean for the next presidential election in two years’ time and who the Republican candidates might now be. The first thing to recognise say political analysts is that Trump will remain an enduring presence in the 2028 election cycle especially on foreign policy. That said, attention is already moving towards his potential successors and the field is now wide open.

Future Republican candidates will likely pivot away from Trump's highly unpopular foreign policy footprint (such as the war in Iran and Russian fuel deals) and redirect their focus toward domestic economic issues, aiming to counter Democratic momentum on affordability and corporate accountability.

Names already bandied around include JD Vance current vice president, Ron DeSantis, governor of Florida, Marco Rubio, Secretary of State, and Nikki Haley former UN ambassador. Each have already taken their stance over the midterm outcome and shown signs of a strategic shift.

In Vance’s case he has publicly blamed the result on voter anxiety over the war in Iran and shifting consumer prices, while simultaneously laying groundwork for a solo 2028 run. While currently odds-on favourite to win the Republican nomination, history shows that sitting vice presidents seldom win the presidency. Vance in the White House might also be a dismal prospect for many European leaders many of whom still regard his now infamous speech at the Munich Security conference in 2025 as tantamount to a declaration of “ideological war” on European allies

DeSantis like Vance has also strongly distanced himself from the Trump national strategy, calling the GOP's widespread defeats an “extinction-level event” for the party.

Marco Rubio or “little Marco” as Trump sometimes dubs him, is meanwhile reportedly preparing to resign from the administration to officially launch a more traditional, wide-appeal 2028 primary campaign.

A recent New York Times poll found that Rubio enjoys the support of nearly one out of four self-identified Republican primary voters. But like Vance, he too could find it hard to establish himself as his own man outside of Trump.

Which brings us to Nikki Haley who is positioned to capture the anti-Trump or moderate lane of the primary, aiming to appeal to voters turned off by the administration's foreign policy and corruption scandals.

Other candidates could of course enter the field such as Chris Christie the former Governor of New Jersey who mounted two previous campaigns for the GOP presidential nomination, but for the moment it’s worth noting the extent to which all the key figures are starting to distance themselves from Trump.

*Massive task*

Laurel Rapp is Director of the US and North America Programme at Chatham House and believes that Republicans, “face the massive task of revitalising a party defined by Trump for the last decade – while also managing his likely moves to compel loyalty and cement a MAGA succession plan.”

“As the Republican party looks ahead to 2028, Trump himself may be one of their biggest obstacles to forging a new platform that retires some of the more unpopular elements of Trump’s legacy – expensive wars, tariff-related economic disruption and allegations of corruption – while retaining winning elements for their base,” says Rapp.

It’s a view shared by many including another former Trump chief of staff, Mick Mulvaney.

“If you want to be the Republican president in 2028, not just the nominee, but actually the person who sits behind the Resolute Desk, you have to be close enough to Trump to win the primary, but not so close that he drags you down in the general,” said Mulvaney speaking in an interview last week. “That is a tall order,” Mulvaney added.

A tall order indeed, and while it's one that potential Republican nominees have doubtless already weighed up, they have other things on their mind for the moment. Things like simple political survival as well as unfinished score settling after last week's day of reckoning.
