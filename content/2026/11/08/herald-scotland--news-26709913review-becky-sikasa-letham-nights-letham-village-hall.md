Becky Sikasa Letham Nights, Letham Village Hall, Fife. Teddy Jamieson Four Stars

The ending of Becky Sikasa’s first song is so abrupt that the audience in Letham Village Hall doesn’t know how to react.

There’s no spontaneous applause, no cheering and whooping. Just a kind of possibly stunned silence as we all take in what’s just happened; Sikasa singing up close to the mic, a voice pure and supple and full of restrained emotion, held and buoyed up by the cat’s cradle of her bandmates’ supporting vocals, swimming in and around Sikasa’s, a cushioning confection of silk and cotton and sweetness.

Finally, there’s a ripple of surprised, delighted laughter as Sikasa’s drummer Kyle Sharp beats out the introduction to the next song. Was that for real? The following hour proves that, yes, it most definitely was.

This, the first date in a short Scottish tour that sees Sikasa play Aberdeen, Edinburgh and Glasgow in the next few days, proves an up-close audience with one of Scotland’s brightest new talents. The result is both a calling card and a statement of intent. Watch this face.

Sikasa - who was named BBC Introducing Scottish Act of the Year in 2025 and who supported Sam Smith at the London Coliseum the other month - is part of Scotland’s burgeoning neo-soul scene (see also Brooke Combe and James Emmanuel), though she actually grew up in Germany.

That said, she speaks with a Scottish accent and while her vocals have only the slightest of local flavouring she does at one point croon the word “Darling” this evening with an unmistakeable Caledonian lilt.

Tonight, she sings song after song of heartbreak, without ever sounding defeated. “There is no democracy in love,” she laments in new number Monsters, her voice by turns sweet and yearning, conjuring up faint echoes of everyone from Erykah Badu, Lauryn Hill, Olivia Dean (particularly on new song Sky is Close) and maybe even Marie De Salle (Lisa Bonet’s character in the film High Fidelity). But then comparisons are unnecessary when she sounds this good.

“This is special,” Sikasa suggests near the beginning of her set. And it is. Letham Nights have been running since 2008 in this small village hall in Fife.

The whole evening has a homespun, homemade feel (you can now buy local honey at the bar). It is both pleasingly incongruous and wonderfully intimate.

Intimate enough that the first support act of the night, the poet and musician Euan Stamper, can play guitar and harmonium without amplification.

Intimate enough, too, that you can watch the interplay of looks and glances and smiles between Sikasa’s band members Lissa Robertson (who also plays keyboards), Keli Thomson and guitarist Eriff (who doubled up as a support act, singing his own indie folk songs beautifully beforehand) as they weave vocal harmonies in and around Sikasa’s.

All of them are clearly thrilled by the noise they’re making. As they should be.

After playing her own songs, Sikasa and her band return for an encore, launching into a cover of Bob Marley’s Is This Love reframed for the warp and weft of their combined voices. It feels like a gift. But then you could say the same of the whole evening.
