A government inspector has agreed that a basement flat would “not provide acceptable living conditions.”

In May, Bradford Council refused a retrospective planning application to convert the basement/lower ground floor of 66 Kirkgate in Shipley into a studio flat.

Officers claimed the fact that the living space is below ground level means the development “will result in substandard living conditions due to the self-contained lower ground floor studio apartment being served by limited outlook and natural day light.”

## Appeal submitted

Applicant A Khan appealed the decision, saying the lightwell and glazed door to the flat would allow enough light into the flat, and a sunken forecourt meant there would be some outlook for residents.

The appeal said: “The proposal provides acceptable accommodation in context.

"It makes efficient use of existing lower ground floor space in a sustainable urban location and does not give rise to wider planning harm.”

A recent estate agent brochure for 66 Kirkgate mentions the lower ground floor flat, and says it has a £600pcm rental income.

The plans show the 37 square metre flat has studio living space, a kitchen area, toilet and shower room and one square metre of storage space.

## Government inspector sides with the council

The appeal was appointed to planning inspector Chris Mayes, who has now sided with council planners and dismissed the plans.

His inspection report says: “At my site visit, I observed that the apartment was occupied.

“The depth of the apartment and the restricted nature of window openings would limit the penetration of daylight into the accommodation, particularly towards the rear of the open-plan living space and the kitchen area.

“In addition, occupants' outlook would remain largely confined to the enclosed forecourt and its retaining structures. Consequently, occupants would be unduly reliant on artificial lighting.

“The fully glazed entrance door would open directly into the living and sleeping space. The apartment is set below the remaining front garden, which provides access to the main dwelling.

“Consequently, people using that space or approaching the main dwelling would be able to see directly into the area in which occupants of the apartment would undertake most of their everyday domestic activities.

“The restricted outlook, inadequate natural daylight and the privacy and security implications of the glazed entrance door would result in poor-quality accommodation.

“The development would not provide acceptable living conditions for occupants.”
