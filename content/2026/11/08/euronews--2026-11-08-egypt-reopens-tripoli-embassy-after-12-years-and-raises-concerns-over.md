**Dogyu Lee & Euronews Doha bureau**

## Egypt is seeking closer ties with Libya’s western government after years of backing its eastern rival. Talks in Tripoli covered energy projects and Egypt’s objections to Libya’s maritime deal with Turkey.

Egypt reopened its historic embassy building in the Libyan capital, Tripoli, on Saturday during a visit by Foreign Minister Badr Abdelatty for talks on trade, energy and a disputed maritime boundary.

Libya’s acting foreign minister, Taher al-Baour, joined Abdelatty at the ceremony. Egypt closed the building in January 2014 after gunmen kidnapped five embassy staff. Its diplomatic mission resumed work in Tripoli in May 2021.

Cairo has long backed Khalifa Haftar, the military commander whose forces dominate eastern Libya, where a rival government is based. In Tripoli, Abdelatty met Abdulhamid Dbeibah, the prime minister of Libya’s internationally recognised Government of National Unity.

The two discussed ways to make trade easier. Dbeibah praised the role Egyptian companies and workers have played in rebuilding Libya, according to Egypt’s foreign ministry.

Abdelatty also met Libya’s acting oil minister at the reopened embassy. He called for faster progress on gas pipelines between the two countries and on plans to refine Libyan crude in Egypt for export. The Libyan minister said he would travel to Cairo for further talks, Libya’s state news agency reported on Sunday.

## Why the maritime deal is disputed

In his talks with Dbeibah, Abdelatty raised Egypt’s objections to a 2019 agreement between Turkey and Libya’s then government in Tripoli. He said Egypt’s concerns should be taken into account when maritime boundaries are drawn, according to his ministry. Dbeibah told Egyptian broadcaster Al-Qahera News that officials from both countries would discuss the maps and their respective claims at their next meeting.

The agreement draws a boundary between Turkish and Libyan maritime zones in the eastern Mediterranean, where several countries claim rights to explore for oil and gas. Greece says the boundary ignores the rights of Greek islands, including Crete, which lies between the Turkish and Libyan coasts. Egypt and Cyprus also oppose the deal.

Libya’s eastern-based parliament ratified the agreement on 20 October after opposing it for years. The EU said the next day that the deal infringed on other countries’ rights and could have no legal effect on them. Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan defended it as “an entirely legitimate agreement, both from a legal and a political perspective”.
