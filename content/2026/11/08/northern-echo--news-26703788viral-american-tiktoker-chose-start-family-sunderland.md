A Kentucky woman who moved to Sunderland for love has become an online ambassador for the North East, building a following by documenting her accidental British life.

Seven years ago, Kaylena Mushen swapped rural Kentucky for Sunderland after falling for a Mackem she met in an Iceland hostel.

The viral TikToker, known online as Kentucky Fried Brit, has built a loyal following by documenting life’s culture shocks and challenging outsiders’ views of the North East.

Mrs Mushen, 33, grew up in Brandenburg, Kentucky, and moved to Hillview, Sunderland, in November 2019.

“What dragged me over the sea was a Mackem boy,” Mrs Mushen said, explaining that meeting her now-husband, Ben, in Iceland began a "whirlwind" transatlantic romance.

The couple became engaged within six months. Ben then spent about three months in Kentucky before they began the visa process for Kaylena to move to the North East.

She arrived in Sunderland just before the Covid pandemic and became “stuck” in the UK when restrictions began.

Despite the circumstances, she said being in the North East during that period helped her avoid homesickness as she settled into a new life.

Mrs Mushen said her expectations of England were very different from what she found, having imagined the Cotswolds, rural fairy-tale villages and an aristocratic English family.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21677163.jpg?type=mds-article-620)“What dragged me over the sea was a Mackem boy,” Mrs Mushen said. *(Image: Kaylena Mushen)*

Instead, after flying to London and taking a train north, her first stop was The Bridges shopping centre in Sunderland.

"I do appreciate The Bridges now but, oh man, it was just confusing. Just total culture shock," she said.

Understanding the local accent was another challenge, but after seven years in the North East she can now follow Mackem with ease.

Her favourite local expression is “pet”, one of the British terms of endearment she finds most charming.

Public transport brought another major culture shock.

In rural Kentucky, she said, there was no public transport and people had to rely on designated drivers or scarcely available taxis, making the transition to Britain's widespread train and bus links a challenge.

On her first North East bus journey, Mrs Mushen was thrown off for handing the driver a penny instead of a pound coin- and had no idea why.

She said: “Everything about public transport blew me away.

"You're so connected to everything around here and to the rest of Europe."

Mrs Mushen said her favourite spots in the North East are Seaham beach, Durham City and her local pubs and bars, praising British towns for their connection to the wider community without having to drive everywhere.

Work and family life in the England, when compared to the United States, have also changed her perspective, she said.

She described US work culture as a “rat race” where work came first. In England, she found more emphasis on balance, socialising and family.

“I wake up in the morning, and I'm able to drop my kid off at nursery. Work doesn't bat an eyelid, or ask any questions about it,” Mrs Mushen said.

"I'm very fortunate with the work-life balance that we have. I didn't even play with the idea of having a kid in the States."

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21677162.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Kaylena Mushen described US work culture as a “rat race” where work came first. In England, she found more emphasis on balance, socialising and family. *(Image: Kaylena Mushen)*

Now, she has one daughter, who is nearly two and has dual British and American nationality.

Maternity leave, nursery costs and the NHS in the UK helped encourage Mrs Mushen to have her daughter, she said.

She added her stepbrother’s partner in the US had just six weeks of maternity leave before returning to full-time work, while her nursery fees ran into thousands.

The Mushens now celebrate Thanksgiving and the Fourth of July from Sunderland, connecting their daughter to her American heritage.

Mrs Mushen said: "I don't want her to miss out on too many of those things. She's got the best of both worlds with that, really."

Her home state of Kentucky and Sunderland are more similar than she'd imagined, the mum said, sharing links to coal mining, industry and working-class communities.

She said the regions’ industrial heritage had shaped how people socialise, making Southerners and North East residents more likely to chat to strangers and take life as it comes.

Mrs Mushen added: "I think that breeds a certain type of person. I think it breeds someone that doesn't take life too seriously, that is willing to give you the shirt off their back.

"I think we've got quite a 'whatever happens, happens' type of attitude, because it's out of our control.

"It almost is identical, the type of characteristics and the people I knew in Kentucky, and the people I've met here."

Mrs Mushen’s TikTok account has attracted more than 11,000 followers with videos about motherhood, slang, food, culture clashes and “accidentally becoming British”.

Some fans have dubbed her "an unofficial ambassador for Sunderland."

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21677216.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Mushen has built a loyal following by documenting life’s culture shocks and challenging outsiders’ views of the North East. *(Image: @kentuckyfriedbrit)*

Mrs Mushen said outsiders often assume Sunderland lacks education and culture, echoing stereotypes about the American South.

“I think poverty and living in areas that are deprived, breathe that type of culture,” she said. Her videos began as a way to showcase Sunderland’s people, food and culture without mockery or judgement.

Many Sunderland residents were surprised an American had chosen to live there “of all places," she said, but grew to value her fresh perspective on the city.

“Americans do like living in Sunderland and choose to do so, which is probably the craziest thing for people,” Mrs Mushen added.

Seven years after crossing the Atlantic, Mushen's enthusiasm for her adopted home shows no sign of fading.

Through her videos, she has found a way to bridge two very different worlds, helping Americans understand the North East while reminding local people that the places they often take for granted can look pretty special through fresh eyes.
