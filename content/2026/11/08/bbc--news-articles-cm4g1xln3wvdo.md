Deborah Powis says putting her feet in a bag of chopped onion helps reduce her cough

**Deborah Powis has a trick she swears by when she gets a cough.**

I have stopped her on the street in the market town of Horncastle, Lincolnshire, to ask what cold remedies she turns to at this time of year.

"You chop up an onion," she begins. "You put it in plastic bags, put your feet inside and put your socks over the top.

"You do this overnight – yes your sheets smell in the morning, but you can wash your sheets."

The 62-year-old midday school supervisor says her daughter has also started using this method.

"I know it works. I don't know the science behind it, but it does stop the cough."

It may sound like folklore, but as one expert explains, other homespun remedies have been tested in the lab and work – at least for some people.

According to Prof Alyn Morice, head of respiratory medicine at the Hull York Medical School, genetics mean some remedies, such as menthol, work better within particular families.

"Sometimes a person can have a good response to menthol and other people don't. And it often runs in families," he explains.

And how our brain and nervous system responds to treatment can also make a difference – even to a placebo.

![A head and shoulders shot of a man with closely cropped grey hair and a matching beard, smiling as he looks into the camera. He is wearing round glasses, a grey blazer and blue shirt.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/944b/live/34b5cb60-d9ea-11f1-bbe5-797ee05bee69.jpg)

Prof Alyn Morice recommends honey and lemon for a cough

Morice runs the Hull Cough Clinic, one of the largest centres in Europe, helping people with chronic coughs.

"Sore throat, you can have paracetamol for. But the cough can be really troublesome," he says.

"What I recommend in the first option is a dollop of honey in the bottom of a cup, then squeeze of lemon, pour in hot water and mix it all up. This does actually work.

"It coats the lining of the throat and decreases the amount of irritation of the receptors in there, which are giving you the tickle, which is making you cough.

"It's called the demulcent effect. And that's why most remedies that you can buy over the counter are syrups."

According to the UK Flu Survey, external, 47 per cent of us take time off work or school with flu-like symptoms. Most of us treat ourselves at home, with the NHS recommending, external rest, plenty of fluids and taking paracetamol or ibuprofen to lower the temperature and treat aches and pains.

Aden Kelly says Jakemans makes about three million menthol lozenges a day

One substance many of us have turned to is menthol, whether by dissolving crystals in a steamy bowl, rubbing ointment on our chest or sucking on cough sweets.

Jakemans, in Boston, has been making what it calls "menthol confections" for almost 120 years.

As it is autumn, staff have been turning out about three million lozenges a day. Even outside the factory, there is a warming aroma of menthol and aniseed.

"It can help if you have a scratchy throat, it can help ease that. It has menthol in it, so can give you that lift which we all need." says sales director Aden Kelly.

"Are they a placebo? Or do they work?" I press.

"I certainly get a lot of relief," says Kelly. "I do a lot of talking in my job. So, I personally find they work very well."

You might expect that answer from the sales director. But Morice says menthol has a proven effect.

"Menthol actually reduces the amount of coughing – I showed that many years ago," he says.

"So, you can either inhale menthol or you can have it in a cough lozenge, which decreases the amount of coughing."

![Inside a factory, in the foreground, a conveyor belt is covered in small, shiny black oval shaped lozenges. In the background a worker is collecting batches of the lozenges into trays.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/7312/live/ecf34930-d7b7-11f1-93e1-3b657b665bcf.jpg)

Jakemans, based near Boston, has been making lozenges since 1907

Back in Hull, the consumer health firm Reckitt produces well-known self-care treatments, including Lemsip.

It contains paracetamol and a decongestant, phenylephrine. But home remedies have influenced the product.

"It was introduced back in the 1960s. It was a lemon-flavoured hot drink designed to relieve cold and flu symptoms," says Dr Mark Ripley, a senior vice-president for research and development.

"When people get sick, they'll have a warm cup of tea or cocoa. And the idea here was to combine that ritual of a warm beverage with real medicine.

"And of course, lemon, honey, ginger, blackcurrant – some of the flavours that we have – they're all associated often with home remedy."

## Family lore

Like Powis with her onions, parents and grandparents pass down advice to the next generation.

And as Morice explains, there is a genetic reason why certain treatments become family favourites.

"The thing with all these agents and remedies is that different individuals respond differently to them," he says.

"This tendency to cough and have relief of cough from certain remedies is inherited.

"So, if there's a family lore that says 'we use menthol and it works very well', then it's probable that children in the family will also respond to the same menthol lozenge or inhaler."

So why do placebos also work for some people?

"Normally, coughing is held with the brakes on by the brain," Morice says.

"In some people, if you stimulate these brakes, you reduce the coughing. It's not just them imagining it – it is a demonstrable, neurological nerve effect from the brain controlling the cough."

So could this be why Deborah knows her onions work for her?

Morice says it is possible there is "a germ of truth" in this remedy and that the smell has an impact.

"Perhaps they have found in her family, it alters their perception of the cough."
