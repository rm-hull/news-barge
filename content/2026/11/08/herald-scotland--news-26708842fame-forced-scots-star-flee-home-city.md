Here's another of these ones where I think, ‘Surely I’ve done him already?’ But naw.

Hitherto, David Hayman, described variously by The Herald as “Scotland’s iconic character actor” and “Scotland’s most loveable hard man”, has slid under the radar.

He’s one of Scotland’s most acclaimed actors. He played a man who smeared himself in poop. For two years, he couldn’t walk the streets of Glasgow. Sounds a bit over-qualified to be an Icon.

But here we are. And there he was, on 9 February 1948, being born in the aforementioned Glasgow. He went on to star in too many films, TV dramas and theatre productions to mention.

Here are just a few: Sid and Nancy (1986), Hope and Glory (1987), Rob Roy (1995), The Jackal (1997), Trial & Retribution (1997–2009), Ordinary Decent Criminal (2000), The Boy in the Striped Pyjamas (2008), The Nest (2020), Bull (2021) and Andor (2022).

How did that happen – to a boy raised in a single end and kitchen in Bridgeton? Well, perhaps it helped when he was six and the family moved to Drumchapel, at that time a new scheme on the edge of Glasgow. Countryside loomed just over the hill, nurturing no doubt a cultural sensitivity or – help me out here – just a widening of horizons, ken?

At any rate, he’d a “very happy childhood”, enjoying family days out on his dad’s motorcycle, with David and his brother in the sidecar, and mother riding pillion.

At 16, he left school with no qualifications and followed his father into the shipyards as an apprentice electrician. However, a serious thespian tendency was detected by the local amateur dramatics group, which encouraged him to apply for the Royal Scottish Academy of Music and Drama.

One day, returning home from work, the normally shy young man got off the bus, marched up the steps of the Academy in his boiler suit and announced: “I want to be an actor!” He hardly recognised his own voice and could think only that he’d been “called”, even if that “sounds a bit wky”.

**Play time**

Proper treading the boards began at Glasgow’s Citizens Theatre, where his many classical plays included Hamlet in which, rather unclassically, he played a most unprincely prince, staggering about half-naked, howling out his mental hassles.

Outrage followed. Schools cancelled bookings. Nothing daunted, Hayman later appeared bare-chested as Lady Macbeth. Nice work if you’ve got the moobs for it.

None of this impressed his father until his workmates noticed his boy “on the telly” as Jimmy Boyle in 1981’s critically acclaimed adaptation of A Sense Of Freedom, the autobiography by notorious Barlinnie convict turned sculptor Jimmy Boyle.

Boyle’s life had taken a new direction in the prison’s experimental Special Unit, which aimed to rehabilitate prisoners through radical new approaches, including arts therapy. Hayman and others from the Citz used to visit the unit regularly, doing improvisations with the prisoners. Soon, Boyle became a friend of the actor.

Hitherto, he’d been an enemy of everyone, waging a one-man war against the penal system, smearing himself in his own faeces, incurring punishment beatings and solitary confinement. Then Boyle, a convicted gangland murderer (who always denied the crime), was introduced to art.

Rehabilitation, right enough, but it wasn’t enough for the Establishment, which obstructed the television production at every turn. Newspapers editorials said the programme would bring shame on Glasgow. A fire engine blocked filming on a Shettleston street. Policemen forbade entry to a paid-for hall that was going to be a court. The Scottish Prison Service refused permission to film the jail, so Dublin’s Kilmainham Prison had to serve instead.

**Cast the net wide**

The obstructions only served to bond the cast, which also included Fulton Mackay, Jake D’Arcy, Alex Norton, Sean Scanlan and Jimmy Yuill. Hayman has since admitted that the film was “visceral”, telling the Daily Record: “Up until then, TV was fairly safe. Dixon of Dock Green, Z Cars – it was very tame. Suddenly you have this 90-minute film about a supposed killer, stripped naked, covered in his own in a cage and railing against the authorities.”

Of course, this is manna from Heaven for television viewers, particularly Glaswegians, who mobbed, so to say, Hayman in the streets, making him, as the Record put it, “a prisoner in his hometown”. So, he fled.

“I couldn’t walk the streets of Glasgow for two years,” he told the paper. “It was screened on the Monday and I walked into a pub on Maryhill Road to get a carry-out because I was going to watch it with my pals. I said to the barman, ‘Where the hell is everybody?’ And he said, ‘They’re home, son – they’re all watching you.’”

He spent much of the following two years away from Glasgow “just because I was hounded wherever I went. I wasn’t hassled or challenged by anyone – I just couldn’t breathe.”

He wasn’t finished with Barlinnie, though, making his directorial debut in 1990 with Silent Scream, a somewhat hallucinatory portait of another Special Unit prisoner, Larry Winters, a murderer and poet who died in his cell from an overdose in 1977.

Hayman maintained Winters had “the soul of an artist” and that he’d learned much about Hamlet from him because of his “wonderful insight into tortured minds”. Fair to say this production fared better on the Continent than in the UK.

Fair to say, too, that Hayman was rather stereotyped in hard man roles after A Sense of Freedom, though he steered clear of “shallow” Hollywood, not to mention celebrity Shakespeare at Stratford upon Avon, preferring the sustain appreciation of his home city, where he happily returned to play King Lear at the Citz in 2012.

**A fair cop**

On television, apart from Boyle, he’s best known for playing Detective Chief Superintendent Walker in Lynda La Plante’s Trial & Retribution, which ran for 12 series from 1997 to 2009.

All grime and crime but, in real life, Hayman is a hard man with a soft heart. In 2021, he founded Spirit Aid, a charity dedicated to children across the world harmed by war, poverty, abuse or lack of opportunity.

Good guy then. But he does love a baddie, on stage at least. “To play evil, power, ruthlessness – there’s a journey to go on there,” he told The Herald in 2018. “It's lovely to think, ‘Can I find a chink in that armour of evil? Is there someone who has been bruised or abused or damaged that explains their behaviour there?’ That is what I find fascinating.”
