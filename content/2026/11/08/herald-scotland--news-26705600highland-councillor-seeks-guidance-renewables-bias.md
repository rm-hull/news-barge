A Highland councillor has written to the Ethical Standards Commissioner seeking “clear guidance” on bias and conduct when councillors publicly express different views on renewable energy development.

Speaking to The Herald, Councillor Matthew Reiss described a climate at Highland Council’s North Planning Applications Committee of “virtually zero tolerance for any voices that express doubt, caution, or dissent”.

Last year, Cllr Reiss, an independent councillor, resigned from the North Planning Applications Committee (NPAC), saying the experience had caused him **“** some worry and stress” and also "frustration and disbelief".

Mr Reiss had given a lengthy interview to a journalist from the John O’Groats Journal, in which he suggested that further renewable developments should be “paused”.

In his recent letter to the commissioner, he described how, following the publication of the article, the Highland Council head of legal services informed him “that one or more people had brought the article to his attention”.

He was advised, he wrote, that if he “did not resign from NPAC” he was “at risk of being reported to the commission”. “I asked,” he said, “if this meant he might report me and he declined to answer. I resigned from the committee.”

He also detailed how several months later he emailed staff to ask permission to attend a meeting of NPAC in November 2025 where a planning application for a large substation project was to be considered – Banniskirk Hub.

He received consent to attend, which he did, sitting in the public gallery, but, he recalled, “just as the meeting was about to commence the head of legal, accompanied by a colleague, approached me and advised me to leave... The reason given was that my presence might influence Committee Members.”

Mr Reiss feels his caution over renewables has been validated since. “The point,” he said, “I was trying to make was simply that there should be a pause to major renewables, not just by Highland Council but the entire system - the Scottish government, to a certain extent, the UK government - until we assessed what the ability of the grid was or wasn't.

“Only a few weeks after that, the National Energy System Operator did start looking at this," he said. It revealed that there were around three times more Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) projects planned or in the connection queue than the permitted or required capacity.

He said he felt his “caution and reservations” had also been vindicated by the Raeshaw Farms v Scottish Ministers case, in which planning permission was quashed over the consideration of a wind farm’s grid connection.

Mr Reiss questions whether the same standards are being applied to the conduct of councillors in support of renewables.

In his letter to the Ethical Standards Commissioner, he gave two “hypothetical scenarios”. In one, a councillor expresses support for renewable developments, and in the other a councillor publicly calls for a “pause” in renewable energy applications.

He requested a response “identifying the relevant provisions of the Code of Conduct and explaining how they apply to both scenarios”.

However, the Ethical Standards Commissioner wrote back saying it wasn’t possible to comment on hypothetical scenarios regarding other councillors.

Last year a member of the Skye Windfarm Information Group, which has been campaigning against new windfarm developments on Skye, also wrote to the Ethical Standards Commissioner, alleging that two councillors had failed to demonstrate the impartiality required of members of the NPAC by reason of their previous public statements and activities supporting renewable energy developments.

A response from the Ethical Standards Commissioner to their letter said this was not a contravention of the code. “The Code,” it said, “regulates councillors' conduct rather than perceptions arising from their publicly expressed policy views. Councillors are entitled to advocate for particular policy objectives and to express those views publicly, provided they comply with the requirements of the Code when participating in decisions on specific matters.”

According to Reiss, the general mood in the council is one favourable to renewables. “But," he said, " in many parts of rural Highlands, especially north of Inverness, there is a widespread public feeling that we are having to host ever larger developments, and that there is effectively no really significant compensation in terms of community benefit.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21667897.jpg?type=mds-article-620) *(Image: PA)*

“Slowly but surely lots of organizations, particularly community councils, are realising that the environmental cost to our scenery and our landscapes is becoming increasingly significant. Running parallel to that is a dawning realisation that the grid can't get the power south because it's inadequate, and therefore the constraint payments are rising exponentially. “

Mr Reiss described his reasons for resigning. “If I continued with membership of NPAC, there was a risk that I would be reported to the Standards Commission. I was a police officer in my main career, and I do have a respect for authority, and I wouldn't want to be reported to the commission if I could avoid it. And while some people have very tough skins, we don't all.

“I haven't got a closed mind towards renewables, but I am cautious because I can see glaring inconsistencies in the fact that the rate of development and the rate of planning applications has completely overtaken the ability of the grid to keep up with the amount of power that's being produced.”

A Highland Council spokesperson said: “It is not appropriate for the Council to offer a comment. Matters relating to the Code of Conduct are primarily for the Standards Commission/Ethical Standards Commissioner. The Standards Commission Advice Note advises Elected Members to try, where possible, to seek any advice or support from the Council’s Monitoring Officer in private and any advice or guidance received should be treated as being confidential.”

Following Mr Reiss’s resignation, fellow NPAC member Councillor Richard Gale wrote in the Northern Times, voicing concern: “The central issue is the rule that councillors cannot participate in official planning decisions if they have previously expressed an opinion on the application in question.”

He described Mr Reiss’s comment as “modest” and noted that his resignation was a “significant loss for both the committee and the North Highland community”. He described the “denial of access” to the chamber, as an “extreme and unnecessary reaction and is indicative of the pressure that Councillors are under to keep their comments to the planning meetings”.

He added: “These pressures are exacerbated by the Scottish Government’s National Planning Framework 4 (NPF4), which is heavily weighted in favour of renewable developments and gives minimal weight to the concerns of the communities directly affected. As a result, councillors are limited in their ability to advocate for local interests when those concerns conflict with national policy priorities.”

Though not a member of NPAC, Councillor Helen Crawford, Highland Conservative councillor for Aird and Loch Ness, has spoken out about renewables.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/20465969.jpg?type=mds-article-620) *(Image: Helen Crawford)*

She said: “Matthew, myself and a number of other councillors regularly speak up on behalf of the communities expected to host this infrastructure. We are not anti-renewables - we recognise that they have an important part to play in our future energy mix, but we still want to examine the evidence, look at the strategy – and yet council is moving ahead at this enormous pace.

“Through the Community Council Conventions, involving communities across Highland and subsequently other parts of Scotland, we have been calling for a coherent national energy policy, and a proper assessment of cumulative impacts, a step back to look at the whole picture.

“My concern is that, at council level, we are increasingly presented with papers which treat this scale of development as an inevitable fait accompli, concentrate on the anticipated benefits and leave little room for meaningful challenge about the wider strategy or the impact on our communities.

“There is an important distinction between a councillor prejudging an individual planning application, which would be wrong, and a councillor expressing legitimate views on national energy policy and the cumulative consequences of that policy for their constituents; we should be able to do the latter without it compromising our ability to participate in local democracy."
