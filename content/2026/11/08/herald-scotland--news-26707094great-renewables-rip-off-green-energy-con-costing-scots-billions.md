*The STUC is investigating the renewables industry. Our Writer at Large hears how the SNP is allowing money which should stay in Scotland to go to private and foreign investors*

It's dubbed ‘Scotland’s renewables rip-off’. Billions of pounds which could be going into Scottish government coffers for public services are flowing out of the country to foreign and private companies due to how the SNP has shaped the renewables industry.

The Herald on Sunday met with the team from the Scottish Trades Union Congress (STUC) investigating the losses.

The team includes Dave Moxham, STUC deputy general-secretary, and Ryan Morrison, STUC Just Transition Officer and an expert on renewable energy.

Here’s some evidence they’ve compiled: more than 75% of the capacity of the ScotWind project, for example, is owned by companies based overseas.

Scotland has no operational nationally owned renewables. In terms of community ownership of renewables the level stands at under 3%.

Analysis of Scotland’s 105 onshore wind farms shows that 90% of the £2.83billion in post-tax profits is paid as dividends to corporate shareholders.

The amount of money invested in projects and supply chains - which create jobs, for example - has fallen by 7%, roughly £6.3billion.

That means in total, Scotland appears to be losing out by almost £9billion.

A pot of £9billion could pay for an estimated 10-14,000 nurses for 20 years.

Just one job is created in offshore and onshore wind per £1million of turnover. In the oil and gas sector, every £1million invested creates 14 jobs.

Meanwhile, £500million in public money is subsidising Scotland’s renewables industry. This money from the Scottish government goes to the private companies which own or build wind farms.

The STUC says the government is assisting private companies “who are making a killing out of Scotland’s renewables” at the expense of citizens and communities. The way the industry is structured leads to “wealth extraction”.

While Scotland has no publicly owned wind farms, in Denmark 52% of the industry is publicly owned. Wales has established a public energy company.

The Scottish renewables industry has become a “Klondike” for private companies and foreign state-owned energy firms.

The ScotWind deal, which auctioned swathes of the nation’s seabed to bidders, saw foreign state-owned companies - including national governments in Ireland, Sweden and Denmark, and regional governments from Germany and Belgium - take a 12% stake.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21681169.jpg?type=mds-article-620) Dave Moxham, deputy general secretary of the STUC Photograph by Colin Mearns 2 December 2019 *(Image: Colin Mearns)*

*SHAREHOLDERS*

The world’s largest asset management company, American firm BlackRock, is a top ten shareholder in 14 out of 20 ScotWind projects. Its rival Vanguard is a top ten shareholder in 15. In total, five investment firms own 18% of ScotWind.

Eight ScotWind projects are owned in whole or in part by six fossil fuel companies, including Shell, BP and TotalEnergies - which is around 25% of the entire ScotWind capacity.

Only four of the 29 parent companies involved in ScotWind are headquartered in Britain, including BP and Shell.

Dave Moxham, STUC deputy general-secretary, compared the present set-up to “what happened to Scottish oil in the 1970s. The private sector was invited in and gave very little back in community benefit”.

The UK, he says, “didn’t do what Norway did” and create a sovereign wealth fund. A sovereign wealth fund could be created around renewable energy.

Norway’s sovereign wealth fund is the largest in the world worth more than $2trillion. It helps fund the nation’s public services. “The question of who owns our wind is absolutely fundamental,” Moxham adds.

Indeed, he believes ownership of wind is more crucial than oil as the long-term benefits from oil - such as jobs - endure over time, whereas once wind farms are up and running there is less need for employment.

“It is a fundamental mistake to give 20-30 year revenue flows to the private sector for so little,” he adds.

Ryan Morrison, the STUC’s Just Transition Officer who leads on energy policy, said: “There is a very close relationship between the renewables industry and the Scottish government.”

The STUC says the public is mistaken if it views the renewables industry as benevolent. “These companies are pursuing whatever is in their interests in a way that’s seriously undermining what is returned back to the people of Scotland either in terms of jobs or local wealth.”

Unlike the oil and gas industry, the renewables industry is not creating the sort of supply chains which benefit local economies. “Without political direction,” Morrison says, “the renewables industry has been able to do what it chooses.”

There is much talk of ‘community benefit deals’ for local areas from renewables. These are meant to involve renewable companies paying a set dividend to communities, such as £5000 per megawatt per year.

However, the STUC says most renewable projects don’t have community benefit deals attached. Such deals which do exist are referred to as “crumb’s from the king’s table”.

In terms of who owns Scotland’s renewables, Morrison says: “It’s everyone but us. It’s a combination of venture capitalists, global asset management firms, and the publicly-owned companies of other countries and big sovereign wealth funds. It’s every other form of ownership other than domestic national ownership or domestic local ownership.”

*OVERSEAS*

Often, Morrison says, local wind farms will be owned by firms which appear local and green but are in fact “holding companies” for an international financial corporation. Alternatively, local firms are bought up by overseas or private corporations once they have got permission to build.

Moxham says community benefit deals - such as £5000 per megawatt per year - are welcome but under the current system “that’s the best we get”. Publicly owned infrastructure, as in Denmark, would allow all profits to be spent in communities across Scotland.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21681170.jpg?type=mds-article-620)First Minister John Swinney speaking at the Global Offshore Wind Investment Forum at the Caledonian Hilton in Edinburgh. Picture date: Monday March 17, 2025. *(Image: PA)*

Moxham describes ScotWind as “a terrible deal”, adding: “Should we not have publicly owned companies at every level? Should we not be generating energy ourselves?”

Given the Scottish government chose to auction the seabed to the private sector, Moxham questions why equity stakes weren’t taken and why better “conditionalities weren’t written in” to ensure more jobs and supply chains. There were multiple mistakes, he says.

Although there are no nationally owned renewables, there is some local and community ownership. According to the STUC, of the 17,897 megawatts of Scottish renewable capacity in 2025, 1154 megawatts were local and community owned. That’s approximately 6.4%.

However, those statistics are misleading, Morrison says, as “the largest single group” is “estate owners, who are probably not who people would typically recognise as community owners”. Once this group is removed the true figure for public and local ownership falls to around 2.4%.

More than half of Denmark’s wind sector is owned locally and by communities.

Referring to the fact that no renewables are nationally owned, Morrison adds: “There is one demonstrator turbine that’s owned by the UK government, but the Scottish government doesn’t own any energy generation capacity.”

It’s misleading to claim that the Scottish government lacks the powers to set up nationally owned renewables, Moxham says. It is certainly not possible for Scotland under the devolution settlement to “nationalise the whole energy sector”. However, it absolutely can “own manufacturing companies and generating companies”.

There is money to invest, though, in private projects. The Scottish government - via the Scottish National Investment Bank (SNIB) - put £50million into the Pentland floating offshore wind farm. It is majority owned by Denmark’s Copenhagen Infrastructure Partners. The UK government also took a £50million stake through GB Energy.

The Scottish government could set up its own energy company and use SNIB to invest in publicly-owned renewables. Councils could use what’s called ‘prudential borrowing’ to build local infrastructure. So a model similar to Denmark is possible in Scotland, the STUC believes.

The Scottish government owns vast tracts of public land which could be developed via, for example, the Forestry Commission.

*WALES*

Morrison points out that Wales has set up its own government-owned energy company. “The Welsh government is aiming for one-gigawatt onshore wind generation initially.”

He contrasted this with, for example, investment by SNIB into a renewables company backed by the private equity firm Corran Capital of £20million. Morrison said that the Scottish government was “giving money for asset managers to own wind farms”.

The Welsh government, he said, set up its publicly owned company to “get maximum reward” while “limiting the amount of risk. I have never, in my experience with the Scottish government, heard them make this kind of calculation. Their interest is securing private investment and making that an attractive proposition”.

Morrison added: “Why is the Scottish government not scoping out an equivalent to the Welsh electricity generating company? Why are they not at that stage of thinking? It’s not even on the table.”

Moxham says the message from the Scottish government is one of “being open for business and sweeping away regulations. You have private sector hawks in the SNP now who believe the same as the Conservatives - that private industry does it better”.

Moxham adds, however, that the Scottish government clearly doesn’t have the same capital borrowing powers as the UK government. The STUC favours more capital powers for the Scottish government.

However, the Scottish National Investment Bank could be used “to mitigate” the problems the government faces over capital investment.

In terms of the significant decline in spending on supply chains by private renewable firms, there is little that can be done to punish companies. Fines are possible but these are smaller than the money a company saves by not investing in Scotland.

“The fines don’t make a difference,” Morrison adds, when it comes to “procuring domestically as opposed to overseas”.

Much of the supply chain spend goes on white collar jobs in Scotland - like lawyers - as most construction work is carried out overseas.

The jobs which are available for offshore workers in the renewables industry have wages at about 60% of what can be earned in the oil and gas sector. So workers transitioning from oil and gas to renewables take a “significant wage cut”, Moxham adds.

As levels of employment in renewables decline once wind farms are operational, the result can be “people end up working in Amazon”. Meanwhile, he says, Leith docks are filled with boats bringing in wind farm kits from China.

Private renewable companies are also subsidised by the taxpayer through what is called the ‘contract for difference’. This basically guarantees price security for generating companies. The contract for difference guarantee costs UK taxpayers around £2.3billion annually.

*SOVEREIGNITY*

Moxham describes the system put in place by the SNP as “an abandonment of Scottish sovereignty. If people want to take that as meaning not sticking up for Scotland in the way they should then I am quite happy with that”.

Given the current system, Moxham says it is a “myth” to claim that if Scotland had been independent in the 1970s when oil was discovered the SNP would have behaved any differently to the UK government of the time.

He says there are claims that an independent Scottish government would have set up a sovereign wealth fund like Norway. “Yet there’s nothing in the way the Scottish government is behaving now which suggests that it would have behaved differently to Westminster then.

“You can’t have it both ways. You can’t say you’re sticking up for Scotland, and then do things exactly the same as the UK government did in the 1970s.”

Other prominent trade unionists, who did not want to be quoted by name, said they see the current set-up as “irrational and illogical, and the greatest missed economic opportunity of the devolution era”, given the money that could be used on collapsing public services. Clearly, money raised would not come from additional taxation.

“It’s an abject failure to grasp a huge opportunity for what Scotland needs for generations to come. We’ve just handed it lock stock and barrel to private companies and foreign governments.”

At one point, an Austrian viscount applied for planning permission for a green project.

Morrisson said that private companies “hold power over the Scottish government” due to the way the sector is run.

However, in other nations where the state is in the driving seat, the same companies behave differently. Indeed, “some are publicly owned companies in countries whose own citizens would never accept this kind of relationship”.

Morrison said GB Energy - the UK’s publicly owned energy investment company, which is often criticised by the SNP - has performed better than the Scottish government.

“It was putting solar panels on public buildings like schools and libraries within 12 months.”

Labour MP Michael Shanks recently claimed that a proposal was put to the Scottish government to be part of that programme but it was declined.

Morrison added that the present system was “a con. We’re being lied to. The image is that this is all going well, and it’s something we should be pleased about, that it’s a great success.

“Some of that is politics, I understand that, but at some point it just becomes a complete misrepresentation of how this is being managed and who is benefitting from it”.

*DEPRIVED*

There is a mismatch between what the government says and what communities are experiencing. A system which put Scotland first would see cash flowing into some of the nation’s most deprived areas.

Moxham calls the current system “directionless”. Rather than synching up all the levers of the state to drive economic growth, the best on offer is “compensatory payments for the inconvenience of multinational activity” which are paid to communities near wind farms.

Although there is some marginal community-owned activity in Scotland, most “happens without government support, not because of it”, Morrison says.

Figures such as 90% of wind profits going to shareholders underscore how “money is just leaving the country”.

The number of jobs created through wind is “derisory” at one per every £1m of turnover, Moxham says. “The only way to meaningfully improve the figure is by having supply chains and construction in Scotland.”

The available Scottish manufacturing facilities, which could be used for constructing turbines, for example, “aren’t being used for what should be the jewel in the crown of the Scottish economy”. So China becomes an export winner.

The two central dysfunctions of the present system are “poor supply chains and the model of ownership. Big multinationals come in. They leverage government. The government says they’re open for business and there’s a downward compensatory culture for communities.

“In Europe, it’s all upwards. They generate. They sell. That’s because it’s publicly owned.”

When it comes to “power and finance”, Scotland and Europe “run in completely opposite directions. That’s a fundamental flaw”.

Moxham said that the Scottish government didn’t need to follow the STUC’s preferred route of public ownership to fix the system. It could go for local ownership and community wealth building.

“Even a system that was small business-owned would be preferable to the way it is at the moment,” he added. “There’s more than one model that the SNP government could embrace, rather than the one that has us with our begging bowl out.”

Whoever owns an asset, has the power to decide what happens with its profits.

First Minister John Swinney has called for an end to the windfall tax on oil and gas companies. Moxham said that instead he should be calling for “a proportion of that money to be used for a Scottish energy company to invest in the next generation of renewables.

“That should be his demand, rather than taking the shackles off the oil and gas sector so they can carry on with the same model they’ve run for 50 years”.

*PROFITS*

The SNP should be calling for “the capacity to invest in our future. That would be a much more powerful message than basically saying ‘let’s increase Shell’s profits’. That seems a much more attractive message to the SNP base as well as others”.

Morrison noted that when energy prices soared in recent years, EDF, the French state-owned energy company, limited the impact on its consumers. “They could make that choice as they are a public utility supplier.”

Meanwhile at home, consumers “get a shockingly bad deal, and it’s very deliberate. That’s why the term ‘rip-off’ is accurate. It’s an active thing, not passive”.

Morrison said that in terms of jobs there was “no fair work commitment” by any of the ScotWind developers. Many workers are self-employed or short-term.

Most renewables are based in rural areas where there are problems with population retention, and therefore good, secure, reliable jobs are needed to keep people in local towns.

Highland Council, Morrison explains, “has taken the lead” in Scotland by setting conditions for prospective private developers, including a sizeable community benefit fund, commitments to invest in housing for workers, and fair work conditions.

“The renewables industry weren’t happy,” he says, and complained to the Scottish government asking ministers to get involved.

While community benefit matters, Moxham says, public ownership could be used to pull levers like investing in childcare across the nation, better transport, or job creation - policies which change people’s lives. A sovereign wealth fund could also be established.

The UK government doesn’t escape blame. “It controls the entire economic system,” says Moxham. The electricity grid is also so “primitive”, he adds, that wind turbines need to be switched off as too much power is being generated. Private companies are then compensated for switch-offs.

Nevertheless, Morrison says, “there is nothing at a UK level which prevents the Scottish government” from setting up publicly-owned energy companies. “It’s so frustrating. The Scottish government won’t own a turbine. It won’t own a solar panel. It’s all a bit ludicrous”.

Moxham suggests that the SNP is more interested in social and fiscal policies than industrial policies. While “baby boxes and the child payment are very positive, ministers never seem oriented towards the strategic. They’ve been in government for 20 years and we’ve not really seen any major industrial or economic interventions”.

He adds: “You would have thought that the Green Party, when they went into power, would have had some really big asks about what would make a meaningful difference to the greening of the Scottish economy. But they didn’t."

Policies like subsidised public transport “are fine, but it doesn’t get to the long-term reorientation of the Scottish economy in a way that works for people”. Moxham says that the SNP is “in thrall” to big business.

*LOBBYING*

Morrison said the SNP doesn’t need to be lobbied by private business when it comes to renewables as corporations are already invited “into the room” when policy is being made.

The Scottish Offshore Wind Energy Council is where government policy is effectively shaped. “These are spaces where the Scottish government is inviting industry involvement to the exclusion of others.”

The green industrial strategy is “very business-led, and the outcome is clear. It tells us how they’ll make things quicker, easier and cheaper for business. It doesn’t tell us how it will make things better for workers and communities”.

Moxham says corporations don’t need to worry about lobbying the Scottish government. “They’re in the room without having to pay anyone to put them there.”

The trade union group stresses none of their criticism of the Scottish government should be taken as opposition to net zero. The STUC believes that public ownership is a faster route to achieving net zero as it gives more control to government. Targets won’t be met if the matter is just left to the market.

Many renewable companies are also in the oil and gas industry. Moxham says: “If you listen to the Scottish government, they effectively say that their strategy for oil and gas is almost beholden to the transition strategies of these companies with a foot in both camps.

“That’s giving away an awful lot of power over net zero and an awful lot of leveraging power. The Scottish government’s transition policy is in many ways being run by offshore oil and gas.”

That has made net zero “viscerally related to and dependent upon” fossil fuel companies. The link needs to be broken, Moxham adds.

He notes that the SNP has “already essentially got rid of their targets” on net zero. In 2024, the SNP scrapped its annual and interim targets for cutting greenhouse gas emissions and replaced them with a system of measuring every five years.

“They’re failing on so many levels it’s difficult to know where to start,” he adds. Morrison says “multinational companies have no real care for net zero”.

More public ownership would improve energy security, they believe, and give government the power to make decisions on pricing.

*WAGES*

It would also allow for better wages for workers which would then be spent in local communities.

“We’d have the power to decide what to do about bills,” Morrison says, rather than money disappearing into the boardrooms of foreign multinationals and venture capitalists.

He adds: “People should not expect that the same wind that is blowing in their face is being used to extract profits for a company like BlackRock.”

Moxham speaks regularly to the Scottish government. He says ministers have a “tendency to create straw men”. When asked why they won’t establish an energy company, ministers will instead talk about their lack of powers to nationalise energy across Scotland - even though that isn’t the issue under discussion.

Creating a publicly owned energy company is not the same as the nationalisation of Scotland’s energy.

“There’s wilful misunderstanding,” Moxham adds, “and a lack of bravery.” He says the government is also “all over the place” and “feels sclerotic and not mission focused”.

Morrison says that in terms of SNP energy strategy the role of the private sector is clear but there is no “vision for what the public sector can do”. The role of the public sector “isn’t even a consideration”.

South Lanarkshire council, he added, has been fitting solar panels on its non-residential buildings, in projects which have already paid for themselves. Money saved can be spent on services.

Morrison spoke to the Scottish government about this and suggested it be replicated in every council area. He’s not heard anything in 14 months.

“You don’t even need to look at this from a climate perspective,” he adds. “The council is saving money.” He assumes the failure to act is “ideological”.

Moxham believes that the SNP has the opportunity to change tack given statements from the UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham about “rolling back neoliberalism. This is a moment where the Scottish government could step up. Let’s just start having some vision. It is Scotland’s wind after all.”

*GOVERNMENT RESPONSE*

The SNP energy minister Stephen Gethins said: “We determined that Scotland’s abundant energy wealth delivers clear public benefit and that the transition to renewables creates jobs, investment and lasting opportunities for communities across the country.

“Through our £500million commitment to Scotland’s offshore wind supply chain and infrastructure, we are helping communities from Stornoway to Montrose benefit from new investment, skilled jobs and stronger local economies, ensuring more of the value from Scotland’s renewable energy resources is retained here at home.

“SSE’s recent appointment of Aberdeen-based Seaway7 for the Berwick Bank B project, supporting 400 offshore wind jobs in Scotland, demonstrates how Scotland’s wider supply chain is benefitting.

“Our pioneering approach to ensuring communities benefit from the renewable energy infrastructure they host has enabled communities across the country to share in the benefits of the energy transition, with over £40million offered in 2025 to support local priorities and build long-term community wealth.

“Through our Community and Renewable Energy Scheme we have also backed over 1200 community-led energy projects across Scotland, helping people reduce energy costs, increase local resilience and generate lasting value for their communities.

“From supporting skilled jobs and Scottish supply chains to helping communities invest in their own priorities, the Scottish Government will continue work to ensure the benefits of Scotland’s renewable energy success are felt across the country for generations to come.

“I am always keen to engage with partners including the Trade Unions on ensuring that the move to renewables benefits citizens whilst giving certainty to business - within the limited powers we have.”
