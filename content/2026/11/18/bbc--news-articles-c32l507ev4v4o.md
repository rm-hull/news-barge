Watch: Inside the gas pipeline where Ukraine is fighting an underground war with Russia

**There's a new front in the war in Ukraine, and it is underground.**

For the past seven months Ukrainian forces have been living and fighting Russian troops in an old Soviet gas pipeline in the north-eastern Kharkiv region.

With a diameter of just over a metre there is enough room only to crawl. But it is relatively safe from the drones that now saturate the skies above the front line – and make it nigh-on impossible for either side to advance.

Going underground is now the "only option", says Zanuda, who has helped plan Ukraine's tunnel operation and calls it a new world of fighting. But, he acknowledges, it is hard.

"On the surface you always have some room to manoeuvre, but here you can only engage the enemy head-on."

The journey to get to this subterranean battleground in the Kharkiv region only reinforced the dangers on the ground: a high-speed move by vehicle across open fields, followed by a long march across muddy ground – accompanied by Ukrainian troops with their weapons loaded, ready to shoot down Russian drones.

Arriving at the tunnel entrance, the first thing to hit you is the lingering smell of gas and fetid air.

Both sides use openings in the pipeline to get to the surface, where most of the fighting takes place. They have made breaches in the ground, and used welding tools to break through the thick steel.

The Russians were the first to exploit the pipe to infiltrate the nearby city of Kupyansk in September 2025. They have used underground pipelines before, during the capture of the city of Avdiivka in 2024, and last year in their bid to recapture areas of Russia's Kursk region.

But Ukrainian forces have slowly turned the tables.

![Soldiers sit on sleds to mve through a narrow pipeline in eastern Ukraine](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/92e9/live/a4cfab60-e353-11f1-acd4-25eaaa5def63.jpg)

Troops move through the tunnel on special sleds - they call it the Kupyansk metro

A specially designed electric cart transports troops and supplies through the pipeline.

They call it the Kupyansk metro. It occasionally breaks down. We travelled on it for 40 minutes, heads bowed and legs crossed. There is a regular bump as you cross the welded sections of the pipe. It is like an austere fairground ride, without the fun.

Our destination was a break in the pipeline where Ukrainian troops have built dug-outs to store ammunition and equipment, with just enough room to stand. It is where the man they call Musician conducts the movement of troops and supplies. He has been living like a mole, his face engrained with dirt and earth.

Musician tells me he has spent the past three months there, with a few brief visits above ground to clean up or go to the toilet. From time to time we can hear the thuds of artillery rounds above. Musician tells me we are close to the line of contact: "But it's safe underground."

![A soldier, call sign Musician, stands under wooden logs in a trench, staring down at the camera](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/9701/live/d944ee00-e353-11f1-acd4-25eaaa5def63.jpg)

Ukrainian troops like Musician rarely see the sun - but they say they feel safer underground

The cart continues its journey into the bowels of the earth. It returns about half an hour later with more troops covered in dirt. They have been fighting in positions on the surface.

There is a look of relief on their dust-caked faces.

"It's more comfortable in the pipe because drones don't fly here, and neither do artillery shells or glide bombs," says Vovochka, who has been fighting on the ground for the past three weeks.

But there has been fighting in the pipeline, too. Operation Veles, named after a mythical Slavic god of the underworld, began in April. Since then Ukraine has advanced 15km (9.3 miles) along the pipeline.

It has been painstaking, backbreaking work – with the ever-present fear of coming into contact with the enemy.

Back above ground we meet a few of the soldiers, from the 13th Khartiia Brigade of Ukraine's National Guard, who have taken part in the mission. Their first task was to clear the detritus, water and Russian barricades inside the pipeline.

The Ukrainian troops say some of the barriers were boobytrapped or piled with Russian bodies. "When we moved them or climbed over them, we had no idea what was underneath," says Chyzh.

The other danger, he said, was the ricochets from gunfire when they tried to advance. "You never know where a bullet is going to go or how it will bounce."

His comrade Kort says "we were all firing, we were pushing them back". Over the course of the operation Ukraine says it killed more than 100 Russian soldiers and took 30 prisoners. "Yes, there were bodies," says Kort. "I even had to sleep right next to them."

![Soldiers smoke and eat near the mouth of a pipeline in eastern Ukraine](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/8ef7/live/c3a04f90-e353-11f1-acd4-25eaaa5def63.jpg)

The men live and fight in very tight quarters - but have managed to clear 15km of the pipe

Their commander's former life as a miner helped prepare them for the physical and psychological challenges of operating underground.

From a building on the surface, Biashym keeps in regular contact with his men still in the pipeline. Behind him is a cabinet full of medicine.

Soldiers in the pipeline spend much of their time on their knees and suffer abrasions. Biashym admits some have had panic attacks from living in such a confined space. Others have struggled to breathe.

Hundreds of Colombian volunteers have also been involved in the operation, part of the RUG 23rd Assault Regiment.

Foreign fighters have helped boost Ukraine's depleted ranks. One of the Colombians, whose call sign is Gecko, tells me it took them 30 days to just clear 500m.

"It was difficult, it was hot and I had fear inside the pipe," he said. "But at some point I just thought, OK, just deal with it and let's advance."

The battle of the pipeline is not over.

Russian forces, like Ukraine's, have built fortified positions inside the pipeline. They still try to infiltrate Ukrainian positions, burrowing their way in and out of the pipe. From a control room above ground we watched Ukrainian drones target another Russian attempt to outflank them.

> Ground operations are now nearly impossible... everyone understands, for now, the only way to fight is underground

!["Ground operations are now nearly impossible... everyone understands, for now, the only way to fight is underground", Source: Zanuda, Source description: Ukrainian soldier, Image: A Ukrainian soldier call sign Zanuda stands in a black hoodie and black cap in front of a sign reading Khartiya](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/news/920/idt2/idt2/767c5be7-92d1-44f6-ac78-1299a9d70102/image/460.jpg)

While some of the fighting taking place underground, there are still battles on the surface and in the air. It has been a combined operation.

But Zanuda, who is chief of the planning section of the 13th Khartiia Brigade, believes it is the best way to fight in the future. "Ground operations are now nearly impossible," he said, adding that "everyone understands, for now, the only way to fight is underground".

There'll be more missions like this, he believes. "Pipelines run everywhere," he said. "You have to remember, back in the Soviet times, oil, gas and ammonia were being transported to Europe, with the entire transit route running through Ukraine."

In reality it has been brutal, gruelling and dangerous work in the harshest of conditions.

It has taken Ukraine seven months to clear 15km of the pipeline.

Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelensky has hailed it as a success.

It has helped retake lost territory and been a boost for morale, but on its own it has not changed the course of the war.
