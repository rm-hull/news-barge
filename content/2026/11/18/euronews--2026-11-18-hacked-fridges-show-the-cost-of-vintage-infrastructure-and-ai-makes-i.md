## The real cyber threat is ageing infrastructure as well as AI, a Cisco executive tells Euronews Next.

This year proved that no device is too ordinary to be a target. Even smart fridges have been easily hacked, with devastating consequences.

Dozens of European supermarkets saw fridge and freezer temperatures turned up in early November, following a cyber attack in France in October.

It shows a start cybersecurity reality: the biggest threats are not new hacking techniques but an old infrastructure problem combined with the speed at which artificial intelligence can uncover weaknesses across connected devices.

"These are not new or novel hacks… These are exploiting things that have a bit of underlying vulnerability," said Anthony Grieco, chief security and trust officer at global technology company Cisco.

“And it is that ability to discover it... The fridges are an interesting example because it just shows you the breadth and scale that you have to think about and operate in," he told Euronews Next.

Smart fridges are just one small example of other connected devices that are at risk. Grieco warned that a far more dangerous version of the same problem is unfolding inside critical infrastructure, with hospital medical devices and factory assembly lines, water utilities and digital networks also at risk.

Many of these essential systems run on ageing "end-of-life" hardware and software that manufacturers no longer support with security updates.

"If you're driving around in a 30-year-old vehicle, you probably can't go and buy the brake pads for that vehicle from the original manufacturer anymore," Grieco said.

"There are many critical infrastructure systems that just keep working, but are end of life and end of support. And the risk equation on that has changed dramatically in the last six months… We have too many critical infrastructure systems that are running that you would call vintage," he added.

To minimise those risks, he urges companies to focus on enforcing stronger credentials and passwords, and to retire the "vintage tech" that can no longer receive updates, segmenting environments, and patching faster.

## AI fighting AI

But cybersecurity is being threatened by artificial intelligence as the technology uncovers long-hidden software flaws at machine speed, automates attacks, and becomes more human, leading people to give away sensitive information.

"When we look at what adversaries are doing, they're starting to use AI tools and agents to compromise systems and compromise the IT systems that are out there," Grieco said.

This shows them being able to "do things at a pace and speed that would have taken humans a lot longer to do in the past," he added.

But he said AI is also helping cybersecurity defenders in that you can flip the script so the technology is used to counter threats.

"We’re using AI to defend at a pace and scale that we haven’t been able to before," he said.

Cisco is working alongside partners, using frontier models from companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic, to find and patch flaws before criminals can exploit them.

Earlier this year, Cisco said that its teams used AI tools to scan 1.8 billion lines of source code in just eight weeks, a massive milestone that would have otherwise taken human engineers roughly eight years.

AI is also eliminating the "noise" of false alarms that has long plagued security teams, pinpointing genuine threats with near 100% efficacy so engineers can immediately fix real risks, he said.

"There are real things that can be done today," Grieco said, adding that no matter how clever AI gets, suspicious network activity can still be seen and stopped.

"Ultimately, the bits on the wire don't lie," he said.
