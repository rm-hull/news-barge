# 'Farms need proper and stable government support to thrive' says CLA president

## The leader of a group representing the owners of more than half the country’s rural land has outlined a vision of how thriving, resilient and sustainable farms should have become by 2036, saying generating money for natural capital will be key.

Country Land and Business Association (CLA) president Gavin Lane told the Northern Farming Conference natural capital, whether carbon, biodiversity net gain or water, would become a secondary income stream alongside food production, making farm businesses more stable, resilient and able to invest.

However, the Norfolk farmer emphasised a successful agricultural sector would only be achieved with “proper and stable government support”.

He said other pre-requisites that would need to be in place included a tax investment framework that encourages long-term ownership, alongside a revitalised confidence and ambition in the industry.

![The Country Land and Business Association has outlined a vision for farming in 2036.](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOjAzMzM3M2NjLTQzNzQtNDRlYi04NjFlLTE5Mzg5MmEyNTEyMDoxZWFjZTc3Ny05NjI1LTQ3Y2EtOTc4ZC01Y2VkYzVlN2NlMzQ=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)


Mr Lane said in a decade’s time arable varieties going in the ground would have been bred faster and more precisely than ever before, meaning crops using less nitrogen, bred for wetter springs and harder summers as well as better disease and pest resistance.

He said for livestock farmers the most significant change would be through observation, with censors, collars and cameras in parlours picking up lameness and respiratory diseases days before experiences stockmen could see it.

Mr Lane said: “Earlier treatment will mean fewer losses, lower antibiotic use and welfare that can be demonstrated rather than simply asserted.”

By 2036, he said, the debate over emissions would have moved on with feed additives, better grassland management and breeding for more efficient lower emission animals are already reducing methane.

![CLA president Gavin Lane.](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOjYzYzA3ZmY2LTZmN2QtNDJhOS04NmYzLWY0YTYzZDkyYzYwMzoyMzk3YjI3MS0xNzRjLTQ4NTYtOWNkOC00MTUxZmYzYzA0NWE=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)


"Within a decade I believe British beef, lamb and dairy will no longer be on the defensive on climate. They will be the benchmark others are measured against,” the CLA president said.

Upland farming, the conference heard, was often discussed as a problem to be managed, but need to be regarded in terms of the meat produced from land that could grow little else, the cherished landscapes and those areas’ carbon and water storing capacilities.

He said: “It’s worth remembering that tourism in this country supports one in 15 jobs.

"In 2036, those who farm should be paid fairly for all of it, not just what leaves the farm gate on the lorry. Government has to look at the uplands differently."

He said there had been “glimmers of understanding” evident with the sucess of Farming in Protected Landscapes and the emergence of the Collaboration Fund, but the “glacial pace” of higher tier stewardships and the “car crash” of the Sustainable Farming Incentive had cut off revenue funding for many.

Mr Lane said: "Water is where the North has a particular story to tell. For much of the country the problem is scarcity.

"Here it’s often too much water arriving too fast. In 2036 upland farmers will be recognised as the frontline managers of the nation’s water.

"Restored peat, leaky dams and well-placed woodland will slow the flow, protect houses downstream from flooding and improve water quality for millions.

"Water companies and local authorities will pay for that service because it will be cheaper and more durable than building concrete defences.

"On lowland arable farms winter-filled reservoirs will carry crops through drier summers.

"Precision application and buffer strips will have cut nutrient run-off and rivers that are failing today will be measurably recovering with farmers credited as part of the solution rather than constantly blamed as the cause.”
