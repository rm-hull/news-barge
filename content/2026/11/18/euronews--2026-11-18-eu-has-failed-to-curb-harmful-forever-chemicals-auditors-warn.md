## EU auditors warn that slow regulation, patchy enforcement and outdated food and drinking water safety limits are leaving Europeans exposed to harmful chemicals, six years after Brussels pledged to phase out their non-essential uses.

The European Union has failed to deliver on its pledge to phase out harmful "forever chemicals", known as PFAS, because of sluggish regulation, weak enforcement and outdated safety limits, according to a damning report by the European Court of Auditors (ECA).

Six years after the European Commission vowed to eliminate non-essential uses of PFAS, thousands of substances remain largely unregulated, while national authorities are failing to check whether existing restrictions are being respected, the auditors said.

PFAS are widely used in consumer and industrial products, from non-stick cookware and waterproof textiles to medical equipment and food packaging. They break down extremely slowly and have been linked to cancer, reproductive problems and weakened immune responses.

An awareness campaign involving EU ministers and Commissioners last year found that everyone who underwent blood tests tested positive for PFAS. Yet Commission President Ursula von der Leyen has repeatedly declined to meet EU citizens affected by PFAS pollution in recent years.

“These chemicals may last forever, but there are ways to limit their harmful effects on people and the environment,” said Joëlle Elvinger, the ECA member who led the audit.

“The EU must tackle this problem firmly and without delay to defuse this ticking time bomb before it is too late.”

The auditors found that while the EU had made political commitments and funded research, it had failed to translate these efforts into effective protection for citizens.

The audit comes a day after a separate investigation by campaign group Corporate Europe Observatory (CEO) accused the chemical industry of stepping up its lobbying efforts to water down the EU's proposed PFAS restrictions, potentially leaving large parts of the sector exempt from the planned ban.

"The ‘forever pollution’ crisis caused by the manufacture, use, and disposal of PFAS is getting worse. It is clear that citizens across Europe (and beyond) are contaminated with these chemicals, some at far higher levels than is considered safe," reads the CEO's report.

## Sluggish regulation and weak enforcement

Although the EU was among the first to restrict PFAS, its regulatory framework has struggled to keep pace with the scale of the problem.

The ECA audit said the regulatory process is "too slow" to address chemicals that can persist in the environment for generations. Assessing the hazards of individual PFAS takes an average of six years, the ECA auditors said, while adopting restrictions takes almost five years.

The substance-by-substance approach has also encouraged so-called "regrettable substitution", whereby banned chemicals are replaced with others with similar properties and potentially similar risks.

A proposal to restrict PFAS as a group, covering thousands of substances and uses, was submitted in 2023 by five European countries. But its complexity means the scientific assessment alone is expected to take around four years, before the Commission can move towards a final decision.

Even where restrictions are in place, enforcement is patchy. A third of EU member states carried out no targeted checks on PFAS compliance between 2020 and 2025, the auditors found.

Limited laboratory capacity, high testing costs and competing priorities were among the reasons cited by national authorities for the lack of enforcement.

## EU chemicals agency split

Neither the Commission nor the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) has a comprehensive overview of national enforcement efforts, leaving implementation fragmented and heavily reliant on companies policing themselves.

ECHA has already backed a ban on PFAS, although the final decision rests with the Commission.

However, inside the Helsinki-based agency, two scientific committees — risk assessment and socio-economic analysis — held somewhat divergent views, with the latter seeking to ensure that industries reliant on some PFAS wouldn't be affected.

ECHA's socio-economic committee is expected to deliver a final assessment of the impact of a PFAS withdrawal soon. After ECHA's submission, Brussels is under pressure to propose a restriction.

Meanwhile, no new EU restriction procedures have been launched since the universal proposal, apart from measures covering firefighting foams and PFHxA.

Martin Dermine, executive director at the NGO Pesticide Action Network Europe, said PFAS pesticides, beyond their intrinsic toxicity to humans, contaminate groundwater with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), a forever PFAS metabolite.

"TFA levels in drinking water are very high in intensive agriculture areas, and TFA was recently classified as Toxic to Reproduction. It is urgent that the Commission bans all PFAS pesticides," Dermine told Euronews.

## PFAS in food and drinking water

The EU's food and drinking water rules are also lagging behind scientific evidence.

Food limits cover only four PFAS in certain products, despite more than 50 different PFAS having been detected in food.

The ECA auditors calculated that if certain foods contained the maximum permitted PFAS level, a person could exceed their tolerable weekly intake after eating only around 7 grams of anchovies, 39 grams of wild salmon, 181 grams of eggs or 237 grams of beef.

According to the ECA audit, five EU countries submitted no food-monitoring data between 2020 and 2024, while the Commission has yet to use the growing database to decide whether existing limits should be revised or extended to other substances and food categories.

"We are not naming and shaming. It’s our job to analyse the data and the legislation and to make our recommendation to the Commission," Elvinger told reporters on Wednesday, as all 27 EU countries were asked to reply to ECA's survey for the audit.

Denmark, Germany, France and the Netherlands account for more than half of all PFAS food-monitoring data submitted to the EU between 2020 and 2024.

Nonetheless, Bulgaria, Hungary, Romania and Slovakia were urged to introduce PFAS checks in their 2026 national food-control plans.

The ECA auditors also found that the Commission had exceeded the five-year review timetable for drinking-water limits, waiting for new World Health Organization recommendations expected in 2027.

Eight of the 25 EU countries responding to the audit had already introduced additional national limits, exposing differences in the level of protection across the bloc.

The ECA is calling on the Commission to accelerate the universal restriction process, overhaul regulatory procedures, strengthen national enforcement and update food and drinking-water limits where justified by scientific evidence.

Most recommendations have deadlines stretching to 2028 or 2029, although the Commission is urged to reach a decision on the universal restriction proposal by 2027.

"Better and faster action is necessary to protect our citizens. We hope the Commission will implement our recommendations," Elvinger told reporters on Wednesday.
