A growing number of U.S. colleges and universities are rolling out redesigned three-year bachelor’s degree programs in an effort to lower the overall cost of higher education and get students into the workforce faster.

Rather than compressing a standard 120-credit curriculum into a shorter timeframe, these programs generally require around 90 credits, allowing students to pay the same annual tuition rate for three years instead of four, as reported by *The Wall Street Journal*. Roughly 150 shortened bachelor’s programs have been announced this year, a sharp increase from just five in 2024, Jason Lee, a senior policy researcher at the Rand think tank, told the newspaper.

Many of the initiatives have been encouraged or mandated by state governing bodies, and the Trump administration has praised the expansion as a boost for affordability and workforce development. Some conservatives have also argued the condensed tracks will curb unnecessary university spending and what they view as “ideological sprawl.”

Though three-year bachelor’s degrees are already standard practice in the U.K. and across much of Europe, the concept marks a significant shift for American higher education.

“The 120 credit hours isn’t necessarily a magic number,” Scott Fleming, executive director of the State Council of Higher Education for Virginia, which is currently exploring a similar initiative, told *The Wall Street Journal*.

![Around 150 three-year bachelor’s degree programs have been announced across the U.S. this year as universities look for ways to cut tuition bills and address workforce shortages](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/18/34/GettyImages-1542688.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Johnson & Wales University in Providence, Rhode Island, was among the first institutions to launch three-year tracks last year. Applications for those programs on its Providence campus have risen 27 percent this year, and student retention rates are slightly higher than in traditional tracks, Chancellor Mim Runey told the publication.

Provost Richard Wiscott told the outlet he anticipates up to 70 percent of the university’s majors could eventually offer three-year options, comparing current pushback to the early skepticism surrounding online education.

“I really do believe that will happen in the three-year degree space as well,” he said.

To protect students during the rollout, the university's accreditor, the New England Commission of Higher Education, requires a four-year version of every expedited program during its trial phase, commission President Lawrence Schall told *The Wall Street Journal*. This allows students to transfer into a standard track if graduate schools or employers hesitate to recognize the shorter credential.

![Supporters — including the Trump administration and state governing bodies — say the expedited tracks lower student debt and speed up workforce entry, while faculty critics warn they risk hollowing out higher education](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/18/34/GettyImages-1505802668.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

To cut a full year without sacrificing core instruction, universities are trimming electives, integrating internship models and consolidating coursework — particularly in high-demand career fields like nursing, social work and hospitality.

At New York University, President Linda Mills recently proposed three-year bachelor’s degrees in nursing and hospitality, noting that one in seven NYU undergraduates already finishes in three years. The school previously introduced a three-year medical degree in 2013 and offers a three-year dance degree due to the shorter career windows in that field.

For the proposed nursing track, an introductory scientific foundations course — covering key elements of biochemistry, organic chemistry, genetics and nutrition — would replace a standalone chemistry requirement, nursing school Dean Angela Frederick Amar told the outlet.

“What we’re proposing is sort of not really taking away anything from either, but thinking about how we may better integrate the content,” Amar said.

![Universities including New York University, the University of Oklahoma and Suffolk University are trimming electives and consolidating coursework in career-focused tracks like nursing, social work and hospitality](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/18/52/GettyImages-465891666.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Similarly, the University of Oklahoma is developing a three-year social work degree to address state workforce shortages and attract prospective students deterred by a four-year commitment, President Joe Harroz told the newspaper.

“How many students are being left behind because 120 [credit] hours is just insurmountable?” Harroz said.

Despite growing interest from administrators and state officials, faculty groups and higher education critics warn that shortened degrees risk lowering academic standards and eliminating the exploratory phase of college life.

“We don’t want to hollow out the higher education system that we have from its core,” Todd Wolfson, president of the American Association of University Professors, which represents more than 50,000 members, told *The Wall Street Journal*.

Calling the programs a misguided approach to college affordability, he added: “People are looking for an easy solution: ‘Why don’t we just make it three years?’” and “This seems like a shortcut answer.”

![While three-year bachelor’s programs are already standard in the U.K. and much of Europe, American colleges are testing the model with built-in safeguards, including four-year fallback options during trial periods](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/18/10/GettyImages-1765396140.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Even administrators implementing the degrees acknowledge that faster timelines limit student flexibility. At Suffolk University in Boston, which plans to launch a three-year degree in healthcare administration and innovation next fall, students will have restricted options for minors, double majors or studying abroad, though they retain the option to switch to a four-year track.

“Students who come into this program are not going to be able to change their major three times,” Suffolk President Marisa Kelly told the publication. “Part of what they’re losing is flexibility.”

Other institutions are restricting who can enroll. Utah Tech University in St. George introduced three-year programs this fall aimed primarily at working adult learners, while actively discouraging traditional undergraduates from applying, senior associate provost Chris Guymon told *The Wall Street Journal.* Traditional students who still wish to join must obtain a faculty recommendation proving their commitment to the field.

“There’s a benefit to giving students time to process and think about what they want to be, but also be as immersed as possible in what a university is,” Guymon said.

In Virginia, Fleming noted that state officials are weighing career readiness against “education for the sake of education,” pointing out that employers value graduates who have had time for “learning how to adult.”

“Any initiative is not without some trade-offs,” Fleming said.
