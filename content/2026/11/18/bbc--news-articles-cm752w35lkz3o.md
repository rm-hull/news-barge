'Within six hours I was unwell': Was wastewater to blame?

**The official sign on the banks of the River Tone at French Weir Park in Taunton advises people not to swim.**

But that hasn't stopped Jo, Jenny, Sue and a second Jo from enjoying a regular dip together.

"People think, oh you're swimming down by the supermarket, that can't be very nice, but it's gorgeous," Jenny Hillier says, as the women huddle round with their coats and hot drinks after a bracing October plunge.

People have been swimming at the spot on the River Tone in the centre of Taunton for more than a hundred years.

But it is only since 2024 that French Weir Park has been designated an official bathing site, which means from May to September the water gets tested by the Environment Agency (EA).

Those tests regularly confirm what the women's senses have been telling them for a while - the water quality is poor.

"There are times when we've come down and we haven't got in, either because of the look or the smell," says Jo Macdonald. "We don't take risks. We don't put our faces in. We don't swim with cuts or anything."

"This summer I got quite poorly," Jenny says, explaining that she'd had diarrhoea, vomiting and muscle cramps.

"I'd swum and then within six hours I was unwell. I was poorly for probably about seven to 10 days and it was not nice. I think it was the river that day."

So why is she back swimming in it?

"There are germs everywhere, all over the place. You can catch Covid in Tesco," she says.

The BBC has been investigating whether discharges of treated sewage might be responsible for poor water quality at a bathing site in Taunton.

This summer French Weir Park failed its tests on 13 of the 19 days it was sampled. The BBC analysed data from all four regulators across the UK for 2026 and found French Weir Park's failure rate of 68% was the highest of all the bathing sites

The regulators look for two types of faecal bacteria – E. coli and intestinal enterococci – that originate either in human or animal guts and can make people ill if ingested.

So where's it coming from at French Weir Park? The local water company Wessex Water says it could have come from wildlife, agriculture, private septic tanks as well as its sewerage works.

We decided to take a closer look to see if we could work out where the truth lies.

## The testing regime

Our wastewater treatment system is built on the assumption that faecal bacteria - from our poo - once discharged into nature will be rapidly diluted and perish.

The bacteria are known to have little impact on wildlife but they can make humans sick if ingested – so the rise in popularity of wild-swimming and testing of bathing sites have increased awareness.

As part of a BBC investigation we analysed the water sampling results from this summer across all the UK's 700 official bathing sites on the coast, in lakes and rivers.

We found that more than 10% of the UK's coastal sites and 37% of its inland sites failed to meet regulators' minimum standards across the summer.

That difference is likely to be because the sea acts as a more effective dispersant and can dilute and kill faecal bacteria quicker than a lake or river.

Of the 11 worst bathing sites in England in 2026, seven were on inland rivers or lakes.

## Why are our rivers so dirty?

Poor water quality has historically been linked to raw sewage spills. These are much more likely after it has rained. So why, in a summer that was extremely dry, did bacteria levels remain high in so many rivers?

In Taunton the official testing data suggests there has been a constant flow of bacteria entering the river.

So the BBC carried out its own sampling near three wastewater treatment works upstream of the Taunton site. We found the treated liquid coming out of the nearest sewage works at Bradford-on-Tone had about 240,000 colony forming units (CFU) of E. coli per 100ml - that's a level about 250 times higher than that considered safe for swimming.

![A map showing sampling around the wastewater treatment site at Bradford-on-Tone. ](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/5681/live/a73e7380-e1c9-11f1-99b6-a35a62fa4264.jpg)

"The counts from just outside the treatment works in my experience were high for treated effluent", Dr Rob Morley from Index Microbiology told us at his laboratory near Bristol as he peered down at small dots of E. coli bacteria on a transparent plastic plate.

What was of particular interest to Morley were the results of the test on water samples we'd taken above and below the point where the treated wastewater enters the River Tone.

It showed 199 CFU of E. coli above, which is fine for swimming and 7,701 CFU just below, more than eight times the regulators limit.

"You can see where it's seeding the river with pollution," Morley says.

"If we were to look further down the river, we would expect the levels to get lower with the dilution effect. But that does quite clearly show the impact that treated effluent can have on the river."

We shared our findings with Dr Andrew Thompson, a fellow at the Institute of Chemical Engineers. He was involved in a 2024 report that warned high concentrations of bacteria and viruses were common, external, particularly in the summer when river levels are lower and could cause serious diseases if ingested.

Thompson told the BBC the levels of E. coli we found in the River Tone were not illegal, or even particularly unusual, because there are currently no laws or regulations about how much bacteria water companies are allowed to discharge in what the industry calls treated effluent.

## Animal or human?

We also wanted to know whether analysing the E. coli samples from the bathing site could give us clues to where it came from.

With assistance from researchers at the University of Bristol, Morley arranged for e-DNA analysis to be carried out to identify whether the E. coli bacteria were more likely to be from a human or animal gut.

The answer, as is often the case with water quality, is complex. There were lots of different types of E.coli in the samples.

"Some were from humans, some were from animals and some were just generic from the environment," Morley says. "It definitely suggests that some of the E. coli is definitely coming from human origins, therefore potentially from that treatment works."

As with all of our testing, more needs to be done to reach firm conclusions.

Both the EA and Wessex Water are investigating what's causing the water quality in Taunton to be so poor. But sources at the EA told the BBC that constant flows of treated effluent is high up the list of likely culprits.

In response to our testing Wessex Water told the BBC its treatment works at Bradford on Tone was working normally and was meeting all its permit conditions set by the EA.

So could steady flows of treated effluent loaded with bacteria be behind many of the problems at bathing sites across the UK?

"All the sewage treatment works in the UK and in England have permits to protect the ecology and the wildlife within the river," says Jim Flory, a regional manager for the EA in the south-west.

"They've not set permits for bathing water regulations, which are all about protecting people who may want to be swimming, canoeing or using the water."

That has led to a system where a popular bathing site gets officially designated – the testing regime discovers it has high levels of faecal bacteria – and then a lengthy process begins to try to work out where the sources are and whether it's affordable to fix them.

![A woman in a bright orange suit stands by some large water pipes. ](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/de20/live/c912e500-e1cd-11f1-99a1-253766e7ad8b.jpg)

Tessa Fayers of Thames Water says installing UV disinfection, like they have at Cassington near Oxford, everywhere is just too expensive

Wolvercote Mill Stream in Oxford was the second inland river to get bathing water status in 2022 and since then the water has always been rated "poor".

In an attempt to improve things, banks of ultraviolet (UV) lights have just been installed at a cost of £4m upstream at Cassington wastewater treatment works to kill bacteria before they reach the River Thames. But there's no question of it being installed everywhere.

"The reality is it's expensive. We have to be sure we're spending money wisely," Tessa Fayers from Thames Water says.

UV disinfection has been installed at 160 of the nearly 7,000 wastewater treatment works across England – almost always as targeted interventions to protect either shellfish or bathing sites downstream.

Wessex Water said it had no plans to introduce UV disinfection at Bradford-on-Tone and that it would publish its conclusions on why the water quality is so poor in April 2027.
