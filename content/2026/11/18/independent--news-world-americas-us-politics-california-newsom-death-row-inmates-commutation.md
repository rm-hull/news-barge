California Gov. Gavin Newsom is facing a lawsuit from prosecutors and victims’ families seeking to block his plan to replace hundreds of death sentences with life in prison terms.

The lawsuit, filed Monday by the Criminal Justice Legal Foundation, includes district attorneys from 30 California counties and over two dozen relatives of murder victims. The victim rights group is asking the California Supreme Court to block Newsom’s plan, arguing that he did not follow the proper steps before announcing the sweeping move.

The group claims Newsom failed to properly notify prosecutors and victims’ families, giving them no chance to weigh in before he decided to move forward. They also argue that the governor did not review each inmate’s case individually before announcing his plan to commute every death sentence in the state.

Newsom announced November 5 that he planned to commute the death sentences of all 548 inmates on California’s death row, including 529 men and 19 women. If approved, the inmates would serve life in prison without the possibility of parole, meaning they would never be eligible for release but would no longer face execution. The last California execution was in 2006, but the sentences still hung over the convicts in case they resumed.

“Only this court can stop Newsom’s disregard of the law — his abuse of power,” the lawsuit reads.

Anne Marie Schubert, president and CEO of the Criminal Justice Legal Foundation and a former Sacramento County district attorney, accused Newsom of putting his personal beliefs ahead of decisions made by judges and juries.

“Thousands of jurors and hundreds of judges set aside their personal views on the merits of the death penalty to render judgments based on the evidence and the law,” Schubert wrote in the filing.

“Governor Newsom decided his personal views should override all those who actually participated in the process, followed and applied the law,” she added.

The lawsuit also questions whether Newsom can use his power as governor to reduce hundreds of sentences at once without following the rules that typically apply to individual cases.

California law requires governors to notify prosecutors and victims or their families when considering certain requests to reduce a prison sentence. But Newsom has argued those rules do not apply because he initiated the process himself rather than responding to individual inmates' requests.

Newsom has said his office would notify prosecutors after sending the proposed commutations to the state Supreme Court and ask them to help contact victims’ families.

The governor has long opposed the death penalty. In 2019, he halted executions in California by placing a moratorium on the practice, even though the state still had hundreds of people on death row.

![‘Governor Newsom decided his personal views should override all those who actually participated in the process, followed and applied the law,’ one prosecutor said](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/29/13/1130296521..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

"I take this action because I firmly believe that state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California’s values, undermines the foundations of our legal system, and diminishes each of us," Newsom said in an open letter announcing his decision.

He also acknowledged that his plan could bring back painful memories for families who lost loved ones to murder.

"As Governor, I alone bear responsibility for this decision and its impact, just as I alone would bear responsibility for each execution," he wrote.

Under the California Constitution, the state Supreme Court must also approve clemency for people convicted of multiple felonies. That requirement applies to 382 of the 548 inmates Newsom wants to include in the plan.

The move comes as Newsom faces growing speculation about a possible 2028 presidential run, though he has not announced a bid.
