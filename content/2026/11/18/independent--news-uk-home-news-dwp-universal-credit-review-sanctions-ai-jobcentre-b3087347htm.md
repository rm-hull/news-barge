The Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) is trialling artificial intelligence to decide which Universal Credit claimants should face “intrusive” benefit reviews.

A live test of a machine learning model is currently in use for Targeted Case Reviews (TCRs), a programme used to reassess more than one million claims every year, according to the department’s permanent secretary.

The anti-fraud initiative is set to save £13bn by the end of the decade. However, only 21 per cent of reviews find any incorrect payment, researchers from the Administrative Fairness Lab found earlier this year, warning that hundreds of thousands of people are subjected to the process unnecessarily.

In a letter to Parliament’s Public Accounts Committee (PAC), the DWP’s permanent secretary Dame Sarah Healey said it is running a “limited live test” of the model to support agents in deciding which cases to target for review.

She stressed that “human consideration remains central” to the process, insisting the model does not make decisions on whether a review proceeds, benefit entitlement, payment changes, suspensions, or claim closures.

![](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/06/03/15/2214408292..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Mark Bennett, of the University of Liverpool and Administrative Fairness Lab, warned that the deployment of machine learning marks a “potential step change” that “comes with clear risks”.

The law lecturer points to issues with a separate AI tool previously used by the DWP to screen Universal Credit advance payments which disproportionately flagged older, disabled, and non-UK claimants for fraud checks.

Mr Bennett said: “The use of machine learning as a fraud and error prevention tool in other areas of the welfare system was previously found to have shown bias in selecting people on the basis of age, disability, marital status, and nationality when identifying claims to investigate for possible fraud.

“There are serious questions to be asked, therefore, about the reliability of data used within the machine learning model and how, in using such technology, the DWP might avoid repeating previous mistakes.”

Dame Sarah said the trial operates under the usual safeguards and equality standards, noting that the DWP would “consider alternative options” if it fails to deliver as expected.

However, she confirmed that specific criteria to flag cases and other features of the model must remain confidential to protect the “integrity of the benefit system.”

Earlier this year, the research by the Administrative Fairness Lab found that many claimants chosen TCRs are repeatedly subject to multiple reviews – often only months apart – causing “feelings of criminalisation” and exacerbating anxiety.

Mr Bennett said: “Given the huge scale of the scheme, the impacts of any such mistakes are likely to be widely felt.”

A DWP spokesperson said: “Our targeted case review program has already saved the taxpayer £1.7 billion by blocking incorrect Universal Credit payments, and we continue to test, develop and invest in advanced analytics.

“Decisions on cases selected for targeted case review will always be made by a DWP member of staff and we are committed to supporting all customers, especially those with vulnerabilities, throughout the review process.”
