People across the country are rushing to surplus stores for doomsday preparations after a recent string of outages to America’s technology infrastructure.

Despite no evidence of nefarious activity causing the rash of outages, they have sparked concerns about Americans relying too heavily on hyper-connected systems that can easily go offline and throw lives into chaos.

So, like in any uncertain time, people are stocking up — but instead of wiping out the toilet paper during the COVID-19 pandemic, they are clearing the shelves of radios and emergency food.

It started with Microsoft on Thursday when millions of global users lost access to services such as Outlook and Teams. A day later, a nationwide ground stop went into effect for more than four hours thanks to a technical failure.

This week, thousands of customers at major banks reported difficulty accessing their online accounts thanks to a software bug in JPMorganChase systems; an internal update at Apple caused issues with its services, and an internal network that the National Security Agency uses to communicate classified information went down during routine maintenance.

So, like in any uncertain time, people are stocking up on radios and emergency food](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/21/26/GettyImages-2305765243.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“We got totally sold out of the MRE'S and the usual things that go along with people worried about, you know, collapse of infrastructure,” Mark, the owner of the Brigadoon Army Surplus in Del City, Oklahoma, told local outlet Fox 25. “Water purification tablets are gone. The water straws, which is a personal water filter, we got sold out of those.”

Jaime Naylor, the store manager at The Surplus Store in the Los Angeles neighborhood of Palms, said shoppers are reaching for battery-powered radios.

“Some sort of communication radio because we are so dependent on our phones. You’re not going to be able to charge your phones,” Naylor told local outlet KTLA. “Having a battery-powered radio that you could use to get information, that’s really important.”

![But tech experts suggested pumping the brakes on the doomsdaying, with one saying people should be ‘concerned enough to plan, not to panic’](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/21/38/GettyImages-2305765097.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

There’s been an increase in doomsday prepping videos on social media, some serious and some humorous, in recent days.

One shopper at The Surplus Store, Harris Washington, was influenced, telling KTLA there’s “been a lot on my feed” about doomsday prepping.

“When we are looking at the survival stuff, it puts into perspective that you never really had a plan to begin with,” fellow shopper Cherish Washington said.

But tech experts have suggested people pump the brakes on the doomsdaying.

Danielle Lemons, vice president of operations at Nashville-based IT firm DirecTech Connect, previously told *The Independent* that people should be “concerned enough to plan, not to panic.”

“The real cause for concern isn't a single master threat—it is the underlying reality that our digital economy has become so hyper-connected that isolated software bugs, database corruptions, or routing errors now trigger immediate, high-visibility disruption across millions of lives,” Fran Villalba Segarra, founder and CEO of INTERNXT, a cybersecurity company based in Spain, said.
