**Today's top story: Germany plans to enact fuel price cap and electricity tax cut from start of 2027**

The planned fuel price cap is set to kick in from January 1st, 2027, according to an agreement by the leaders of the black-red (CDU/CSU-SPD) coalition.

The coalition committee decided that the Federal Cabinet should adopt a corresponding draft law on December 2nd, in order to activate a fuel price cap from the start of the new year. The cap would initially be valid for three months.

A fuel price discount, which has been in effect since the beginning of October, expires at the end of December. It was intended to mitigate price increases resulting from the ongoing war in Iran.

In mid-September, the leaders of the coalition agreed to introduce a fuel price cap, modeled on those in Luxembourg or Belgium.

This cap is not intended to be permanent and is "crisis-related," they said. Still, it remains unclear exactly what the fuel price cap should look like.

Economics Minister Katherina Reiche's initial proposal stipulated that the cap would only take effect if price increases for diesel or gasoline exceeded the average price increase in other EU countries by more than five percentage points for three consecutive weeks. This had never occurred in recent years.

Members of the SPD had rejected that model as unsuitable, arguing that it would not be effective.

In other money related news, the German Press Agency reported on Wednesday morning that the black-red coalition leaders have also agreed to bring forward an electricity tax cut for all customers.

The financing for the tax cut will initialy only be secured for 2027.

Germany has seen a significant rise in applications for assessments for long-term care benefits amid concerns that planned government reforms could make it harder to qualify for support.

In October, 419,000 requests for care assessments were submitted across Germany – 69,000 more than in the same month last year, according to data from the Federal Association of Medical Services (*Medizinischer Dienst)*.

Preliminary figures suggest an even sharper rise in such requests for November.

It comes as the government prepares to reform Germany's long-term care insurance system in response to rising costs and a growing number of people needing care.

More than six million people received benefits from the statutory care insurance system in 2025, compared with around 2.6 million in 2015.

The proposed reforms, expected to take effect partly from January 1st, 2027, include cuts to certain benefits, such as the monthly relief allowance of up to €131 for people with care level one and respite care support for family carers. The changes could also make it more difficult to be officially recognised as needing care.

**Germany heavily affected by climate change consequences**

According to a study by environmental organisation Germanwatch, Germany is among the top quarter of countries most affected by climate change. This is the conclusion of a Climate Risk Index, which Germanwatch presented at the COP31 climate conference in Antalya, Turkey.

![](https://apiwp.thelocal.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/633600211.jpg)

Within the past thirty years, Germany ranks 34th out of 174 countries in terms of climate damages and fatalities.

“Heat waves cause the most fatalities in Germany, while storms and floods cause the greatest damage,” explained co-author Vera Künzel. “Overall, more than 33,000 people lost their lives in Germany as a result of extreme weather events between 1996 and 2025, and the property damage totalled [120 billion euros], adjusted for inflation.”

While it is impossible to say what proportion of this damage is attributable to climate change, it is largely undisputed that climate change increases the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events.

Meanwhile, speaking for Germany at the climate conference, Environment Minister Carsten Schneider said that other countries should spend more to mitigate climate change and its impacts.

"We expect strong emerging economies to show solidarity and contribute more," said Environment Minister Carsten Schneider (SPD) at the climate conference.

He did not name specific countries, but in the past, however, Germany has called for greater participation from China and the wealthy Gulf states.

The environment minister said that Germany would continue to "pay its fair share", but Germany's public financing for climate finance has slowed since 2025.

Financial expert Jan Kowalzig from Oxfam said, "In his speech, the minister should have admitted that German budget funds for supporting low-income countries in climate protection and adaptation are set to decrease in the coming years."

**Germany demands 'transparency' from Deutsche Bank after data leak**

Germany's finance minister Tuesday urged "full transparency" from Deutsche Bank after international media outlets published reports based on millions of leaked documents that shine a light on questionable past business practices at the banking giant.

The leaks deal with the bank's business over the past 20 years, including ties to Russian oligarchs, the convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein, and Donald Trump before he became US president.

The data, shared by unidentified whistleblowers, raised questions about internal controls at Germany's largest bank and reignited questions regarding chief executive Christian Sewing's efforts to turn around the institution.

Deutsche Bank shares fell by around four percent in Frankfurt after the reports emerged.

Lars Klingbeil, Germany's finance minister and vice chancellor, said the lender must "provide full transparency regarding how it has addressed past failures and internal control deficiencies, and how it will effectively prevent them in the future."
