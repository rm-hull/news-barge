Government departments frequently fail to consult risk experts prior to making major decisions despite the growing threat of incidents such as cyber attacks, the public spending watchdog has warned.

Citing the example of PHP, an open source scripting language that underpins an estimated 70% of websites globally and recently suffered security vulnerabilities, the National Audit Office (NAO) said vital public services could suffer due to varied risk management capabilities across different departments.

Publishing its report on Wednesday, the NAO stressed that "effective risk management matters" owing to multiple risks such as extreme weather, climate change and pandemics, in addition to fraud and digital crimes like hacking.

Yet it concluded that risk management "is not consistently integrated into leadership, governance and decision-making across government".

The watchdog acknowledged that the government had "strengthened the foundations" of risk management over recent years by introducing the "risk profession" across the civil service while broadening the guidance, training and support provided by the Risk Centre of Excellence in the Treasury.

The report identified good practice in individual organisations, including a transparent “no surprises” culture at the Bank of England which enabled concerns to be raised during the modernisation of a critical payments system, strengthening collaboration and shared accountability.

But progress remains inconsistent, the NAO said, with many government bodies not making full use of information on performance and early-warning indicators to anticipate threats.

Amid limited evidence of major decisions, or the broader direction of organisations, being influenced by risk professionals, a survey of this cohort found just over half said they were typically involved “either too late to shape major decisions, primarily to check and approve decisions already made, or not at all”.

The NAO said the engagement of senior leaders was also inconsistent, with some giving risk “limited priority”, increasing the probability that concerns are overlooked, addressed too late or opportunities are missed.

It also found government bodies “frequently” lacked the oversight needed to manage risks across departments, arm’s-length bodies, suppliers and local government.

The report said: “The government’s risk environment has become more complex and uncertain.

“Recent events, including the Covid-19 pandemic, geopolitical instability, economic disruption and climate impacts such as wildfires, have highlighted weaknesses in the government’s ability to anticipate and respond to system-wide risks.

“At the same time, the government increasingly expects public bodies to take well-managed risks to support innovation and improve public services.”

Head of the NAO, Gareth Davies, said: “The last few years have highlighted the scale and complexity of the risks facing government and how quickly they can escalate into crises, with lasting consequences for public services.

“Whilst it has made good progress, the government must now put risk management at the heart of how it plans, spends and delivers, making sure that senior leaders are equipped to spot vulnerabilities early and to take well-manged risks to improve public services.

“This will become ever more important as more decisions and public spending are devolved.”

The government has been approached for comment.

A recent report by the Lords National Resilience Committee said Britain is “seriously underprepared” for emergencies and threats such as cyber attacks and extreme weather.

Limited stockpiles of food and medicine are leaving the country “dangerously exposed” to disruption in global supply chains, while ministers are “complacent” in the face of “potentially catastrophic risks” as the world becomes increasingly turbulent and insecure, the committee warned.
