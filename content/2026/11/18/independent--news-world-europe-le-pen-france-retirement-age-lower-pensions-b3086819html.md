Marine Le Pen has pledged to tear up Emmanuel Macron’s flagship pension overhaul and allow French workers who started their careers young to retire at 60 if she wins the 2027 presidential election.

Unveiling her economic programme on Tuesday, the National Rally front-runner sought to convince sceptical bond markets that she can deliver one of her party’s core promises to working-class voters without worsening France’s already-strained public finances.

At the centre of her proposal is a new €900bn (£750bn) retirement fund built up over a generation alongside France’s existing pay-as-you-go system, financed by redirecting a portion of existing employer payroll contributions.

“We are going to bring down one of the last privileges in our country, the privilege of access to the financial services that make a minority wealthy today, while also improving the financing of our national economy,” Ms Le Pen told reporters.

![Le Pen has pledged to lower France’s minimum retirement age to 60](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/23/2026-11-17T105555Z_1253347817_RC2MPOAY0B7Q_RTRMADP_3_FRANCE-POLITICS-LEPEN.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The far-right leader promised that hundreds of thousands of people would be able to leave work two to three years earlier than under Mr Macron’s legislation, which gradually lifts the minimum retirement age from 62 to 64.

France’s current statutory retirement age of 62 years and nine months is already well below the 65-to-67 benchmark across most major economies, even as French life expectancy ranks among the highest.

“For those who wonder whether the French social model will still be standing when they retire, I have heard them,” she said. “This reform is a promise for the future and an act of faith in the French model.”

“Hundreds of thousands of workers will retire earlier, gaining two to three years compared with what [Mr Macron’s] reform would have imposed,” she added.

President Macron’s 2023 pension changes provoked months of mass strikes and street protests before the government agreed to freeze their implementation in exchange for Socialist support on the budget.

But the higher retirement age threshold remains due to come into force in January 2028, shortly after the next president takes office.

![Macron’s pension reforms have drawn widespread criticism, triggering mass demonstrations last year](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/23/2218064524..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

How to fund France’s generous retirement system – the single biggest drain on the public purse, projected to cost more than €430bn (£360bn) next year – has become a central test for the National Rally’s economic credibility.

Ms Le Pen has committed to finding €138bn (£115bn) in wider public spending reductions by 2032, arguing that her overhaul would eventually generate up to €20bn (£17bn) in annual savings.

Economists and market analysts remain unconvinced that those figures add up if Mr Macron’s age increase is reversed.

To keep costs in check, National Rally is shifting emphasis away from a fixed age threshold towards the total number of years paid into the system.

Workers who entered employment early would qualify for retirement between 60 and 62 after 40 to 42 years of contributions, while university graduates would generally need to remain in work well into their sixties to receive a full pension.
