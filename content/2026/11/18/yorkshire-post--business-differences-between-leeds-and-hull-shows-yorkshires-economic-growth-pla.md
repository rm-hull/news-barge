# Differences between Leeds and Hull shows Yorkshire's economic growth plan can't be one-size-fits-all: Caroline Gorton

## If SMEs are the backbone of our economy, then large businesses are its heart and lungs. Towns and cities across Yorkshire have been shaped by the industries and large-scale employers that have called them home for generations.

From the manufacturing and industrial heritage of Hull to the financial and professional services that have helped establish Leeds as one of Britain’s major economic centres, businesses have created jobs, attracted investment and helped forge distinct local economies across the county.

But their importance is not simply a story of Yorkshire’s past. These established strengths also hold the key to the region’s future growth.

Take Leeds as example.

![Caroline Gorton shares her expert insight](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOjYyZTU2NWU0LTYyNzItNGNiNS05YjdmLWJlZThmOTUxNmZlZjplMDRlOWFhZC02NjU5LTRkMTEtOTRlOS1mMjcxMDZiYmJlNTg=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)


The city has been identified by new HSBC UK and Centre for Economics & Business Research (CEBR) research as an ‘Anchor Economy’ – a place benefiting disproportionately from the presence of large employers, inward employment and concentrations of economic activity.

It is one of four Anchor Economies identified across Yorkshire, alongside Kingston upon Hull and two other local authority areas.

Across Great Britain, the analysis identified 147 Anchor Economies in total, which together support around 17 million jobs and generate approximately £1.4 trillion for the economy each year.

Residents in these areas earn, on average, nearly £3,000 more annually than those in other locations.

Leeds is classified as a Resilient Anchor Economy.

Unlike some Anchor Economies, its strength does not come from reliance on a single dominant industry.

Financial and insurance activities are its largest sectors, and whilst growth of the Northern Square Mile, with the likes of the Bank of England and FCA expanding, is clearly bringing more opportunities to the city, these sectors still account for just 13.5 per cent of local output.

This points towards a diverse range of large employers contributing to collective growth, and therefore an opportunity for more diverse employment opportunities.

To realise the potential of this opportunity, large employers in Leeds can deepen existing strengths to attract further investment, strengthening local supply chains and ensuring businesses can access the skills they need to grow.

However, the diversity of Yorkshire’s Anchor Economies also demonstrates why there can be no single prescription for regional growth.

Kingston upon Hull presents a very different picture. Hull is classified as a Developing Anchor Economy.

Manufacturing accounts for a substantial share of its economic activity, with the importance of the growing renewables sector widely recognised as creating an important source of skilled employment, productivity and investment.

But that concentration is not yet matched by the same strength across the income, skills and growth measures captured by our research. Hull has the lowest share of residents with higher-level qualifications of any major city examined in the report, at 28.1 per cent.

That creates a different challenge: protecting and building on the industrial activity already rooted in the city while strengthening the skills, incomes and wider economic foundations needed to make that success more resilient.

That contrast between Leeds and Hull is precisely the point.

Both are Anchor Economies, but the route to future growth looks different for each.

For Leeds, the opportunity is to capitalise on a diverse economy, major employers and established clusters.

For Hull, the priority is to strengthen the foundations around its existing industrial base, widening access to higher-value employment and creating the conditions for further investment.

And there is a wider lesson for Yorkshire. The county does not lack economic strengths. The challenge is understanding what makes individual places successful and what they need next.

For mayors, local authorities and other regional decision-makers, that means resisting a one-size-fits-all approach. Established and resilient Anchor Economies need support to help successful employers and clusters expand.

Places more exposed to economic change need a focus on skills, diversification and resilience. Elsewhere, investment in infrastructure and the wider business environment can help attract the large employers capable of becoming anchors themselves.

Economic renewal does not have to mean erasing a place’s past and inventing a new future. From Leeds’ diverse corporate and employment base to the manufacturing strength of Hull, much of the blueprint for Yorkshire’s future growth is already hiding in plain sight. The task now is to build on it.

Caroline Gorton is Managing Director, Head of Large Corporate Coverage for the North, Scotland and Midlands at HSBC UK’s Commercial Banking division
