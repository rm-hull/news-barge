Half a million of the most vulnerable people in England will get proactive help with their health needs from new teams that will act as a “downpayment” on the promised national care service, Yvette Cooper has announced.

Elderly people, care home residents, those with dementia and people near the end of their lives will receive help from new joint teams of NHS and social care staff. Initially about 500,000 people by next April will benefit from new joined-up support teams providing personalised care.

In a speech on Wednesday, Cooper described the measure as a “downpayment” on the national care service promised by Andy Burnham to address the social care crisis. The approach will be expanded and eventually cover about 2 million people, the health secretary said in a speech at the annual conference of the King’s Fund health thinktank.

![Yvette Cooper](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/0f5f5f3d677ebd30eeccee61afcc27a2316a535c/0_0_2000_3000/master/2000.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

Once fully operational the major shift in how key care services work will help relieve the pressure on overcrowded hospitals by cutting 4.3m bed days and reducing the number of people – mainly the frail elderly and those with dementia – who experience “corridor care”, Cooper said.

It is a significant milestone in the government’s ambition to transform how the NHS works through “three big shifts” – from an analogue to digital-based service, prioritise prevention of illness over treatment and move a lot of hospital-based care into community settings and people’s homes.

The planned “neighbourhood support teams” come together to identify the most vulnerable members of their local community and devise a plan for keeping them as well as possible and provide care in their own home or care home – and out of hospital – for as long as possible.

They will receive regular check-ins to assess their health from teams who know them, to ensure they are receiving the right support. They and their family will also have access to a 24/7 helpline that will act as a single point of contact, though it is unclear when that part of the new service will start.

Everyone being helped by the joint teams of NHS and local council personnel will also have an individual care plan that makes clear what their needs are and who should be helping with them.

Cooper billed the creation of the teams as a way of providing proactive, personalised care that will make care much easier to access while also reducing the strain on the NHS, which has been struggling to meet the growing demand for healthcare caused by the ageing population.

In her speech, the health secretary was expected to say that the new way of working is “a downpayment on a national care service: a practical step towards bringing health and social care together around people’s needs”.

Wednesday’s speech builds on Cooper’s announcement last week that GPs in England will receive £40m this winter to help give 300,000 vulnerable patients better care at home, to reduce their risk of ending up in hospital.
