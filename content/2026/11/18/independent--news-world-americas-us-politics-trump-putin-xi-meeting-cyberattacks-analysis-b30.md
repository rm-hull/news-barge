Chinese president Xi Jinping isn’t known as a hugger, but he made an exception for President Donald Trump Wednesday.

The Chinese autocrat made a rare trip to the airport in Shenzhen, South China, to greet the American president when he landed on Air Force One for a series of meetings that will include a trilateral sit-down between Trump, Xi and Russia’s Vladimir Putin.

Xi’s move was explained by the Chinese leader as part of a “tradition of reciprocating courtesy” after Trump met him at Joint Base Andrews when he arrived for a state visit in the U.S. two months ago.

The warm welcome continued a few hours later when the two leaders were joined by Russian President Vladimir Putin for a group photo before the evening banquet for leaders at the Asia Pacific Economic Cooperation event.

Trump’s visit to China came on the back half of a four-day trip through Asia that will conclude with the unprecedented sit-down he has obsessed over for months. Despite the long travel days, Trump seemed in good spirits, telling reporters every one of his meetings with Xi Jinping had been “very pleasant.”

![Chinese president Xi Jinping isn’t known as a hugger, but he made an exception for President Donald Trump Wednesday](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/09/2305160371..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Yet the experience of Americans back home has been decidedly less so as people across the country have been subjected to the consequences of a series of massive system failures.

Six days ago, Microsoft’s email, Teams and Copilot services, used by all sorts of organizations, crashed and denied workers access to messages and other applications for most of the day. On Friday, the Federal Aviation Administration grounded every U.S. flight for more than four hours, marking the longest nationwide ground stop since the September 11 attacks.

The snafu resulted in at least 14,000 flights getting canceled across Friday and Saturday, with Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy initially blaming the system’s “incredibly old air traffic control system,” but later claiming that the failure was attributed to a router that a contractor had installed during routine wiring work at a site in Arkansas.

Things got worse from there. On Monday, a software bug locked thousands of JPMorgan Chase customers out of their accounts, United Airlines was forced to delay flights due to the various outages, and the classified internal network of the National Security Agency was affected as well.

These companies were not hacked, the Trump administration says, and there is nothing to indicate otherwise. But the attempts of administration officials to convince Americans that there is nothing to fear have not had the desired effect.

The White House has denied that there are any “credible threats to the homeland” at this time, and although a bevy of bold-faced names ranging from Vice President JD Vance to Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin has stated that these unrelated attacks are not cause for alarm or evidence of a series of malign attacks on the country’s infrastructure, even members of Trump’s own party aren’t convinced.

Senator Josh Hawley of Missouri has said that he does not believe the outages were a coincidence and demanded that Congress be briefed on the situation, whatever that may be.

Karoline Leavitt, Trump’s former press secretary, also said on Fox News that the White House should be more transparent with the American people about what is going on.

When asked about the outages by reporters in Manila on Tuesday, Trump said: “It’s like when you’re big and powerful, and you have all the money. Things like that happen. It’s fine.”

His National Security Adviser and Secretary of State, Marco Rubio, later told reporters that the failures were actually a positive development because companies were being more transparent about their vulnerabilities in order to avoid being accused of covering up something.

Both officials’ response evoked the Catch-22 which led to Trump’s party taking massive losses in the midterm elections as voters soured on the president’s economic performance — or lack thereof. Just like voters rejected Trump’s proclamations of a “golden age” - when diesel fuel has hit $7 per gallon in many states - people aren’t buying the president’s claim that “it’s fine.”

![President Donald Trump, centre left, China's President Xi Jinping, centre right, and Russian President Vladimir Putin, third from right, pose for a picture before the welcome banquet at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen, Guangdong province, China, Wednesday](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/13/download.-3..jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

But for now at least, Trump is all smiles as he prepared for his sit-down with dictators. Like most lame duck presidents, he is now pivoting to burnish his legacy with foreign policy items.

The meeting with Xi and Putin on Thursday will be the first time a U.S. president has sat down with the leaders of the world’s two other largest nuclear powers.

While a Russian lawmaker has compared the meeting to Yalta — the 1945 conference at which Franklin Roosevelt, Winston Churchill and Josef Stalin planned out the post-World War II global order — the Kremlin has dismissed such comparisons as “games of political scientists.”

The White House has also downplayed expectations by telling reporters not to expect any agreement on nuclear weapons, the only subject it has said would be discussed.

Trump doesn’t want to bring up Putin’s war against Ukraine or the alleged Chinese spying efforts targeting the former CEO of America’s most valuable company, Nvidia, for fear of angering Xi. This is even after both Xi and Putin criticized Washington in speeches at the APEC summit Wednesday.

The Chinese leader used his remarks to call for “free and open trade” and defended the World Trade Organization while Putin described the U.S. and its allies as “former hegemons” and said that Europe was in “humiliating decline.”

Sanctions and tariffs, Putin said, “are incapable of halting growth—let alone crushing the economies of competitors.”

Were either man a democratically-elected leader, Trump would likely rant and rave about the purported insult to the U.S. and threaten or impose tariffs or some other penalty on their respective countries — just as he did with Canada after Prime Minister Mark Carney criticized his tariff policies.

But Trump doesn’t dare risk crossing Putin or Xi — not because he has some broader geopolitical aim, but because he sees them as his friends.
