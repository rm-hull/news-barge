Persistent loneliness is strongly linked to dementia but occasional solitude can be harmless, a large decades-long study reveals.

Previous research shows loneliness can aggravate dementia symptoms but exactly what level of social isolation triggers the greatest harm remains largely unclear.

The latest study surveyed 3,777 adults aged 65 and above for three types of loneliness – "never", "occasional" and "chronic" – and assessed their risk of developing dementia in the next 20 years.

Researchers found the incidence of dementia varied significantly across the three groups, ranging from 33.1 per cent for the "never lonely" group to 49.1 per cent for individuals with chronic loneliness.

Comparing the groups, they found that people who experienced chronic, persistent loneliness had a nearly 40 per cent higher risk of developing dementia than those who never felt lonely.

Occasional loneliness, on the other hand, showed no significant association with increased dementia risk, according to the study published in the journal *Aging & Mental Health.*

![A man rests at a retirement facility](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/4/36/Man-rests-at-retirement-facility.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The study challenges the assumption that any experience of loneliness poses equal danger to cognitive health.

“This distinction is critical. While most people experience loneliness at some point in their lives, it’s when loneliness becomes chronic and persistent that it may increase the risk of developing dementia,” Karine Pérès, an author of the study from the Bordeaux Population Health Centre in France, noted.

Researchers suspect chronic loneliness triggers sustained inflammation through prolonged activation of stress and immune pathways. “It's also associated with physical inactivity and poor diet, contributing to cardiovascular disease and diabetes, both dementia risk factors,” Dr Peres said. “Additionally, lack of social interactions limits cognitive stimulation, while unresolved social stress leads to an accumulation of biological wear and tear over time.”

The study also shows that people living with partners or in larger families may still experience chronic loneliness. Nearly 30 per cent of the study participants who were chronically lonely did not live alone.

"It shows this vulnerability may also stem from unsatisfying relationships, caregiving burdens or lack of meaningful activities," Yvanna Simon, the study’s lead author, explained. "Healthcare professionals, social workers and policymakers should not exclusively focus on living arrangements as a proxy for social risk, but should also incorporate subjective experiences of connection and loneliness.”

Researchers call for comprehensive approaches to address chronic loneliness and tackle dementia. “As some situations of loneliness are potentially preventable or reversible, it may represent a critical actionable target for interventions aimed at promoting healthy cognitive aging,” they concluded.
