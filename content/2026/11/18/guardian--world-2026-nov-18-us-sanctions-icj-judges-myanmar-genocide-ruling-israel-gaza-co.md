Fears are growing that the US could impose sanctions on judges at the international court of justice, including this year’s Nobel peace prize winner, Navi Pillay, if the court rules on Thursday that the Myanmar government committed genocide against the Rohingya.

The ICJ has never found a state responsible for committing genocide, and a significant factor in previous cases has been how to prove “intent” in a crime defined as acts committed with the “intent to destroy, in whole or in part” a national, ethnical, racial or religious group.

Lawyers representing Rohingya survivors say patterns of behaviour by Myanmar’s military, including killing people as they fled, show clear attempts to destroy the group during assaults on Rakhine state in 2016 and 2017.

It is the role of independent judges to interpret the genocide convention. However, judges at the court have taken advice on how to protect their assets from potential US sanctions, as a genocide ruling could be interpreted by Washington as lowering the “legal threshold” for the crime.

The Foundation for Defense of Democracies, an influential thinktank close to the White House, has predicted a genocide ruling will lead to the US imposing sanctions on the ICJ.

The US and Israel fear the final judgment may clarify how to establish a government’s intent to commit a genocide, and so set a precedent that makes it more likely the court will later rule that Israel committed genocide in Gaza.

Myanmar, which has denied carrying out a genocide, claims its actions targeted a military uprising by insurgents.

The ICJ, sometimes described as the UN’s top court, is responsible for settling interstate disputes, unlike the international criminal court (ICC), which prosecutes individuals accused of war crimes and other international crimes.

The US has already imposed crippling sanctions on judges at the ICC after the court issued arrest warrants against the Israeli prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu. The US has also announced sanctions against the entire ICC as an institution, which it described as a “rogue court”. Washington fears the ICC could go after US troops.

The Gambia, as a litigant, was entitled to appoint an ad hoc judge in the case against Myanmar and chose the South African lawyer Pillay, who in October was awarded the Nobel peace prize. She is required to act as a jurist and not an advocate for the Gambia.

Mike Waltz, the US ambassador to the UN, has sent a warning shot to Pillay and the 15 ICJ judges, saying Washington “will not allow globalist, faux judges to weaponise the law against our troops”.

Waltz asserted last month: “The ICJ ad hoc judge Navi Pillay is tipping the scales on the ICJ’s current proceedings on Myanmar, intending to lower the legal threshold for proving genocide so that weakened and false standard can later be weaponised against American and allied soldiers whenever they raise a finger against terrorists that hide behind civilians.”

In remarks addressed to Pillay, he said: “We see your agenda and we’ll be watching the ICJ’s handling of these proceedings very closely.”

In a speech last Friday, the US secretary of state, Marco Rubio, said: “For this global elite, international law … is an instrument to advance their interests under the pretence of universal principles.”

He warned the ICC against quoting laws to “men with swords”.

Israel has previously criticised adverse advisory opinions issued by the ICJ and refused to implement 2024 orders requiring Israel to admit more aid into Gaza, and to cooperate with Unrwa, the UN relief and works agency.

In the Myanmar case, a 24-page submission jointly filed by the UK and five other mainly European states, argued that the ICJ, in judging intent to commit genocide, should examine a range of “facts and circumstances” assessed “comprehensively and holistically”.

It also stressed that genocide was not solely defined by killings, and that the treatment of children, gender-based violence and forced displacement were also relevant.

The US until recently had taken a relatively more benign view of The Hague-based ICJ, and under Antony Blinken as US secretary of state determined that Myanmar had committed genocide.

The 15 ICJ judges are elected for nine-year terms by the UN security council and the UN general assembly.

Rubio has given the ICC until April to respond to his criticisms before he imposes sanctions.

On Friday, the ICC formally decided to drop plans to prosecute US soldiers and intelligence agents for potentially unlawful acts in Afghanistan. The shelving of the near-dormant inquiry came after it was agreed evidence would be hard to obtain, but one side-effect may be a greater willingness on the part of the US to abandon its plan to impose sanctions on the ICC.
