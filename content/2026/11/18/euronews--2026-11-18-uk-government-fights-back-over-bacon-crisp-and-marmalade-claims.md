## From smoky-flavoured snacks to ketchup sachets, ministers are pushing back against claims that closer ties with Brussels will mean a new wave of EU rules for British consumers.

Smoky bacon crisps are safe, marmalade is staying put, and the United Kingdom won't have to ditch its ketchup sachets.

As Brexit tensions resurface ahead of a crunch UK-EU summit, the government is scrambling to quash claims that closer ties with Brussels could mean a fresh wave of European rules for British consumers.

Prime Minister Andy Burnham’s team has released a "factsheet and mythbuster" document, which it hopes will clarify "several points" about a package being negotiated with the European Union.

The summit, set to be held in Brussels on Friday, will focus on three specific agreements – cutting red tape for food exports, linking the two sides' emissions-trading schemes and finalising a "youth experience" scheme.

The UK government hopes the food and drinks deal alone could add some £5.1 billion (€6.12 billion) to the country's economy.

As Burnham’s government makes moves to get closer to the EU in a post-Brexit world, some parts of the UK media have been pushing a narrative which suggests this relationship could usher in a new era of EU influence on Britain.

"Smoky flavours in crisps, sauces, and soups will remain on UK supermarket shelves," the statement put out by the government said.

It also added that "British marmalade will not need to be relabelled, as most British marmalade brands on supermarket shelves already meet European labelling rules.”

The statement also makes it clear that a ban on small sachets of ketchup and mayonnaise or on single-use plastics, including straws, cutlery and cotton buds, will not feature as part of the draft agreements on the table.

### Burnham’s detractors worry Brexit could be reversed

Despite Burnham’s keenness to forge a closer relationship with the bloc, many pro-Brexit campaigners fear he’ll soon push to rejoin the EU.

In 2016, a tiny majority voted to leave the 27-nation alliance, many for the supposed financial drain the EU had on the UK.

To quell some of that fear, Burnham’s statement also dismissed "largely speculative" reports that the UK would have to pay millions of pounds into the EU budget under the new package.

The Prime Minister has also committed to his predecessor, Keir Starmer's "red lines" on Europe for this parliament – that means there will be no return to the single market, customs union or freedom of movement.

Speaking this week, Burnham said discussions about the long-term relationship with the EU beyond the next election – due by 2029 at the latest – would only begin after Friday's summit.

"The deal that's on the table will bring growth to the British economy," he said, “The truth is the Brexit deal that they negotiated wasn't good enough," he said.

"They've burned bridges with our largest market, old friends and neighbours. They just destroyed all of those relationships,” Burnham added.
