## Good morning! It's Wednesday, and my name is Jorge Liboreiro, ready to crunch numbers. Why? Because today is all about financial support for Ukraine, which is becoming harder to pin down. Let's break it down.

**Show of strength:** The European Commission will today present its hotly anticipated proposal to issue a €45 billion support loan to Ukraine to help plug the cash-strapped country's gaping wartime deficit.

The announcement will be made by President **Ursula von der Leyen** herself, flanked by**Valdis Dombrovskis**, Commissioner for the Economy, and** Andrius Kubilius**, Commissioner for Defence, a sign of the high stakes at play.

"It's important to continue both supporting Ukraine and putting the pressure on the aggressor, Russia," Dombrovskis told us yesterday on our new show Europe Tonight. "Russia only understands a position of strength."

The money will be raised through joint debt (the arch-famous **Eurobonds**), which means member states will have to foot the annual interest. As in previous operations, the repayment of the principal will be made contingent on Kyiv receiving war reparations.

"What happens if Russia does not pay reparations?" Dombrovskis said. "We have explicitly foreseen in the regulation that in this case, the Union reserves the right to use immobilised Russian sovereign assets."

These assets, we must remember, are worth a whopping €210 billion on EU soil. Despite a push to use them now, Brussels think it's best to keep them for later on.

**AAA graded:** Though the Commission still enjoys a triple rating, the scheme won't come cheap. Borrowing costs have soared this year amid high energy prices, geopolitical volatility, investor jitters and the costly AI race. Still, the EU is determined to support Ukraine for "as long as it takes", as von der Leyen often says. The prevailing opinion is that the costs of a failed Ukrainian state would exceed any financial undertaking.

The proposal is set to be discussed by EU leaders later this month at a special summit. Also on the table will be the next EU budget. Technically, the two subjects are separate entities, but they have *de facto*become one single package. It's all money after all.**But wait:** Didn't EU leaders approve a €90 billion support loan meant to cover Ukraine's military and financial needs for 2026 and 2027? Yes, they did.

But that loan, which is still ongoing, was designed under the (completely unfounded) assumption that Russia's war would end sometime this year. Don't ask me what prompted Commission officials to assume such a thing, because I'm as baffled as you are. The premise, of course, has fallen apart. Moscow is escalating airstrikes to make Ukraine's winter insufferable and destroy the levers of the local economy.

The non-stop attacks are disrupting business activity, hampering trade and causing widespread blackouts, all of which diminish Kyiv's ability to collect revenue and maintain public services. This makes the country even more dependent on foreign aid.

The devastation has drilled a €70 billion hole in Ukraine's 2027 budget. The bloc's new €45 billion loan is meant to cover two-thirds of this shortfall. The remaining third will fall on other allies, such as the UK, Norway, Canada and Japan.

According to an internal document I've seen, the 2027 gap could be reduced by up to €13 billion if all NATO allies deliver on their pledges made at the July summit. The proposal might offer some ways to maximise (or incentivise) external contributions.

**Count them out**: The joint debt of the €45 billion loan will not be so "joint" after all. Four member states – Slovakia, Hungary, the Czech Republic and Bulgaria – have all indicated they will opt out. Slovak Prime Minister** Robert Fico**seized on the resumption of the peace talks to say no to "war loans" and yes to peace.** Count her out**:** Marine Le Pen**has confirmed that, should she become president of France next year, she would end financial support for Ukraine due to France's high public debt. Le Pen said she would still honour the existing commitments and carry "active diplomacy", our Paris correspondent** Estelle Nilsson-Julien** reports.

Meanwhile, the French government has rejected as "absurd" Moscow's claims that French troops had been deployed to the battlefield in Ukraine. Deputy Defence Minister **Alice Rufo** said the soldiers were there for "training and equipment maintenance".**What now?** The €45 billion support loan comes as Europe, Ukraine and the US prepare for multilateral talks with Russia in the peace process. Moscow, however, continues to procrastinate and regurgitate old grievances to divert attention from what matters.

"If anybody's going to talk about European security order, Europe has to be there," Norway's Foreign Minister **Espen Barth Eide** told my colleague**Angela Skujins**.

"We're not happy about this language about architecture. We believe that we have European security architecture. There was nothing wrong with it until Russia violated it."

Brussels is already preparing a new package of economic sanctions to tighten the screws on Russia. Don't rule out the possibility that Moscow uses the loan and the sanctions as a convenient excuse to refuse the multilateral format.

## How Ukrainian forces struck Putin's prized Crimean bridge

Russian President **Vladimir Putin** may be hoping for his own Yalta moment alongside**Donald Trump** and**Xi Jinping** when the three leaders meet in China on Thursday. But the historical reference carries a particular irony.

Ukraine has dealt a major blow to the Crimean bridge, a symbol of Moscow's occupation of the very peninsula where the original Yalta Conference took place in 1945.

Kyiv's forces struck the heavily defended bridge, partially destroying the crossing in an unprecedented seven-stage operation involving eleven different weapons systems.

Inaugurated by Putin in 2018, the bridge has long been both a vital transport link for Russian forces and one of the Kremlin's most prized symbols of its control over Crimea.

The ingenious military strike complicates Putin's efforts to portray Russia as winning the war ahead of his sought-after meeting with Trump and Xi.

"There will be more of our operations – operations in response to Russian aggression," Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said.
