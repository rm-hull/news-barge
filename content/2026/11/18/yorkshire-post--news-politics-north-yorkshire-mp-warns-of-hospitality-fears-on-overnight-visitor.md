# ‘Really frightened’: North Yorkshire MP warns of hospitality fears on overnight visitor levy

## Labour ministers have been accused of bringing in a “tax on working people” with their plans for an overnight visitor levy, which will leave North Yorkshire hospitality businesses “on their knees”.

The Visitor Levy Bill would enable the collection of nightly fees from overnight stays to be reinvested locally in high streets, public spaces and tourist attractions.

Such taxes have been common in cities across the continent for years, with Barcelona raising almost £125m every year.

York and North Yorkshire Mayor David Skaith and West Yorkshire’s Tracy Brabin have both stated they will introduce some sort of levy in their regions, despite the protests of some tourism and hospitality businesses.

Communities Secretary Angela Rayner defended the policy, saying the money which mayors decide to charge could be used for “more investment in local priorities, more new transport infrastructure and more regeneration”.

She told the Commons that because visitor levies would be set as a percentage of the cost of accommodation “low-cost holidays will stay low”.

The Bill was approved at second reading by 296 votes to 96, majority 200.

Skipton and Ripon MP Sir Julian Smith voiced his disapproval, saying: “North Yorkshire hospitality, like so many areas of the UK hospitality sector, is only just recovering from covid.

“It has been on its knees and it is now being battered by high energy costs, high wage costs, high rates costs, inflation pressure impacting spending, and a welter of employment legislation that is about to come down the track.

“The UK hospitality sector is worried about the Government’s plans, but those in North Yorkshire hospitality are really frightened by the additional layer of tax and bureaucracy at a time when they and their businesses are only just surviving.

“The Tourism Alliance has talked about the seasonal aspect for rural and coastal destinations that compete on price and are more exposed.

“As we have heard, rural destinations depend much more on domestic and price-sensitive visitors.

“Communicating the fragility of the current situation in rural hospitality is key, because almost all the hospitality businesses I work with are not making a profit.

“They are losing money, obviously, but one more hammer blow could tip them over, and that means less revenue and fewer jobs.”
