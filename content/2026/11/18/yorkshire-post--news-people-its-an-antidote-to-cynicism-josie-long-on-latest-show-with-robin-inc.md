# 'It's an antidote to cynicism': Josie Long on latest show with Robin Ince

## ​Two decades after they first began making things together, comedians Robin Ince and Josie Long are a creative partnership on tour. Laura Reid reports.

Josie Long was a teenager starting out in the industry when she first met Robin Ince at a gig on the comedy circuit. "He had a leather jacket and I remember thinking ‘wow that’s a cool guy’,” she laughs. “I also remember him being very friendly and kind, which was good, because when I was younger, it was quite tricky to start on the circuit. Some people were quite intimidating.”

Ince has a slightly different memory of their first encounter – of Long with a temporary tattoo that was essentially a piece of A4 paper sellotaped to her stomach.

They properly became friends a few years later, when then 21-year-old Long returned in earnest to stand-up after a hiatus to study for an English degree. It wasn’t long before their first collaboration – and now 20 years has passed and they’re on tour together celebrating two decades of friendship, creativity and comedy.

![Comedian Josie Long. Photo: Stephanie Gibson](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOjBkN2EzM2U0LTFmMGEtNDVhNi05MWEyLTI4YmMwMjM1NzliYzplZTBhNDk5MC0wNDIxLTQ2MDMtYjJmNy01YTE0MmY1YzMyZDQ=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)


Their show, 20 Years of Shambles, is in Yorkshire this week and is billed as an evening of stand-up, poetry, conversation, “and whatever else happens when two endlessly curious minds share a stage”. “We love bouncing off each other and seeing how excitable and chaotic our show can be,” says 44-year-old Long. “Robin is very bookish and thoughtful and interested in the world and how it works. I’ve recently got into writing poetry and I think I’m quite friendly and silly and earnest.

“It’s quite an antidote to cynicism, I think, as a show. It’s celebrating having known each other so long, worked together so much and also sharing what we’re interested in and our enthusiasm for the world. It’s quite a sweet and silly show and one that’s full of friendship and our excitement about performing and making stuff together.”

For Long, that thrill of entertaining began at an early age, a child obsessed with TV comedy, who loved any opportunity to show off. At 14, her mum booked her onto a comedy workshop at an arts centre in the suburbs of South East London, where, as the only young person, the adult learners took her under their wing. They helped her book her first gigs in the capital.

“I loved it,” she reflects. “I think I had less fear then than I did when I was older. It was so exciting for me. I felt like a spy with a double life, gigging in the evening then at school the next day. At that time, the comedy circuit was so much smaller and it really did feel like ‘oh wow I’m the only one doing this’. I knew I wanted to do it from the second I started. I was like I am desperate to do this for my life.”

![Comedian, author and broadcaster Robin Ince. Photo: Robin Clewley](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOmNjMWM2Nzk0LTJhNDYtNDRlZi04MGFjLWU2ZTU4MjE1NDM4NDoyMzBiMTQ1MS1kMmJjLTQzMmItODM1MS0wOGU1MTEwNzZhYmQ=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)


Straight from university, she was back out gigging, and in 2005, supported Stewart Lee on tour, whilst also contributing sketches and one-liners to BBC Radio. It was in the BBC offices that she struck up a proper friendship with Ince, now 57, who was writing and performing at the time.

In 2005, he launched his Book Club comedy night in London, where acts were encouraged to perform new and experimental material and Ince would read aloud and humorously review extracts from second-hand books. Long became part of that space, one that would go on to become an important creative home for a generation of performers. “It changed my life — and a lot of other people's — by building a space for stranger, softer, nerdier comedians to make up delightful weird stuff,” she says. “It had a really wonderful atmosphere.”

Soon after the launch, Ince and Long made their first podcast together. For Ince, perhaps best known as the former co-host and co-creator of BBC Radio 4 series The Infinite Monkey Cage with Professor Brian Cox, the appeal of working with Long has always been the freedom to experiment. “Both of us carry a lot of different ideas in our heads and neither of us are aiming to win, or to go ‘I was best, I got most laughs’,” he says. “I really enjoy being in Josie's company both on and off stage.”

Twenty years of working together have produced plenty of memorable moments. Long recalls performing with Ince at Laugharne, where an unexpected hen party appeared among their usual cardigan-clad audience and Ince ended up doing a strip show while singing The Smiths and ruining his favourite shirt. She also remembers performing alongside The Cure at the Apollo as part of Ince’s Nine Lessons shows — an experience she describes as one of the privileges of her life.

“Robin is always, always innovating and it's so exciting to be around,” Long says. “Intellectually he is relentless and I am very inspired by him. And he is incredibly generous, always creating shows and fun gigs for friends to participate in.”

“I’m really proud of the stuff we’ve done and it’s really great to have long term friends like Robin in comedy," she continues. “I am proud of the contribution to culture that we have both tried to make. You don’t have that much control over how many people come to see you or what you do and don’t get in a comedy career but what I love is that you do get to choose what you make and who you make it with and I’m so glad about my choices.”

The pair hope their audience feel buoyed by their energy and enthusiasm. “I hope they will take away the excitement of being creative, and leave thinking ‘Yeah, let’s just make stuff!,” Ince says. “We aim to share a real sense of joy and camaraderie, not merely on stage, but I think the people that we play to, they know we're all in it together. There is an activist bent to what we do and I hope people feel encouraged to keep speaking up and I hope they know that they're part of something. Sometimes people do turn up to a show and they're feeling down, looking at the oppression of the world and they go ‘oh look at those two weirdos - they're alright!’”
