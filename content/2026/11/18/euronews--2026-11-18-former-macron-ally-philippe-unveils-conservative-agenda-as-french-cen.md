## Socialist, centrist and conservative candidates have been struggling to gain ground ahead of France's presidential elections, amid a race dominated by the hard left and far-right.

Former Macron ally and presidential hopeful Édouard Philippe unveiled his key policy proposals on Wednesday, setting out a conservative agenda on immigration, public order and criminal justice.

The former prime minister from 2017 to 2020 backs halving immigration to pre-2010 levels, tightening asylum rules, reducing France’s public debt and raising the retirement age to 65 years old.

France's government is struggling to push its 2027 budget proposal, which includes a string of austerity measures, through a divided parliament.

On foreign policy, he has called for stronger European defence capabilities and renewed support for Ukraine.

He has also accused the far-right National Rally party's Marine Le Pen and the hard-left France Unbowed party's Jean-Luc Mélenchon of cultivating an “unhealthy fascination” with Russian President Vladimir Putin.

## Centrists and conservatives sliding in polls

Le Pen and Mélenchon are currently dominating the race, respectively polling around 32–36% and 17-19.5%, according to a survey published by Ifop-Fiducial on 18 November.

Meanwhile, Philippe's popularity has been sliding, hovering around 11%. Former prime minister Gabriel Attal and conservative candidate Bruno Retailleau have also dropped in the polls.

The trend points to a broader dynamic, as left-wing, centrist and conservative candidates have been struggling to gain ground. Philippe has ruled out taking part in a primary to select a joint candidate for the centre-right, despite some contenders seeing it as a way to consolidate support.

Instead, he has pledged to unite the two camps behind his own candidacy, stating, "I believe that if the right and the centre are not united, they have no chance of prevailing in the presidential election and no chance of providing a sufficiently strong political base to govern afterwards."

Former conservative prime minister Dominique de Villepin has accused Philippe of "chasing after the National Rally", criticising his rhetoric on immigration.

The remarks followed Philippe's recent claim that young people from immigrant backgrounds were "overrepresented" during the violence and looting, which overshadowed peaceful protests on 17 October.

He described this as evidence of decades of "undeniable" integration failures.
