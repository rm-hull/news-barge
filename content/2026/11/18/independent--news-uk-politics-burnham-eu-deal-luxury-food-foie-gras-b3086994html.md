The UK will have to reportedly ditch its pledged ban on importing foie gras once it signs a food deal with the EU.

Andy Burnham is due to sign a series of deals with the EU on 20 November in Brussels, focussed on bringing down post-Brexit trade barriers on food and farming as well as working closely on emissions trading, making it easier for young people from the UK to work, live and study in Europe and vice versa.

As reported by the *Financial Times*, foie gras will be affected - putting Labour’s promise to ban the import of the luxury product - a paté made from the livers of force-fed ducks or geese - at risk.

The ban was not part of the party’s 2024 manifesto but the then-shadow environment secretary Steve Reed said before the election that a Labour government would “ban the commercial import of foie gras, where ducks and geese are aggressively force-fed”.

The production of foie gras has been banned in the UK since 2007 and that ban would remain in place under the new deal, sources told the *FT*.

A government spokesperson told *The Independent*: “We are negotiating a food and drink deal that will slash red tape and costly paperwork, make it easier and cheaper to export seafood, boost the British economy by up to £5 billion a year, and back British businesses and jobs.

“We are working closely with businesses to ensure a smooth transition so they can make the most of the benefits this deal unlocks.”

Ahead of the summit, the prime minister repeated his pledge to stick to the 2024 Labour manifesto’s red lines – which rule out returning to the customs union, single market or free movement – before a general election and that the “British public will be in charge of whatever comes next”.

A “factsheet and mythbuster” published on Tuesday by the government also noted that “this explicitly rules out rejoining the EU”.

It also rejected claims that smoky flavours in crisps, sauces and soups would be banned and that British marmalade would need to be relabelled as “citrus marmalade” under the proposed food deal with the bloc.

Banning single-serving sachets of ketchup or other condiments is also not part of the deal but it does mean that food products will largely no longer need to display “Not for EU” labelling, it said.

On Tuesday, Mr Burnham said he would set out “later this year” whether “there are options to further the relationship with the EU,” amid accusations he was kicking into the long grass plans to do so.

He promised in his Labour conference speech in September that he would lay out different options to the country around the time of the UK-EU summit.
