Wetherspoon is introducing a change across its UK pubs after a successful trial at 88 venues.

The pub chain is changing how it maintains urinals, using a biological treatment called URIZAP, which is designed to tackle the build-up that can cause blockages and smells in men's toilets.

The change covers its pubs in the UK and Ireland, and the company told the Mirror that the rollout was completed around July.

It is a change to washroom maintenance rather than a new rule that customers must follow.

The treatment comes from Thrive, a Lancashire-based company which began in a Lancaster University laboratory in 2019, according to Wetherspoon's magazine.

Its scientists developed the product as an alternative to chemical cleaners used to deal with uric scale and blockages in urinals.

The formulation works by digesting uric acid build-up and can be used in all types of urinals.

Thrive says the treatment clears urinals and pipes at source instead of simply masking odours.

Wetherspoon says the product is suited to busy washrooms and reduces the need for strong-smelling chemical cleaners traditionally used in such venues.

In its winter and spring 2026/27 magazine, the chain said pubs using the treatment had reported a marked reduction in plumbing maintenance visits.

The Mirror reported that staff at the 88 trial pubs noted improved odours, fewer maintenance issues and the removal of scale build-up from urinal pipework.

Wetherspoon says the aim is to deal with problems before they arise, rather than waiting until a blockage needs attention.

That matters during busy periods, when a urinal problem can pull employees away from serving customers or other work, according to the company.

The chain says the treatment's continuous action can free staff to concentrate on other tasks and help avoid urinals being shut off at busy times.

Wetherspoon also links the change to its sustainability ambitions, saying reduced reliance on chemical-based cleaners means fewer single-use plastic containers and lower volumes of chemicals entering wastewater.

The company says the treatment allows it to make use of low-flush systems already installed in its washrooms to reduce water consumption.

It has not given figures in the magazine for the amount of water, plastic or chemicals saved by the switch.

Wetherspoon said: "Urinal issues generally demand attention at the worst-possible moments, pulling staff away from serving customers or other duties to deal with issues. URIZAP's continuous action frees staff to focus on other tasks."

Vince Graham, chief executive of Thrive, said: "Wetherspoon leads the way on washroom standards. We're proud that one of Britain's best-known hospitality businesses has chosen URIZAP to take that standard even further, improving facilities for customers and staff."

For customers, the intended result is fewer odours and fewer occasions when urinals need to be taken out of use for maintenance.
