**David Lammy has urged Andy Burnham to take Labour into the next general election with a pledge to rejoin the European Union.**

The former deputy prime minister argued only full membership of the bloc could deliver economic growth alongside a British voice on EU single market rules.

It comes after Burnham confirmed he would set out options to recast the UK's long-term relationship with Europe next month, in the wake of a summit this week to unveil a series of deals to "reset" relations.

The prime minister has held out the possibility he could ask to rejoin at some stage, whilst ruling out major changes before the next election.

Burnham will travel to the summit with the EU this Friday, where both sides hope to unveil new agreements on food, youth visas and re-linking British industry to the EU's carbon trading regime.

The prime minister inherited negotiations on those deals from his predecessor Sir Keir Starmer, who kicked off talks on the agreements last year.

But he has also started a broader discussion over the UK's long-term relationship with Europe, which has been welcomed by some within his own party but seen him accused by critics of betraying the result of the 2016 decision to leave.

Speaking on BBC Radio 4's Today programme, Lammy, who was deputy PM under Sir Keir, said Burnham had been right to "begin a debate" about the options to revise the future relationship.

He said his preference would be for Labour to advocate rejoining at the next election, then negotiating a deal to return to the EU and giving the public a vote on whether to accept it.

Returning to the bloc's customs union, in which EU member states charge the same taxes on imported goods, would not deliver "enough" economic growth, he said.

He added that rejoining Europe's single market from the outside was "not a runner" as it would mean applying swathes of EU law without a say on the content.

Lammy, who is also a former foreign secretary, argued the UK would be able to rejoin the EU whilst staying outside the passport-free Schengen travel zone and without committing to adopt the euro.

But he appeared to concede that Britain's EU budget rebate, first negotiated in the 1980s, could become less financially generous, adding that a "different formula" for financial contributions would be "up for negotiation".

He also revealed he had made an unsuccessful attempt to persuade Sir Keir to "tear up" his red lines on Europe towards the end of his two-year premiership, but the former prime minister had encountered resistance from within his cabinet.

Labour's 2024 election manifesto committed the party to "make Brexit work" whilst ruling out rejoining either its customs union or single market.

Any move away from those red lines by Burnham would set up a huge dividing line with the Conservatives and Reform UK at the next election.

But Lammy argued the prime minister's status as a former mayor from the north of England meant he was "uniquely placed" to convince the public of the need to reverse the decision to leave.
