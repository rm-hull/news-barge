NHS hospital and ambulance trusts in England have recorded over 3,000 cases of staff improperly accessing patient information in recent years, new figures show.

The data comes amid high-profile privacy breaches by health service workers, including dozens who viewed the medical records of victims of attacks in Southport and Nottingham.

Patient advocates warned that such incidents "erode trust" in the organisations involved and the wider NHS, with a "lack of transparency" deepening the overall damage.

NHS England chief executive Sir Jim Mackey described the figures as “extremely worrying” and said the health service “will not tolerate this behaviour”.

The findings are based on Freedom of Information requests submitted by the *Press Association* to 143 acute hospital and ambulance trusts in England.

Some 79 trusts disclosed precise figures, detailing 3,029 incidents of staff inappropriately accessing files between 2021/22 and 2025/26 inclusive.

The actual total will be higher, as other trusts cited "fewer than five" annual cases to avoid the risk of identifying individuals.

Trusts must report breaches posing a risk to patient rights to data protection regulator the Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO) within 72 hours.

The ICO revealed last week that it received over 300 reports this year of staff inspecting records without good reason, checking details of acquaintances, or sharing information.

The regulator’s chief executive Paul Arnold wrote to hundreds of health leaders across the UK calling for an end to such breaches.

Mr Arnold said: “When we seek medical care, we trust the NHS with some of our most sensitive information and we need to be confident that only people with a legitimate reason will ever access our records.

“Looking at medical records out of curiosity is unacceptable and jeopardises the vital trust people place in the health service.

“These figures are deeply concerning and I know how much distress this type of behaviour causes patients and families.

“Recent incidents have shown that the culture and behaviours needed to protect patients have not been good enough, and that needs to change. Organisations need effective access controls, monitoring, training and consistent action when things go wrong.

“Last week I wrote to health leaders across the UK asking for assurance on this. Where safeguards don’t work in practice, we expect organisations to put that right.”

Recent examples of high-profile incidents include dozens of health workers who were found to have looked at the records of victims of the 2024 Southport attack, which left three girls dead and 10 others injured.

![Dozens of health workers were found to have looked at the records of victims of the 2024 Southport attack](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/07/24/13/30/southport-victims-split.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

At the time, Leanne Lucas, who was the instructor at the Taylor Swift-themed dance event and is one of the three adult survivors, said she was “devastated and horrified”.

In May, the Health Service Journal (HSJ) reported that staff at another hospital in the city, run by University Hospitals of Liverpool Group, had inappropriately viewed survivors’ files.

The group, which runs Aintree Hospital in Liverpool, said almost 50 of its staff had inappropriately accessed the records.

A separate investigation is also being held at North West Ambulance Service Trust in relation to staff looking up the victims of the attack without the right to do so.

Liverpool Heart and Chest Hospital also said it was “carrying out an investigation into two potential instances of inappropriate access connected to the Southport incident”.

Elsewhere, 11 staff were dismissed and a further 14 had action taken against them at Nottingham University Hospitals Trust after they accessed the medical records of students Barnaby Webber and Grace O’Malley-Kumar, plus grandfather Ian Coates, who were stabbed to death by Valdo Calocane in 2023.

The records of a three-year-old boy hurt after falling into a crocodile pit in Cambridgeshire in June were viewed by around 40 hospital staff, with Cambridge University Hospitals (CUH) has referriing itself to the Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO).

While two workers at Yorkshire Ambulance Service are believed to have accessed or attempted to access the records of William Chapman after he was mauled to death by a tiger at Yorkshire Wildlife Park.

In September, Sir Jim said he expected any workers suspected of accessing patient records inappropriately to be suspended immediately and have their access to online systems cut off.

![William Chapman was mauled to death when he entered the tiger enclosure at Yorkshire Wildlife Park](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/16/11/534449fbf3514bb593a420471744efc1Y29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzkyMjMzNzE1-2.86175286.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Days after launching the crackdown, 10 staff at East Suffolk and North Essex NHS Foundation Trust were suspended or removed from duty after a possible data breach of the medical records of three-year-old Noah Woods, who was found dead in a lake a day after going missing in Brantham, Suffolk.

Sir Jim said: “These figures are extremely worrying and it’s completely unacceptable that any staff would risk betraying patient confidentiality in this way and causing harm to families in our care.

“The NHS will not tolerate this behaviour – whether being done out of curiosity or to see their own notes or results, accessing a health record without a legitimate professional reason is never acceptable and is unlawful.

“It is right that trusts are now proactively identifying and investigating this abhorrent behaviour – and our new guidance makes it crystal clear that staff face immediate suspension, as well as dismissal, if they are found to have accessed confidential patient information without an authorised reason.”

Rachel Power, chief executive at the Patients Association, told the Press Association: “Patients share some of their most sensitive and personal information with the NHS, and they should be able to trust it will only be accessed when there is a legitimate reason to do so.

“We are concerned by recent media reports alleging that staff at multiple trusts accessed the records of patients.

“Cases like these erode trust, not only in the trust involved but in the wider NHS.

“Lack of transparency deepens that damage, especially in the context of previous data breaches and leaks.

“Patients need confidence that strong safeguards are in place, that inappropriate access will be detected and acted on without delay, and that they will be the first to know, not the last, when their confidentiality has been breached.”

Of those trusts that responded to the Freedom of Information request with precise data, University Hospitals of Morecambe Bay had the highest number of incidents across the period 2021/22 to 2025/26, at 242.

This was followed by West Suffolk with 213 and Northumbria Healthcare with 195.

Anna Telfer, chief nursing officer at Morecambe Bay, said the trust has a “zero-tolerance approach” to breaches.

A Northumbria Healthcare spokesperson said that in 2024, it introduced a system to proactively monitor access each month which has reduced the number of incidents.

Matthew Hopkins, acute and ambulance network director at the NHS Alliance, said: “NHS staff work hard every day to protect confidential patient information and any misuse of records is a serious breach of trust.

“Strong data governance is essential as digital technology becomes increasingly central to patient care. Any requirements around access controls, audit trails and monitoring must be achievable within current systems.

“Maintaining public confidence in the use of patient data is crucial.”
