A councillor who contemplated quitting after a row with Scotland’s ethics watchdog over what he saw as a restriction on his freedom of expression has had a change of heart and will contest next year’s election.

Andrew Wood, who represents Mid and Upper Nithsdale, said support from constituents had persuaded him to remain in the fight after his one-month suspension.

The ethical standards regulator acknowledged his suspension restricted his freedom of speech rights, but insisted the move was necessary to protect the rights of others and public confidence in local government.

The Standards Commission for Scotland made the admission when setting out why it suspended Andrew Wood, an independent member of Dumfries and Galloway Council, for one month over his public criticism of the authority’s chief executive.

Mr Wood said: "I am a fighter and will not accept the present situation where councillors are losing control to the officers."

The independent councillor had been considering standing down after the Standards Commission for Scotland found that his public criticism of Dumfries and Galloway Council chief executive Dawn Roberts breached the councillors’ code of conduct.

The Commission suspended him for a month after a hearing in September, following comments he made about Mrs Roberts and her role in the council’s purchase of 12 buses for £2.66 million after Stagecoach withdrew services from the region.

The panel cleared him of bullying and harassment, but found that his comments had been disrespectful and discourteous. The decision has raised questions about the boundary between councillors’ right to speak out on matters of public concern and the rules governing their conduct towards council employees.

In its decision, the commission acknowledged that the sanction was “a restriction on his enhanced rights to freedom of expression”, but said it was justified and proportionate to protect the rights and reputations of others, working relationships and public confidence in the council and its decisions.

Mr Wood said he had considered whether he would seek re-election if Mrs Roberts remained in post. He has now decided to stand again, saying the support he has received has changed his mind.

“After a lot of soul searching, I have decided to stand again, with having heard from quite a few of my constituents along with the fabulous support from your many followers who feel it would be letting the chief executive get away with it, ” he said.

He added: “I will therefore be standing as an independent councillor and urging as many new people to come forward as independent councillors.”

Mr Wood is the latest to speak out about restrictions of freedom of speech for Scotland's elected representatives as the number of freedom of speech cases involving councillors investigated by Scotland’s ethical standards watchdog has more than doubled in a decade.

Amid growing disquiet over a system that is creating a "climate of fear", The Herald revealed that there were 244 distinct councillor cases investigated in 2024/25, compared with 111 ten years earlier, sparking concerns over the curbing of the human rights of elected representatives in Scotland who are fighting for their local communities.

Those resulted in the Standards Commission for Scotland holding 31 public hearings across the last two reporting years, compared with 21 across 2015/16 and 2016/17.

The political ethics body is already facing a formal investigation by the nation's records regulator over the destruction of records from dozens of 'freedom of speech' disciplinary cases.

National Records of Scotland has confirmed there are “sufficient grounds” to examine whether the Standards Commission for Scotland failed to comply with an agreed records management plan following the destruction of 24 case files in June after it changed its retention policy.

It comes as **Dumfries and Galloway has voiced its concern over how the commission is acting and has is demanding an independent review** of its operations.

The decision follows a **Court of Session ruling that the Standards Commission acted unlawfully by refusing to admit certain evidence in proceedings involving Glasgow councillor Fiona Higgins**. The Commission has appealed, but the case has fuelled debate about fairness, transparency and the ability of elected members to challenge decisions.

The Dumfries and Galloway dispute began after Mr Wood criticised Mrs Roberts in a newspaper column over the emergency purchase of buses. He argued that the chief executive had taken decisions that should have been subject to greater scrutiny by elected members.

Mrs Roberts told the hearing that she had acted quickly to avoid a “significant service failure” after Stagecoach’s withdrawal left the council needing to secure replacement services. She said Mr Wood’s comments had left her feeling “disappointed, frustrated and undermined”, and that they had affected her confidence in her role.

The Commission’s panel found that Mr Wood’s criticism had undermined an individual employee and breached rules on courtesy and respect, as well as the protocol governing relations between councillors and council staff. It did not find that his conduct amounted to bullying or harassment. Mr Wood has criticised the way complaints against councillors are handled, saying he felt at a disadvantage during the hearing because he did not have legal representation.

“I truly felt out on a limb with having to present my own case against the commissioners' legally trained representative, then the chief executive also had support of our council's governance office all of which are legally trained and not impartial along with the Board who had a clerk who I suspect was also legally trained, then myself who just says it as it is,” he said.

He added: “Make no mistake all councillors regardless of their party politics will be vulnerable to losing their case if not properly represented by proper legal representation which would be at an astronomical cost to most councillors.”

The Commission’s decision said Mr Wood’s concerns could have been raised through other routes, including privately and through the council’s procedures. But he disputed that, saying he had been responding to questions from constituents about the handling of the bus issue.

Mr Wood said constituents had urged him to put the facts into the public domain and that he had done so through the local press. He accepted that the panel had found a breach of the code, but said he believed the hearing had exposed problems in the way councillors could challenge senior officers.

“I had no problem going through the hearing because it highlighted that if you get caught up with a senior officer reporting you, then that senior officer has governance behind them, and will have the commissioner fighting on their behalf,” he said. “As a councillor, you are on your own and have no real chance of winning in these cases – unless you are a trained lawyer or can afford to have a legal expert representing you.”

Mr Wood said he wanted more people to stand as independents and called for a major overhaul of the council’s senior management.

“I would further like to see all senior staff fired and re-hired through a proper human resources committee so as to eliminate jobs for the boys and who you know within the council as this must stop and be replaced with people who are qualified to do the job,” he said.

“It would also free up monies to spend on those working the coalface chores that we are seriously lacking.”

The councillor has served for nearly 20 years and previously had spells with the SNP and Conservatives before becoming independent. He said his decision to stand again was driven by the response from constituents and his belief that councillors should be able to hold officials to account.

“The simple challenge I put to the people of Dumfries and Galloway is, if you want your voice heard and you further want councillors back in control of your council, please vote for independents and remove politics out of local government. Politics should be retained for parliaments as they are the ones that set the finances for our local authorities to deliver the services,” he said.

Mr Wood’s suspension began on October 12 and lasted 30 days.

The Standards Commission said: "Mr Wood was advised throughout the process that he was entitled to obtain legal representation, albeit this was not something the Standards Commission could provide. He was also provided access to the Standards Commission’s Guidance for Unrepresented Respondents.

"Additionally, the chair of the hearing panel confirmed to him, at the outset of the hearing, that Standards Commission Panels were accustomed to dealing with unrepresented councillors and would ensure that he had the opportunity to give evidence and make any arguments and points he wished.

"The Standards Commission is satisfied he was afforded that opportunity at the hearing, and in general that its hearings process is fair and affords parties, whether represented or not, a full opportunity to present their case.

With regard to the finding itself, the Standards Commission’s hearing panel was clear, in its published written decision, that it did not consider there was anything that would have prevented Mr Wood, in any way, from commenting publicly on, or even criticising the decision-making regarding the matter in question, without engaging in a public personal attack on a council officer’s performance, conduct and integrity.

"The hearing panel noted in its decision that it was satisfied in the circumstances that the finding of breach, and imposition of a sanction, was sufficient and proportionate and could not be said to have a negative or ‘chilling’ effect on Mr Wood’s ability to raise questions and concerns.

"Mr Wood was advised of his right of appeal in respect of the Standards Commission’s decision, as outlined at Section 22 of the Ethical Standards in Public Life etc. (Scotland) Act 2000."
