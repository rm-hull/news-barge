In late June, a video featuring two CDU politicians, Katharina Günther-Wünsch and Kai Wegner, circulated on the platform X. A user named John Conceison claimed that Berlin’s Senator for Education had blackmailed the Governing Mayor in order to acquire several properties. The same post alleged that Senator for Finance and Berlin CDU leader Stefan Evers had played “Whac-A-Mole” using live guinea pigs.

The post was part of a larger Russian disinformation campaign observed in connection with the state elections in Berlin, Saxony-Anhalt and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, as well as the local elections in Lower Saxony. The Center for Monitoring, Analysis and Strategy (Cemas) has summarised the attempts to exert influence on X and other platforms between June and September in a research paper obtained by the Berliner Morgenpost. The findings show both the limitations of these campaigns and why their long-term effects should not be underestimated.

### Günther-Wünsch and Kai Wegner Were Frequently Targeted

Cemas identified three campaigns, all of which were known from previous elections. Ahead of this year’s state elections, Cemas identified 196 fake videos on social media attributable to the disinformation campaign “Operation Overload”, which has been active since at least 2023. Most of the videos featured the logos of media organisations, while the campaign also circulated fake newspaper and magazine front pages, particularly those imitating the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung.

The campaign aimed to discredit candidates from the CDU, SPD, Greens, Left Party and FDP in the four federal states holding state or local elections in September. Saxony-Anhalt was by far the most frequently targeted (42.9 percent), followed by national politics (27.6 percent) and Berlin (17.4 percent). Günther-Wünsch and Wegner were among the politicians most frequently targeted, mentioned four and three times respectively. However, Friedrich Merz was a particular focus (49 percent), as was the CDU as a whole.

According to Cemas, audio deepfakes of politicians were sometimes used to spread false claims about electoral fraud, tensions between eastern and western Germany, and individual misconduct. One video purportedly from *Bild*, for example, claimed that Bettina Jarasch, leader of the Greens’ parliamentary group in Berlin, had abused her position to secure jobs for at least seven family members.

### Fake News: A Merz Lookalike and Shredded AfD Postal Ballots

Cemas also identified the Russian disinformation campaign “Doppelganger”. It is known for spreading fake news through deceptively realistic websites imitating major media outlets. The campaign‘s central narratives include dissatisfaction with the governing parties, the SPD and CDU/CSU, and portraying the AfD as a source of hope.

The CDU and AfD were also the focus of the “Storm-1516” campaign. In October, for example, a false report circulated claiming that Chancellor Friedrich Merz had suffered a stroke and was being replaced by a lookalike. Shortly before the Berlin election, those behind the disinformation campaign circulated a video supposedly showing postal ballots containing votes for the AfD being cut up in Berlin. “However, the election documents shown were missing polling cards, and the ballot boxes in the video were marked with a Berlin bear emblem, which is unusual,” Cemas writes in its analysis.

### False Claims Are Artificially Amplified

At first glance, the video appears to have been shared by genuine users around midday and in the afternoon. However, the video was shared particularly frequently over two consecutive minutes at around 1 am and again at 8 am. There was hardly any activity before or after these bursts. According to Cemas, “these patterns suggest additional coordinated, potentially inauthentic amplification of the posts”.

Cemas also believes that the reach of false claims spread through “Operation Overload” was artificially boosted by accounts specifically created for that purpose. Very few apparently genuine users engaged with the fake-news posts. “The monitoring also found hardly any evidence of the false claims being spread further by independent accounts or on other platforms,” the research paper states.

“Foreign influence attempts are not automatically effective,” the analysts explain. “However, Russia’s influence strategy is designed for the long term and has the potential to cause lasting damage to trust in electoral processes, democratic institutions and the media.”

### Training, Tools and Monitoring: Measures Against Disinformation

Cemas therefore recommends preparing for influence attempts at state level as well. It calls for training staff in state governments and parliaments, developing crisis and communication plans, and raising awareness across civil society. In Berlin, the “Gretchen AI” tool was launched this year, allowing users to check content for disinformation.

Cemas also calls for greater scrutiny of the financial flows behind these campaigns. Social media companies, meanwhile, have a responsibility to systematically combat networks of inauthentic accounts, identify previously inactive networks at an early stage and make it more difficult to establish new ones.

Incidentally, the account belonging to John Conceison, who spread the false claims mentioned at the beginning of this article, has since been suspended.
