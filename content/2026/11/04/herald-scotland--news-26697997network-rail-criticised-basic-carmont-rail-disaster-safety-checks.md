Basic safety checks could have prevented the Carmont rail disaster which killed three people, a fatal accident inquiry has found.

Sheriff Lesley Johnston ruled that Network Rail should have ensured a newly built drain was constructed properly and that the required safety documents were handed over.

The inquiry found that the drain was unable to cope with heavy rainfall because it had not been built according to its design, allowing debris to wash on to the track and derail the train.

But the determination also highlighted a warning sign during construction: water had flowed through a gully and caused slight erosion to the surface of the new drain before the work was complete. No action was taken.

The Rail Accident Investigation Branch described that as “a missed opportunity to recognise the effect of the bund on water flows” and a “possible causal factor” in the disaster.

ScotRail said that it had worked closely with infrastructure owners Network Rail to implement the recommendations made by the RAIB "to do everything possible to reduce the risk of something like this ever happening again".

The findings concern the derailment on August 12, 2020, when the 6.38am service from Aberdeen to Glasgow was travelling north towards Stonehaven at about 73mph.

![The crash at Stonehaven is the subject of a fatal accident inquiry](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/20349825.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The crash at Stonehaven is the subject of a fatal accident inquiry *(Image: RAIB/PA Wire)*

The train struck debris washed from a drain on to the railway line after a period of very heavy rainfall. The collision caused it to derail, killing passenger Christopher Stuchbury, 62, driver Brett McCullough, 45, and conductor Donald Dinnie, 58. Six other passengers were injured.

The inquiry found that now-liquidated Carillion Construction had been instructed by Network Rail to build the drain, using a design prepared by engineering firm Ove Arup and Partners.

But the drain was not built in accordance with that design and could not accommodate the volume of surface water during the intense rainfall on the day of the crash.

Sheriff Johnston found that Carillion should have built the drain as designed, including leaving out a raised earth feature known as a bund which was not part of the plans. The inquiry found that the bund was the significant cause of the drain washing out.

The sheriff also found that Carillion should have prepared drawings showing how the drain had actually been built and provided them to Network Rail.

Network Rail, in turn, should have followed its own standards by obtaining those drawings and a health and safety file when the work was completed.

The sheriff said the precautions could realistically have prevented the crash. She found that Carillion should have built the drain in accordance with the design and that Network Rail should have obtained the required records.

The inquiry found that Network Rail did not know the drain had been built differently from the design and therefore did not take steps to correct it.

The failure to ensure the drain was properly constructed and matched the design was found to be a defect in the way the work was managed, which contributed to the accident and the deaths.

The inquiry also recorded a series of other departures from the design. These included a catchpit being installed in the wrong position, an existing field pipe not being connected, and a section of the trench not being fully lined with the material specified.

![Three people died in the crash in 2020](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/20463003.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Three people died in the crash in 2020 *(Image: Jane Barlow/PA Wire)*

A proposed catchpit was not installed, while a section of pipe was fitted at an angle that left its perforations on the side rather than at the top. The inquiry found that these defects had not been discovered before the derailment.

The construction-stage warning came in December 2012, shortly after the drain had been completed but before fencing work was finished. A landowner photographed water flowing through a gully and slight erosion to the gravel surface of the drain.

He said he passed the evidence to Carillion or Network Rail, but the inquiry found no evidence confirming who received it or what action, if any, was taken. A further photograph in March 2013 showed that the flow had become established, but no remedial work was suggested.

Sheriff Johnston did not find that either company could reasonably have been expected to investigate the photograph, because there was insufficient evidence about whether it had been received. But the RAIB concluded that the lack of action was a missed opportunity to recognise how the bund was affecting water flows.

The sheriff found that no routine inspection had been carried out on the section of drain above catchpit 18 between March 2013 and the crash. The RAIB said it was likely that the upper section had never been inspected, adding that “the absence of proper inspection of a safety critical asset is of great concern”.

The drain had been part of work to address a known risk of landslips in the railway cutting. A landslip had blocked both lines in the area in August 2008, prompting Network Rail to instruct Carillion to design and build remedial work, including a new drainage system.

The new drain was intended to intercept water flowing over the surface and through the ground beside the railway. It replaced an older drain that was not working effectively.

The sheriff’s findings also recorded shortcomings in Network Rail’s arrangements for managing extreme weather. Route control staff in Scotland were not adequately trained in the national standard for managing extreme weather, including how to classify routes according to risk.

The National Rail Weather Service was not configured in the Scottish control room to provide alerts based on real-time weather data. Control room staff had also not been trained to use the service in response to real-time data to help make decisions.

The inquiry found that Network Rail’s competency assessments and audits had not identified the gaps in training.

At the time of the derailment, there was no basis for the control room to impose speed restrictions solely because of extreme rainfall, regardless of flooding levels or whether a location had been identified as being at risk.

The sheriff’s determination followed a detailed investigation by the Rail Accident Investigation Branch, which made 20 recommendations to the railway industry after the disaster.

Sheriff Johnston considered those recommendations comprehensive and wide-ranging, and found that most had been implemented, with the remaining work being actively progressed.

She concluded that the issues identified by the accident investigation and the fatal accident inquiry were either being addressed or had been addressed by the rail industry. She did not consider that further reasonable precautions or improvements had been identified that might realistically prevent similar deaths.

No recommendations were made in the determination.

Network Rail had already admitted a health and safety offence following the derailment. On September 7, 2023, it pleaded guilty at the High Court in Aberdeen to failing to ensure, so far as reasonably practicable, that railway workers and members of the public were protected from the risk of serious injury or death.

The company was fined £6.7 million.

The inquiry’s findings set out how the drain’s construction, the failure to provide records of the completed work and Network Rail’s oversight of the project combined to leave the railway exposed to the risk of the washout.

The sheriff found that the drain should have been built in accordance with the design and that the required drawings and safety file should have been handed over and obtained.

The derailment happened after the train had earlier been stopped by an emergency call following reports of a landslip. The signaller told the train to return to Stonehaven because it could not proceed south.

The train began moving again at about 9.28am. Around nine minutes later, as it travelled north towards Stonehaven, it struck the debris washed from the drain.

The inquiry determined that the three men died from multiple blunt force injuries sustained in the derailment.

David Ross, ScotRail chief operating officer, said: "Nobody at ScotRail will ever forget the accident at Carmont, and the tragic loss of Brett McCullough, Donald Dinnie, and Christopher Stuchbury. The families and loved ones of all those who have been affected continue to be in our thoughts and prayers.

"The safety of our customers and staff is our priority, and we only operate trains that meet the stringent safety requirements expected of our fleet.

“The Fatal Accident Inquiry has been another very important step in the process to ensure that safety lessons are learnt, and any necessary changes are made to improve safety across the railway. Along with the wider rail industry, we’ll now take time to consider the sheriff’s detailed determination, including any findings and recommendations relevant to ScotRail."  
  
Liam Sumpter, Network Rail Scotland managing director, said: “The tragedy at Carmont was a terrible day for our railway and our thoughts remain with the families and loved ones of Brett McCullough, Donald Dinnie and Christopher Stuchbury, along with all those affected by the derailment.

“Since August 2020 we have been working hard to make our railway safer for our passengers and colleagues, to deliver on the RAIB recommendations and to fully support the FAI process to reach its conclusion. We have made significant changes to how we manage the risk of severe weather since the accident and to increase the resilience of Scotland’s railway.”
