Scotland’s top prosecutor is facing a mounting criminal justice crisis as the number of serious cases entering the High Court is expected to more than double, while the backlog of outstanding trials has almost trebled since before the pandemic.

High Court indictments are projected to exceed 2,000 this year, compared with 935 in 2023/24, according to the Scottish Courts and Tribunals Service. At the end of March 2026, 1,002 High Court trials were outstanding, up from 390 before the pandemic.

The Faculty of Advocates has warned that the growing volume of criminal cases raises questions about whether one person can manage the workload of Scotland’s prosecution service.

The scale of the increase is reflected in the number of advocates handling High Court prosecutions, which has risen from around 14 in 1998 to about 80 today.

In a briefing seen by ministers, the Faculty said: “The volume of criminal cases being prosecuted in Scotland has increased vastly since devolution and it continues to increase.”

It warned: “There comes a point when the question has to be asked whether any one person, no matter how skilled and diligent, can handle the workload.”

The Faculty said there may be a case for allowing the Lord Advocate to focus solely on prosecutions, adding: “There may, on that basis, be a case for saying that the Lord Advocate should be afforded the opportunity to concentrate exclusively on criminal prosecutions.”

The Lord Advocate Ruth Charteris heads the Crown Office and Procurator Fiscal Service and is responsible for Scotland’s prosecution system. The office also carries ministerial responsibilities and acts as the Scottish Government’s senior legal adviser.

The Faculty said concentrating responsibilities in one office-holder had administrative advantages and saved public money. But it questioned whether the growing demands of the job could continue to be managed by one person.

It said legal practice was becoming “ever more complex”, with regulatory regimes and the requirements of the law “constantly expanding”.

The Faculty raised the possibility of dividing the responsibilities, with the Lord Advocate heading the prosecution service and the Solicitor General becoming the Government’s legal adviser. But it warned that such a move would raise difficult questions about where other

functions should sit, including responsibility for investigating deaths and conducting Fatal Accident Inquiries.

The Faculty also said separating the roles would not eliminate every potential conflict. It pointed to the Lord Advocate’s responsibility for assessing the legal competence of proposed legislation, which can involve challenging a bill promoted by the Government of which the office-holder is a member.

The Faculty said the current system was “unique” but had worked for centuries and should not be changed without good reason.

The role has come under scrutiny because the Lord Advocate is responsible for deciding whether criminal cases should be prosecuted, while also advising the Government on legal matters and serving as a minister. Critics argue that the arrangement risks creating a perception of a conflict of interest, particularly when investigations involve the Government or its political allies.

The controversy intensified during Operation Branchform, the investigation into the SNP’s finances, which resulted in the prosecution of former party chief executive Peter Murrell. The Lord Advocate had no role in decision-making on the investigation, with the case handled by professional prosecutors and decisions taken by independent Crown Counsel. Questions were later raised about her communications with the First Minister.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/20960694.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Former SNP chief executive Peter Murrell *(Image: Derek McArthur)*Dorothy Bain was appointed Lord Advocate in June 2021 after she was nominated for the post by former first minister**Nicola Sturgeon**.

Her resignation earlier this year came as she was criticised by opposition MSPs after it emerged she informed the First Minister John Swinney of the charges against Mr Murrell around 10 months before they were made public.

The Faculty of Advocates has defended the independence of the prosecution system, but acknowledged that the dual role can create an appearance of bias, even where no actual bias exists.

And they say a debate over the Lord Advocate’s responsibilities should not be driven by the immediate political controversy, warning that changes to the office could have lasting consequences for the justice system.

It said the role had undergone significant reforms since devolution, including the removal of the Lord Advocate’s power to appoint judges and the introduction of a right for victims to seek a review of decisions not to prosecute.

But it warned that any further reform needed to be carefully considered, rather than being driven by the political row surrounding Operation Branchform.

The Faculty evidence warned that the controversy should not dictate decisions about the office’s future and said that the constitutional questions had “far reaching ramifications” and should be considered “in an apolitical manner free from the passing distractions of any current controversy”.

The Faculty said the independence of prosecution decisions was protected by the Scotland Act 1998, which requires the Lord Advocate to act independently of any other person when exercising prosecution functions.

It acknowledged that the Lord Advocate’s combination of prosecutorial and ministerial duties could create concerns about the appearance of bias. But it said the evidence did not establish that the independence of the prosecution system had been breached.

And the Faculty said the Lord Advocate and Solicitor General for Scotland provide a safeguard because one can take over if the other has a conflict. If both are conflicted in a criminal case, it said, the matter can be handled independently by Crown Counsel.

But its warning about workload was clear, that the volume and complexity of criminal cases have grown, and the it believes the demands on the Lord Advocate’s office now warrant serious consideration of whether the role should be changed.

According to an Audit Scotland examination, the number of outstanding scheduled trials across Scotland’s criminal courts had fallen to 13,268 by the end of 2025/26, around a third of the peak in 2022.

But the number of outstanding High Court trials had risen to 1,002, almost three times the pre-pandemic level, with the increase in serious and resource-intensive cases putting pressure on the wider justice system.

The Scottish Courts and Tribunals Service has warned that the average wait for the court stage of a High Court case could rise to 135 weeks by 2028/29, adding to the time victims, witnesses and accused people wait for a resolution.  
Neil Gray, Cabinet Secretary for Justice said: "Change across our justice system is being taken forward to respond to increasing demand and deliver modern, user-centred digital services. This includes expanding digital evidence-sharing capability and enabling police and expert witnesses to give evidence remotely. We are also providing targeted investment to support a more efficient, trauma-informed justice system. We will invest £11.6 billion across justice from 2026-27 to 2028-29 to support frontline services, safer communities and victims and witnesses.”
