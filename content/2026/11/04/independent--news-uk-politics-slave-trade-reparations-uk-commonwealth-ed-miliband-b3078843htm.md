Ed Miliband has insisted British taxpayers will not pay a penny more as a result of Prime Minister Andy Burnham's “new approach” to the Commonwealth.

The Foreign Secretary accused previous governments of having had a tendency to “sweep under the carpet” the issue of slavery but ruled out extra financial contributions, as well as the prospect of reparations, for Britain’s historical role.

Speaking to BBC Newscast, Mr Miliband criticised what he described as a “sorry, not sorry” from Sir Tony Blair, who in 2006 expressed “deep sorrow and regret” for the UK’s involvement in the slave trade but stopped short of an official apology.

The Prime Minister has left the door open to calls for a formal apology on behalf of the state as he pursues a “structured dialogue” about the issue with Commonwealth nations. Mr Miliband said that process was likely to conclude within a year.

Asked whether the Government might reach the point of a formal apology by the time it hosts the G20 leaders' summit in Manchester next November, he said: “Broadly speaking, yes.”

Speaking to broadcasters on Monday, Mr Burnham also refused to be drawn on whether the change in position could mean higher costs for taxpayers.

![Britain's Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/04/07/2302164718..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

But asked whether the new approach could end up costing UK taxpayers a penny more, Mr Miliband said: "No.

"I mean, not… no. And that's – for us, that's never been what it's about. And that's why it's been important that you know, and I did have this conversation with our partners in advance. I said, ‘look, we are going to say we are willing to say that we’re open to an apology, but we’re not going to shift our position on reparations.’

“It’s not for us. It’s not the right answer. It’s not practical. It’s not feasible for us, but there are things we can work on co-operatively.”

He added: "And you know there's been a lot of acceptance of that, and I mean people have been honestly a bit bruised by the past, what happened in the past, the sense that we just really just wanted to sweep this issue under the carpet and not confront it.

“My sense is there’s a bit of a sigh of relief that OK well at least you know at least there’s a sense of we’ve got a shared interest now in moving the Commonwealth on.”

Mr Miliband said Sir Tony “did sort of do a kind of sorry not sorry situation”, adding: “He sort of, yeah, he kind of expressed sorrow, but and then said sorry, and then it was walked back by No 10.”

A new poll by YouGov has found Britons support a slavery apology but oppose reparations.

It found 52 per cent were in favour of an official apology, with 37 per cent against.

However, 65 per cent are opposed to paying reparations to affected countries, with only 21 per cent in favour.

The Prime Minister has used the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting (Chogm) to set out plans for a “debt reset” aimed at helping poorer countries struggling with repayments. He has, however, ruled out offering reparations for the harm caused by the transatlantic slave trade.

Meeting counterparts from across the group in St John's, the capital of Antigua, this week, he argued that work to support member states amid rising global interest rate costs must include non-G7 donors and the private sector.

![Prime Minister Andy Burnham speaks to the media on the sidelines of the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting, at the American University of Antigua in Osbourn, Antigua](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/04/08/01M6J2EV3S9BQ76SQ026Y6377X.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The UK will also allocate £40 million over three years from within existing budgets to speed up critical infrastructure investment including green energy in the Caribbean.

Calls for an apology and reparations have grown in recent years.

St Lucia's Prime Minister Philip J Pierre, chairman of the 15-member Caribbean Community (Caricom), reportedly said the economic and political bloc was “ready to engage” in the “structured dialogue” Mr Burnham had proposed.

The Prime Minister said the UK wanted to “work closely with you to make sure we get that right” as he met the president of Ghana for one-to-one talks on the sidelines of the summit later on Tuesday.

Exchanging warm words at the top of the meeting, John Dramani Mahama in turn said Britain had shown a “shift in some very strong positions” and is now “more open to interacting with the rest of the world” and the Commonwealth.

After the last Chogm in 2024, when Downing Street was resistant to any discussions about reparations, the Ghanaian leader said the new Prime Minister’s approach “creates a first step for us to continue to work to see how we can put this whole issue of transatlantic slave trade and colonialism behind us”.
