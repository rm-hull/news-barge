People's blood and urine samples will be tested for ‘forever chemicals’ in a health study.

The UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) is analysing the samples for PFAS and other substances to build a picture of exposure and inform research into potential health effects.

The samples come from the existing Health Survey for England, although the agency says the work will help establish typical background levels of PFAS exposure in the UK.

NHS England says the survey covers people living in private households in England.

Initial findings from UKHSA’s wider biomonitoring programme are expected in 2027.

Dr Edward Wynne-Evans, UKHSA’s director of radiation, chemicals, climate and environmental hazards, said: “PFAS are an area of significant public interest and we understand the concerns people may have about these chemicals and what they could mean for their health.”

PFAS, or per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, are a group of human-made chemicals used in industrial processes and consumer products.

They are sometimes called forever chemicals because they do not break down easily.

The review says products containing PFAS include food packaging, cosmetics, outdoor clothing and cookware, while the chemicals are also used in some firefighting foams.

According to the review, PFAS can enter the environment through industrial processes, firefighting foams and the disposal of consumer products in landfill.

It says people can be exposed through everyday products or by consuming contaminated drinking water or food grown in contaminated soil.

Alongside PFAS, the study will examine samples for lead, arsenic, bisphenols, phthalates and flame retardants.

UKHSA says the work will help identify which substances are present and at what levels, contributing to the evidence base on exposure.

Dr Wynne-Evans said: “While PFAS can be detected in people, there remain important evidence gaps around what different levels of exposure mean for an individual’s long-term health.”

He added: “The evidence available to date suggests that any health effects are likely to be limited.

“The strongest evidence from across the globe currently relates to possible associations with raised cholesterol and potentially lower birth weight, but further work is required to better understand these findings and their significance.”

UKHSA’s rapid review, which focused on studies of communities exposed through industrial activity, said findings in the wider evidence base were not always consistent and varied with factors including the chemical studied and the level and duration of exposure.

It found 35 eligible papers, but no eligible studies based in the UK were returned by its searches.

The agency says the independent Committee on Toxicity is also reviewing the evidence on PFAS and health effects, while UKHSA is working with international partners.

UKHSA says the government’s PFAS Action Plan takes a precautionary approach and that restrictions have been introduced on some compounds in certain products and applications.

Routine blood testing for PFAS is not currently recommended by UKHSA.

Dr Wynne-Evans said: “At present, there is no established clinical intervention linked to a particular PFAS blood level.

“While testing can identify whether PFAS are present, there is currently insufficient evidence to determine what action should follow from a specific result.

“For that reason, routine testing is not recommended at this time.”

The agency advises anyone concerned about their health to speak to their GP or another healthcare professional.
