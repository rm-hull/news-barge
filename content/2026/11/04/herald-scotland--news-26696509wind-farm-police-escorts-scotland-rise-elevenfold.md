The number of wind farm loads needing a police escort through the Highlands has risen elevenfold in two years, raising concerns about the pressure on Police Scotland's road policing units.

Freedom of Information responses obtained by the Scottish Liberal Democrats reveal that Highland Council approved 340 "Special Order" movements for wind infrastructure between 2019/20 and 2025/26.

While just 27 took place across the first five years combined, approvals spiked to 193 in 2024/25, followed by another 120 in 2025/26.

Because Special Orders cover only the largest cargo — loads exceeding 6.1 metres in width, 30 metres in rigid length, or 150 tonnes gross weight — the true volume of wind-related freight moving across Highlands roads is likely far higher

Police Scotland separately identified 160 escorts as wind farm-related over the same period.

The force's records also show three officers providing escort cover at Invergordon for seven consecutive weeks between July 20 and September 4 this year, each week recorded as "invoice not raised".

It is understood the service intends to recover the costs.

Escorting the loads can be a slow and intensive process. Wind turbine blades travel at around three miles an hour, with single jobs often taking as much as six hours.

They are usually treated as secondary, overtime work for Police Scotland, carried out at weekends or in the evening when roads are quieter.

With escorts booked well in advance and liable to be cancelled at short notice if police need to respond to something more pressing, the delays are becoming a significant cost on projects that have often already taken years to get through planning.

Firms are increasingly being quoted six to eight-month waiting times for escorts.

The delays could get longer as more projects get the green light. Analysis for Scottish Renewables projects that demand could peak at more than 7,300 abnormal loads in 2029, almost three times current capacity.

In November 2024, HM Inspectorate of Constabulary in Scotland warned that escort demand was affecting Police Scotland's ability to keep people safe on the roads, describing the routine use of trained road officers to support private haulage as "untenable".

Scotland is the only part of the UK where abnormal loads need to be escorted by specialist road traffic police officers.

In England and Wales, the UK Government's National Highways agency performs the role.

For that to happen north of the border, there would need to be an amendment to the Road Traffic Regulation Act 1984.

The Scottish Government says that the legislation which underpins this requirement is reserved to the UK Government.

David Green, the Scottish Liberal Democrat MSP for Caithness, Sutherland and Ross, said: "The Highlands are doing the heavy lifting on Scotland's energy transition.

"The components coming through Invergordon and Nigg bring jobs and investment to the north, and I want that to continue.

"But movements of the largest loads have risen and each one relies on multiple police officers. With demand set to grow further, there are concerns about whether police resources can keep pace.

"The Scottish and UK governments must work together on a long-term fix that both takes the pressure off Police Scotland while protecting jobs, growth and investment across the Highlands."

A Department for Transport spokesperson said: “The UK Government is committed to supporting a thriving Scottish renewables sector, which is why we are working with Police Scotland, the Scottish Government and Scottish Renewables to identify solutions that will make it easier for abnormal loads, including wind turbine parts, to be transported around Scotland.”

Police Scotland and the Scottish Government was approached for comment.
