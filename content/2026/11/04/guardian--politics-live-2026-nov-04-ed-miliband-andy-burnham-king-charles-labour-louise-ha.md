Burnham says US election results have created 'opportunity' for world to focus on cutting cost of living and ending Iran war

Andy Burnham has said that he hopes the sweeping losses for Donald Trump’s Republican party in the US elections will give the world an “opportunity” to focus on ending the Iran war and easing the cost of living pressures.

In an interview at the Commonwealth summit in Antigua, asked if he was worried the results might make Trump more unpredictable, Burnham replied:

President Trump has accepted the results, and I feel the world has an opportunity now to unite around the issue that is affecting us all, and that’s the cost of living.

That has been the issue that I have put front and centre in my first three months in Downing Street, and we’ve made moves.

But I can’t get people’s cost of living pressures down until the conflict in the Middle East is resolved. The conflict in Ukraine started by Russia.

These are the things we have to focus on now, and I hope the world has a moment to do that. And certainly, in my calls with leaders, that is what I will be asking everybody to do.

Some politicians and commentators have interpreted the results as evidence that rightwing populism is in retreat around the world. But when asked if he thought the elections were part of a global pattern, Burnham was reluctant to make that claim. Instead, he replied:

I think people want to see the world coming together and focusing on the things that matter to people, and that’s what the voters, in my view, are saying in the United States.

Let’s get focused in a laser-like way on those cost of living issues. And I think around the world there’s a recognition that countries can’t do that on their own, and can’t do that when we have division between nations.

I’ve been working hard as prime minister to bring a new approach, a new approach in the Middle East, and the foreign secretary set that out [with the announcements about sanctions on illegal Israeli settlement in the West Bank].

A new approach to Europe, and we believe that will help us get growth back up and reduce those cost of living pressures. But a new approach with the Commonwealth too.

Asked if Britain would be pushing for de-escalation in the Iran war, Burnham said:

I’ll be working for de-escalation. To make it very clear, we want to see a resolution, the Strait of Hormuz fully opened again. That’s what we will be working towards.

Burnham claims there's 'solution' to Poundland's problems, as he urges its bosses to work with government on rescue plan

Andy Burnham has urged Poundland bosses to work with the government on trying to find a “practical solution” to the problems that have left the company on the brink of administration.

In a pooled interview, asked if he had a message for Poundland executives, Burnham replied:

Focus on the people in your employment, meet with the business secretary because we are setting up that meeting, come up with a practical solution that saves jobs and saves those stores because many of them are viable propositions, they’re on high streets, they matter in our communities. There is a solution to be found here, sit down with us and let’s find it.

When it was put to him that there was a limit to what the government could do if the company was not profitable, he replied:

There’s always a limit, but we’re prepared to find what we can do. We believe in this instance there is a solution. There are many viable outlets here. Other retailers are expressing interest. Get around the table. Let’s find a solution.

Here is an initial take fromPippa Crerar, the Guardian’s political editor, on what the US election results might mean for the UK.

While the public here in the UK - which overwhelmingly believes his presidency has been bad for Britain - may welcome the ‘blue tsunami’, ministers and officials will be watching nervously for how Trump reacts.

The US president acknowledged overnight the Iran conflict - which has pushed up the cost of living globally - had cost the Republicans votes and has previously suggested a deal could be struck with Tehran post-midterms.

But there are concerns here that a bruised Trump - whose domestic agenda is constrained after losing control of Congress but who still wields a veto and significant power over foreign policy - could now lash out like a caged animal.

There could be a backlash on the world stage as he tries to cement his legacy during his last two years, potentially including more military action in Iran, Ukraine and even Cuba; more trade tariffs; and more unhelpful interventions in UK and European politics.

But for now, there is also relief that the US has cast its verdict on Trump and his combative style of politics, and hope that if the Democrats get their act together (for this was a referendum on the US president rather than a ringing endorsement of his opponents), the 2028 election could transform the special relationship.

If you are deputising at PMQs, it may be nice to get a good write-up from the sketchwriters, and satisfying to craft a line that will play well on the TV or radio later, but, frankly, one task matters above all others: don’t mess up. And, in that respect, Louise Haigh and Alex Burghart both performed well – in exchanges likely to be quickly forgotten.

For Haigh, this was her first time, but it did not particularly sound like this. She sounded confident and authoritative, without being brash and hubristic. She did not have anything very new to say about welfare, and Labour does not have a firm answer yet to the question as to when it will cut the benefits bill, but Haigh swerved this quite adequately with attacks on the Tories’ record and by making the Labour argument about now wanting welfare spending on failure. She said:

We have been repeatedly clear that we cannot afford to pay for failure and must invest in success – and that is how we will bring the welfare bill down.

We will make support more conditional on taking up opportunities, ensuring young people get help they need earlier, tackling the long-term drivers of welfare spending, including high housing costs, and we will do all this in a Labour way.

She would not rule out tax rises to pay for more welfare spending (see 2.16pm), but that was the sort of question opposition parties ask when they can’t think of a proper, specific one and so instead have to resort to simplistic politics-by-slogan.

Burghart has done PMQs before, and he was a bit more polished. His pre-scripted riffs about “two 10-year plans in 10 months”, and John Healey’s Today programme welfare gaffe last week, were elegantly done, but not quite withering or funny enough to be truly damaging. He didn’t fail, but he didn’t score.

The most interesting question came from the Lib Dem deputy leader Daisy Cooper, who is probably the first MP to get a ‘Trump might be a rapist’ allegation into Hansard. (See 12.29pm.) Rather ingeniously, she tried to suggest there might be some sort of UK government angle. But there probably isn’t. This is one for Congress.

If anyone had a bad PMQs, it was probably Lindsay Hoyle. It was not clear who he was referring to when he delivered his ‘don’t shout a woman down’ bollocking to some Tory MP. (See 12.20pm.) But it sounded a bit chauvinist. Haigh is the second most senior person in government; she seemed perfectly capable of coping with a bit of heckling; and if shouting down a minister is unacceptable, it should not matter what sex they are.

Munira Wilson (Lib Dem) says Haigh recently said the government would give local leaders more power to invest in things that matter. But it is cutting funding for Richmond council by more than £100m over the next four years, 60% of its budget? Will Haigh meet her to discuss this to find a solution.

Haigh says the government inherited an unfair council funding system. She offers Wilson a meeting with a minister.

Bobby Dean (Lib Dem) asks if the government will back legislation to make US tech companies pay fair compensation to victims of online scams. He says, given the US election results, the government should be tougher with the US administration.

Haigh says the Online Safety Act requires tech companies to take robust action on online scams.

Lib Dems ask government to support any new congressional inquiries into Trump, citing 'scandals' including rape allegation

Daisy Cooper, the Lib Dem deputy leader, says US voters have rejected the policies of Donald Trump. But Trump is threatening more strikes against Iran. Will the government tell him “no more strikes”.

Haigh says the government’s policy is de-escalation. She says she hopes the Tories and Reform UK will reconsider their decision to back the war.

Cooper says US voters are also sick of Trump’s scandals, including an allegation of rape. Will Haigh join her in saying we will not tolerate powerful men who abuse women? And will the UK support any investigations that Congress launches.

The American people have also sent a message that they are sick of Trump’s scandals, from his blatant corruption to his close friendship with Epstein and, most recently emerging from the Epstein files, an allegation of rape. Will the first secretary of state join me in saying that in this house we will not tolerate powerful men who abuse their position against women in this parliament or any other? And will the government commit to assisting any and all inquiries that the new Democratic Congress may launch?

Haig replies:

The honourable lady raises an incredibly important point about the Epstein files and everything that they revealed. She’s absolutely right to highlight the importance, always of challenging power and standing on behalf of vulnerable victims.

Cooper is referring to allegations contained in some of the Epstein files, recently set out at some length in a report from the Democrats on the House judiciary committee.

This is probably the first time rape allegations about Trump have been raised in the Commons.

Burghart says the Labour way is more welfare, more tax and more borrowing. They are too weak to do the right thing, he claims. Everyone else is paying the price.

If Labour doesn’t want to do the right thing, why doesn’t it just get out of the way?

The Speaker reprimands a Tory MP for “trying to shout a woman down”.

Haigh says Burghart, a historian, once wrote a paper titled “Profile of a Doomed Elite”. It could have been about the shadow cabinet, she suggests.

She stresses the government’s achievements.

This administration has in our first 100 days [been] cutting business rates for 70,000 businesses, cutting tax for entrepreneurs, cutting VAT from electricity bills, helping 20 million households with energy bills, scrapping standing charges, capping bus fares, investing in our high streets, extending parental pay, tackling rough sleeping.

Burghart says the government won’t fix welfare. The Milburn review was delayed until after the budget. And Pat McFadden once said Labour MPs just wanted more welfare spending. So will the government commit to not putting up taxes to pay for more benefits.

Haigh says the government will bring welfare spending down, but ensuring that it now longer pays for failure. She says Burghart is on record as saying the Tories should apologise for what happened when they were in office. But Kemi Badenoch was in cabinet at the time.

Burghart says, in her comments about the budget, Haigh missed out the bits “where the Resolution Foundation said that this budget would be catastrophic for living standards” and where the OBR said tax rises could cost jobs.

He asks about the 10-year plan.

I’m old enough to remember Keir Starmer’s 10-year plan because it was six months ago. This is the only political party in history to have two 10-year plans in ten months.

Burghart says last week John Healey said in an interview he would bring welfare spending down in this parliament. This caused panic in Downing Street, he says. He goes on:

The prime minister was sent out to clarify things. He said the welfare bill needs to come down, but this can’t be done by cutting the welfare bill.

Then, then at teatime, the chancellor conceded defeat and said it’s not correct to say that we’re going to bring the welfare bill down.

So will the first secretary of state be clear? Why isn’t Labour going to bring the welfare down.

Haigh says Burghart was not listening to here earlier answer. And she criticises the Tories for voting against the budget last night.

They voted against a council tax cut for 20 million households. They voted against VAT of electricity bills. They voted against taking 70,000 small businesses out of business rates, and they voted against a tax on banks to fund it all.

That’s the highest figure ever. It’s higher than anything during Covid. It is higher than defence schools and policing combined. It is nearly £10,000 for every working household in this country. So will the government commit to bring that bill down in this parliament?

Haigh says the government is taking measures to bring welfare spending down.

Alex Burghart starts with a short question. How much will the government spend on working age welfare?

Haigh congratulates Burghart on his job. He has said he will correct Kemi Badenoch when she is wrong. That will make him the busiest man in Westminster, she says.

She says the government is committed to getting welfare spending down. Under the Tories, welfare spending rose by £88bn, she says.

Matthew Patrick (Lab) asks Haigh to restore the opportunities for young people that Brexit took away.

Haigh says she agrees. She says an SPS deal with the EU will help the economy, and the government is also negotiating a youth experience scheme. She says Alex Burghart has already promised to rip up these deals.
