A major shake-up of Scottish culture funding is on the cards to lift the threat of a financial “cliff edge” for arts organisations every few years, The Herald can reveal.

Creative Scotland has revealed plans to introduce “rolling” funding for festivals, events and venues from 2028 to address the “significant jeopardy” faced by applicants.

The Scottish Government’s arts agency plans to take a "lighter touch" approach by offering organisations “extended periods of support” rather than expect them to go through a lengthy application process every three years.

Creative Scotland has pledged to consider requests for support “beyond the current three-year-limit” in its next round of long-term funding, which it is due to make decisions on next year.

The extended fund would be offered to organisations which already receive long-term funding based on an updated business plan and if they are deemed to be “performing well”.

It is hoped the revamp of how multi-year funding is allocated will give organisations “greater long-term stability” than the current system.

Creative Scotland’s current three-year programme, which was announced at the beginning of 2025, is worth £74m a year and supports more than 260 organisations.

Among those supported are the Edinburgh International Festival, Glasgow's Celtic Connections festival, Pitlochry Festival Theatre, Dundee Rep, Aberdeen Performing Arts, Ayr Gaiety, Eden Court in Inverness and the Macrobert Arts Centre in Stirling.

The planned shake-up is expected to create a “rolling review cycle” which will see “renewal” decisions being made on the future funding of organisations at different times.

Creative Scotland has insisted the revamped funding would still be open to “new entrants” and has warned that some organisations could lose their long-term funding under the new-look programme.

However, the arts agency said the changes were being made in response to pleas to ease the “administrative burden” of the current system.

An independent review of Creative Scotland, which was published almost a year ago, highlighted “widespread criticism” of the current system of multi-year funding.

The report, by former local authority chief executive Angela Leitch, said both successful and unsuccessful organisations had found the application process “extraordinarily burdensome”, criticised “excessive information requirements and unclear expectations”, and were “frustrated” that a previous track record of delivery did not appear to be considered.

Ms Leitch highlighted research which found that 91 per cent of applicants for multi-year funding reported “harm to their wellbeing” as a “direct result” of the application process.

The report also highlighted concerns that requesting detailed information from organisations at the same time had “overloaded” and put “strain” on Creative Scotland.

Festivals, events and venues had to go through a two-stage application process which was launched in the summer of 2023 and did not know if they had long-term funding until January 2025.

Final decisions were delayed for three months by Creative Scotland due to uncertainty over its future funding from the Scottish Government.

Ministers eventually agreed to allocate an additional £40m a year to the long-term funding programme, with the increase rolled out in two phases, to bring the overall budget to £74 million by the start of the current financial year.

The extra investment has allowed Creative Scotland to increase the number of organisations on long-term funding from 119 under the previous programme to 262.

The current programme supports the Citizens Theatre in Glasgow, the Royal Lyceum Theatre in Edinburgh, Universal Hall in Moray, An Lanntair in Stornoway, Dundee Contemporary Arts, the East Neuk Festival in Fife, The Cumnock Tryst Festival in Ayrshire and the St Magnus International Festival in Orkney.

Details of the planned funding regime shake-up as Creative Scotland revealed first details about its next long-term funding programme, which is due to begin in April 2028.

It has invited “expressions of interest” in the new programme to gauge “likely demand” in terms of the number of organisations likely to apply and the level of “financial ask” that would involve.

The new guidance states: “Throughout the development of multi-year funding, feedback consistently called for the future of the fund to move beyond a single, large-scale application process, ease the administrative burden it places on applicants and the wider sector, and shift towards a rolling, continuous programme. Creative Scotland is currently developing this model.

“Under this approach, organisations already receiving multi-year funding and performing well could, subject to appropriate review and provision of an updated business plan, be offered an extended period of support without going through a full reapplication process.

“Alongside this, new entrants would still have the opportunity to join the programme, while some other organisations could see their funding period end and leave multi-year funding.

“To deliver this approach, funding terms extending beyond the current three-year limit would be considered.

“This would give funded organisations greater long-term stability, while also creating a rolling review cycle, with different organisations coming up for renewal at different times.”

Speaking at Holyrood’s culture committee, Creative Scotland’s director of strategy, Alastair Evans, said: “We have noted the difficulty that a three-year cliff edge presents for the sector. It involves significant jeopardy, and work is required to apply to us every three years.

“However, that is how we have worked and it is how cultural agencies and arts councils around the world tend to work.

“We want to move to more of a rolling programme, which we are working to design. To do that, we want to understand the demand.

“The expression of interest is both for organisations that are not currently multi-year funded, to give us a sense of their interest in joining that cohort, and for current multi-year funded organisations, to give us a sense of their interest in rolling funding - which we expect to be considerable - rather than programme plans.

“We expect to elicit a significant ask. There will be significant interest. The approach gives organisations stability and strong planning horizons, and it helps them to develop partnerships, because they can point to money the following year and the year after.”

Martin McGoldrick, Creative Scotland’s chief operating officer, added: “When we get round to the next phase of multi-year funding, we are looking to implement a lighter-touch approach to applications if the organisations that are existing recipients are performing well.

“From a continuous improvement point of view, we are learning from what has happened previously and from organisations to which we are speaking. That lighter-touch approach should not only make it easier for those organisations, but make the process more streamlined and more effective for us to administer. The intention is that the process would not pull the same level of resource as it did previously.

"With those that are not struggling, are not in financial difficulty or are not involved in a variety of other measures, we hope to take a lighter touch when they apply, because we recognise that it is a complex process. We want to work as proactively as we can with organisations."

Culture secretary Màiri McAllan told MSPs that the independent review of Creative Scotland would “underpin” any changes to its multi-year funding programme.

She suggested there had to be careful thought over how to “not only continue but improve” the application process, as well as support and encourage organisations to diversify their income and “achieve sustainability in the long term”.
