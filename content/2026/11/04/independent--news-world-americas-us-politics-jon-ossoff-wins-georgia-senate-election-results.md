Democratic Sen. Jon Ossoff of Georgia secured a second term on Tuesday, fending off Republican Rep. Mike Collins to retain a crucial Senate seat in the midterm elections.

The victory has elevated the chamber's youngest member into a prominent figure within his party, as Democrats seek to counter President Donald Trump during the remainder of his term in office.

Entering the campaign as the sole Democratic senator seeking reelection in a state won by Trump in 2024, the 39-year-old was initially viewed as a primary Republican target. However, he launched a sharp campaign focused on criticizing the administration, regularly sharing posts denouncing "a failed president" that circulated widely on social media nationwide.

His campaign generated over $100 million in donations, fueling talk of a prospective 2028 presidential run. Throughout the race, he accused the chief executive of abusing the presidency for personal financial gain while undermining the nation's economic and political foundations.

![Georgia Sen. Jon Ossoff was reelected Tuesday to a second term, holding a key seat for Democrats](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/04/1/56/GettyImages-2294699600.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Speaking to voters on Tuesday evening, Ossoff reinforced his central message, alleging widespread corruption within the Trump administration and arguing that working families were being left behind.

"Together, we’ve affirmed that our task is not just to contain one president’s wickedness, but to heal the brokenness beneath," Ossoff told the crowd. Pointing to broader Democratic successes across the country, he added: "The American people have rebuked the depravity, corruption and incompetence of the failed lame duck president Donald J. Trump."

## Ossoff's opponent struggled to find momentum, raise money

For Trump, Ossoff’s victory is a notably bitter confirmation of his decline toward lame duck status. Trump endorsed Collins for the Republican nomination in a state where the president anchored his false claims that his 2020 defeat was rigged.

But the two-term congressman, first elected echoing Trump’s election lies, never found momentum. He struggled to raise money, navigate his alliance with the president and defend against his ties to white nationalists and an unresolved House ethics investigation.

Collins told supporters Tuesday night that it had been an honor to serve under Trump, who he called the best president of his lifetime. “The results may not be what we wanted tonight, but y’all, that’s OK,” he said.

It had been a blistering campaign with Ossoff hammering Collins repeatedly as a “bigot,” emphasizing the congressman’s past social media posts, his onetime chief of staff’s communications with white supremacists and white nationalist advocacy from Collins’ son-in-law.

“If John Lewis were marching today from Selma to Montgomery, Mike Collins would be grabbing a billy club,” the senator said, referring to the late civil rights icon’s role in Bloody Sunday and the Voting Rights March in 1965.

Collins insisted he had always denounced racism and antisemitism, but the onslaught left him largely unable to land counterattacks that Ossoff was too liberal for Georgia.

“No one has ever asked or held Jon Ossoff accountable for any of his votes,” Collins said, accusing the senator of supporting open borders, allowing transgender athletes to compete in girls’ and women’s sports, and voting “100% of the time with Bernie Sanders,” the democratic socialist senator from Vermont.

Most Georgia voters, about two-thirds, say Ossoff is not “too extreme,” according to The AP Voter Poll. That included most independents and about 3 in 10 Republicans.

## Ossoff's aggressive approach against Trump became a model

Ossoff was first elected in 2020, defeating Republican Sen. David Perdue in a runoff held the day before Trump supporters attacked the U.S. Capitol with hopes of stopping Democrat Joe Biden’s certification as president-elect. Ossoff’s fellow Democrat, Sen. Raphael Warnock, won a special election at the same time, both breaking historical barriers: Warnock as Georgia’s first Black senator, Ossoff as its first Jewish senator.

Warnock won reelection in 2022 with a campaign targeted more at Georgia’s moderate swing voters, reflecting the difference for a Democratic senator running for reelection with Biden in the White House as opposed to Trump. Still, Ossoff’s unabashedly aggressive approach initially struck some Democrats as a gamble. It quickly became a model, with Ossoff holding carefully produced rallies where he delivered his withering critiques of the president as young aides quickly cut video clips that were distributed online in real time.

![Georgia U.S. Senate candidate Rep. Mike Collins speaks to members of the media during a campaign event on Monday, Nov. 2, 2026, in Atlanta, Georgia](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/04/1/56/AP26306512087928.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Ossoff mocked “Prince Don Jr.,” Eric Trump and Jared Kushner, the president’s sons and son-in-law, for their international business deals. The senator called for ending the Iran war, rolling back many of Trump’s tax cuts, and blasted the president’s tariffs. He highlighted his stalled legislative proposal to bar members of Congress from trading individual stocks and pitched a constitutional amendment to limit corporate money in campaigns.

The senator also carefully navigated the politics of Israel, saying he values Israel’s security and “the lives of innocent Palestinians.” He has voted for some U.S. military aid to Israel and against other munitions grants. Ossoff said the Israeli military has violated international standards of warfare in Gaza but avoided the question of whether Israel is committing genocide — a charge levied by some on the Democrats’ left flank. Ossoff said that’s a matter for the International Criminal Court to decide.

Ossoff also leveraged the work he’s done for constituents in Georgia, earning endorsements from some local Republican officials in the historically conservative state who said he’s been an effective advocate for hurricane relief, infrastructure investments and other federal funding.

“This is what small men like Donald Trump and Mike Collins will never understand,” Ossoff said as he campaigned, “that our national greatness flows not through our blood or our genes, but through our ideas and our aspirations and our care for one another.”
