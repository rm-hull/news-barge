## York Castle's dark past as Yorkshire's feared prison and execution site

Walls of York Castle in 1920. Photo courtesy Explore York Libraries and Archives

TODAY, York is a beautiful historic city that relies on tourism and is designed to attract visitors.

Yet visitors often find York Castle confusing; it looks nothing like what they expect.

No large walls, no moat, no great hall, and no other signs of daily life.

Even the ruins, seen at so many other castles in Britain, are just a keep on a motte where it has stood for centuries and, if you know where to look, a small section of curtain wall. Most of the castle is long gone, replaced by buildings designed around its later life.

This is because behind the façade of the buildings you see today lies a darker past, not just for York, but for the historic county of Yorkshire.

Every county had a County Gaol. In Yorkshire, the largest of the historic counties, that County Gaol sat at the centre, on the site of York Castle.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21651967.jpg?type=mds-article-620)An image of York Castle showing just how imposing the walls surrounding the site were. Hiding the often grim inside from public view. Image courtesy of York Museums Trust

Today, hundreds of thousands of visitors visit the site every year; however, it was once the one place in Yorkshire you certainly didn't want to visit.

It was a complex of justice, or at least what people once considered justice.

The County Gaol was primarily a holding area for those awaiting trial for felonies, or even treason.

But it was much more than that. It was a hub for both criminal proceedings and civic function.

As a result, York Castle was the site of the assize court, where travelling justices visited twice a year to decide the fate of those held in the gaol.

However, it also served as a prison where people who owed money across the county were brought to pay it off. Debts, which often increased because they also had to pay for their own accommodation within.

Finally, because the courthouse also served as a civic centre, new monarchs were proclaimed there on behalf of the county, and elections were held there.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21651966.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Inside York Castle in the 1800s. Outside the court, you can see the public gathered to witness trials unfold inside. Image courtesy of York Museums Trust

Most famously, in 1807, the slavery abolitionist William Wilberforce was elected a Knight of the Shire in the grounds of York Castle.

But what happened inside gave York Castle its reputation. Many different groups came through York Castle over time.

Witches, pirates, Luddites, Jacobites, Chartists, to name just a few. However, in sheer numbers, the most numerous group at the County Gaol was not felons, but debtors, people who owed money they couldn't repay at the time.

This was more common than you might imagine, with some estimates for certain periods in the 1700s suggesting nearly a third of the country spent time in a debtor’s prison. This meant many people in Yorkshire would have known someone who had passed through York Castle.

Most would eventually pay back their debt, mainly with help from family and friends. But for some, desperation could lead to extreme measures; in 1771 John Hutchinson was so poor that he pickpocketed a watch while begging at the front of York Castle prison (something only allowed for the poorest debtors), just so he would be transported.

This at least gave him some kind of future outside of his debt, and it's why he is said to have received the sentence with the ‘greatest of joy’.

While the debtors were the most numerous category of prisoner at York Castle throughout most of its history, it was the felons that got the attention. After all, the County Gaol system was set up to deliver the king's justice, in his absence, to criminals who risked execution if found guilty.

As a result, almost every famous criminal in Yorkshire from 1166 to 1863 passed through York Castle, after which time Leeds Assize Court took over the West Riding.

The list of famous criminals held at York Castle is long, but just a few include: Dick Turpin - the infamous highwayman; Mary Bateman – The Yorkshire Witch; Jonathan Martin – who tried to burn down York Minster; Eugen Aram – who escaped justice for 14 years for murder before being caught; the Pendle witch Janet Preston; the chartist martyr Samuel Holberry; and the martyred saint Margaret Clitherow – pressed to death on Ouse Bridge. All of these, and thousands more, came through the walls of York Castle to have their fate determined.

For some, this fate ended their lives.

The tourists who visit the site today are often unaware of just how many have died on the site.

Between 1802 and 1868, many were executed just outside of the castle's walls in public executions.

From 1868 until York's last execution in 1896, these executions would take place within the walls of York Castle.

*The history of the prison at York Castle can be further explored in the Gareth Verney's new book, York Castle Prison: The history of Yorkshire's County Gaol. Available from Pen and Sword now at:**www.pen-and-sword.co.uk*

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21651965.jpg?type=mds-article-620)York Castle Prison - a history by Gareth Verney

The large white doors are still clearly visible on the side of the buildings that now form York Castle Museum, but many people walk past them every day without noticing.

Today, this wonderful tourist site lets you admire the buildings or climb to the top of Clifford's Tower for some of the best views of York.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21651964.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The doors that once opened to show York's last set of gallows

Yet it also has a dark, terrible past that saw the suffering of thousands over the centuries. Touching everywhere in Yorkshire in some way.
