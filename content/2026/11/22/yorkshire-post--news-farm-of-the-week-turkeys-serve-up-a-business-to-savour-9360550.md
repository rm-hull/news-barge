# Farm of the week: Turkeys serve up a business to savour

## It’s coming up to that time of year when many take a one-day shift from beef, lamb, pork and chicken and queue, in the two days prior to Christmas Day, to ‘pick up the bird’ from a local farm or village butcher.

Twenty-three years ago East Riding arable farmer John Holtby of Dowthorpe Hall, Skirlaugh joined the turkey-growing throng when he responded to an advertisement by the family that produces what has become known as the Rolls Royce of turkeys, the KellyBronze, from their farm in Essex.

‘I was looking for another enterprise in addition to our arable cropping and pigs that we have kept on a bed and breakfast arrangement for the past forty years,’ says John.

‘Kelly’s Turkeys were looking to set up a franchise arrangement around the country and I bought into it. We now rear 420 turkeys a year and supply the local community, farm shops and butchers.

John and his wife Caroline were joined on the farm, five years ago, by the return of their daughter Rosie and her husband Henry when they moved in next door having moved up from Dorset. Henry explains why their KellyBronze turkeys are so sought after.

‘Bronze turkeys are bred and thrive in the wild. Ours are all outdoor reared. We keep them out in the woods adjoining the shed in the daytime and they come in at nighttime, to avoid predators such as the fox.

‘They arrive on the farm as day-old chicks at the beginning of July and we keep them inside for round about a month. The key to the Bronze’s ongoing success is that it is slow-reared and allowed to grow naturally through to maturity. That means it builds up its natural fat, which is what gives its great flavour. We also give ours a protein rich, well concocted feed.

Rosie says the birds are happy in their environment.

‘Their different sounds mean quite different things, like an alert sound and a happy sound. We have quite a few apples in our orchard and when we give them those it causes great excitement.

‘When they are chicks they look very sweet, being stripey and fluffy. They then seem to go through a slightly ugly, almost prehistoric, teenage phase before starting to look as they do now, which is really impressive. Their feathers are amazing, with bronze and green colours amongst their largely black appearance.

Providing the right number of birds at the weight each customer needs is an annual conundrum and Rosie explains how they get close to achieving it.

‘We get customers that want smaller birds because they only need enough for two or three but turkeys are naturally quite big birds. Our chicks are different strains, like the Roly Poly, a big one, and the Super Mini. We try to supply enough of everything from the four to five kilo bird to eleven kilos. We also sell crowns, the breast and legs.

The Holtbys’ Christmas 2026 turkeys have another week and a half before processing starts the first week in December.

‘We’ve a big team on hand plucking while the turkeys are still warm, we hang them pretty much complete for two weeks, which adds to their flavour, before processing, dressing and putting them into smart boxes that come with a thermometer and cooking instructions.

‘Collection is on farm from 9am to 5pm on December 23; and 9am until midday on Christmas Eve. It’s always a jolly festive atmosphere. We have a pop-up farm shop selling a local butcher’s bacon and sausages and last year we had warm sausage rolls that proved very popular.

Rosie says turkeys collected from the farm accounts for half their birds with the other half going to local butchers and farm shops such as Sledmere and Drewton’s.

Rosie and Henry took up an extreme learning curve when they came to Dowthorpe Hall with their two sons – Arthur, now 9 years old, and Charlie, 8.

‘I grew up here,’ says Rosie, having worked in the art world and at Sotheby’s. ‘It was amazing how much I didn’t know about farming.

‘We enrolled in a part-time HNC course in agriculture at Bishop Burton College to give us a grounding. We now also have a few ideas of new ventures, such as finishing cattle. I’ve started hosting school visits through The Country Trust teaching children about grinding wheat to make flour; rape being ground to see the oil; pond dipping, nature walking and generally teaching where food comes from. We have also hosted Scouting camps through my work with Beavers.

‘I was a farming virgin,’ says Henry. ‘I’m learning all the time. I was a plumber before coming here and farming certainly beats being squashed up in an airing cupboard and crawling through people’s lofts.

John farmed with his father for eighteen years before his passing in 2001 and the family has farmed at Dowthorpe Hall since 1890.

‘My great grandfather moved here. The farm runs to about 980 acres rented from The Crown Estate and the Church Diocese of York.

‘After university, I became a land agent before returning home, as my father wanted to pursue other interests as well as farming. He ran the Red Cross in this area. He’d had a suckler herd and my grandfather had had sheep.

John has always concentrated on arable, pigs and latterly turkeys.

‘We have 920 acres of arable cropping and 60 acres in various stewardship and SFI schemes.

‘Winter Wheat is our biggest crop. Last year we grew 410 acres of varieties Beowulf, Graham and Hexton, which was new to us. It did very well, producing a lot of straw. We averaged 3.9 tonnes per acre, just below our 4 tonnes average. We were all done on this year’s wheat by August 2.

‘We harvested 180 acres of oilseed rape; 120 acres each of winter barley and spring oats.

‘Our land type is clay loam, deep bodied Holderness clay. We try not to use the plough too much. We will usually use it a bit after we grow spring oats or if we have a particular blackgrass issue. Our wheat goes into Sedamyl in Selby; the oats to Morning Foods.

‘I’ve worked in pigs with another local farmer, Ashley Foster, in conjunction with Yorkshire Farmers Livestock Marketing for 40 years. Our pigs are all on bed and breakfast. They go to Malton Bacon Factory.

John says the recent SFI debacle over the window for applications open and shut within less than six hours is a major concern.

‘I missed it by 25 minutes. Our current SFI ends at the end of February. It’s an absolute mess. If they don’t have another window before, I shan’t be in it next year and will lose a substantial sum.

John is concerned not just for Dowthorpe, but also as a ward councillor for Mid Holderness on the East Riding Council; in addition he is taking on a new role next year.

‘I’m going to be Master of The Company of Merchants of the Staple of England, one of the oldest livery companies and based in York. I’m not a sheep farmer but I’ll be adding my voice to promoting the use of wool.
