## 'Things fell apart' - York man goes from sleeping in his car to his own flat

Scott McCrorie, a City of York Council housing navigator (left) with Damien Goudy, a former rough sleeper who now has his own flat in Acomb *(Image: Kevin Glenton)*

“When I left, I felt not only a lot more confident, but capable of dealing with things myself.”

Damien Goudy is a former rough sleeper in York now living permanently in a flat in Acomb who has been through a “resettlement pathway” working alongside housing navigators at the city council.

He worked with Scott McCrorie, one of five navigators in a City of York Council team who go into the city centre streets working directly with people sleeping rough, helping them access safe accommodation and tailoring support to address issues such as health, benefits and other barriers in maintaining a home.




After losing his job as a groundworks machine driver in the civil engineering sector, having previously worked all his life, Damien said he also lost his place to stay and had slept in his car and in hallways.

He said he’s been “round the system twice” but said things "fell apart" after finding a flat the first time because he wasn’t quite ready, especially over the period of the Covid pandemic where his situation proved more difficult.

City of York Council said it aims to make homelessness rare, brief and non-recurring with a view to person-centred support which seeks to prevent homelessness and rough sleeping and respond when it happens.

Council figures show 25 people were sleeping rough in York in 2025/26, according to a headcount done during that year.

The amount was down from 30 in 2024/25.

The council's Labour administration said in July more up-to-date counts showed no one with a local connection in York was sleeping rough in the city.

An approach to getting people into permanent housing as soon as possible was adopted by the council in 2024.

An update to the council’s Homelessness and Rough Sleeping Strategy was discussed at the end of last month, with a report stating a total of 80 of these ‘Housing First’ cases – such as Damien’s – have been dealt with since 2024.

### 'I’ve gone through the system, I’m trying to get back to work now'

Speaking to reporters at the Peasholme Centre, a council homelessness and resettlement hostel in York, Damien said going through the resettlement pathway and working with navigators like Scott made him more responsible.

He said: “I went through the whole support system this time, I had people working with me the entire time.

“I went to drug and alcohol support, then obviously I had support workers here.

“I did that 18 months ago and I have been off everything since then.

“When I left, I felt not only a lot more confident but capable of dealing with things better myself.

“I’ve gone through the system, I’m trying to get back to work now.”

Scott McCrorie said Damien’s example of going round the system twice is an example which shows housing is very rarely the only issue.

He said: “People see homelessness and go ‘put them in a flat’ without realising the understory that goes with it.

“Damien went into a flat and didn’t have the support he needed at the time.

“Ultimately that failed, and he’s come round again.

“This time he’s accessed more support and gone through the service properly.

“It shows that people’s needs are often much more complex than simply having a roof over their heads.

“The whole idea of Housing First is that although they get put into a property, the intensive multi-agency support they get in that property is ultimately the difference to throwing them into the environment and leaving them to it.”

Damien added that the way Scott and other workers responded helped him see that they have the range of experiences relating to the problems he had, whereas many people he has come into contact with make their own assumptions.

He said: “When you’re trying to deal with everything yourself, going through and finding all the information yourself, having somebody who’s trained that can offer you that information, without blinking, that benefits you massively.”

Scott added: “Generally, signposting is one thing, supporting them through that pathway is the important part.

“Sometimes people are perfectly aware of what they need to do but having that oomph to introduce that accountability can make a difference sometimes and I think we’re in a good position to offer that to people.

“That all-round approach helps us as a team work with the variety of issues.

“There’s no one-size fits all in terms of support – everyone can be so different, and how you engage with them is all very person-centred.

“Housing First includes a support package, so although Damien is no longer rough sleeping, our role will only come to an end when it’s mutually agreed that he doesn’t need that support any more.”
