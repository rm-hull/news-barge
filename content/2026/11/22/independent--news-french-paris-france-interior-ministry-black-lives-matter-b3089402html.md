French activists and lawmakers are demanding after recent street protests that authorities suspend the use of tear gas to disperse crowds over concerns it's caused menstrual changes and other reproductive health problems, though scientists have not confirmed a medical connection.

The demands come after police used tear gas during student protests and demonstrations over the cost of living, some marred by violence. Some women reported painful cramps, unexpected bleeding and disrupted menstrual cycles after exposure, and an advocacy group says it has collected more than 10,000 complaints. Opposition lawmakers are seeking a ban and independent research into possible health effects.

Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez has rejected the calls, saying police use of tear gas is essential to crowd control as an alternative to using more dangerous weapons.

Here’s what is known — and what is not — about tear gas and reproductive health.

Tear gas is commonly used by police to disperse crowds

Tear gas grenades used by French police contain CS, a chemical irritant that affects the eyes, skin and respiratory tract. CS is a crystalline powder dispersed into the air as fine particles. Its effects depend upon how much is used and how long people are exposed to it.

Those exposed during the protests include peaceful demonstrators — including children, pregnant women and older people — and residents near the sites of protest marches.

Women reported pain and unexpected bleeding after exposure

Mélina Galland, 24, was working as an independent photographer during Oct. 17 protests in Paris that were marred by rioting and looting. She wore a double surgical mask along with a diving mask when tear gas grenades were fired.

“Heading back home, I felt intense belly pain which was unusual because I never experience pain that intense at that stage of my period,” she said.

Chloé Baroux, 25, also a photographer, first noticed pain and irregular periods after covering protests against a security bill in 2020 and 2021. She said her menstrual cycles eventually became more regular, and she didn't think of a potential link to tear gas until she covered the recent protests, noting, “over the past two months, it’s become irregular again.”

Eva-Luna Tholance, a former journalist who investigated the issue six years ago, decided to found the “Lacrymos de sang” ("Tears of blood") advocacy group during the protests to raise awareness about possible effects on menstrual cycles and pregnancies. The group asked people last month to complete a questionnaire describing their exposure and symptoms. Its collection of accounts doesn't establish how common symptoms are among those who say they were exposed to tear gas, and they were not reviewed by independent doctors.

Still, Tholance said, many women described pain or unexpected bleeding, and thousands are asking questions about what’s happening in their bodies.

“Some people experience mild cramps, but a huge number tell us about cramps that are far worse than what they normally experience during their periods,” she said, adding that bleeding reports range “from spotting to extremely heavy bleeding lasting several days. Some people bleed for weeks.”

Others reported bleeding despite being postmenopausal or taking hormonal contraception that normally prevents menstrual bleeding, Tholance said.

Investigations have yet to establish a causal link

An Associated Press investigation during the 2020 Black Lives Matter protests in the U.S. found there had been limited study and oversight of tear gas, though it did raise concerns about tear gas exposure in confined spaces, excessive use and canisters fired directly at people.

A recent study collected accounts of symptoms reported after the BLM protests. It found a “potential relationship” between exposure and “adverse reproductive health outcomes” but did not prove cause and effect.

That’s in part because the study relied on self-reported information collected at one point in time — similar to the French group’s questionnaire — and because people who felt most impacted may have been more likely to respond.

“There’s a lot of theories. We don’t really 100% know,” said the study’s lead author, Asha Hassan of the University of Minnesota Medical School. The study calls for more research “urgently.”

Hassan said much early research on tear gas involved healthy young men in military settings, which could help explain why menstrual and other reproductive health effects have gone undocumented.

Alsetex and Nobel Sport, two leading suppliers of tear gas grenades to French law enforcement, did not respond to requests for comment.

The Interior Ministry says no published scientific studies show that CS causes menstrual changes or miscarriages. It said other effects of tear gas generally resolve within hours, although it acknowledged that some longer-lasting symptoms affecting the skin and eyes “cannot be ruled out.”

Some other possible effects in the body “have been suggested, but not demonstrated,” it said.

The ministry said it conducted several studies in 2021 and 2022 assessing individual exposure to tear gas, particularly among law enforcement personnel. It did not respond to an AP request for copies.
