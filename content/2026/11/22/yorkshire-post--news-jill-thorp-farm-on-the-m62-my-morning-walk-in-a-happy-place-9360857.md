# Jill Thorp Farm on the M62: ​My morning walk in a happy place

## An early morning walk took me up onto the tops, away from the motorway and the constant din. Regardless of the number of times I’ve headed up there, it never ceases to make me smile. I followed the old cart track as it snaked its way up the slope, the tall dry stone wall looming above the path with it’s unique “cock and hen” topping stones giving the wall a battlement-like appearance.

This type of walling is prevalent on our farm, presumably as a deterrent to any steeplechasing sheep as it adds height and certainly looks more challenging. I followed the ancient parish boundary marker stones as the sun just began to appear on the horizon, casting a warm golden hue stretching out towards Huddersfield on one side and Saddleworth on the other. Most of the boundary stones lay in the grass now with only the odd one still upright. It seems such a shame that they’re slowly sinking into the ground, the intricate carving on them lost in moss and molinia. Perhaps a plan for the future; get them back upright for all to see. Once away from the motorway the sound of birds began to fill my ears. Apart from the occasional bleat from one of the Herdwicks that had escaped the great dispersal sale, the vocal chatter of the beautiful Red Grouse was the only thing breaking the silence. Red Grouse have appeared on our moor in abundance in recent years and do have the most unique vocabulary. They are at their most talkative between February and April when the males assert their territory. Once one starts, they all start and their neighbours join in, sounding out across the heather clad moors. Their general call can be heard year round and it was this that I listened to as I strode past the first Deanhead parish stone. The moor is now cloaked in it’s rich copper and bronze hues of Autumn. The heather with it’s faded blooms, now a muted russet and the last of the molinia grass glowing golden-yellow in the early morning sunshine amongst patches of deep burgundy bilberry. The ever changing moorland is always so breath-taking from season to season. Higher up I disturbed a great flock of Golden Plover, that quickly took to the wing leaving me standing in awe watching their mesmerising aerial display. Their summer colouring of black throat and bellies has now been replaced with a pale speckled gold which glittered as they swooped low across the moor, tightly knitted together, flying at speed, their soft whistle just audible. I stood for some time watching them until a solitary figure appeared on the path. His bent gait, head down with a very slow, sweeping movement told me he was on the search for a very specific type of mushroom that clearly grows in abundance up there! He briefly glanced up before continuing his steady, methodical forage.

I’ve walked up there so many times, often at the start of a gather or in recent months, to check the cows. Just when I think I know every inch, every marker stone, gate, wall or stream, something new crops up. A cripple gap in the wall this time caught my eye. The large cap stone had letters etched into the stone, barely legible but definitely there. I traced my fingers across the rough surface but couldn’t make out the word, however, I know a man who will know and I’ll be asking him next time our paths cross!
