Unreal Engine 6 will not launch with the engine's long-standard Blueprints scripting system. The news came in a Friday, November 19 post on the Unreal Engine website, written by Epic Games executive vice president of development, Marcus Wassmer. Here's Wassmer's top-level summary from the post:

![Unreal Engine logo, a &quot;u&quot; in a circle.](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/ma3UMuAvgxkaSJP2kmHrTg-140-80.png)

"UE6, including UE6 Early Access, won’t include Blueprints or the Actor Gameplay Framework. Gameplay will be built in Verse and on the new Scene Graph framework.

"In June, we said Blueprints and Actors would coexist in UE6 Early Access and 6.0, so UE5 projects could upgrade incrementally while the new Scene Graph framework matures. However, with recent advances in AI capability, we expect AI to massively help with converting UE5 projects to UE6, if not make that process fully automatic. That also means we can deliver UE6 on time (or sooner!) while meeting its design goals: a new data model, a Verse-first architecture, modularity, and AI operability."

![PC Gamer headshot - Ted Litchfield](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/8DyQVBz7FCynDY9QiJyH9D-140-80.png)

Blueprints have not (and it appears, will not) be deprecated in Unreal Engine 5, and the company maintains that projects will still be easy to convert from UE5 to UE6. Wassmer cited a declining cost in AI task completion as one reason for this belief, as well as the team's ongoing, but apparently successful conversion of Fortnite's Blueprint library to UE6's Verse programming language through an AI-assisted process.

Blueprints are a widely-used feature of Unreal. Former Valve developer Chet Faliszek, an AI skeptic, has put out a vlog on the subject. Essentially, Faliszek argues that Blueprints (and features like them in other game engines) represent a middle ground between artistic and technical work, an introduction and skill builder for less technically-minded developers. Without them, such developers seemingly have to either learn Verse code, or integrate LLM prompting into their workflows.

![Goodbye Blueprints, Hello Verse AI! - YouTube](https://img.youtube.com/vi/-EPL0AxfHuc/maxresdefault.jpg)

"We made a new skill in the middle there, between normal and expert," Faliszek said of intermediate tools like Blueprints. "To lose that determinism from game design, and instead *have* to prompt AI⁠—or write your own Verse … It's losing those tools that made all those designers and all those artists so powerful, to be able to do really powerful things and have access to really deep-down things, but in a safe way that the engineers can decide what that meant to them."

A dark horse game of the year candidate, the first-person RPG Queen's Domain, was made with Unreal Engine. I recently spoke to co-developer Eric Berger, who specialized in art and design alongside the more technically-focused Bradley Boothe, a relationship that rhymes with the ones Faliszek outlined in his video.

I spoke to Berger before seeing Epic's latest press release, but Berger already indicated that Unreal's AI pivot is prompting Freshly Baked Games to switch engines for its next project. I followed up with Berger about this Blueprint news, and the developer said that Blueprints were a major part of the workflow on Queen's Domain.

I'm a layman in game development, but I think Wassmer's post elides that there is a large qualitative difference between doing any work via LLM prompt versus the old-fashioned way⁠—I don't like or want to use these tools, myself, and I don't think that's an unpopular stance. Commenter ZILtoid1991 on Faliszek's video compared the move to Apple removing headphone jacks on the iPhone, a comparison I find fruitful.
