## Turkish prosecutors have sought asset freezes on 108 high-profit traders amid a $17 billion investment fund scandal involving alleged market manipulation and political connections.

Turkish prosecutors investigating a sprawling investment fund scandal have requested the freezing of securities held by 108 individuals who each allegedly earned more than $2 million in trading profits during periods of suspected market manipulation, according to a prosecutorial document cited by an opposition lawmaker.

The November 18 document also calls for financial institutions to closely monitor the individuals' asset movements, including large withdrawals, transfers and property transactions. However, it remains unclear whether the requested freezes have been implemented.

The development adds another dimension to a financial crisis that has engulfed investment funds worth approximately $17 billion, disrupted Turkey's stock market and raised questions about regulatory oversight and the relationship between political influence and financial markets.

According to an AFP report published on November 22, Şık, a member of the Workers' Party of Turkey (TİP), disclosed excerpts from a November 18 prosecutorial document identifying 108 individuals who each earned more than 100 million Turkish lira, equivalent to over $2 million, during periods when investigators detected suspected manipulation.

The document reportedly stated that no action had previously been taken against these individuals and that prosecutors had subsequently requested relevant institutions to freeze securities held in their accounts.

Although the document did not appear to formally identify all 108 individuals as criminal suspects, Şık said the list included people with connections to the state bureaucracy, political circles, corporate management and the financial industry.

The reported measures raise a central question in the investigation: whether the crisis was caused primarily by excessive financial risk-taking or whether some market participants benefited from coordinated trading practices while ordinary investors were exposed to potentially substantial losses.

## **How Turkey's investment fund crisis unfolded**

The crisis erupted in September when several investment funds failed to meet investors' withdrawal requests, exposing weaknesses in portfolios that had accumulated large positions in relatively illiquid stocks.

On September 17, Turkey's Capital Markets Board (SPK) ordered the liquidation of 131 funds managed by seven asset-management companies.

According to Reuters, the affected funds had accumulated more than $20 billion in assets during the preceding three years, attracting hundreds of thousands of investors seeking returns that could outpace inflation and the depreciation of the Turkish lira.

## **Allegations of manipulation and privileged access**

At the center of the investigation are allegations that certain investment funds were used to inflate the prices of selected shares through concentrated or coordinated trading.

Under such a scheme, repeated purchases can push up the prices of stocks with limited freely available shares. As their market prices rise, funds holding those securities report substantial paper gains, potentially attracting additional investors.

The process can become self-reinforcing until investors attempt to withdraw their money or regulators intervene.

If large shareholders sell before prices collapse, they may realize substantial profits while remaining investors bear the consequences.

Investigators are examining whether such practices occurred and whether individuals with privileged information benefited disproportionately.

The newly reported requests for asset freezes are significant because they extend attention beyond the executives directly responsible for managing the troubled funds.

The list described by Şık reportedly includes individuals with political and bureaucratic connections, suggesting investigators are examining a potentially broader network of beneficiaries.

However, unusually large trading profits do not independently establish wrongdoing. Prosecutors would need to determine whether the gains resulted from illegal manipulation, privileged information or legitimate investment activity.
