**Parents are being urged to recognise the signs of online stalking, amid concerns technology is making it easier than ever for perpetrators to monitor and repeatedly contact young people.**

Experts say persistent unwanted contact, attempts to bypass blocks by creating new accounts and efforts to track someone's online activity can all be early indicators of stalking behaviour.

Their advice follows the case of a Wiltshire teenager who became "obsessed" with a girl after a five-day relationship, monitoring her online activity and creating fake social media accounts after she blocked him before later attacking her with a knife.

It also comes as stalking offences have risen across Avon and Somerset, with police recording 1,348 offences last year, up from 954 two years earlier.

In response to the increase, officers are receiving additional training to help identify stalking behaviour, patterns of control and links to other offences.

## How one Wiltshire case escalated

Gloucester Crown Court heard the 14-year-old boy had become "obsessed" with the girl after she ended their relationship.

The victim's mother told the BBC: "She had no idea that he was logging when she was online, who she was speaking to, what she was saying.

"There were diaries found in his house [detailing her online activity]. It makes me feel sick."

The court heard the boy created numerous fake social media accounts to contact the victim after she blocked him, before later attacking her with a 6in knife.

He admitted wounding with intent, inflicting grievous bodily harm and possession of a bladed article in a public place, but avoided a custodial sentence.

"This is something you watch happen to other people and you're horrified by it. But now this is my life, and that happened to my child," the victim's mother added.

## How can stalking happen online?

Police guidance , externaldescribes stalking as repeated, unwanted behaviour that is fixated or obsessive and affects a person's daily life.

While many people associate stalking with being followed in public, experts say it can increasingly take place online through social media platforms, messaging apps and gaming services.

Researchers have suggested tools including GPS trackers, social media, home surveillance systems and smartphone apps may be contributing to a wider rise in stalking cases, making it easier for perpetrators to monitor victims remotely.

Trowbridge-based stalking charity FearFree said concerns should not be dismissed, even when behaviours appear minor at first.

"We know that sometimes - particularly when the behaviour first starts - it can be hard to recognise the risk it might pose, but it is important to not dismiss your concerns," a spokesperson said.

"Evidence shows us that stalking is a high risk behaviour, which can lead to serious violence."

Research by the University of Gloucestershire and the Suzy Lamplugh Trust found 94% of murders examined featured prior stalking behaviour.

Campaigners say concerns about recognising stalking behaviours early have been raised nationally for years, with high-profile cases highlighting the dangers of warning signs being overlooked.

## What are the warning signs?

Police say stalking is often characterised by a pattern of unwanted behaviour rather than a single incident.

Warning signs can include somebody continuing to send messages after being asked to stop, creating new accounts after being blocked, or keeping track of when a person is online and who they are speaking to.

Other behaviours can include attempting to infiltrate friendship groups, turning up unexpectedly at places a person regularly visits, sending unwanted gifts or communications, or spreading rumours about them.

In some cases, stalkers may also try to gain access to email, social media or other online accounts to monitor a victim's activity.

## What should parents do if they are worried?

If stalking is carried out online, victims are encouraged to make social media accounts private, avoid publicly sharing their location - unless it is with a trusted friend or family member - and protect their devices and accounts with strong passwords.

Although blocking a stalker may seem the obvious response, Gloucestershire-based charity the Hollie Gazzard Trust warns that obsessed perpetrators may be "more likely to physically come round and find you".

Instead, Gloucestershire Constabulary advises victims to keep contact with the stalker to a minimum.

"Blocking can result in a stalker changing their behaviour, which in turn escalates the risk of violence," a spokesperson said.

The force says previous messages, posts and records of unwanted contact can provide important evidence about the volume and nature of the behaviour.

"However, we also understand how distressing it can be when you are the victim of this type of behaviour," the force continued.

"We would encourage anyone who is experiencing this to speak to us or a recognised organisation who can help provide advice and support."

## When should stalking be reported to police?

Under UK law, stalking and harassment offences generally require two or more related incidents of unwanted behaviour to warrant police intervention.

However, police stress victims do not need evidence before making contact, although information such as dates, times and witness details can help investigators.

Personal safety charity the Suzy Lamplugh Trust offers online resources for tracking and recording stalking behaviours, external.

"Anything you've got like that can be useful to us, but don't delay reporting to get it," Wiltshire Police advises.

"Your information could help us bring the offender to justice and make sure you, and other people in a similar situation, are kept safe."

## What happens after a stalking report is made?

Once a report is made, police will assess whether an offence has been committed, how serious the behaviour is and whether the victim is at immediate risk.

If there are viable lines of enquiry, an investigating officer will usually be assigned and the case may then be referred for further action, including criminal prosecution.

Stalking carries a maximum sentence of 10 years in prison, and a Stalking Protection Order (SPO), external can also be enforced to protect a victim of any age.

The conditions of the order could include prohibiting the stalker from entering certain locations, approaching or contacting the victim, making direct or indirect references to the victim on social media, recording images of the victim, and more.

Breaching an SPO is deemed a criminal offence under the Stalking Protection Act 2019, and could carry a maximum sentence of up to five years in prison.
