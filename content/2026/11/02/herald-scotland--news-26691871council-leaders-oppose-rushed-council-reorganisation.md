Council leaders have urged the Scottish Government not to "hurry into a costly reorganisation that will throw up more questions than answers".

Writing in The Herald, Councillor Steven Heddle, vice-president of COSLA, said local authorities had already delivered their share of public sector savings and that reform should start with funding, not structures.

There is still little detail on how it would work in practice, despite ministers deliberately rushing the plans. The government wants a “shared direction” with COSLA and local government by December.

Ministers say the process will not go beyond the May 2027 council elections, when Scotland’s 1,226 councillors will be elected. Those councillors would then sit on both regional and community authorities.

John Swinney has insisted the reforms are needed because some councils are “too small to be strategic and some are too big to be local”.

Cllr Heddle said: "The assertion is that the current structure is unsustainable, but this is primarily because of the budget choices that the government has made to fund their own policy priorities at the expense of councils, not least Council Tax being frozen or capped for years."

![Deputy First Minister of Scotland Jenny Gilruth](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21199439.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Jenny Gilruth said the government wanted to get the reforms done quickly *(Image: Andrew Milligan)*

The Orkney Islands councillor said councils believed a shared position was already in hand.

COSLA and the Scottish Government jointly published Democracy Matters: A Routemap to Reform in March, the product of nine years of the Local Governance Review.

He described it as "an exemplary and important piece of joint work" but said that First Minister John Swinney had now "jolted the conversation into one about wholesale reorganisation".

He said the new community authorities would have "limited powers akin to England's 13,000 parish councils as we understand it".

On money, Cllr Heddle said councils had made £5 billion of savings since 2011.

"When the government says that collectively Scotland's budget requires £4.7bn in savings, councils feel they have more than delivered their share already," he added.

COSLA leaders met at Verity House in Edinburgh on Friday to agree their response to the government's three-month dialogue.

Shona Morrison, the COSLA president, said afterwards that the government's proposals and its approach to engagement "need a rethink".

COSLA's own vision starts with the existing 32 councils, with better regional collaboration and voluntary consolidation where it has local democratic support.

In his column, Cllr Heddle also calls for fiscal reform, including "the overdue reform" of council tax, funding guarantees and further powers for local authorities. He says these must be "considered from the outset, not after structures and governance have been designed".

"It has perhaps been lost that the SNP Scottish Government re-entered this parliament not with restructuring of councils in their manifesto, but with a vow to reform the whole system of public services.

"For the average citizen this means much more than changing lines on a map: it means truly working across the whole of the public and civic sector to make sure services are joined up and tailored to their needs."

"So let's not hurry into a costly reorganisation that will throw up more questions than answers for our communities and workforce," he added. "Instead, COSLA will meet the First Minister and Cabinet Ministers this week in good faith to call for the future of local services to be shaped by robust evidence, by our communities and done in a way that provides clarity as citizens go to the polls in 2027 to elect a new set of local councillors to represent them."

![The Cosla headquarters in Edinburgh](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/13076941.jpg?type=mds-article-620)COSLA wants to keep the existing 32 local authorities *(Image: PA)*

The row over pace of the reforms has run since the proposals were published in September.

Jenny Gilruth, the Deputy First Minister, said at the time: "We've quite deliberately actually set quite a tight time period of three months which will take us roughly to the beginning of December. And we've done that quite deliberately because I think there's an urgency required around about local government reform."

Ministers have also been under pressure from their own side.

At the SNP conference in Aberdeen, Simon Barrow, convener of the party's trade union group, said a three-month timetable for reaching agreement on local government and social care "isn't credible".

Ivan McKee, the Public Service Reform Secretary, defended the pace. He said: "Frankly we don't want to be sat talking about this for ever, we want to get it done."

COSLA and the unions Unison, Unite and the GMB said last week that the model "is creating uncertainty for the council workforce".

They said questions about jobs, the transfer of staff and the impact on terms and conditions "remain unanswered".

A Scottish Government spokesperson said: “Our programme for reform is about making services work better for the people of Scotland and empowering local communities.

"Ministers are focused on improving outcomes for people, strengthening local democracy and delivering sustainable public services and are committed to further engagement as this work progresses.

"No decisions have been taken on future structures. Once formal reform proposals are developed, appropriate consultation will follow in the usual manner."
