Wellbeing in Scotland remains above the UK average but has failed to improve over the past year, according to new research from Carnegie UK.

The organisation’s Life in the UK Index found Scotland’s collective wellbeing score remained at 63 out of 100 in 2026, compared with 61 for the UK as a whole.

The findings are based on a survey of 6,731 adults across the UK, including 1,046 respondents in Scotland.

While the overall score was unchanged, the report highlighted concerns about job opportunities, trust in public institutions and persistent inequalities linked to income, disability and housing.

The index measures wellbeing across four areas: social, economic, environmental and democratic.

Democratic wellbeing was Scotland’s lowest-scoring area, at 40 out of 100. Among disabled people, the score fell to 34.

Trust in government also remained a concern, with 52% of Scottish respondents reporting low trust in the UK Government and 34% reporting low trust in the Scottish Government.

Job opportunities were another area of concern, with 39% of people dissatisfied with employment prospects in their local area.

The report also found that environmental wellbeing had improved since 2023 and remained above the UK average. However, 80% of respondents continued to experience problems with litter, while around half reported noise issues in their neighbourhoods.

There were some positive signs in household financial resilience. More people in Scotland said they could afford to keep their homes warm and meet an unexpected expense of £850 compared with 2023.

Sarah Davidson, chief executive of Carnegie UK, said: “Now in its fourth year, this research shows wellbeing in Scotland holding up but also reveals the huge challenges ahead of us.

“Too many people in Scotland are leading lives that are unacceptably hard.

“This should focus the minds of government decision makers.

“While Scotland performs slightly better than the UK average, there are real concerns about local economic opportunities and low levels of trust in key institutions.

“Scotland’s political leaders need to make a systematic approach to improving people’s lives central to their decision-making.

“As a start, the Scottish Government should put an improved National Performance Framework, the country’s key tool for working towards better outcomes for its people, at the heart of its plans for the parliamentary term ahead.”

Heather Fisken, chief executive of Inclusion Scotland, said: “Inclusion Scotland welcomes the findings of Carnegie’s Life in the UK 2026 report for Scotland.

“Though we are disappointed, we are not necessarily surprised that the data reveals disabled people and people living in social housing tend to have lower wellbeing scores across collective, democratic, economic, and social wellbeing.

“As disabled people are more likely to live in social housing, these inequalities are often connected and compounded.

“These findings underline the need for intersectional approaches to tackle inequality in Scotland.

“Wellbeing cannot be separated from the circumstances we live in or the interconnected barriers we face.”
