On Sunday night, millions of Ukrainians were sent an alert to their phones that would usually spark panic and fear.

Citizens were told that there was an imminent threat of radiation, chemical attacks and fighting on the streets of Kyiv through an app used by the government to issue nationwide notifications in emergencies.

It was quickly followed up with a clarification that the system had likely been hacked by Russia and that no such threat existed.

“The Air Alarm application has probably been subjected to hostile interference,” read a follow-up message on Telegram. “There is no radiation threat! Stay calm and follow our updates on our channel.”

Shortly after, Russia told its citizens that Ukraine had hacked its systems to warn its people of imminent nuclear strikes.

Neither country has claimed responsibility and it is unclear where or how the incidents were sparked. But they have been widely interpreted by officials and analysts as tit-for-tat attacks, started by Russia.

A hacker within the Ukrainian “hacktivist” community told the BBC that the breach of the Russian alert system was likely to be a “retaliatory attack” by Ukraine.

The ensuing chaos has laid bare the increasingly devastating impact of cyberattacks.

According to the Ukrainian security service (SBU), citing its cybersecurity department, more than 16,000 Russian cyberattacks and “critical cyber incidents” had been neutralised between the start of the war in February 2022 and July this year.

Brigadier general Volodymyr Karasteliov referred to one large-scale DDoS attack that lasted three hours against a nationwide Ukrainian television channel.

“The botnet generated up to 200,000 requests per minute from nodes in Asia, Europe, and the United States,” he said.

“The attack targeted the website's application layer, with hackers imitating the behaviour of real users in an attempt to overload the server with complex requests and make the site unavailable.”

According to research published in PLOS in April 2024, Russia ranked as the number one hotspot for cybercriminals worldwide, followed by Ukraine.

Cybersecurity firm CybelAngel noted that the attacks between Russia and Ukraine are largely asymmetric. The company’s REACT team recorded 1,651 cyber incidents targeting Ukrainian entities with 575 targeting Russian entities, between January 2025 and January 2026.

“The asymmetry isn’t new, since the prior twelve months looked nearly identical in shape and scale,” it said.

![](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/01/12/2302864925..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Experts told *The Independent* that the attacks serve a more sinister purpose than simply the disruption of communication systems.

“A message from your air raid alert system generates emotion straight away, almost like a phishing email, with that sense of panic and urgency, and you respond and react,” says Adam Pilton, cyber security adviser at Heimdal.

“That’s exactly why the air raid systems were hit. The attack strikes at the point where people trust their government.”

He compares the incident to the cyberattack on ASOS a few weeks ago, explaining that when communication is disrupted from official platforms, it leads to uncertainty and confusion for civilians.

“If you can’t trust communications that purportedly come from official platforms, whether a business or your government, then what can you trust? That’s exactly the purpose of this attack, to erode trust,” he says.

Pilton reiterates that the purpose of the strikes is to sow panic and uncertainty despite claims that the systems are legitimate targets as communication platforms.

“Russia has claimed that this is not an actual air raid system but just a communication platform. Whether that’s true or not is irrelevant. What matters is that people look to their leaders to understand what’s happening,” he explains.

“There’s a degree of trust in those communications. If they receive an official communication, they will follow it. That’s different from the media, which they’ll take with a pinch of salt and probably look a bit further.”

Speaking to the BBC, Oleksandr Merezhko, chair of the Ukrainian parliament’s foreign affairs committee and a member of Volodymyr Zelensky’s Servant of the People party, said that the country and its people are prepared for all scenarios.

“It's a very unpleasant fact but it is part of cyber war and people in Ukraine understand that. It is possible and we are quite prepared for such possibilities,” he said.
