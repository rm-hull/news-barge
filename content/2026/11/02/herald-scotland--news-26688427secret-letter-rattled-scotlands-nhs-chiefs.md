Angela Constance has sparked anger inside the NHS after asking the bosses of NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde and NHS Lothian to police their peers.

The Health Secretary reminded Caroline Hiscox, chief executive of NHS Lothian, and Jann Gardner, chief executive of NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde, that they are responsible for providing “grip and control" across Scotland.

She tasked them with coming up with a “support, engagement and performance process” for boards facing “performance challenges”, including the triggers for support and the “escalatory processes” leading ultimately to formal intervention.

One insider described the letter as “outrageous”.

The command came in a letter in which the minister expressed “significant concern” about the “deteriorating performance” of NHS Tayside, NHS Highland and NHS Ayrshire and Arran on waits of more than a year.

The minister's missive was written on September 4, the day after she held a meeting with all of Scotland's NHS chief executives.

Prof Hiscox and Prof Gardner lead the Sub-National Planning and Delivery Committees (SPDCs) for Scotland East and West, which were set up in November last year.

But one source told The Herald that putting ministerial pressure on two chief executives to hold their peers to account for the performance of their boards went far beyond their remit.

They said that this was not what the SPDCs were supposed to do. They said there was no statutory guidance and no basis for this demand.

"And yet, this is what's happening," they added,

![NHSGGC chief executive, Prof Jann Gardner, ordered the revamp](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21366103.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Prof Gardner leads the Sub-National Planning and Delivery Committee West *(Image: Clydebank Post)*

The row comes as tension grows within the NHS over the Scottish Government's plan to cut the number of territorial health boards in Scotland from 14 to two.

The proposals were part of John Swinney's Programme for Government, but unions and insiders say the two-board model has been quietly in development for months, with staff excluded.

Under arrangements introduced by the Scottish Government in November 2025, health boards were directed to organise themselves into the two Committees.

SPDC East is anchored by NHS Lothian, and includes NHS Borders, Fife, Forth Valley, Tayside, Grampian, Highland, Orkney and Shetland.

SPDC West, led by NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde, takes in Lanarkshire, Ayrshire and Arran, Dumfries and Galloway, and the Western Isles.

In a letter sent to the two chiefs in September, and seen by The Herald, the Cabinet Secretary questioned the effectiveness of their oversight, demanding an explanation of their "grip and control".

She then asked them to "develop proposals for a support, engagement and performance process" and to set out the "escalatory processes leading ultimately to the SG support and intervention framework".

Ms Constance wrote: "In our meeting with NHS Chief Executives yesterday following publication of the Programme for Government I reiterated that reducing waiting times remains a key priority for Ministers alongside delivery of public sector reform.

"While activity levels continued to increase, progress in reducing long waits has slowed and the national new outpatient waiting list has not reduced in the latest published data."

She added: "I have now taken the step of writing directly to the Chief Executives of NHS Ayrshire & Arran, NHS Highland and NHS Tayside regarding their deteriorating performance for waits over 52 weeks (based on Board management information).

"I consider this a significant concern and believe it underlines the importance of ensuring issues are identified and addressed at the earliest stage through future joint support and oversight arrangements."

According to the most recent Public Health Scotland data, waits rose in all three boards between March and July. Tayside had the biggest rise in Scotland, with the number waiting more than a year for a first outpatient appointment up from 1,468 to 2,508.

Highland's rose from 951 to 1,469, but has since fallen to 1,122 and Tayside's to 2,064.

Ayrshire and Arran's has risen every month since April, from 319 to 792, and the number waiting more than a year for inpatient or day case treatment has climbed from 808 to 1,054.

Across Scotland, outpatient waits of more than a year fell to 12,683 in September.

In the letter, Ms Constance said there was a need to be clear "about the distinct but complementary roles" the Scottish Government and SPDCs will "play in supporting and driving improvement across the system".

She added: "The Scottish Government will continue to set national priorities, monitor delivery and intervene where necessary, while the sub-national arrangements have a critical role in identifying emerging risks, providing support and challenge, and helping Boards address barriers.

"I want to be clear that this work is intended to complement, rather than duplicate, the existing Support and Intervention Framework.

"The purpose is to establish a more structured and consistent approach to identifying emerging risks, deploying support and challenge, and improving performance at an earlier stage. This should help ensure concerns are recognised and addressed before formal intervention arrangements require to be considered.

"While prompted by current concerns regarding planned care performance, my expectation is that the approach developed should be capable of application across a range of performance areas."

She called on the two to come up with a "structured support offer" for boards "experiencing performance challenges".

"This should include consideration of the triggers for support, the nature of interventions available, and how improvement can be monitored and sustained.

"As stated in the funding letters earlier this year, as Sub-National Leads you are responsible for providing grip and control across the East and West.

"I am keen to understand what your engagement and grip has been thus far. I would like to understand emerging processes that you are developing for monitoring performance and driving improvement when concerns arise and how you identify emerging risks, support Boards to address barriers to delivery, and how you hold them to account for agreed actions and trajectories."

Ms Constance asked the two "to develop proposals for a support, engagement and performance process that reflects the distinct functions of the Sub-National Planning Committees, NHS Boards and the Scottish Government and sets out the escalatory processes leading ultimately to the SG support and intervention framework".

The minister set them a deadline of today, November 2.

"Your support will be critical as we work together to deliver sustained improvements across the system and ensure patients across Scotland receive safe, effective and timely care," she added.

Each board is a separate legal body, each with its own chief executive and board accountable to ministers.

The committees Prof Hiscox and Prof Gardner chair are not boards, and, it is understood, they have no statutory powers over the boards in their areas.

In papers for the most recent board meeting, Fiona Davies, Chief Executive of NHS Highland, said she had "been clear" in communications "that NHS Highland remains a statutory body with unchanged responsibilities until any new arrangements are established, and that rural, island and health inequality considerations must be visible in whatever structure follows."

A Scottish Government spokesperson said: “We are seeing sustained progress in bringing down the longest waits in our NHS. Ministers are clear that each NHS Board must focus their efforts on ensuring people get the care they need as quickly as possible.

“As part of this, the Health Secretary has asked the nominated sub-national planning leads who engage directly with Government each week on behalf of all boards and sub-national delivery committees to work through existing governance arrangements, to drive further progress and reduce long waits. Statutory accountability for NHS Boards remains unchanged.

“Our reforms to NHS boards will simplify accountability, reduce unwarranted variation and remove the current bureaucratic barriers impacting how, when and where patients receive care. We have been clear that we will engage with a wide range of stakeholders, particularly with staff and trade unions, as we develop plans.”
