**Supermarkets in Sweden, Belgium, Luxembourg and Estonia were hit by freezer and fridge problems apparently caused by cyberattacks, forcing some to dispose of spoiled food.** The problems follow a case last month in which attackers remotely took control of refrigeration systems in hundreds of supermarkets and independent stores across France, sending temperatures soaring.**Read More: UK supermarkets warned after French cyber attack spoils food**

In Sweden, ICA Group supermarket chain spokesperson Gustaf Nilsson told news agency TT that “a limited number” of stores were affected overnight by an incident that affected refrigeration and freezing systems. He declined to give a specific number.

The ICA Kvantum branch in Sjobo in southern Sweden wrote on its Facebook page that its fridges and freezers had been hacked and set to 50 degrees Celsius (122 degrees Fahrenheit).

It said it was working to discard the affected items.

**Read more**

The Coop chain said that six of its stores in central Sweden had problems with their fridges and freezers, TT reported, though it didn’t immediately have details.

The Delhaize chain said that an “IT incident” had affected refrigeration systems in several dozen of its stores, mostly in Belgium but also a few in neighbouring Luxembourg.

“In the affected stores, the range of fresh and frozen products is temporarily somewhat limited. Our distribution centres are operating normally, and there has been no disruption to deliveries to our stores,” spokesperson Sarah De Meester said.

The Carrefour supermarket chain in Belgium said a “cyberattack targeting refrigeration installations only had an extremely limited impact”, affecting 15 of its more than 700 stores.

In Estonia, a company that makes the affected technology said that one of its clients had been hit by a cyberattack in which attackers accessed a computer controlling refrigeration and turned up the temperature to 50 degrees Celsius, public broadcaster ERR reported.

Jann Mutso, the chief executive of Kitman Coldtech, declined to say which store’s cooling system had been affected but said that the attack was responded to within minutes and there was no significant damage. The Selver chain said one of its outlets was affected.

Philip James, Partner and Co-head of International Data, Privacy & Cybersecurity Group at Browne Jacobson, commented: “The weaknesses recently exposed in the grocery supply chain, and the run of freezer incidents, are exactly the kinds of risks EU and UK regulators are now tackling. Many of these risks come from connected devices, cloud systems and outsourced IT services. New laws aimed at them include the EU’s NIS2 Directive, Cyber Resilience Act and Critical Entities Resilience Directive, as well as the UK’s PSTI regulations and the proposed Cyber Security and Resilience Bill.”  
  
“This mirrors the risks that the introduction of EU Digital Operational Resilience (DORA) which is aimed at supply chain resilience risk in financial services, but is quite a bit more embedded and mature.  
  
“Retail, food and consumer businesses are now on that same journey; they’re mapping where they’re exposed and who they depend on, and we’re seeing a growing number of clients ask for help reshaping their risk and compliance processes. That’s both to tackle the threats themselves and to keep pace with what has become a significant, overlapping body of cyber regulation.”

The cyberattacks in France appeared to target old systems.
