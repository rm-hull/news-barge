A rare species of bird has bred in the UK for the first time, according to the latest report from the Rare Breeding Birds Panel (RBBP), the independent body that monitors the country’s rarest breeding birds.

A pair of the scarce Blyth's Reed Warblers raised young at a site in the Scottish Highlands in 2024.

The species typically breeds in Scandinavia and Russia, but its arrival as a UK breeding bird had been predicted. Its breeding range has shifted west across Sweden and Norway in recent decades, and singing males have been reported in the UK since 2000.

It is the first time, however, that a pair has been recorded. Blyth’s Reed Warbler is the fourth new confirmed breeding species that RBBP has reported in the last 10 years, following the Iberian Chiffchaff (2015), Black-crowned Night Heron (2017) and Glossy Ibis (2022).

In addition, a new species features in an RBBP report for the first time; two male Red-breasted Flycatchers were reported holding territories in 2024, in woodland in north-east Scotland and Upper Forth, although neither succeeded in attracting a mate.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21658800.jpg?type=mds-article-620)A Black-crowned Night Heron *(Image: PHILIP CROFT/BTO)*

Beyond these additions to the UK’s breeding birdlife, the report offers grounds for optimism - many rare breeding bird populations flourished in 2024. Wetland birds including Eurasian Spoonbill, Eurasian Bittern, Western Cattle Egret, Great White Egret and Little Egret all reached record totals. These increases reflect a combination of conservation measures, particularly wetland management and creation, and, in some cases, the effects of climate change.

Several birds of prey also continued their recovery from historic depletion. Osprey, Marsh Harrier and White-tailed Eagle all reached record totals. Two pairs of Montagu’s Harrier bred, in Suffolk and Wiltshire—the first in the UK since 2020—although neither pair succeeded in raising young. Their return nevertheless eased fears that the species might disappear as a UK breeding bird.

In Scotland, several wader species of northern Europe continued the increasing trends reported in recent years. There were high numbers of Red-necked Phalarope and Wood Sandpipers, three confirmed breeding pairs of Purple Sandpiper was the highest total since 2003, and Temminck’s Stint chicks were recorded in the UK for the first time since 1993.

After two years in which High Pathogenicity Avian Influenza (HPAI) H5N1 affected rare breeding species including Roseate Tern*,* Mediterranean Gull, White-tailed Eagle and Peregrine Falcon, very few cases were reported in 2024. Near-record levels of productivity in the Roseate Tern colony on Coquet Island, Northumberland, suggests that a recovery is underway already.

Dr Mark Eaton, RBBP Secretary, said: “While much of the UK’s wildlife is struggling, we know that appropriate conservation effort can turn trends around. Many of our rarest breeding bird species are benefitting from conservation actions such as site management, habitat creation and legal protection, and flourishing as a result. The monitoring by the RBBP reveals increases in many species.”

Dawn Balmer, British Trust for Ornithology's Head of Surveys and RBBP Chair said: "Thanks to everyone who makes their records available to the Rare Breeding Birds Panel. The majority of records come from birders who submit their records, with breeding evidence, to the network of county bird recorders, who then do a fantastic job of compiling the records to send onto RBBP. The Panel really appreciates all records received, which enables our rare breeding birds to be monitored."

Dr Helen Baker, JNCC representative, added: “The RBBP continues to provide crucial evidence to support conservation actions and the way in which we manage these highly sensitive data has progressed in recent years, thanks to extra funding from JNCC, Natural England, Natural Resources Wales and the RSPB to keep pace with technical developments. Because of this, the ease with which we can analyse the data has improved and we are now able to report even more information about these important species.”

Andrew Stanbury, RSPB representative, said: “Here is some really fantastic news about some of our rarest birds doing really well, many supported by some great conservation and heroic work, but the scale and pace of change is alarming. While we have shown how we can bring back and protect some of our rarest birds, we are at the same time losing the common and widespread birds around us at an accelerating rate.”
