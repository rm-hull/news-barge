*(Image: Cooper Gallery)*## Sophia Yadong Hao, director & principal curator of Cooper Gallery, Dundee**Tell us about your gallery …** Cooper Gallery is Dundee’s longest-running contemporary art gallery, with a history spanning more than 70 years. As the University of Dundee’s largest public-facing gallery, it presents a curated annual programme and has become internationally recognised for its experimental curatorial projects, international outlook and public engagement. It is the only university gallery in Scotland to be part of Plus Tate, a national network of 40 leading visual arts organisations facilitated by Tate. At its heart, Cooper Gallery is a place where artists, publics and community groups come together to share stories, learn from one another, challenge dominant ways of thinking and engage with urgent social and political questions. Key projects include The Ignorant Art School, initiated in 2021, which explores future possibilities of art education in society and celebrates the emancipatory and transformative potential of creative learning.**How do you decide which artists to exhibit?** I’m drawn to artistic practices that are critically rigorous, risk-taking and imaginatively engaged with the urgent social and political questions shaping our lives and how we understand the world. I’m particularly interested in work that opens up complex debates rather than offering simple platitudes or merely illustrating contemporary issues. For me, the most compelling projects create spaces for social encounter and collaborative cultural production, challenging hierarchies and moving beyond a top-down transmission of ideas. Ideally, a project at Cooper Gallery offers a framework that artists, audiences and communities can inhabit, disrupt and challenge, taking it in directions none of us could have anticipated.**Have you noticed shifts in what visitors are responding to?** I’ve noticed a stronger desire for genuine participation and conversation than when I first started here. People still want visually compelling exhibitions, but they also want spaces where they can spend time, encounter others and feel their own experiences matter.**How important is it to balance accessibility with artistic experimentation?** I don’t see accessibility and experimentation as opposites. Some of our most challenging projects have generated the strongest engagement. For me, accessibility means engaging with people’s lived realities and the social and political conditions we share, then finding creative, collaborative ways to open up an artist’s ideas, visual language and chosen media. Through imaginative curatorial approaches, we can create multiple points of entry into complex work and deeper encounters with overlooked histories and marginalised perspectives. I’ve witnessed a growing commitment to accessibility, inclusion, justice and sustainability, alongside important progress in fair pay for artists.**The financial crisis and closure threat facing Cooper Gallery is happening amid severe pressure on universities and creative education. Is this simply about finances, or is something more fundamental at stake?** The crisis is part of a wider hollowing out of the humanities and creative disciplines across Higher Education. This is not simply a budgetary issue; it is political and cultural. Universities are increasingly pressured to justify knowledge through immediate economic return. The result is a deliberate marginalisation of disciplines that cultivate historical understanding, ethical reflection, imagination and dissent. The humanities and creative arts do more than produce graduates. They equip individuals and communities with the ability to question inherited assumptions, challenge entrenched prejudices, and recognise the ideological narratives that shape public life. Cooper Gallery has always offered such a space: connecting art, education and public life, while enabling people to question assumptions and imagine alternatives. That is why attacks on these disciplines should not be understood as isolated fiscal decisions. They are part of a broader cultural shift that treats critical inquiry as expendable and public culture as a luxury. A society that weakens its capacity for critical thought weakens its capacity for democracy itself.**What defines a sustainable gallery today?** Sustainability is not only financial, although funding matters enormously. It also means realistic workloads, properly supporting artists, retaining knowledge, taking environmental responsibility, and nurturing potential and long-term relationships locally and globally. At Cooper Gallery, most programme funding is secured externally through partnerships with public funders, research bodies and other organisations. Our relationship with art education is fundamental. Situated within Duncan of Jordanstone College of Art and Design, the gallery is intrinsic to teaching and learning at the University of Dundee, and plays a critical role in decolonising knowledge production, dissemination and institutional practice. It also acts as a critical and creative hub, bridging academia and the city.**How do digital platforms fit into your strategy?** For Cooper Gallery, rigorously curated digital platforms extend the reach and life of our programme, making it accessible beyond Dundee and creating an enduring archive. While an exhibition may last only a few months, the questions it raises persist. Our digital presence helps these conversations continue across time and place. That said, face-to-face social encounters remain essential. There is something irreplaceable about people coming together and sharing a physical space.**How do you nurture emerging artists while keeping established names in the mix?**

I’m less interested in separating artists into “emerging” and “established” than in creating generative conversations between different generations and practices. Younger artists deserve the same curatorial seriousness as internationally recognised artists: time, research, production support and critical attention. Being based within an art school makes this especially important.

At Cooper Gallery, we create shared spaces for younger practitioners and students to encounter established artists, writers and intellectuals directly, while bringing their own questions and energies into the exchange. Both generations can be challenged and stimulated by the encounter.

**What themes or mediums feel especially relevant now?** Questions of access, environmental sustainability, whose histories are heard and how institutions might operate differently feel especially urgent today, intersecting with ecology, labour, technology, education and social justice. I’m interested in the permeability between mediums, rather than privileging any particular form. For me, what matters most is art’s capacity to connect artistic practice with social reality and civic imagination. Art can equip us with the critical and creative capacity to navigate a world marked by the ongoing genocide in Gaza, brutal wars and escalating humanitarian crises, and more importantly to empower us to confront the resurgence of fascism and the destructive forces of capitalism, while enabling us to imagine alternatives. In an increasingly divided world, I’m particularly interested in collaboration as a medium in its own right: creating connections across differences, challenging entrenched ways of thinking and imagining how we might live together differently.**What public misconception about galleries would you most like to correct?** That you need to know about contemporary art before entering a gallery, or that there is a correct response to an artwork or an artist’s practice. You don’t have to “get” it. You can be moved by it, irritated by it, confused by it or disagree with it completely. All these responses matter, and each can add to the conversation. Galleries become more interesting and relevant when they stop presenting themselves as holders of some kind of cultural authority and instead become spaces where artists and audiences alike can collaborate, participate and contribute to making a better world.**Cooper Gallery is currently hosting Jamming The Ignorant Art School, an experimental exhibition and event project drawing on previous iterations of its The Ignorant Art School events (until December 12)**
