**A campaign group based in Shetland has launched a campaign seeking the "lawful removal" of 103 wind turbines and the restoration of any damage to the island.**

The Sovereign Nation of Shetland (TSNS) is launching a public programme of investigation, accountability and environmental restoration, beginning with the Viking Energy wind farm.

Viking Energy Wind Farm is a 443MW onshore wind farm on Central Mainland Shetland, developed by a partnership involving SSE Renewables and the Shetland Charitable Trust. It comprises 103 Vestas turbines and was completed in 2024 after construction began in 2020. The project can generate around 1.8TWh of electricity annually, enough to power the equivalent of almost 500,000 homes.

The £580 million development is one of the UK's largest onshore wind farms and was key to securing the Shetland HVDC transmission link, which connects the islands to the GB electricity grid.

The group says that the campaign will examine the financial benefits, environmental effects and legal authority behind developments transforming Shetland.

Its declared objective for Viking is the "lawful removal of all 103 turbines and restoration of the affected environment, pursued through evidence and lawful procedures".

The self-styled Sovereign Nation of Shetland has launched an investigation into the environmental impact of Viking Energy Wind Farm, demanding extensive information on turbine blade erosion, the potential release of microplastics and chemical compounds, environmental monitoring, and the project's legal permissions.

The campaign statement says: “Shetland’s natural beauty, clean waters, wildlife, fisheries, crofting and landscapes are an inheritance beyond monetary value.”

In a formal evidence request, the group asked Viking Energy to disclose maintenance and inspection records, details of a turbine blade failure reported near Vidlin in October 2024, and any studies into the environmental effects of blade wear. It is also seeking monitoring data for the Burn of Lunklet and other watercourses, alongside documentation relating to the wind farm's planning consents, land rights and regulatory compliance.

TSNS said that it was "drawing on the spirit of the annual Voar Redd-up, and asked how that same commitment to protecting Shetland can address the effects of large-scale industrial development".

The group claims that Viking began as a joint venture with the people of Shetland and is now "wholly owned by energy giant SSE".

TSNS says it will examine its financial arrangements, including constraint payments, profits and the proportion of benefits remaining in Shetland.

The statement acknowledges approximately £2.1 million in annual community benefit funding, while asking whether the wider arrangements deliver a fair outcome for island households!.

It also asks "why fuel poverty persists in a community that produces electricity for export, while recognising that Viking alone does not determine household electricity prices".

The investigation says it will also examine Shetland Islands Council’s role, the Shetland Partnership and EmPowering Shetland: how development priorities are set, whose interests are represented, and how elected councillors exercise oversight.

TSNS said it intends to publish evidence gathered through the process and has asked for a substantive response within 21 days.

A spokesperson for SSE said: “The issues raised have been extensively addressed through the relevant planning, regulatory and environmental processes. Viking is delivering significant long-term benefits for Shetland, including £80 million spent locally during construction and around £72 million for communities over its lifetime.”
