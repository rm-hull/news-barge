**Every year, hundreds of thousands of people gather to watch the Royal Edinburgh Military Tattoo on metal bleachers high above the Castle Esplanade.**

For decades, a process has been repeated like clockwork. Construction crews build the stadium in May for use in July and August and take it down by October.

It can be an expensive and time-consuming process.

However, Edinburgh-based product manager Kal Turnbull has proposed a solution that has caught the eye of architects and local politicians — the construction of a permanent stone arena.

Speaking to The Herald, Mr Turnbull said the current structure is “polluting in multiple senses”.

“You have the trucks and the cranes bringing in many tonnes of steel, that's causing air pollution,” he said, “while the construction itself is bringing noise pollution.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21652909.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Kal Turnbull says a permanent arena should be built. *(Image: Supplied)*

“And of course, the thing is just ugly, so it brings visual pollution.”

Mr Turnbull has posted several videos about the need for a permanent amphitheatre, raking up more than 2.5 million views on social media — and plenty of critics.

However, he said that a lasting structure would pay for itself and ensure long-term savings.

“Do we just accept this temporary structure that, you know, has half a year of impact every year forevermore?” he remarked.

“Or do we try and do something that embraces it in a way that has year-round benefits?”

A chance meeting with Benjamin Tindall at the Cockburn Association offices led to the city architect throwing his support behind the campaign, drawing up plans for a permanent stone amphitheatre.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21652937.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The arena would be sunk into the ground to avoid disruption to sightlines. *(Image: Benjamin Tindall Achitects)*

“Sinking the arena into ground preserves the city’s famous silhouette,” Mr Tindall’s firm posted on LinkedIn, explaining their drawings.

“It also preserves views of the esplanade with all its memorials and monuments, as well as the views of the castle and of the city. Beneath the arena extensive and accessible facilities can be provided.

“A key element would be maintaining pedestrian and vehicle access at all times.

“The underlying stone is likely to be the fairly soft pinkish stone that much of the Old Town was built with. This stone was of course protected from glacier erosion in the ice ages by the very much harder castle rock.

“With careful excavation, the stone might be suitable for the arena’s construction, and this would meet our ideal of building materials being sourced from within 400 yards.”

Mr Turnbull hopes to meet with Historic Environment Scotland, which manages the site on behalf of the Scottish Ministers, and has already secured the backing of his MSP, Edinburgh North Eastern and Leith’s Ben Macpherson.

Mr Turnbull told The Herald that a permanent structure built into the ground will improve sight lines of the castle during the summer months.

“The architects are very keen to excavate the esplanade and so that the amphitheatre is built such that it doesn't change the silhouette [when viewed from] Princes Street Gardens and Johnston Terrace.

“There'll be some adjustment and so on, but largely the silhouette remains the same with this proposal.”

“It would put Scotland on centre stage,” he added. “This is such a unique location. You don't see many stone amphitheatres getting built around the world.

“This would bring a lot of attention to Scotland in a good way and would show that we're capable of contributing to our built environment.”

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21652926.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The arena would seat around 6,300 people. *(Image: Benjamin Tindall Architects)*

A reduction in seating could prove contentious, as the blueprints call for an arena with 6,330 seats, 28 per cent less than the temporary stadium’s capacity of 8,825.

However, Mr Turnbull said that savings from the approximately £2m in construction costs each year would go “a long way in helping to rebalance” the loss of income from fewer ticket sales.

He said: “The remainder that would have to be rebalanced to have the exact same revenue as 8,800 seats is a slight increase in ticket price and/or having more events throughout the year.

“There are options so that the tattoo has basically no impact financially from a reduction from 8,800 to 6,300 seats.”

Mr Turnbull said that building a new amphitheatre would be preferable to another way of dealing with the status quo, which would be to move the tattoo altogether.

“If you're really exploring all the different ways of solving it, you might consider relocating the tattoo, right?” he asks. “Like this only really happens because of the tattoo.

“But, I think that [the tattoo] is as much part of our heritage as the esplanade itself.

“It's a cultural activity that brings people from around the world and many people enjoy it. The backdrop of the castle is key to that.

“We're not moving it, so what else can we do?”
