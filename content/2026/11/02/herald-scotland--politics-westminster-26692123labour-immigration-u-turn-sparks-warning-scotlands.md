The SNP has accused Labour of "dancing to Nigel Farage's tune" and warned that its immigration reforms could damage Scotland's economy, despite a major U-turn over plans to make migrant care workers wait longer for permanent settlement.

Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood has dropped proposals to make some overseas care workers wait 15 years before they can settle permanently in the UK.

Instead, eligible care workers who arrived before May 2025 and earn at least £25,000 will be able to apply after five years, provided they have met their visa conditions.

The concession will also cover qualifying healthcare and education workers, offering greater security to some international staff working in Scotland's NHS, schools and social care sector.

However, many other migrants will face a ten-year wait, double the current standard qualifying period.

The changes have raised concerns about their impact on Scotland, where employers rely on overseas workers to fill vacancies in care and other industries.

SNP Home Office spokesperson Pete Wishart accused Labour of putting politics ahead of Scotland's economic interests.

The MP said: "The evidence on the multi-billion pound cost of the Labour Party's immigration proposals couldn't be clearer – these proposals will damage Scotland's economy and our public finances, making us all worse off."

Mr Wishart welcomed the decision to ease restrictions on care workers but called for the wider plans to be scrapped.

"While it's positive that the Labour Party has scaled back its crackdown on health and social care workers – after criticism from all sides, including from the SNP – we need to see a full reversal of Labour's costly and chaotic proposals," he said.

"Scotland can't afford a UK Labour Government dancing to Nigel Farage's tune."

The Scottish Government criticised the plans, warning they would unfairly affect people who had already moved to Scotland and built their lives here.

Business and Fair Work Minister Tom Arthur said: "These proposals fail to recognise the vital contribution migration makes to our economy and our communities.

"The decision to retrospectively extend the wait for settlement to those already in the country is not fair reform, it is unnecessarily punitive and is unfair to the people who came to Scotland in good faith and who have worked, contributed, and built their lives here."

He said the Scottish Government had raised concerns with Westminster in February and was disappointed that the Home Office had not engaged with ministers on the details.

Mr Arthur welcomed the concessions for social care workers but said the immigration system must recognise the contribution of migrants across all sectors.

He added: "I urge the UK Government to work with us to build fair and just immigration system that is tailored to Scotland's needs."

The SNP's comments follow research by the Institute for Public Policy Research (IPPR), which estimates that skilled migrants and their adult dependants expected to settle in Britain between 2026 and 2030 would contribute a net £115 billion to public finances over their lifetimes under the previous rules.

The figure is not an estimate of the cost of Labour's reforms.

Separate analysis by the Institute for Fiscal Studies (IFS) has suggested that lower migration forecasts could reduce UK fiscal headroom by £4 billion, while reports citing Treasury sources have put the potential impact at £6.5 billion.

Those figures relate to falling migration forecasts rather than the direct cost of the settlement changes.

The U-turn could offer relief to Scotland's under-pressure social care sector, where employers have become increasingly reliant on overseas staff.

A Scottish Care survey, cited by the Scottish Government, suggested international workers account for more than a quarter of the sector's workforce.

Care providers have warned that tighter immigration rules could worsen staffing shortages, particularly in rural and island communities.

However, the UK Government's ban on recruiting new care workers directly from overseas, introduced in July 2025, remains in place.

The Scottish Government has repeatedly called for that restriction to be lifted.

Ministers have also introduced support to help international care workers who have lost their sponsored jobs find new employers. By August, the scheme had helped 37 employers recruit 120 workers.

Ms Mahmood told MPs that 250,000 care workers and their dependants had entered or switched onto the social care visa route between 2022 and 2025.

Those who have not met their visa conditions will be offered the chance to apply for another five-year visa.

The Home Secretary said "no one will be punished for the sins of their employer".

Announcing the reforms in the Commons on Monday, Ms Mahmood said the immigration system must be "guided above all by fairness".

She insisted: "The right to live in this country must now be earned, it will no longer be automatic."

Under the proposals, migrants earning more than £50,000 could qualify for settlement after five years, while those earning more than £125,000 could qualify after three.

Many other workers will face a ten-year qualifying period.

People arriving by small boat could face waits of up to 30 years for settlement if they do not work or study.

The changes follow strong opposition to the Government's original proposals.

More than 205,000 responses were received during a consultation, with 84% of individual respondents and 79% of organisations opposing the proposed changes.

The Conservatives also criticised the concessions.

Tory MP Chris Philp told MPs that Ms Mahmood had spent a year building a reputation for being tough on immigration, only to dismantle it in "12 short minutes".

The debate comes as UK net migration has fallen sharply, with official figures showing a net increase of 171,000 people in 2025, almost half the previous year's figure.
