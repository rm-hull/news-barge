Dougie Payne was five, maybe six, when he first met David Bowie. In spirit at least. The boy who would grow up to be the bass player with Travis (and is now a children’s author) was at home in Glasgow’s southside when his big sister Gill came in and said she had a tape she wanted him to hear.

“She pressed play and those autumnal golden chords of Changes, the first song on the record, began. And it was like a magic spell,” he recalls when I speak to him at the Wigtown Book Festival in September. “I just sat and listened to the whole record and every single song wasn’t like a song, it was like an atmosphere. It changed the colour of the room. Or changed the temperature. I was a Bowie obsessive from that point on. It was like I came online.”

At the time, Payne admits, he wasn’t really sure what he was listening to. “It was fascinating. I had no idea what he was talking about, making references to things I had no clue about, but it didn’t matter. From Changes right through to the end of The Bewlay Brothers – when it’s the goblin voices, dark and a little bit scary – I was just captivated. Out of all the cultural moments that’s probably the most profound.”

It wasn’t just the music though, he tells me when we speak again a week later. “I loved the songs initially, but I was fascinated by the visuals. I would sit and listen to my sisters’ copies of Aladdin Sane and Scary Monsters once I’d been introduced to them and I would just sit and draw the covers of both those records.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21602643.jpg?type=mds-article-620)David Bowie by Masayoshi Sukita *(Image: Masayoshi Sukita)*

“It was like entering a world and I was just completely obsessed. The first record I bought when I was 11 was Let’s Dance. Up to that point he’d been my sister's. Let’s Dance came out and it was a new thing and it was mine. I went to HMV on Union Street and bought the album. I wanted to get into every aspect of him and the brilliant thing about him in 1983, 84, is he was everywhere.”

You could argue that today, more than 40 years since Payne bought his copy of Let’s Dance in Glasgow, Bowie still is.

Bowie was born David Jones in 1947. He summarised his own future in the lyrics of that Hunky Dory track, The Bewlay Brothers. Chameleon (undoubtedly), comedian, Corinthian (there was a time in the 1970s when Bowie was the definition of dissolute) and caricature (the Bowie and Jagger Dancing in the Streets Street video perhaps). He was the Starman, the Man who fell Who Fell to Earth, an alien visitation, perhaps. He was musically, visually, even perhaps sexually fluid. He declared himself bisexual in 1972 (though he’d later recant this statement). With Ziggy Stardust he reinvented the idea of what a pop star could be. With Blackstar, his final album, released just two days before his death in 2016, he found the most remarkable way to say goodbye.

It is now 10 years since David Bowie’s death, but he remains a vital presence in the culture. And in the minds and record collections of Dougie Payne and millions of others.

Next January would have been Bowie’s 80th birthday and it will be marked by a number of events on both sides of the Atlantic. In New York there will be a new photography exhibition at the Morrison Hotel Gallery. The Rock & Roll Hall of Fame in Cleveland is planning a Bowie80 celebration and in Copenhagen next spring there will be the world premiere of BOWIE (breaking) GLASS, a new live symphonic concert combining Bowie’s songs (and voice) with “a new musical and visual dialogue with Philip Glass.” Whatever that might be.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21628026.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Costume designed by Freddie Burretti for David Bowie on The David Bowie Tour of 1973 *(Image: Yili Liu)*

Closer to home David Bowie: You’re Not Alone, the immersive multimedia exhibition that has been on show at the Lightroom in London is touring to Manchester, Brighton and Hamburg in 2027.

But before all of these Dundee will take centre stage in Bowie’s posthumous memoria. On November 4 David Bowie: On Tour opens at the V&A Dundee. Bringing together somewhere in the region of 100 items from the V&A’s Bowie archive, it promises to be a celebration of the man and his art.

“He is a creative in the fullest sense of the word,” suggests Svetlana Panova, assistant curator at the V&A Dundee. “The way he’s across music, visual arts, performance is incredible. He had quite a genius way of piecing them together.”

There’s a German word for this, isn’t there? “Gesamtkunstwerk,” Panova confirms. A totality, a universal work of art. Bowie’s career is that.

Panova speaks of him in the present tense and maybe that’s appropriate. He is still alive to so many all these years after his death, if not in life then in his art.

“He means a lot of different things to different people,” Panova agrees. “For some people it’s their personal memories of growing up and finding who they are. For some people it’s his artistry, his image. He was quite unapologetically himself. He broke some societal and gender boundaries before many other performers did and I think that’s really built his legacy.”

What can we expect of the exhibition? Costumes, musical instruments, handwritten lyrics, notes, scripts for some of his movies with hand-written handwritten annotations, Panova explains. “The exhibition is incredibly personal, I would say.”

It includes Bowie’s guitar from the Ziggy era, his sketches for the Ashes to Ashes video, the clapperboard used on Nic Roeg’s film The Man Who Fell to Earth.

It also gives us a way into another aspect of the Bowie story; a tenuous one at first glance, but perhaps more persistent than you might think. The story of Bowie in Scotland.

November 7, 1969: David Bowie starts his first headlining tour at the Salutation Hotel in Perth. Bowie arrives in his second-hand Rover (he sat in the back, sometimes cross-legged, strumming his guitar).

After Perth he will go on to play the Community Centre in Auchinleck, the Grand Hall in Kilmarnock, the Kinema Ballroom in Dunfermline, the Adam Smith Hall in Kirkcaldy, the Caley Picture House in Lothian Road in Edinburgh and the Caird Hall in Dundee. But half of the remaining Scottish gigs are cancelled due to lack of sales.

A couple of months later he returns to record a solo appearance on the Grampian TV show Cairngorm Ski Night where he is backed by the show’s resident musicians The Alex Sutherland Band. The tape is later wiped.

Hamish Hawk is standing halfway down Drummond Street in Edinburgh. The singer-songwriter who has just finished recording his latest album Intriguer/Tenderness (out next April) has joined me to explore Bowie’s Edinburgh past. He wasn’t aware that Bowie is reputed to have spent a few days here in the early 1970s.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21628029.jpg?type=mds-article-620)David Bowie's passport issued in 1988, part of V&A David Bowie: On Tour, a tour of around 100 highlights from David Bowie’s archive *(Image: David Parry/PA Media Assignments)*

There’s some debate about this. Was it number 17? Number 34? Was it Drummond Street at all?

Whatever the truth, there’s no question that he was in Edinburgh in 1969 for a few days after the cancellation of his tour before the Lothian Road gig, according to Kevin Cann’s book Any Day Now, which charts Bowie’s life on a day-to-day basis from 1947 to 1974. Bowie then returned to Edinburgh in January 1970 with Angie, his partner and soon-to-be-first wife (that was definitely Drummond Street, according to Cann), staying and working with dancer and choreographer Lindsay Kemp (Kemp had been Bowie’s dance tutor and perhaps his lover).

The legend goes that Bowie first wore make-up onstage for a gig at the Edinburgh College of Art. Ziggy, you could maybe claim, was born in Scotland.

Growing up a few miles away in Fairmilehead in the 1990s, Hawk, like Dougie Payne, became a fan when he first heard Bowie’s 1971 album Hunky Dory. Like Payne, he uses the word magic to describe the experience.

“For me as a lyricist when I first came across David Bowie I was like, ‘What is this magic thing?’” he recalls. “‘The girl with the mousy hair.’ It's so imagistic and you could say surrealistic. But it also feels hyper-real at times as well. ‘Take a look at the lawman beating up the wrong guy. Oh, man, look at those cavemen go …’ It’s colourful and very vivid and – this is something I think I learned from Bowie – it brings together lots of images and smashes them off each other to see what sparks fly.”

You can probably draw a line between that childhood experience and Hawk sitting down to write the lyrics of his song The Mauritian Badminton Doubles Champion, 1973, which combines Christopher Wren, Varanasi, Pulp’s Common People and ballpoint pens.

Bowie in Scotland. It’s a story that takes in Gordonstoun, where he sent his son Duncan Jones to board as a kid. (Indeed, Jones spent much of his childhood in a flat in Cornhill Court in Aberdeen with his nanny Marion Skene. It’s where he developed a taste for rowies.) Port Seton also has Bowie links. The singer was friendly with the painter John Bellany and hugely admiring of his work. He would often visit Bellany’s hometown to see the landscape that inspired the artist.

And, of course, this is a story about the Scots Bowie met.

July 6, 1973: Lulu visits Bowie at the Hyde Park Hotel to discuss the Glaswegian singer recording a cover of Bowie’s The Man Who Sold The World, produced by its creator. The recording takes place on July 16. The subsequent single will get to number three in the charts in January 1974. In her memoir, If Only You Knew, released last year, Lulu reveals that she and Bowie became lovers at the time. He seduced you, I suggest when I interview her for The Herald Magazine. “Easy to do, let me tell you,” she admits, laughing.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21122702.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Concept artwork for New Music Night and Day, the working title for David Bowie's album that was to become Low in 1972. *(Image: Sarah Duncan)*

Lulu was not the only Scot who came into Bowie’s orbit. Perhaps the most notable was the Edinburgh-born guitarist Ricky Gardiner who played on Bowie’s 1977 album Low and then on Lust For Life, Iggy Pop’s album produced by Bowie.

The Caithness-born designer Ken Calder, creator of Aero Leather Clothing, meanwhile, has claimed he sold Bowie the leather jacket worn on the cover of his 1977 album Heroes. Annie Lennox famously duetted with Bowie on Under Pressure during the Freddie Mercury tribute concert at Wembley Stadium in 1992.

Bowie, a lover of art, also greatly admired the work of the Scottish painter Peter Howson. He bought two of Howson’s most controversial paintings made by the artist during his time as the official war artist in Bosnia. Bowie even agreed to sit for the artist. “I put him on a plinth with a big drawing board,” Howson tells me. “He just sat on that, but he fell asleep and he fell off. It was quite a fall.”

But ultimately the story of Bowie and Scotland is perhaps less about these physical encounters and more about his viral influence. It is the story of how an artist’s work can seep into the cultural water and can then go on to nurture the art of subsequent generations. It’s there in the music of Travis and Hamish Hawk, after all.

But his influence can be found in more than just music. The world of modern dance for a start. Speak to Joan Clevillé, the Catalan artistic director of Scottish Dance Theatre, and he is more than happy to acknowledge the influence of Bowie on his work.

“I love theatricality, I love playfulness onstage,” he explains. “I love some of the boldness of the costume designs. I also love this fullness. It’s the whole thing; the idea that it’s not the pure music or the pure dance steps or the pure design, the pure costumes, it’s all of that informing each other and influencing each other and I love that.”

Another example of Gesamtkunstwerk you might say.

“When you see the make-up, the costumes, the hair,” Clevillé says of Bowie, “it’s brilliant and breaking boundaries. And also making the body really front and centre. Some of the crazy shapes. The body is completely distorted by the shape of the design and the costumes. And that’s stuff that choreographers and designers are absolutely playing with nowadays. It’s super-relevant.”

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21628095.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Costume designed by Natasha Korniloff for David Bowie in the Ashes to Ashes music video, 1980, part of V&A David Bowie: On Tour, *(Image: David Parry/PA Media Assignments)*

When he joined Scottish Dance Theatre in 2009 Clevillé found himself working with associate director Sally Owen. Previously, she had performed with Lindsay Kemp. So you can trace a direct line from Bowie to contemporary Scottish dance. And that’s before you mention the great Scottish dancer and choreographer Michael Clark who has never been shy of admitting his debt to the Thin White Duke.

Clevillé is happy to admit to his own.

“As a young person coming out as a gay man I knew about his gender ambiguity, his fluidity. He was also part of my system of references that I reached out to when the more normative options didn’t feel quite right for me. Seeing them enacted in such a bold, brilliant, joyful way … It was definitely important to me in my growth.”

Scratch any Scottish creative, it seems, and a Bowie story will leak out. The Scottish speculative fiction writer Lorraine Wilson -– author of The Salt Oracle and We Are Ghosts in the Forest -– grew up listening to Bowie and is happy to admit his work has fed into her own.

“It’s hard to know where the line is, for him. He is not someone I consciously reference in my work, but I would say he was formative to my early understanding of risk-taking and rule-breaking in creativity, of using yourself and your art to speak about the world and its future. More recently, listening to old interviews of him talking about craft and creative integrity, I’ve been uplifted by his clarity of purpose. He was part of a tapestry of creatives working in writing, art and music, who taught me how wild and unapologetic art can be, and that the best art says something unexpected about the world.”

In short, we are all Bowie’s children. Or should be.

“If we are going to imagine braver futures, we ourselves need to be braver in the stories we tell and the expectations we defy,” Wilson adds. “He is a benchmark for that creative courage. I think his presence and his art left an indelible mark on creatives in the UK, whether we notice it or not. But I hope we notice, and remember. He deserves that.”

That remembering continues in Dundee next month. As part of the Bowie On Tour exhibition, Svetlana Panova points out, there will be a section dedicated to Bowie’s appearances in the city. There will be photographs and even a ticket of his Caird Hall appearance in 1973. She shows me some Polaroids of Bowie taken on the night. “They’re so close, they’re incredible,” she says, clearly thrilled.

September 3, 2002: Dougie Payne gets to meet his hero at the GQ Men of the Year Awards at the Natural History Museum in London. “We were getting Band of the Year and he was just getting an award for being David Bowie. We were sitting and Coco Schwab, his assistant, came up to the table. I was starstruck at Coco. She said ‘David would like to meet you.’ And I was like ‘Oh no.’ I immediately started shaking. She led us into his area. He was talking to the designer Paul Smith. And I went up and I went, ‘Smith and Jones!’ It was a terrible start, but after that he was so lovely.”

Bowie in Scotland. It’s a story of yesterday and today. And, as Lorraine Wilson suggests, tomorrow too.

Hamish Hawk agrees. “What’s his legacy? He was such a big character. He totally exploded out of his records onto the screen. There was nobody like him whatsoever. He seems to me utterly singular.

“I think he was partially responsible for defining the outer limits of what a performer and what a musician could be. How they present, how they dress, how they think about their cover art, how they think about their track listing.

“He turned that into real art. He thought about it as art and for me his legacy is that -– going undaunted, confident, unequivocal, unshakeable, out into the world with this hair, with this make-up, with these songs, with these words.

“You see him on chat shows and he looks like he’s from the future. And he was, he was.”
