The founders of Spark York fear they may have to move out of the city if they can’t secure a long-term site here.

The box park, featuring food and drink businesses, start-ups and community initiatives, has permission to stay on Piccadilly until 2030.

After that, it’s been proposed to find them a location amid the £2 billion York Central development behind the station.

But that future isn’t assured, say Spark York co-founders Sam Leach and Tom McKenzie.

They said: “Staying to 2030 is fantastic for safeguarding jobs, economic opportunity and footfall while Piccadilly continues to change, and we have always said we would be here for as long as we are needed.

“We are still really interested in moving to York Central but we are yet to understand where exactly we fit in the developers’ plans.

“We’d love to stay in York for as long as we’re needed and keep adding value.

“Our focus is also on regeneration opportunities across the region, where our social enterprise model can excel. We are realistic about York Central.

![](https://wp.theyorkmix.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/spark-york-5th-birthday-2.jpg)

“We’re a small player on a very big site, and despite positive conversations so far, nothing is guaranteed. If there isn’t a home for Spark on York Central or elsewhere after 2030, we may have to leave York.

“We desperately don’t want that, but we have to plan for all eventualities and the business will grow regardless.”

They say Spark has had a hugely positive effect on York since it opened in 2018.

Last year, it attracted 430,000 visitors and its independent businesses traded over £5m.

More than 65 businesses have launched, and 26 have set up permanent premises after starting at Spark.

![](https://wp.theyorkmix.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/spark-york-construction-main.jpg)

“We’d love to see independent businesses central to Piccadilly’s future, though we can’t control whether that includes us,” Sam and Tom said.

They want to expand what they do. “Live music and later events matter to York’s cultural offer, and being culturally significant in this city matters a lot to us,” say Sam and Tom.

“We can’t do live music events on Piccadilly and we don’t. Other operators on Parliament Street do them regularly.

“We’d love the same room to programme that others in the city centre have. A purpose-built indoor venue could host amplified events in a way a container site close to neighbours can’t. Wherever we are, we’d continue to work respectfully and closely with neighbours.”

So what happens if the York Central plans don’t materialise?

They said: “We have no intention of Spark as a business stopping in 2030. The question for us is whether that’s in York.

“We are from here and we love this city. We’ve spent eight years honing a model that works by giving independent businesses an affordable first step, a venue that pays its way without extracting profit, and a social enterprise that puts its surplus back into the community.

“Other places have asked us to bring this model to them, on much longer-term commitments, which we hope will be transformational.

“We want to build the best-run, most useful, most joyful and impactful community venues in the country, in York and beyond, and to prove that a community-led model can regenerate high streets and fill gaps in places that others have written off.

“Whatever happens on Piccadilly or York Central, Spark will keep evolving and finding new ways to back small businesses and bring people together.

“York is where we started and where we’d most like to stay and we’ll continue to make the case for this being a long-term home, but 2030 won’t be the end of the road for us.”
