People who regularly drink beer and spirits may have a significantly higher risk of developing a sudden acute kidney injury, a new study shows.

Drinking 24 servings of beer a week was tied to a 14.8 percent increased risk of acute kidney injury compared to people who drank three, researchers at England’s Anglia Ruskin University found. A pint of beer is around three units.

People who drank nine servings of spirits were at a 25.5 percent higher risk than those who drank one 25-milliliter servings, too, the 14-year review showed.

The increased risk was seen even after accounting for liver disease and a wide range of other health and lifestyle factors, the university said.

The findings challenge the assumption that alcohol-related kidney injury mainly occurs as a result of liver disease, suggesting alcohol may have a direct effect on the kidneys.

![Regularly drinking larger amounts of certain alcohol is tied to a higher risk of sudden kidney injury, researchers say](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/20/16/16/GettyImages-2305762514.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“While alcohol has traditionally been linked with kidney injury through its impact on the liver, our research suggests that spirits and beer may contribute directly to kidney damage through inflammatory pathways,” lead author Dr. Rudolph Schutte, an associate professor of cardiovascular epidemiology, said in a release.

What about wine? Well, drinking fewer than 14 servings a week of either red or white wine did not raise the risk.

But the findings don’t prove wine protects the kidneys, and drinking above 14 servings of alcohol a week - the recommended limit in the U.K., which is equivalent to seven pints of lager and 14 pours of spirits - was tied to higher risk for all drink types.

Consuming any alcohol has been tied to a heightened risk of cancer and other negative health outcomes, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

The research assessed the health of more than 250,000 adults aged 40-69 who were a part of the U.K. Biobank study, relying on patients to assess their own habits.

## What to know about acute kidney injury

Acute injury occurs when the kidneys can no longer filter out waste in the blood, sometimes leading to fluid in the lungs, muscle weakness, permanent damage and death, according to the Mayo Clinic.

The serious condition - which can start in just hours - can be caused by kidney damage, slowed blood flow and when kidney drainage tubes are blocked.

![Drinking fewer than 14 servings of wine was not tied to a heightened risk of acute kidney injury - but drinking more than 14 servings of any alcohol weekly was tied to a higher risk](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/20/16/09/GettyImages-2248432845.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Blood flow is reduced due to heart and liver problems, allergic reactions and burns. dehydration, and from taking medicines such as aspirin and ibuprofen.

Kidney stones, blood clots, an enlarged prostate and multiple forms of cancer can lead to blocked drainage tubes.

Rates of hospitalization for acute kidney injury are rising in the U.S., CDC research published in 2018 shows. Roughly 13 million people around the world develop an acute kidney injury annually, researchers said.

Acute kidney injury often occurs in patients already in the hospital, with people who suffer from long-term medical problems and those over age 65 at higher risk.

Some cases have no symptoms, but others can experience fatigue, nausea, confusion, chest pain, seizures, coma and swelling in the legs, ankles and around the eyes, according to Virginia’s Richmond Nephrology Associates.
