Among the grass, sagebrush and tumbleweeds on a cattle ranch in Rawlins, Wyoming, workers have been building roads and pads for 600 wind turbines. Meanwhile, emission lines are being strung from the site to a hub in Nevada, where the electricity generated can help power Las Vegas and Los Angeles.

It will be one of the biggest wind farms in the US, a win for the economy and the climate. But it took 18 years to get the approvals to start building. Even less complex projects now typically wait five years to connect to the grid.

These delays are the target of a 400-page permitting reform bill backed by Republicans and US President Donald Trump as well as many Democrats. It has just passed the Senate and will now be taken up by the House of Representatives.

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, or BAAJA, would make it far easier to build renewable energy projects and transmission lines. One of its authors, long-time climate champion Senator Sheldon Whitehouse, says it would be the country’s “biggest implemented climate bill”.

But BAAJA would also speed up permitting for natural gas plants and pipelines, coal mines and data centres, while weakening environmental regulations. The American Petroleum Institute has even sent a BAAJA-branded food truck to hand out free tacos on Capitol Hill. The climate movement is split. Some experts fear the bill would increase greenhouse gas emissions, and former Vice President Al Gore called it a “serious mistake”.

Both sides say BAAJA is a gamble, but supporters say it’s one worth taking. Jigar Shah, who formerly oversaw infrastructure loans at the US Department of Energy, says it would set up a race between clean energy and fossil fuels. “The world’s best energy developers right now are in the clean energy space,” he says. “Why wouldn’t I bet on the world’s best energy developers?”

Climate policies put the world on track for a catastrophic 2.6°C of warming by 2100, and greenhouse gas emissions are not yet falling, partly because additional renewable energy supply has been outpaced by additional demand from data centres. Data centre energy demand will double by 2030, and in the US, 60 per cent of that demand will be met by natural gas, according to the International Energy Agency.

The US grid, which is fractured into three separate grid regions and seven electricity markets, needs to speed up the build-out of renewables and transmission lines from the sunny, windy areas in the central US to the population centres near the coasts. But grid operators and regulators are overwhelmed, developers disagree on who should pay for transmission lines and court battles can last years. As a result, more than 2000 gigawatts of electricity generation and storage projects are awaiting grid connection, most of them renewable.

BAAJA would allow the federal government to override states’ objections and force grid regions to plan transmission lines together. But it would also narrow the timelines and reasons for challenging projects under environmental laws like the Clean Water Act, and, in some instances, cut lower district courts out of these cases. It essentially gives up tools to stop fossil fuel projects in exchange for tools to accelerate clean energy ones.

Environmentalists like Bill McKibben have warned that accelerated pipeline construction could lock in fossil fuel demand, and weaker protections would disempower concerned residents.

“Why would we choose now to give the fossil fuel industry what it’s wanted forever?” says David Watkins at the Union of Concerned Scientists. “Compromise will be required; I just think the balance here is off.”

Following this month’s election sweep, Democrats will take control of Congress in January and could potentially renegotiate the bill. But Trump would probably veto it, and even when the party controlled both Congress and the White House in 2022, it failed to pass a proposed permitting reform bill.

“You have a lot of newly elected oil-and-gas-state Democrats,” cautions Jack Andreasen Cavanaugh at Columbia University in New York.

Think tanks have projected that although the bill would increase liquefied natural gas exports, it would boost the build-out of renewables more than coal and gas plants. This would eventually reduce electricity prices by 3 to 5 per cent and US annual emissions by 0 to 4 per cent, depending on the future costs of clean tech and fossil fuels, according to Rhodium Group.

But modelling by Kevin Kircher at Purdue University in Indiana found that BAAJA would raise US annual emissions by 2 per cent. While energy demand will increase in any case, he argues the bill would increase demand still further, due in part to the Jevons paradox. This is when a decline in the cost of a resource leads buyers to consume more of it. For instance, if electricity prices did go down under BAAJA, cryptocurrency mining operations might run for longer periods or install more hardware.

“Unless all of that additional new electricity demand is met by clean energy, if even 10 per cent of it is met by fossil fuels, then emissions increase overall,” says Kircher. “People are severely underestimating the amount of new fossil fuel combustion that we will also see as a result of BAAJA.”
