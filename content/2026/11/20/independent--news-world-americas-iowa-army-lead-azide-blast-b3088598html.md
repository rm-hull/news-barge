A last-minute legal challenge and local anger have forced the U.S. Army to push back the controlled destruction of a degraded lead compound stored in Iowa.

The blasts were due Friday at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown. Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle announced on X that the detonation would instead take place on Monday. He described Monday as the latest possible moment before a forecast temperature drop that he said would "risk further destabilization of the material."

The compound at the center of the dispute is lead azide, which is used in detonators. The office of Iowa U.S. Senator Chuck Grassley had said the Army was concerned a spontaneous explosion of the material would be "catastrophic" and saw two controlled blasts as the safest way to dispose of the stockpile.

![A sign identifying the facility sits inside a fence surrounding the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant on November 19, 2026 in Middletown](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/20/10/2305973751..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The Army uncovered the degraded lead azide in March, Telle said, and began informing local officials about the danger it posed. Some people in the area said they had only recently learned about it.

On Thursday, the Iowa Environmental Council and Sierra Club filed an emergency motion with the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals seeking to halt an EPA permit that allows the destruction of 68 drums of the compound. The groups argued the disposal method would disperse toxic lead dust over a broad area of Iowa and potentially Illinois.

They also argued the EPA had failed to require sufficient safeguards or adequately evaluate alternatives while denying the public a meaningful opportunity to comment. The EPA did not immediately respond to a Reuters request for comment.

![Some schools in the path of the plume have canceled classes and residents have been urged to leave their homes](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/20/11/2305973674..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The permit it issued imposed numerous conditions, including public notifications, blast-containment measures, and corrective action for any contamination resulting from the blasts. Opposition had been building among local residents, some of whom had been advised to leave their homes during the operation.

They complained that people were given insufficient warning. They also said the Army and local and federal officials had not responded to their concerns. A small group staged a protest on the highway near the facility on Wednesday, and some local politicians urged a postponement.
