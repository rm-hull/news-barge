People who eat and sleep later have a higher risk of metabolic syndrome, a new study finds.

Obese people who tended to stay up and eat later were “significantly more likely” to exhibit traits associated with the condition, medical researchers at UTHealth Houston said.

The findings suggest that the mismatch between the body’s internal clock - also known as circadian rhythms - and daily behavior may contribute to disease risk through changes to bacteria in the gut and intestines.

“We have long recognized the importance of what we eat for metabolic health, but we are increasingly learning that when we eat may also matter,” Dr. Faraz Bishehsari, director of the Gastroenterology Research Center at McGovern Medical School, explained in a statement.

“What makes this study interesting is that it connects circadian behaviors with the gut microbiome, intestinal barrier function, and systemic inflammation,” he said.

![Eating and sleeping later puts people at a greater risk of metabolic syndrome, medical researchers say](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/20/21/03/iStock-1676004222.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The study analyzed the health and patterns of 87 adults with obesity, finding that those with disrupted sleep and eating patterns had increased signs of “leaky gut.”

Leaky gut refers to cracks in the intestinal lining that allow toxins, food and bugs to pass through into the bloodstream, causing digestive problems and inflammation, Harvard Health noted.

The researchers also tested whether improving the gut microbiome - trillions of species that live in our stomachs and intestines - could help to strengthen the intestinal lining.

A better diet and the use of probiotics helped raise levels of beneficial bacteria, suggesting the microbiome may connect metabolic health and how people sleep and eat.

“We know that what we eat matters for our health, but our findings suggest that when we eat is also important,” Bishehsari said.

![The study findings suggest that the mismatch between the body’s internal clock and daily behavior may contribute to disease risk](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/20/21/46/iStock-1487080796.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Because modern lifestyles increasingly challenge natural sleep and eating rhythms, the findings could help lead to new strategies for preventing or managing metabolic syndrome-related conditions, he noted.

“Future research will help determine whether therapies aimed at improving circadian alignment or strengthening the gut microbiome can reduce inflammation and lower the risk of metabolic disease,” Bishehsari said.

About a third of U.S. adults suffer from the cluster of five risk factors that make up metabolic syndrome, according to the Cleveland Clinic.

The conditions include obesity and excess abdominal weight, low levels of good cholesterol, high blood sugar and high blood pressure levels and levels of triglycerides - fat in the blood - that are over 150 milligrams per deciliter of blood.

These conditions raise the risk for chronic health problems, such as cardiovascular disease, stroke and Type 2 diabetes.

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in the U.S., resulting in nearly 920,000 annually.
