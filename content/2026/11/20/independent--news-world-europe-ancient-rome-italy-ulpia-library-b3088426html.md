One of the ancient world's great repositories of texts is reopening to the public in Rome this week, after restoration work brought it back from a century of inaccessibility.

The ruins of the Ulpia library are located in Emperor Trajan’s Forum, beside the iconic column that celebrates his victorious campaigns against the Dacians and the Roman conquest of what is modern-day Romania.

“After the Alexandria Library of Egypt, after the Library of Pergamon in Asia Minor, this one in Trajan’s Forum was certainly one of the most important, given both the number of ‘volumina,’ as books were called in ancient times, and the quantity of documents," said Claudio Parisi Presicce, an archaeologist and Rome's superintendent for cultural heritage.

The place was in a state of ruin when discovered in 1932 during clearing works by Benito Mussolini's government for the road that became the famous Via dei Fori Imperiali.

Later, it was covered in concrete and used as a storage room for some of the countless pieces from Trajan's Forum, the last and greatest of the imperial fora, built in 112 AD.

The niches in its walls were evidently spots to set cabinets for scrolls, signalling that this was one of the two, two-story buildings comprising the Ulpia library referenced in ancient texts.

![The entrance to the western library of Trajan's Forum, which archaeologists believe may have housed the ancient Bibliotheca Ulpia, one of ancient Rome's most important libraries](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/20/Italy_Ancient_Roman_Library_31513.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

But the niches in its walls were evidently spots to set cabinets for scrolls, signalling that this was one of the two, two-story buildings comprising the Ulpia library referenced in ancient texts, Presicce said.

Archaeologists from Rome's superintendency for cultural heritage identify it as the Ulpia library's western building, which ancient texts indicate stored Greek-language scrolls.

The new permanent exhibition offers visitors an idea of the space as it used to be, featuring the remains of some of the library’s rich marble decorations — white for the walls, and different colours for its floor and columns. Immersive videomapping is also projected on its walls, showing the history of the library and its discovery.

Instead of scroll-filled cabinets, spread throughout the space today are broken Corinthian columns and pieces of sculptures.

![Archaeologist Lucia Cianciulli shows a marble frieze-architrave that was part of the western library of Trajan's Forum](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/20/Italy_Ancient_Roman_Library__55_5.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The library was a fundamental part of the Forum, and a place that for centuries attracted citizens from all over Rome with its vast collection of edicts and documents.

“The libraries were used by both common citizens, those who could afford a certain cultural level and a better education than those from the lower classes, but also by magistrates who needed access to older documents, or needed to check the norms that regulated the life of the community,” he said.

Restoration started in 2025, and lasted about a year. The 1.75-million-euro cost ($1.92 million) came from the European Union’s pandemic recovery funds — just one of many restoration projects Rome is carrying out across the city with EU money.

It will initially be open to visitors only on Fridays, Saturdays and Sundays. Visits are scheduled every 40 minutes, 20 people at a time.

The library's eastern building remains buried beneath an 18th-century church, on the other side of Trajan's Forum, and stores Latin-language scrolls. There are no plans for its excavation.
