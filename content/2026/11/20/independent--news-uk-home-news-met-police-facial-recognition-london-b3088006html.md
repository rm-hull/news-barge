A new facial recognition pilot in London allows Metropolitan Police officers to use their phones to photograph and identify suspects without their consent.

During the six-month pilot, which began on November 17, 100 officers can use the technology to check faces against a database of about one million custody images to identify suspected offenders or people they believe may be at risk.

Scotland Yard said officers must tell people before taking their photo, but do not need their permission. Officers will also not use force if someone covers their face.

The Met will review the pilot after six months before approving a wider rollout.

Commander Nick John said the app would help officers identify people faster.

He said: “If a person is not willing or able to confirm their identification, or we believe they might not be telling the truth, then the officer can take a photo on their Met-issued phone, take that photo, upload it into the app, and then the app will search against our custody images.”

![One hundred officers are involved in the six-month pilot](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/26/18/38/iStock-92277168.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

In the first 24 hours, officers uploaded 15 photos, which Mr John said was “more than I was expecting”.

But he stressed: “We are not going to be going around London taking photos of people from a distance who aren’t suspected of being involved in any offending.”

Critics say the technology could disproportionately affect ethnic minorities, and have raised concerns about racial bias and the increased use of police surveillance.

Liberty director Akiko Hart warned the Met is putting “powerful surveillance technology in officers’ pockets”.

She said: “Operator-Initiated Facial Recognition combines all of the problems of stop-and-search with those of facial recognition.

“There is already evidence that these devices are being used disproportionately against marginalised communities.”

![The government has promised tougher protections to ensure police use of facial recognition technology is ’fair, safe and proportionate’](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/23/09/462ca8d428d5d5a1f6132b3ceb05e692Y29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzkyNzY1OTgz-2.81350127.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Habib Kadiri, executive director of StopWatch UK, said: “We’ve seen no evidence that enough has been done to mitigate inherent racial bias in any form of facial recognition technology so far, and we suspect that ethnic minorities will not feel safer knowing that an operator who represents an organisation known to be institutionally racist will be tasked with the responsible use of it.”

But Susan Hall, leader of the London Conservatives, said: “If this technology helps improve the speed and efficiency of officers, then we should look to embrace it more widely.”

Scotland Yard said the pilot will not be used on under-18s and the wider programme will exclude under-13s.

The pilot comes just weeks after the government promised tougher protections to ensure police use of facial recognition technology is “fair, safe and proportionate”.

Ministers also announced plans for a new watchdog to oversee the technology, with powers potentially including banning forces from using it if they fail to meet standards.
