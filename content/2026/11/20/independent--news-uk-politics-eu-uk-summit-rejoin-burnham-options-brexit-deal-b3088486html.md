The EU has been told that Andy Burnham will put forward four simple options for the UK to decide on the future relationship with the bloc – with one already seen as a bad idea.

A senior EU source said they expect the prime minister to put forward the current status quo, becoming part of the customs union, joining the single market or rejoining the bloc altogether when he opens a national conversation on the issue.

The need to have a year-long national conversation on the issue means, in their view, that an early election in May is unlikely because it would not give Mr Burnham time to come down on a position.

But, having heard his speeches at Mansion House this week and the Labour Party conference in September, the EU is clear that Mr Burnham is against the status quo option.

“We assume the status quo option will be one where he's not massively keen on and then you have customs union, single market or rejoin,” the source said.

![Andy Burnham speaks with EU president Ursula von der Leyen](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/13/14/01M78D2VJPQP9KRMFJX8NZXD93.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

But while EU officials and diplomats are making it clear that they will leave the conversation on Brexit to the UK to work out for itself, the pros and cons of the four options are clear.

## 1. Status quo

Essentially this means that the UK stays outside the EU, its customs union and single market and continues to negotiate mini deals for better access and resets. It certainly is what is favoured by Lord Michael Gove and his new Stay Out group.

**Pros:** The UK continues to be able to set its own regulations in important areas like artificial intelligence and tech and can keep trade deals it has negotiated since Brexit including the 11-country Comprehensive and Progressive Trans Pacific Partnership (CPTPP).**Cons:** It remains locked out of its biggest market, the EU, with no prospect of influencing rules and continued underperformance with the economy, as well as travel restrictions.**The Burnham view:** Andy Burnham has made clear that he thinks the status quo has led to a decade of “division and low growth” while it has “lowered Britain’s standing in the world.”

![Michael Gove is leading Stay Out](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/22/2297503183..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

## 2. The Customs Union

Joining the customs union is the Türkiye option and has already won majority support from MPs in the Commons in a vote brought by the Lib Dems December 2025.

**Pros:** The UK can trade goods within the EU customs union area without tariffs and checks. It would deal with a lot of the barriers that Mr Burnham wants to remove in his latest Brexit reset. It can also be bespoke. Türkiye’s customs union membership does not include agriculture goods.**Cons:** Members generally cannot set their own tariffs or negotiate independent free-trade deals for the goods covered – the EU negotiates as a bloc. This means the UK would have to leave trade deals it has just negotiated outside the EU.**The Burnham view:** The prime minister quoted former chancellor George Osborne’s interview with *The Independent* saying Britain should join the customs union. Mr Burnham said: “We could look at what (former Conservative chancellor) George Osborne has said about a customs union.”

![George Osborne wants the UK to join the customs union](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/24/14/2300138468..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

## 3. The Single Market

According to diplomatic briefings, the EU will do no more bespoke deals with the UK after Friday unless it agrees to rejoin the internal single market at least. This would mean accepting EU rules and regulations for complete free trade. It is the position officially adopted by the Lib Dems at their autumn conference and one proposed to *The Independent*by former prime minister John Major.**Pros:** The UK would be able to trade on goods and services completely freely with the EU, the largest single market in the world. This would massively boost economic growth.**Cons:** The UK would have to abandon its previous trade deals and lose face internationally. It would also be a rule taker, not a rule maker, bound by the EU’s decisions – but with no seat at the table to decide on regulations.

This would impact areas like tech and artificial intelligence where the UK has a competitive advantage. It would also see the City having to accept EU rules.

**The Burnham view:** He told the BBC that “we could look at what the Liberal Democrats said at their conference about the single market – or we could go all the way.”

![Sir Ed Davey’s Lib Dems support joining the single market](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/14/48/261118_PMQs-(Maria)-20_76989-copy-dec9d4d9.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

## 4. Rejoin the EU

It would be like 2016 had never happened and we are full members of the EU again. As things stand, we know this is the prime minister’s preferred option and certainly the one which makes most sense if you want to reverse Brexit.

**Pros:** The UK would be full members of the EU with no trade barriers with our biggest trading partner. What would be more is that we would be there able to shape the rules and regulations going forward. This is the best option for economic growth.

Also the UK would be best placed to lead in the new world of defence cooperation required in Europe with the withdrawal of the US under Donald Trump and beyond. We would not miss out on defence procurement schemes or be excluded through issues like Made in Europe.

**Cons:** With great gains come inevitable costs. The UK would not be rejoining on the same terms as it left. Almost certainly it would have to agree to adopt the Euro and the Schengen free travel zone. Neither will be popular. Added to that, Margaret Thatcher’s hard-won rebate on annual membership costs will probably not be allowed.

There will also be the issues of dropping trade deals with the CPTPP and Canada. The UK may lose competitiveness in the City and would have the constant psycho drama about new EU rules and annual European Council negotiations.

But maybe the biggest issue is that rejoining may not be an end to the Brexit issue. Who is to say that, if it fails to be the panacea for the UK’s problems – just as leaving the bloc did not resolve many domestic issues – then there will be a push to leave again?

**The Burnham view:**“I’ve said I would like to see Britain rejoin in my lifetime. But it’s not necessarily the case that that is now… I am not going to move ahead of the public.”** We can safely conclude that Mr Burnham’s preference in order is 4, 3, 2, 1 with rejoin at the top of the list and stay out completely at the bottom.**

## EU does not expect an early election

There has been heavy speculation that Mr Burnham is preparing for an early election, possibly in May next year, although the prime minister has publicly denied the suggestion.

However, EU diplomats do not think that will give him time to formulate a position on what to do with Brexit.

The senior source said: “I guess if you're looking through a domestic lens, you might point differently, but certainly from an EU perspective, I would find it hard to believe there will be an early election.

“I feel like Andy Burnham signalled so strongly that in the next manifesto there will be something about the EU that is different .

“So if you're going to have this national conversation, I think that sort of indicates that you're not going to go for an early election.”

## Undoing Brexit requires a new voter mandate

The EU takes the view that the red lines from Sir Keir Starmer’s election manifesto in 2024 will stick until there is another election with a new manifesto.

The red lines included no rejoining the EU, single market or customs union and ruling out freedom of movement.

“If you're going to change any of the red lines, that can only be in the next parliament,” the source said.

“Then if you go for a quick election, the where's your national conversation?”

They are also sceptical about how detailed Mr Burnham’s 10-year plan expected before Christmas will be without having the year long national conversation on Brexit and the four options.

The senior figure noted: “If you're going to do this 10 year plan and you're going to publish your 10 year plan before Christmas or just after Christmas, whichever it turns out to be, I think it's very hard to do a 10 year plan unless you know what your EU relationship is going to be.”
