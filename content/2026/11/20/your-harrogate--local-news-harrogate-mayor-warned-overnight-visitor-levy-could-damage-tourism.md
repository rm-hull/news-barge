North Yorkshire Council has stepped up its opposition to plans for a tourism tax.

They warned Mayor David Skaith that an overnight visitor levy could damage coastal tourism, increase costs for businesses and deter holidaymakers from visiting the county.

In a “robustly” worded letter, the council’s deputy leader and executive member for open to business, Cllr Mark Crane, said the authority’s concerns had “increased, not diminished” since the Visitor Levy (England) Bill was introduced to Parliament earlier this month.

The intervention comes weeks after Mr Skaith told the Local Democracy Reporting Service (LDRS) he was “very confident” that York and North Yorkshire would ultimately be able to introduce an overnight visitor levy and ruled out the idea of ringfencing revenues to the areas where they were raised.

At the time, the mayor argued that proceeds should be used across the whole region, saying a wider funding pot would help support major projects, particularly in coastal communities.

However, Cllr Crane – who also sits on the York and North Yorkshire Combined Authority – argued that the introduction of legislation did not establish the case for “imposing another charge on visitors”.

One of the Conservative-led council’s main concerns relates to the structure of the proposed levy.

A report by York St John University’s Policy Lab, commissioned by the Combined Authority, previously estimated that a £1-per-night levy could raise up to £26 million annually across the region.

​Labour’s regional mayors in England, including Mr Skaith, have pledged to cap a new fee on visitors’ overnight stays at five per cent, after hospitality bosses warned it could put jobs at risk.

However, Cllr Crane said businesses had already expressed concern when a fixed charge was being discussed and suggested that a percentage-based model could create additional uncertainty.

He wrote:

> “There was already considerable alarm across the sector when a charge of around £1 per night was being discussed.”

The council said accommodation providers ranging from hotels and guest houses to self-catering businesses, campsites and holiday parks were continuing to face rising costs.

The letter states:

> “A higher room price does not necessarily mean a higher profit or a greater capacity to absorb additional costs.”

Particular emphasis was placed on coastal tourism destinations such as Scarborough, Whitby and Filey, where the authority argues businesses are heavily dependent on domestic visitors.

NYC warned that tourists may choose competing destinations outside the county if additional costs were introduced.

Those concerns echo comments made recently by Alison Hume, Labour MP for Scarborough and Whitby.

Speaking in Parliament this week, Ms Hume said she was not opposed to an overnight levy in principle and believed such schemes could work in cities such as York, but argued that coastal tourism markets were often much more price sensitive than urban destinations.

Ms Hume noted that North Yorkshire welcomed 31.2 million visitors in 2025, generating an estimated £4.3 billion for the economy.

She also questioned whether money raised in places such as Scarborough and Whitby would be spent locally if a scheme was introduced.

That remains another key concern raised by North Yorkshire Council.

While the proposed legislation would require authorities to publish spending plans and annual reports, the council argues there is currently no guarantee that revenue raised from visitors would be returned to the communities where it was generated.

The authority also warned that some businesses may reconsider their financial support for existing visitor economy initiatives if they are required to collect a compulsory levy.

Cllr Crane wrote:

> “The prospect of a levy therefore risks undermining the existing model before any new funding becomes available.”

The letter also calls for greater involvement from tourism organisations, business representatives and the Visit North Yorkshire Advisory Group before any decisions are taken.

Among the measures sought by the council are a full independent economic impact assessment, meaningful consultation with tourism businesses and guarantees that any scheme would operate through a simple collection system with transparent governance arrangements.

Despite its opposition, the council said it remained willing to engage with the process as discussions continue.

The letter concluded:

> “North Yorkshire Council remains opposed to the introduction of a levy, but ready to engage constructively to protect the interests of our businesses and communities.
>
> “Our engagement should not be mistaken for support or acceptance that a levy is inevitable.”

The proposed legislation remains at an early stage in Parliament and no decision has been taken on whether a visitor levy will ultimately be introduced in York and North Yorkshire.
