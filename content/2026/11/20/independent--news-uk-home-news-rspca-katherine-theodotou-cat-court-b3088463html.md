A former lawyer is claiming the RPSCA "kidnapped and murdered" her cat amidst a £560,000 court claim now centred on the frozen body of her dead pet.

Dr Katherine Theodotou, 80, is suing the RSPCA and Met Police after cops smashed into her London home and seized her "beloved" 17-month-old black and white cat Rita in March this year, following a report that she was being mistreated.

The former solicitor, who also runs an animal sanctuary near Hertford, insists she was the victim of a "malicious report" to the RSPCA, leading to a warrant being issued and Rita taken away.

Suing in what started as a legal bid to get her pet back plus a £560,000 damages claim, Dr Theodotou's case has now expanded into a fight over Rita's frozen body after the seized cat died in RSPCA foster care in July.

This week, in the fifth High Court hearing in six months about Rita, Mr Justice Cotter heard that the cat's body has now been in a freezer for four months pending a post mortem examination to ascertain the cause of death, with Dr Theodotou telling the judge that she believes her cat was "murdered" by the RSPCA.

She asked the judge for the cat's frozen body - which is currently being held at the University of Liverpool’s Institute of Infection, Veterinary and Ecological Sciences, which was tasked with carrying out the post mortem after a earlier hearing - to be released to her instead.

The judge told her that whilst she "may win" her main £560,000 claim, he was refusing her applications and handing her a legal costs bill for almost £10,000, saying had repeated arguments the court had already heard on a previous occasion when it refused to hand her the cat's body.

"You have got an action. You may win. That's not for me to decide," he said.

"The judge made a decision. You asked for the return of the cat and the judge said 'no.'

"The cat is now dead and in a freezer.

"We are talking about a dead cat and I'm the third High Court judge hearing about the body of a dead cat.

"The High Court has limited resources and we are struggling with the work we have on various important issues. This is the fifth time this case has been before the court....The resources of the court are finite...We have to keep it proportionate."

Ordering that the post mortem in Liverpool now go ahead despite Mrs Theodotou arguing that the freezing process may have destroyed vital evidence relating to the cause of Rita's death, the judge added: "If the cat had been bashed over the head, that would show up in a post mortem, whether its been frozen or not."

Dr Theodotou was born and studied in Cyprus before moving to the UK where, according to her LinkedIn profile, she and her Islington-based law firm Highgate Hill solicitors won a series of awards, including accolades for human rights lawyer of the year and UK female of the year in probate law.

However she was struck off the UK solicitors register in 2022, after she was found to have made "dishonest" statements while applying for professional insurance.

She is also the founder and honorary director of the Greek school of Islington and runs an animal sanctuary at Woodcock Lodge Farm, near Hertford.

An earlier hearing heard that Rita was born disabled and could not properly move her back legs and by reason of this suffered from occasional infections in her bladder.

Dr Theodotou insists that Rita was being well-cared for at her home, receiving regular medical support and steadily learning to walk on all four legs.

However, following what she claims was "a malicious report to the RSPCA regarding the welfare of Rita," a warrant was obtained by the charity from a magistrates court, giving them power to raid her property with police and seize the disputed cat.

Her barrister, Liam Wells, said Dr Theodotou was at home recovering from recent breast cancer surgery when the RSPCA suddenly attended her home with officers from the Met Police.

The police then "proceeded to break down the front door," with Dr Theodotou being "pushed her violently to the ground" in the commotion.

The case reached court in July when, fearing for Rita's safety, Dr Theodotou applied for an injunction preventing the charity from putting her down, with the RSPCA insisting it had no plans to do so.

![Picture shows the door of the property belonging to former solicitor Dr Katherine Theotodou which she says was broken down by police with an axe to allow the RSPCA to seize her cat Rita.](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/07/17/7/52/100-FOR-PICS-AND-WORDS-PLEASE-DO-NOT-REUSE-WITHOUT-PERMISSION-7e053b3e.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The injunction was refused, but just two days before the fight over ownership of the cat was set to go before magistrates, Dr Theodotou was informed that Rita had suddenly died in RSPCA foster care, with the battle over her ownership turning into a squabble over her remains.

Mr Justice Kimblin in July made an order that a post-mortem be carried out jointly between an RSPCA vet and an independent one chosen by Dr Theodotou.

Dr Theodotou, returning to court this week for a fifth time since Rita was taken, was asked by Mr Justice Cotter "You think the cat was murdered?" replying: "Yes".

"Was it bashed over the head? Did it fall down the stairs? What happened in the new house where it was living?" she said, also speculating that "devious medicines given to the cat" may also be to blame for her death.

"Now the cat has been left for over five months in the frost," she continued.

"The RSPCA had no rights over the cat because the warrant was unlawful. They kicked down the door and pushed me to the floor in order to kidnap the cat. There was no need to take the cat anywhere.

"When she was kidnapped, the cat was very healthy.

"The situation is that I love this cat and suddenly some unprincipled people take the cat away and I find myself in the court

"I've got to be given the body of the cat back to see if any realistic post mortem can take place."

Mark Townsend for the RSPCA - which denies any wrongdoing - urged the court to dismiss the applications, saying the post mortem was being needlessly delayed by them.

"The claimant uses these proceedings as a forum to vent her opinions on the RSPCA, the differences between Cypriot and English society, and the court," he said.

"She makes unfounded accusations against the RSPCA and the court.

"Issues relating to the RSPCA’s previous interactions with the claimant, the manner in which Rita was taken into the care of the RSPCA, and Rita’s condition at that time, all form part of the main claim. They are disputed between the parties, and they are not relevant for the purposes of the post mortem examination," he added.

Giving his ruling against Dr Theodotou, the judge told her that her applications were "repeating an argument you have already lost twice," calling it "misconceived and an attempt to prolong a post-mortem exercise which should have been carried out long ago."

"Dr Theodotou owns the cat. It is now dead.

"The application before me concerns what happens to the cat, which is now dead.

"The way in which the cat died is in issue. It is the claimant's assertion that the RSPCA, conspiring with others, have murdered the cat then put the body in a freezer to hide the cause of death.

"The claimant feels extremely strongly about the matter and the defendant and what she describes as its atrocities.

"A post mortem on an animal which has been frozen would be sub-optimal because freezing can produce changes in the tissue.

"But just because it is sub-optimal is no reason not to have it at all.

"Something will show up or something will not.

"As a matter of commonsense, bones are not affected by freezing. If it was bashed on the head that will show up," he said, adding that undertaking a post mortem even on a pre-frozen cat "remains appropriate and potentially informative".

He said that records from the lab in Liverpool showed that "the majority" of post mortems they carry out on animals, approximately 60%, "are on previously frozen bodies."

"The defendant wishes to carry out a post mortem and there is no evidence that, as Dr Theodotou says it, would be a waste of time," he said.

"It may not be conclusive as to the cause of death but the question is whether there may be something that may be useful and the answer is obviously yes.

"I dismiss both applications," he concluded, ordering DrTheodotou to pay £9,361 costs to the RSPCA.

The court previously heard that Dr Theodotou's damages claim - for harassment and negligence against the RSPCA and assault, battery, trespass and negligence against the Met Police - relates to the seizure of Rita, not her death.

Mr Townsend told the court this week that "she seeks as against the RSPCA, damages of £10,000 for harassment and negligence; and as against the [Met Police] damages of £50,000 for pain and suffering and £500,000 for trespass".

RSPCA lawyers previously said that, when seen after the March seizure, Rita was showing signs of being in an "extremely concerning" condition, suggestive of "prolonged inadequate hygiene, nursing care, and monitoring within the home environment."

The Met said police had attended the house and "lawfully executed a warrant of entry" to collect the cat due to "welfare concerns," with cops having no further involvement.
