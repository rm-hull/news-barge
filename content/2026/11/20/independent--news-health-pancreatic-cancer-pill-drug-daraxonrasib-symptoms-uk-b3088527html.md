A new once-a-day pill for advanced pancreatic cancer has wiped out the disease in one in 12 patients when combined with chemotherapy, a clinical trial suggests.

The study, which was presented at the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer in Barcelona, found that combining the drug daraxonrasib with chemotherapy increased the proportion of patients alive after one year compared to standard treatment.

The lead medic on the trial said the results represented the “closest” thing to a “cure” for some patients.

Daraxonrasib made headlines in May after it was found to almost double survival time for patients with aggressive and advanced pancreatic cancer when compared to chemotherapy alone. It also caused fewer side effects.

The drug works by locking onto the mutated KRAS gene, which is found in more than nine in 10 pancreatic tumours and continuously signals cancer cells to grow.

By shutting this off, the drug can prevent tumours from spreading.

![CT scans before and after treatment with Daraxonrasib plus chemotherapy (gemcitabine and nabpaclitaxel)](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/20/08/01M7SE4GN5K234P9XC4RMY5P40.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

In the new study, tumours shrank or disappeared in 60.7 per cent of patients given daraxonrasib and chemotherapy.

This included complete responses in 8.2 per cent of patients over a typical follow-up of 18 months, meaning their tumours were no longer detectable.

Overall, tumours stopped growing in 90.2 per cent of the 61 patients studied (the disease control rate) over a typical period of 18 months.

One year after treatment, 76.6 per cent of patients were still alive. This compares to around 40 to 45 per cent of patients when they are given chemotherapy alone.

It also took 12.8 months on average for tumours to return or start growing again when the whole patient group was taken into account, the phase one/two clinical trial showed.

Dr Wungki Park, oncologist at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Centre in New York, who presented the findings, said he had never seen such responses, adding it represents “a massive, massive breakthrough” in treating pancreatic cancer.

“In about one in 12 patients, it (the cancer) completely disappeared,” he said.

“And then with such a long follow-up, this is real. We’ve never seen such a thing.”

![The lead medic on the trial said the results represented the ’closest’ thing to a ‘cure’ for some patients](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/05/31/12/01KSYDA4SB86PM76M5M3XAXK4G.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Asked if the one in 12 patients whose tumours could not be seen were regarded as cured, he said: “This is the closest to cure.

“This is almost at the discovery level; we have never seen eight per cent complete response in metastatic pancreatic cancer, you don’t see that….

“The median follow-up is 18 months. Just imagine, in an average of 18 months your disease just disappears on the CT scan … that’s closest to complete remission …. it’s incredible.”

He said longer-term survival data would come as the trial progressed, but there is “some survival signal here, which is very strong”, with 76.6 per cent of patients alive after one year.

Fewer than five per cent of patients in the trial discontinued treatment, which is regarded as low.

Dr Park said cancer does frequently develop resistance to drug treatment but “we’re delaying that as much as possible” through the new combined treatment.

The final phase-three study in the US has enough patients but some sites in Europe may have space.

## Main symptoms of pancreatic cancer

NHS

Symptoms of pancreatic cancer can include:

* the whites of your eyes or your skin turn yellow (jaundice), and you may also have itchy skin, darker pee and paler poo than usual
* loss of appetite or losing weight without trying to
* feeling tired or having no energy
* a high temperature, or feeling hot, cold or shivery

Other symptoms can affect your digestion, such as:

* feeling or being sick
* diarrhoea or constipation, or other changes in your poo
* pain at the top part of your tummy and your back, which may feel worse when you're eating or lying down and better when you lean forward
* symptoms of indigestion, such as feeling bloated

Dr Madina Kara, head of research at Pancreatic Cancer UK, said: “Half of people with pancreatic cancer die within three months of diagnosis and their loved ones would give anything to spend more precious time with them.

“This is a small, initial study, however, these encouraging results now show that daraxonrasib and chemotherapy can be effective when used in combination.”

She said the team was excited for the larger clinical trial, adding: “These findings are part of a growing body of evidence that offers real hope to people with advanced pancreatic cancer.”

She said: “The UK Government must ensure we build on the momentum we are seeing by increasing investment into pancreatic research and clinical trials, so that more patients can take part in studies that could improve outcomes.

“It must also ensure there is a rapid pathway to deliver any game-changing new treatments to patients across the UK as soon as they are proven to work.”

More than 11,000 people are diagnosed with pancreatic cancer each year in the UK and more than 10,000 die from it, usually within a few months.

Cancer Research UK chief executive Michelle Mitchell said: “This is the biggest leap forward we’ve seen in decades for pancreatic cancer patients and could offer real hope to people facing one of the deadliest and most difficult-to-treat cancers.

“More trials are now needed before this treatment can be assessed for use on the NHS and despite this advance, pancreatic cancer survival is still very low.

“Further research is needed to look at how this drug could be used alongside other therapies to give patients the best chance of beating the disease.”
