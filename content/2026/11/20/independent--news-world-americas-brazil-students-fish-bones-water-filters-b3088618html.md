Brazilian students have developed a water filter made from the bones of a giant Amazon fish, in a project that could help provide cleaner drinking water to remote communities.

The group of high school students and their biology teacher have created charcoal from the bones of the pirarucu, a fish native to the Amazon that can grow up to two metres long.

The process involves heating the fish's thick vertebrae to temperatures of up to 900C before grinding the charred bones into a powder. The material can then absorb heavy metals and other pollutants from water.

The project was developed at Jose Mota school in Manacapuru, a town on the Solimoes River around 100km west of Manaus.

Biology teacher Galileu da Silva Pires said the technique offered a sustainable alternative to charcoal made from timber, which can require trees to be felled.

“It's a sustainable, ecological form of carbon that does not require the felling of trees,” he said. “It means we can produce drinking water with this new resource.”

![](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/20/11/2216891921..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The project could be particularly useful for communities living along rivers in the Amazon, where access to safe drinking water can become more difficult during periods of drought.

When rainfall becomes scarce, communities can lose access to rainwater supplies. Lower river levels can also cause pollutants to become more concentrated, increasing the risks associated with drinking untreated water.

Water levels in the Solimoes River, an upper stretch of the Amazon, are currently low following a period of drought.

Mr da Silva Pires said the students' work could offer a solution that might be adapted for communities elsewhere.

“This shows the potential these kids have,” he said. “You take a problem that has been around for decades, and with natural resources you can find solutions.”

![](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/20/11/2282209574..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Using pirarucu bones also offers a possible way to tackle another environmental problem: the accumulation of fish waste after the meat has been consumed.

Charred fish bones have previously been recognised as a potential material for filtering water without relying on timber. The students' project explores how bones from the pirarucu could be put to use rather than discarded.

Student Ana Flavia Costa Lopes, 18, said the group was pleased to have created something with a practical benefit.

“We feel great about getting better quality water, filtered water, creating something of value,” she said.

The project highlights how locally available materials could be used to explore solutions to environmental challenges in remote parts of the Amazon.
