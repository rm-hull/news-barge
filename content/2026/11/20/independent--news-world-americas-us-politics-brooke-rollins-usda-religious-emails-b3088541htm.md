A federal judge has ruled that religious emails sent by U.S. Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins to the department's 100,000 employees breached the Constitution.

Judge Trina Thompson of the U.S. District Court in San Francisco found the messages violated the First Amendment's establishment clause. Her ruling on Thursday means Rollins must avoid proselytizing when communicating with workers.

The emails were sent from Rollins' official email account on holidays including Independence Day, Thanksgiving, Christmas and Easter. They contained Christian messages and images. No messages commemorating holidays from other religions were sent.

![A federal judge has ruled that U.S. Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins' religious emails to 100,000 employees were unconstitutional](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/20/09/2026-11-20T032813Z_1459584324_RC2JCNANXUK0_RTRMADP_3_USA-AGRICULTURE-RELIGION-ORDER.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Thompson pointed to Rollins' position of authority, her use of an official email account and the messages suggesting that Christianity is the shared faith of USDA workers. Thompson ruled that employees cannot realistically opt out of reading emails from their agency director.

She also found the messages could coerce workers into hiding their own faith or pressure them to adopt Rollins' Christian faith.

“And so, like the very first disciples to encounter our risen Lord in the Upper Room almost two thousand years ago, this Easter let us too be alive with hope, full of Paschal joy, and confident in the mission each of us has been called for,” Rollins wrote in an April 5 email marking Easter.

Six USDA employees and the National Federation of Federal Employees, a labor union representing 19,000 USDA workers, brought the suit in May.

![Rollins speaks as President Donald Trump announces action to help farmers and ranchers, as a group of farmers and ranchers stand behind](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/01/20/10/AP26304764636924.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

When disseminating religious messages, Rollins must stop if a colleague asks her to, “cannot create a hostile work environment through persistent unwelcome advances,” and “cannot use official authority to pressure subordinates,” Thompson ordered.

Thompson quoted her commands directly from a USDA policy on workplace religious expression that Rollins herself adopted. Rollins may discuss her faith with interested colleagues, Thompson ruled, again quoting directly from Rollins' policy.

She may share why her faith is meaningful to her, politely invite co-workers to religious events and express her views in appropriate conversations. The USDA did not respond to a request for comment.
