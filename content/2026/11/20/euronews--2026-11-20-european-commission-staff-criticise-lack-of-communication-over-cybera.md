## The main civil servants’ union has criticized senior management for letting staff learn of the breach from the press, as employees fear their personal data is at risk.

European Commission staff have voiced frustration over the lack of communication surrounding a large-scale cyberattack that struck the institution in October.

Renouveau & Démocratie (R&D), the main trade union representing EU civil servants, criticised the Commission's handling of the incident, saying staff were not notified when the cyberattack was detected on 22 October and that the institution only acknowledged it after it was reported in the media.

“This failure of internal communication is unacceptable,” R&D chair Cristiano Sebastiani said in a statement on Thursday. Staff should not “have to learn from a newspaper headline” that their personal data could be at risk, he added.

For several days, officials have been unable to access Sysper, the internal HR platform where they check salary information, request holidays and sick leave, submit timesheets and sign up for training.

According to R&D, civil servants have been contacting the union to express their concern and anger.

“Discovering this from the press is absurd. Transparency would have been a better choice,” a Commission official told Euronews on condition of anonymity.

The Commission eventually notified staff in an internal note on Friday, a day after the news was first reported by the media. “The Commission has recently been targeted by a series of cyberattacks, including through the use of AI,” it reads. The note says the breaches affected a limited number of systems and that there is no evidence of a data breach affecting staff as a whole.

In a further statement to Euronews, Sebastiani described the note as “absolutely inadequate given the gravity of the failure” and called on senior management to issue a formal apology.

Most civil servants have experienced only minor disruption to their daily work, but the incident has raised concerns. “The mere possibility of my data being leaked scares me, particularly given all the phishing emails I get every day,” another official told Euronews.

A Commission spokesperson said on Thursday that the attack relied on a publicly available AI tool, without saying whether it was still ongoing or attributing it to a specific actor. The tool, called ARTEX, identifies software vulnerabilities, which can then be patched or, in the hands of malicious actors, exploited.

This is not the first time the Commission has been targeted by a cyberattack.

In March, hackers compromised the cloud infrastructure hosting its Europa.eu websites, stealing around 91.7 GB of data. Earlier, in January, an attack on the Commission's central mobile-device infrastructure potentially exposed staff members' names and phone numbers.
