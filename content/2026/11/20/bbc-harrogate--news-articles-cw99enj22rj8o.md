**A long-serving councillor has announced he will stand down next May, after admitting he is "exhausted" by the job.**

Stuart Parsons, currently leader of the North Yorkshire Independents group, has spent over two decades representing Richmond, first on North Yorkshire County Council and, since April 2023, its successor unitary council.

The 65-year-old said he believed the local authority had become too big and bureaucratic and hoped someone younger would take on his job.

Carl Les, Conservative leader of North Yorkshire Council, said he recognised Parsons' services to the community, wearing "two hats" as both a district and county councillor.

Parsons, who was first elected to the authority in 2005, said the council was now "far too big"

"They talk about being a local council, but they're not," he said.

Parsons said the creation of North Yorkshire Council in 2023 had failed to deliver the improvements promised and had instead made it harder for councillors to help residents.

While the council had some "absolutely fantastic" front-line staff, they were often prevented from helping residents by bureaucracy and decisions made by senior managers, he said.

North Yorkshire Council replaced the county's former district and borough councils in April 2023

According to the Local Democracy Reporting Service, Parsons said routine jobs, which had previously been dealt with locally, now required referrals to teams elsewhere in the county.

He said issues such as tree maintenance in Richmond had to be referred to staff in Harrogate, whereas before a grounds maintenance team at Richmondshire District Council would deal with it.

He also criticised what he described as excessive paperwork, claiming community organisation Heart of Richmond, which installs defibrillators, had been faced with a 97-page document to obtain permission.

"The council isn't helping or respecting local traditions, and if it can't do that, then it needs to change," he said.

Parsons said the workload of being a councillor had increased significantly, describing the role as more than full-time for anyone wanting to do it properly.

"For our last council meeting, we had 589 pages of reports to read, and that was a relatively small agenda," he explained.

"When the budget comes around in February, there will probably be 1,400 or 1,500 pages."

## 'Not going to disappear'

Parsons said council budget cuts since 2010 had made the job increasingly difficult.

"The final thing that really finished me off was home-to-school transport," he said.

"The political shenanigans, and I include both sides in that, became absolutely unbearable.

"I believe we have damaged young people, schools and their futures by adopting that policy."

Parsons said that after May next year, he intended to take a short break, but would continue supporting local organisations.

"I'm not going to disappear from view. If the community still wants me to be involved, that's fine. If they don't, that's fine too," he said.

Responding, Les rejected Parsons' criticism about workload, saying that "all the functions he was dealing with before in two councils have now come into one council".

"When you think back to the two-tier system, that was confusing and was dysfunctional for the community and it was certainly more expensive as an administration," Les said.

But he added that he agreed the job of a councillor was becoming more full-time due to the demands of the community.

He said the authority was looking at increasing backbench councillors' allowances to £18,000 a year, which he said was "a considerable sum of money, but that reflects the amount of work we are expected to do on behalf of the community".
