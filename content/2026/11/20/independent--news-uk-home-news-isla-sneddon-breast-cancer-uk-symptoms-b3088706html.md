The parents of a 17-year-old who died from breast cancer say they hope new guidelines named in her memory will be a legacy that can “create lasting change”.

Isla Sneddon, from Airdrie, died in March 2025, six months after being diagnosed with Cystosarcoma Phyllodes, a rare and aggressive breast tumour.

Isla’s Principles has four key action points for clinicians to consider when a young patient presents with unexplained, persistent symptoms.

Under the guidance, repeated visits to doctors and persistent concerns of patients and their families should raise a suspicion of cancer with GPs.

Health Secretary Angela Constance said the guidance had been “developed to support earlier and more accurate diagnosis of cancers in children and young people”.

![Isla Sneddon, from Airdrie in North Lanarkshire, died in March 2025 at just 17 years old](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/20/13/27/Isla-Sneddon.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Isla’s parents, Mark and Michelle Sneddon, said: “We cannot change what happened to Isla but knowing that her legacy could help another child to receive the investigations, diagnosis and treatment they need sooner gives us a purpose to keep going.”

“Our hope is that Isla’s Principles become more than guidance on a piece of paper. We want them to create lasting change in how children and young people with concerning symptoms are listened to, investigated, and referred.”

The principles use the acronym ISLA to spell out four key actions GPs should take when dealing with a young person with persistent symptoms, stressing that intuition can be important when cancer is suspected.

It also sets out that clinicians should share information clearly with patients and their families, should look again at the patient if problems persist, and should agree a “safety-netting plan” to ensure the patients and their families know when they should seek further help.

The ISLA actions are already being adopted by “many clinicians”, Ms Constance said, and includes:

**I**– Intuition should be acknowledged and respected when reviewing referrals where there is suspicion of cancer.** S**– Share information between primary and secondary care.** L**– Look at the patient again beyond the obvious when symptoms persist, evolve, recur or do not fit the expected clinical picture.** A** – Agree a safety-netting plan and ensure that the young person and their families understand the next steps.

## Main symptoms of breast cancer in women

NHS

Symptoms of breast cancer in women may include:

* a lump, or swelling in your breast, chest or armpit
* a change in the skin of your breast, such as dimpling (may look or feel like orange peel)
* a change in the colour of the skin on your breast – on black or brown skin it may look darker than usual, on white skin it may look red
* a change in size or shape of one or both breasts
* nipple discharge (if you are not pregnant or breastfeeding), which may have blood in it
* a change in the shape or look of your nipple, such as it turning inwards (inverted nipple) or a rash on it (may look like eczema)

You may get pain in your breast or armpit with these symptoms. But you can have these symptoms without any pain.

The Health Secretary said: “My sincere thoughts go out to the Sneddon family over the loss of their daughter, Isla. They have shown tremendous resilience and determination to campaign for meaningful change in their daughter’s memory.

“This guidance has been developed to support earlier and more accurate diagnosis of cancers in children and young people.

“Many clinicians already adopt the ethos of Isla’s Principles but it is important this is applied consistently across Scotland.

“This will ensure that every young person presenting with worrying symptoms has a voice in their care and is given clear guidance over how they will be supported through a diagnosis.”

Phil Hodkinson, from the Centre for Sustainable Delivery, the body which has now published the principles, said: “Isla’s Principles are a reminder of the importance of listening carefully, openly communicating clinical uncertainty, and taking a fresh look when symptoms persist or do not fit the expected pattern.

“The principles highlight that strengthening communication between teams and ensuring young people and their families are clear about next steps, supporting us in delivering safe, person-centred care.”

Meanwhile, Dr Sal Peterson, co-chair of the Scottish Primary Care Cancer Group said: “Isla’s Principles are an important addition to existing guidance. They highlight the importance of GP intuition, especially in people with vague symptoms.”
