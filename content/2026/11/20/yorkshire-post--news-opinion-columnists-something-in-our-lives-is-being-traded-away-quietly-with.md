# Something in our lives is being traded away quietly with over-reliance on AI: Daxa Patel

## The machines are not making us stupid; we are. We need to ask what we lose when we stop doing the hard part and rely heavily on human made tools without batting an eyelid.

My grandmother's recipe was never written down. It was handed over, once, in a kitchen, with flour on her hands and patience in her voice. You learned it by doing it badly, again and again, until one day you did it well. That is how skill has always been passed on: slowly, imperfectly, from one human being to another.

Today that recipe is a rarity. Ready-made food fills the gap, and behind the ready-made food sits a quieter truth: we lost the patience for the art. Nobody sat us down and took cooking away. We simply stopped doing the hard part, and the hard part was where the skill lived and was passed on.

The same is happening with words. Auto-correct and grammar tools now smooth our sentences before we have finished thinking them. There is real value in that; I am not against a helping hand. But something is being traded away quietly. The discipline of getting a sentence right, of wrestling with a thought until it is true and clear, was never just about spelling. It was how we learned to think. A tool that finishes the thought for us does not make us better thinkers it makes us faster ones, and speed is not the same as depth or engagement.

We will soon become accustomed to driverless cars and lose the skill of mirror, signal and manoeuvre, the fundamentals required to judge distance in rain and dusk. Deskilling will cause chaos when systems crash has happened a few days ago with a well-known platform provider. Reliance for speed is good but losing key skills is robbing ourselves to survive and thrive.

The companies building this future tell us not to worry. Their leaders are visionaries, and the technology is genuinely extraordinary but look closely at the story they tell.

The chief executive of Nvidia, the company at the heart of the AI boom, resigned last week after revelations of extramarital affairs. Within days, a petition to reinstate him had gathered close to 200,000 signatures, backed by some of the most powerful names in technology, and the man himself said he would be honoured to return.

I am not here to judge the man; his marriage that is his business. But notice what almost nobody is discussing. Two adult children and a wife of over four decades are living the most public humiliation of their lives, and the world's response is a campaign to restore his power.

Shareholders count and markets count. The family at the centre of it does not even make the footnote. One wonders, quietly, whether a female chief executive caught in the same story would be met with petitions or with pitchforks. If the visionaries steering this revolution are this human, and the crowd around them this selective in its compassion, perhaps we should be slower to hand them the steering wheel of everything else.

That is the paradox at the centre of the AI story as we are told machines will never match human beings, and in the deepest sense that is true. We are sentient. We feel grief and loyalty and love. A machine can predict the next word; it cannot sit with a dying parent, keep a promise nobody is checking, or choose the harder right over the easier wrong. Those capacities are not features. They are the whole point of our being.

And yet the more we delegate, the duller the instrument becomes. A compassionate heart is built by practising compassion, in person, with people who are inconvenient. Loyalty is a muscle and judgment is a craft. None of them survive long on autopilot, and the marketing machine selling us convenience has no interest in telling us so. Shiny speed is an easy sell, but the slow acquisition of wisdom is not.

So where does that leave us? Not in retreat from technology, which is neither possible nor wise. The answer is intention. Use the tools but keep doing the hard parts on purpose. Cook the family recipe from memory. Drive somewhere and pay attention to the road and have the difficult conversation face to face instead of through a screen. Let the machines carry the load but never let them carry the meaning.

Artificial intelligence will not make humans stupid. Humans will make humans stupid, if we stop practising what makes us human. The technology is a mirror, and the reflection is a choice. We can become a generation that outsourced its patience, its judgment and its care. Or we can become the generation that used unprecedented tools and somehow stayed discerning, compassionate, loyal and able, which is to say, fully human.

My grandmother's recipe survives because someone took the time to learn it. Everything worth being survives the same way. The machines are not taking that from us. Only we can give it away.

The same crowd that outsourced its spelling has outsourced its compassion, and the petition is the receipt. Need I say more.

Daxa Patel is an author, leadership coach and solicitor.
