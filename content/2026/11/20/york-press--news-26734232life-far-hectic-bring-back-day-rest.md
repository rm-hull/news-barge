## Life is far too hectic - let's bring back the day of rest

Shops should be closed on Christmas bank holidays. Picture: Pixabay

IN DAYS gone by the closure over Christmas of a shop would not have made headline news.

Now, even on Christmas Day, when a supermarket closes its doors, it’s splashed all over the papers. The latest such headlines involve Aldi, which is shutting on three days over the holiday period - Christmas Day, Boxing Day and New Year’s Day: Friday, 1 January 2027, to give staff a ‘well-earned break’ and allow them time at home with their families.

It will certainly be well earned, as the stores are opening for longer in the run-up to Christmas.

Some of the Christmas closure articles ‘warn’ shoppers of the impending shut-downs with the same level of gravity as a nuclear attack from Russia.

In my view all shops should be closed on these days. This would mean that as well as offering respite for staff, people would no longer get up at 2am to queue on cold pavements in the hope of getting a pair of jeans for a fiver in the Boxing Day sales.

Give us all a break, please. I’d be quite happy, and I’m sure a lot of other people would be too, if shops closed their doors more often. People my age remember Sundays as proper days of rest, with no shops - not even supermarkets - open at all.

People did things other than shopping - things that were good for mind and body, like going for a walk or bike ride, baking, gardening, or simply sitting down to read a book. In those days families did enjoyable things like go to the park - they weren’t dragged by the huge magnetic force that is out-of-town retail centres.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/12853648.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Sundays without shops would lead to more leisurely activities. Picture: Pixabay

In the good old days if there was something you needed you had to wait until Monday, or later in the week, to get it.

When I was growing up shops weren’t only shut on Sundays. Many were closed on Wednesday afternoon. Half-day closing on that day was common, with staff benefitting for a mid-week break. You had to factor in half-day closing when planning a day out - if visiting a town most people avoided Wednesdays. But we accepted it as part of life, and it did us no harm.

Not being able to shop at certain times meant we didn’t feel pressure to head out to by this and that. There was no such thing as retail therapy. Back then, shopping didn’t equate with happiness.

In the late 1970s, when a seven-day opening Spar shop appeared in the town close to my childhood home, I remember the novelty of being able to shop on a Sunday. I’m not religious, but roaming the aisles dropping groceries into a basket on the Sabbath felt like something illicit, forbidden and somehow exciting.

Sunday opening was still in its infancy in the early 1980s when I was at university in London. My flatmates and I would walk half a mile to the nearest corner shop and buy Fray Bentos tinned pies for our dinner. The only other local store open that day was a video shop that kept banned horror films in a box under the counter.

My boyfriend, now husband, lived near Notting Hill, where a supermarket called Nisa blazed a trail for seven-day opening.

I used to hate Sundays for being sluggish and dreary with nothing going on. Now they are peppered with trips to Asda, Sainsbury’s, B&Q or ScrewFix. I know we don’t have to go, but if the stores are open and you need a tub of gravy granules or a bag of nails, off you go.

Life is hectic. Life is full-on. It’s sad that we no longer have a day of rest. We need it, we really do. Maybe that’s something which Andy Burnham could look to introducing in his next election manifesto.
