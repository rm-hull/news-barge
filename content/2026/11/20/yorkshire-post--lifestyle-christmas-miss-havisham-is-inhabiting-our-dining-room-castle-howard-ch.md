# 'Miss Havisham is inhabiting our dining room': Castle Howard Christmas 2026 beings Dickens to life

## In the second of our series on stately home festive experiences, Stephanie Smith finds out how Castle Howard’s A Dickens of a Christmas was inspired by the famous writer’s visits to North Yorkshire. Pictures by James Hardisty.

Mr Ebenezer Scrooge is visiting Castle Howard this Christmas, and he is joined for the festivities by Miss Havisham, Mr Samuel Pickwick and a carnival of characters sprung from the extraordinary pen of Mr Charles Dickens.

Castle Howard’s A Dickens of a Christmas is playing out for more than seven weeks at the North Yorkshire stately home, taking over its interiors and grounds with an immersive and spectacular festive experience.

Inspired by the work of the great Victorian novelist, it brings his beloved tales to life through installations, soundscapes and projections, drawing on Castle Howard itself and its own historical connections to the writer.

![At Castle Howard near York A Dickens of a Christmas brings Charles Dickens' classic novels to life. Horse-drawn carriage arriving at the castle. When Dickens (1812-1870) published A Christmas Carol in 1843, he revived and reinvented the festive season as a family and community-centred festival, a time for charity, peace and goodwill, popularising traditions we still love from eating turkey to exchanging the greeting “Merry Christmas”.

“He is known as Mr Christmas, as the father of Christmas,” says Adrian Lillie of CLW Event Design, which created the experience. “He wrote not just A Christmas Carol, but there are so many Christmas stories that he wrote in book format, but also in other publications.

“A lot of our Christmas traditions started with him, so it’s been great fun exploring some of those elements. We’re going back into history and having a look at what he would have had on his Christmas dining table and what other activities they would have been up to.

“We have, of course, got Miss Havisham from Great Expectations. Her presence will be inhabiting the Crimson Dining Room.”

This ghostly, icy feast will be in contrast to a second festive table presentation. Adrian says: “We are imagining that we are at home with Charles and Catherine Dickens, and they are hosting a fantabulous Christmas feast in the Long Gallery as our grand finale.”

Before that delight, there are a host of magical stories and scenes to discover. Headed by Charlotte Lloyd Webber and Adrian, CLW Event Design is based on the Castle Howard Estate, where the team builds the large set pieces. Last year, they were the subject of Channel 4 documentary A Very British Christmas.

They work closely with the Howards and with Abbi Olive, director of Visitor Attraction. “A high Victorian Christmas has been on our agenda for a little while,” says Adrian. “It just felt like the perfect year to do it, especially as we’ve got a big focus on literature in general this year.

“There’s a history of Dickens in this part of the world, in the Howardian Hills, because we do believe that he wrote part of A Christmas Carol while staying in Malton.”

Charles Dickens’s association with the area began when he befriended Charles Smithson, who ran a legal firm in Chancery Lane in Malton. “Smithson lived at Easthorpe Hall on the Castle Howard Estate (which sadly no longer exists), where Dickens stayed for several weeks in July 1843,” says Malton Museum volunteer Eleanor Whaley.

Dickens told the Smithson family that the Chancery Lane office was the model for Scrooge’s Counting House. A Charles Dickens bench was placed on the estate last year by Howardian Hills National Landscape.

Displayed in the house for the first time is an original Victorian carriage commissioned by Castle Howard’s 9th Countess, Rosalind Howard (1845-1921). In 1864, she married 9th Earl George Howard, a painter who became a Liberal MP. Known as the Radical Countess, she managed the estate and was a campaigner for women’s suffrage and the temperance movement. Dickens might well have visited her and the Earl at Castle Howard, perhaps travelling in that very carriage.

Castle Howard curator Matthew Wood says: “The carriage is a Brougham, a type of enclosed carriage developed in the 1830s by Lord Brougham, which was designed to be light, fast and economical, requiring only one horse.

“The carriage has yellow and black livery which was used by the Howard family on their carriages in the Victorian era, and the doors are further decorated with the Howard lion. In the 1980s the carriage was found in farm buildings on the estate and was then restored.”

Visitors will enter a Victorian world right from the start. “It’s going to be very atmospheric, very wintery, very Christmassy,” Adrian says. “As we enter the staterooms, each picks up on a key Dickens story. We’ve got three different rooms that play back to A Christmas Carol.

“We’ve got a little bit of Oliver Twist. We've got A Tale of Two Cities going on. We’ve got a little journey with the Pickwickians from the Pickwick Papers. That’s very exciting.”

Adrian’s personal highlight is, as always, the Great Hall Christmas tree, which he personally picks out from a York grower. “That is my baby,” he says. “It takes 3,000 decorations alone, that tree, and I reinvent it every year.

“We’ve got something like 57 decorated trees in the house this year, which is more than we’ve ever had before.

“The logistical organisation of ensuring that we’ve got all the right fairy lights, that everything’s been PAT-tested, and then all of the decorations …

![Charlotte Benedict dressed in period costume takes time out from the festive arrangements and relaxes in The Crimson Dining Room. “I work with the curatorial team closely to figure out what we can rake out of cupboards and the secret West Wing that no one ever sees, just to bring some of the house’s history to life in a slightly different way.

“It’s a ‘best of Dickens’ with a lovely surrounding feeling of great Victorian beautiful nostalgia. We’ve been able to pick the very best-known stories, find a little Christmas nugget within the story, and then find the best room to put it in so that we can really go back to our roots of thinking about the house interior.”

On entering the Long Gallery, there is a scene representing ice skating down the river – quite the challenge because real silver-birch trees have been brought in to create a forest.

Theatre company Imitating the Dog is back creating the projections and soundscapes, with Simon Wainwright heading up projection design and James Hamilton composing the sounds.

“It’s beautifully atmospheric, and when the visitor first arrives, there will be the sound of footsteps in snow, and it just brings an extra layer into that whole sensibility, so a full design soundtrack that sits alongside each of the roomscapes,” Adrian says.

“Every year we like to have a little activity that our younger visitors can play along with while en route. This year we’re doing a mouse trail, so we have two very naughty mice who will be appearing in pretty much all of the rooms in different disguises that our younger visitors have to find and answer questions about.”

He adds: “We love a little bit of miniature, so as much as we do big, grand set pieces, we do like to change it up a little bit.”

Therefore, Mark Bond has created a miniature Bleak House, working with Simon Wainwright to put in live actor projection in miniature.

Adrian says: “That’s in the new library, which is one of the rooms that is only open at Christmas, because it’s Mr Howard’s personal study space.

“He very kindly lets us have it for the Christmas period, so I think that’s another lovely little nugget that just feels a little bit different within the presentation this year.”

A Dickens of a Christmas at Castle Howard runs to January 3, tickets from £24. Father Christmas in the Castle runs from November 21 to December 24 (selected dates). New for 2026 is the Jellycat Santa’s Grotto and shop and a Tommy Banks’ range of pies in the Farm Shop and the Christmas Marquee.
