## Lagarde remains bound to political neutrality until her tenure as ECB president ends in February 2027, just two months ahead of the first election round of France's presidential elections.

Christine Lagarde's decision last month to bring forward her departure as president of the European Central Bank's (ECB) has reignited major speculation around whether she could run to become France's first female President — or play a role in the country's election race.

Interest in Lagarde's next move is growing as the former minister, who has no clear political affiliation, could be seen as a potential unifying figure for France's fragmented centrist camp. This comes amid a deeply polarised political climate, just five months ahead of the presidential election.

However, analysts caution that Lagarde's support remains largely confined to an educated, elite electorate, and that her strength reflects the French right's lack of direction rather than genuine enthusiasm for her potential presidential candidacy.

Although Lagarde has not announced that she is running, a survey published by Politico on 11 November predicted that she could perform just as well as the center-right former prime minister Édouard Philippe. When the pair were tested separately against other presidential candidates, they respectively garnered 13.5% and 14% of voting intentions.

Current polls point to Marine Le Pen of the far-right National Rally and Jean-Luc Mélenchon of the hard-left France Unbowed leading the race, highlighting the deep divisions shaping the country’s political landscape.

They are respectively polling around 32-34% and 17-19.5%, according to a survey conducted on 18 November by Ifop-Fiducial. Between them, candidates on the left, right and centre, are struggling to make headway.

## Lagarde: 'I don't think I can remain neutral'

Before presiding over the ECB, Lagarde headed the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and served as France's Economy Minister from 2007-2011 under former conservative President Nicolas Sarkozy.

Upon announcing her decision to **step down from the ECB** on 29 October, she declared that she wished to have the freedom to "engage in matters that are not monetary policy."

“I don’t think that I can remain neutral,” she said, adding that she was interested in the truth, which is "very often damaged, contradicted by misrepresentation."

She remains bound to political neutrality until her tenure as ECB president ends in February 2027, just two months ahead of the first round of France's elections.

Asked about a possible bid, she **told Euronews** in July “I’m not a candidate for anything, but I’m very keen that Europe is protected, that Europe is the framework within which member states operate, including France."

In August, Swiss newspaper Neue Zürcher Zeitung (NZZ) reported that Lagarde had been referred to as a "putative candidate" to lead the World Economic Forum during a board meeting near Geneva earlier this week.

Citing unnamed sources, Lagarde, who is on the WEF's Board of Trustees, reportedly thanked fellow board members for their confidence in her and said she is "ready to serve", the outlet reported.

The following month, she told French newspaper La Croix that running "would not be a good idea," stating "there's the question of ​vitality, energy, and strength. There's also an unavoidable sense ⁠of sacrifice in this type of commitment. And I'm 70 years ​old."

## France's centrists and right searching for direction

According to Bruno Cautrès, a researcher at the Cevipof institute and lecturer at Sciences Po Paris, Lagarde's polling scores reflect a "loss of direction for France's right, rather than a deep adherence to the idea of Christine Lagarde becoming President."

She could resonate with centrist and conservative voters, "who are financially secure, educated and worried about public debt", while another portion of voters "have likely not followed her career and don't really know who she is."

Lagarde's extensive experience at the IMF and ECB could bolster her credentials with certain voters, but for others it could reinforce perceptions of her as an out-of-touch "technocrat" — a criticism that has also dogged French President Emmanuel Macron.

Her public standing within international institutions could possibly act as a double-edged sword, given that "she will be perceived as part of the elite, which the public believes is responsible for the current economic situation", said Cautrès.

"She's been living abroad for fifteen years, working in prestigious international institutions, where she's earned a very good living. There is a question mark around her ability to understand French voters and the population," he added.

## 'She would not take votes from Mélenchon or Le Pen'

Ultimately, a Lagarde candidacy would not "take any votes away from Mélenchon or Le Pen", Bernard Dolez, professor of political science at the Paris 1 Panthéon-Sorbonne university told Euronews.

She is likely to seduce voters inclined to support centrist Gabriel Attal and conservative Édouard Philippe, both of whom served as prime ministers under Emmanuel Macron.

"The challenge for any candidate at this stage in the central and conservative bloc is to expand beyond their existing base. This is a significant challenge, as current candidates, including Glucksmann, Philippe Attal, Retailleau, and even established figures, are struggling to do so", commented Dolez.

Over the past decade, the country's political landscape has been reshaped, precipitated by the election of centrist Emmanuel Macron in 2017, who fractured the traditional left and right with the party he created.

As France's central and conservative blocs struggle to gain ground, they remain divided over the prospect of holding a primary to select a single candidate. Disputes have also centred around including politicians on the right of the political spectrum in a possible primary.

## Public debt remains a major campaign flashpoint

Given that she is not an official candidate, nor the author of a programme, Lagarde's positions remain unknown, beyond her pro-European stance, along with her support for fiscal discipline and structural reform.

Nevertheless, France's public debt and fiscal crisis have emerged as a major campaign flashpoint, as the government seeks to push its 2027 budget through a divided parliament. Its proposed austerity measures have sparked a backlash among opposition parties and trade unions, amid major social unrest.

This comes as protests which started out in secondary schools have broadened into a wider movement, fuelled by the cost of living crisis and rising energy prices, exacerbated by the Iran war and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz.

Bernard Dolez underlined that "economic decision-makers domestically and abroad are counting on the arrival of a charismatic figure who could emerge as a saviour for them."

In September, Lagarde criticised Mélenchon's proposal to cancel a proportion of French debt held by the ECB, warning that such a move would violate EU treaty rules and undermine confidence among financial markets.

A possible Le Pen victory has unsettled markets, who fear that her economic policies could complicate efforts to reduce France's public debt, push up borrowing costs and create further uncertainty across the eurozone.
