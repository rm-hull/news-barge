A lot like the central nervous system of the company, ERP software provides a centralized system that connects core business operations into a shared database. Where the human brain coordinates our heart, lungs and muscles to enable movement, an ERP connects finance, HR, inventory and sales so a business runs as a single, synchronized organism.

Much like the brain is to us humans, ERP systems have become central to how organizations manage suppliers, approve spend, forecast cash flow and make financial decisions. But there is still a fundamental gap between understanding what needs to happen with money and actually moving it.

An ERP can determine what a business needs to buy, which suppliers it works with and what has been approved. From there, invoices can be processed, payments approved and cash flow managed. Yet, in many organizations, the final step still takes place somewhere else, across a collection of bank portals, payment providers and manual processes.

As business becomes more global and financially complex, this disconnect is becoming harder to justify. If ERP is already the system businesses rely on to manage their financial operations, it makes sense for it to play a greater role in executing them too.

## Turning visibility into action

ERP systems have become exceptionally good at giving businesses visibility into their financial operations. They can show where money is being spent, which suppliers are owed, what invoices are due and how a payment will affect cash flow. However, limitations arise at the next stage, when finance teams are required to leave the ERP to act on the information it has provided. This creates a disconnect between the financial decision and the financial action.

A finance leader may approve an international supplier payment within an ERP system, only to then move to one of its banking environments, of which there are likely to be multiple, to initiate the payment, manage foreign exchange and monitor its progress. The information exists in one place, while the action happens somewhere else. To continue the brain analogy, imagine taking information provided by your brain but having to transfer to another body to act on it. Hugely inconvenient, and hardly an efficient way to operate.

Embedding payments directly into an ERP can close this gap. Instead of simply telling businesses what needs to happen, the ERP can enable them to act on that information within the same environment. It represents an important shift in what we should expect from ERP. Rather than stopping at financial visibility and approval, the system can help businesses execute what they need to do next as a natural extension of its existing functionality.

The benefit is straightforward. The more of the financial workflow an ERP can support, the more useful and embedded it becomes within the organization, reducing the number of disconnected steps that customers must manage themselves.

## The hidden cost of fragmented finance

For finance teams, one of the biggest costs of fragmented IT infrastructure is the operational work required to connect it. When payment instructions and bank activity sit outside the ERP, teams spend valuable time reconciling transactions, checking information and resolving discrepancies. Multiple bank relationships and currency corridors add further complexity.

As transaction volumes grow, this can create pressure to add headcount simply to manage the increased workload, adding costs at the same time. Embedding payment execution and reconciliation into the ERP offers a different route. By connecting payment activity with the financial data already held within the system, businesses can remove repetitive manual work and scale financial operations without complexity and costs increasing at the same rate.

For businesses making payments across borders, the opportunity is particularly significant. The underlying payment infrastructure can be complex, but the experience for the finance team does not have to be. Embedding global payment capabilities into the ERP they already use can make international payments feel like a natural part of the existing financial process, rather than a separate activity requiring additional systems and expertise.

## ERP could become the place where money actually moves

Over a number of years, ERP systems have evolved from accounting software into central platforms for managing increasingly sophisticated business processes. But that evolution isn't finished yet. Closing the gap between managing money and moving it could be the next logical step for ERP, with embedded global payments providing a missing piece in the puzzle.

By connecting financial information, approvals, payment execution and reconciliation within the same system, businesses gain a more complete view of where money is going, when it is moving and what is happening along the way. That means greater visibility and fewer disconnected processes for finance professionals, while ERP providers can create a more valuable and deeply embedded platform for their customers.

Businesses are constantly seeking ways to reduce the points of friction between making a decision and acting on it. ERP already sits at the center of many of those decisions. The future of ERP may not simply be about knowing where the money needs to go. It could be about having the infrastructure to move it there too.
