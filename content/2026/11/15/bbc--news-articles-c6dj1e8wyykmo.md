![French judo Olympic champion smiling and holding a medal during his participation at the 2024 Paris Olympics.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/d041/live/ac0f3570-df58-11f1-9172-871e44a840d8.jpg)

Five-times Olympic champion Teddy Riner is one of France's most popular athletes

**Apart from an overwhelming urge to pin one's opponent to the floor of the house, there is no obvious connection between politics and the world of top-level judo.**

That has not stopped France's much-garlanded world heavyweight champion Teddy Riner from throwing his hat into the ring for next April's presidential race.

The Guadeloupe-born 37-year-old may have no experience of public office, no party behind him, and as yet no manifesto. All so far is a slogan *La France qu'on aime* (The France we love), a declaration on social media, and a campaign visit to a hospital in the Seine-Saint-Denis suburb of northern Paris.

But it would be a foolhardy pundit who would totally write off his chances.

Riner is, after all, one of the most popular figures in modern-day France, regularly topping the polls for embodying the nation's "values". He is a proven winner, with five Olympic golds and 11 world championships under his belt, as well as a successful career in business.

More than 28 million people have watched his election broadcast on Instagram and X, shot with proper presidential decorum against a backdrop of books and a French flag. Some 22% told an Ifop poll within 24 hours they "could" vote for him.

![France's five-time Olympic judo champion Teddy Riner appears in a video displayed on a smartphone and released on his Instagram social media account](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/aee1/live/3de055d0-df61-11f1-a8af-15d7a25dc0d1.jpg)

Teddy Riner's video has attracted 14.5 million views on Instagram alone

Actors and television personalities have become presidents in other countries. The ex-leader of Liberia – George Weah – was formerly a professional footballer.

So why not a judoka in the Élysée Palace?

"Anyone who thinks that being out of the political in-crowd disqualifies Riner from running for the Élysée would be very wrong," says Guillaume Tabard of Le Figaro.

"But [Riner] too would be wrong if he thinks not belonging to the in-crowd is all it takes."

For now the whole of the French political world has decided to take the Riner candidacy seriously – or at least to pretend to. That is partly because he is such a popular personality that mocking his political hopes could easily rebound badly.

But it is also because the state of French politics – outside of the burgeoning forces of the hard left and hard right – is one of total disarray.

The centre-ground is floundering, with no candidate yet emerging who can inspire anything like the sense of mission of hard-right leader Marine Le Pen and Jean-Luc Mélenchon on the hard left, who for now look set to face off in the final round of the presidential election in May.

So when a popular figure like Riner enters the race - proclaiming himself "neither left, nor right", wanting to "unite the country" and "put money back in people's pockets", railing equally against "racists" and "yobs who scare people off our streets" – politicians have to take note.

]([https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/42ef/live/73d510b0-df5a-11f1-a8af-15d7a25dc0d1.jpg](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/42ef/live/73d510b0-df5a-11f1-a8af-15d7a25dc0d1.jpg))

Hard-right leader Marine Le Pen currently leads the polls in the race to be France's next president

It is perfectly possible, probable even, that Riner's campaign will fizzle out after a brief period of hype, and that the judoka will after all be a joker in the pack. But it is also possible that it won't, in which case the pre-election arithmetic will need a hasty readjustment: and it won't be the centrists who are smiling.

Thus far, Riner has shown himself a master of the codes required for this kind of out-of-nowhere candidacy, the first of which is to press home the message: "Don't Worry! I'm NOT a Politician!"

In this day and age in France, anyone who smacks of the "system" is automatically downgraded in voters' eyes, so it is important to emphasise that you are only running for office because, one, you have experienced real life (unlike those loafers in Paris); and two, you have discovered that there are solutions out there for the country's many problems. Solutions that are invisible to those same blinkered apparatchiks.

All this Riner was able to express in last Monday's social media broadcast by using sophisticated rhetorical devices.

"People say I am a fool for wanting to run for the presidency," he said.

"Am I a fool? Well, if refusing to see France sink under the weight of debt; refusing to see mothers and fathers grit their teeth every time they do their weekly shop, and tremble with fear when their kids go out on the street… if all that means being a fool, then yes call me a fool."

It was a promising oratorial start for the sportsman, and certainly attracted the viewers.

But if he is serious – and Riner says he never does anything half-heartedly - that's not how he became an Olympic champion – then the hard work begins now.

What does Riner actually want to do? Who will be on his team? Where does he position himself on the all-consuming matters of the day: pension-reform, investment in education, the cost of living, public debt, the rise of populism or Russia's war in Ukraine?

In short, how does he intend to transform his undeniable popularity as a sporting figure into a political mandate?

Protesters have for weeks taken to the streets in France over wages, the cost of living and public services

Broadly, Riner appears to be on the right on economic matters and law and order, but being black and of Caribbean background he is also very strong on racism. When he says he is anti-extremism, he is referring mainly to Marine Le Pen.

In other words his pitch is somewhere in the centre, and against the extremes.

The trouble is, this is already an extremely crowded area, with important figures like ex-prime ministers Edouard Philippe and Gabriel Attal signally failing to take off.

Currently there are a dozen or so candidates in the area between centre right and centre left, stretching from the Socialist-backed Raphaël Glucksman to the Republicans' Bruno Retailleau on the right.

The latest possible addition is Christine Lagarde, who steps down as head of the European Central Bank next February.

![French President Emmanuel Macron (R) awards French judoka Teddy Riner (L) with the National Order of Merit during an award ceremony at the end of the parade of French athletes who competed in the Paris 2024 Olympic and Paralympic Games in Paris on September 14, 2024.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/f92e/live/0fe871e0-df9c-11f1-a9ad-6774a3284d10.jpg)

President Emmanuel Macron is among the few politicians who appears genuinely pleased by Riner's decision to run

The addition of yet another contender will dilute their polling numbers even further, making a second-round qualification for Mélenchon more likely.

So no wonder the leader of France Unbowed (LFI) seemed so happy about Riner's decision to run. "We know how it goes: neither right nor left means not left," Mélenchon told reporters on Friday.

The only other politician who seems genuinely pleased, rather than putting on a fixed smile, is President Emmanuel Macron, who said Riner was a "great champion" and that politics "did not belong to the professionals".

That could be explained by the president's cool relations with his former prime ministers, Philippe and Attal, both of whom are doing all they can to distance themselves from their unpopular one-time boss.

Riner said he viewed Macron's praise as a "poisoned chalice", telling reporters on Saturday that he was "not the president's envoy, I'm not a Macronist".

Politics and judo have very little in common, but of the two, as Riner will soon discover, the more vicious is not the one that is fought out hand-to-hand after mutual acknowledgement on the *tatami* mat.

In Japanese, judo actually means "the gentle way".

If he starts looking like a serious candidate, Riner must know his life will be turned upside down, not just his physical form.

In politics you do not bow in respect to your opponent. You give him a metaphorical headbutt.
