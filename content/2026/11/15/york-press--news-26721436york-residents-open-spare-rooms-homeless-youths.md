Residents across York and North Yorkshire are helping youths at risk of homelessness – by opening the door to their spare rooms.

Youth homelessness charity SASH (Safe and Sound Homes) is working with around 20 trained households, known as hosts, who provide emergency accommodation for 16 to 25-year-olds at risk of homelessness.

“These are ordinary people who do an exceptional thing,” said Nick Tilney, placement manager at SASH.

After being given accommodation, Nick said SASH, which is now in its 30th year, continues to support the young person in their day-to-day life to avoid them facing homelessness in the future.

Nick, who has worked at SASH for 19 years, admitted the charity continues to face high demand, and recruiting hosts is “ongoing and a challenge”.

One in 60 young people in the UK faced homelessness or were at risk of homelessness in 2024-25, equivalent to 123,934 young people nationwide, according to Centrepoint.

Nick said that “whatever societal changes arrive”, such as the cost-of-living crisis, “the constant is that there is a need for [SASH] and other services who do great work”.

But he said SASH’s model of working with the community has helped avoid youths facing homelessness in the future.

It has also resulted in youths staying in touch with their hosts later in life which Nick said there is “something very meaningful about”.

“This model does seem to have a little bit more hope built into it,” he said. “It is a community-led solution to youth homelessness … I think by knowing the young people more directly, and knowing the hosts along the way, I can see how effective it has been.”

Nick said there is no one reason behind a young person becoming homeless in York and North Yorkshire.

“It’s usually a combination of challenges that build up over time,” he said. “These run deeper, possibly, than the different societal trends that happen – families that are experiencing some degree of conflict, or there could be domestic abuse, there could be mental health difficulties and challenges.”

However, “history shows” that youth homelessness is “consistently a challenge”, Neil said. “It’s why we’ve existed for 30 years, to try and play our part in meeting that challenge.”

## People should look at youth homelessness 'through a lens of kindness and understanding'

Neil said there is a “lot of good support” for young people facing homelessness but there is still a need for greater investment in the sector to help.

“Providing somewhere to live and somewhere safe is incredibly important,” he said. “But what changes outcomes for people is the support that comes with it: relationships, encouragement, guidance, assistance.

“… We, I believe, genuinely work tirelessly to do that, but we don’t do it alone – we do try to work in partnerships with others. We do have this window of opportunity when young people are with us.”

He said the “biggest thing people could do is look at [youth homelessness] through a lens of kindness and understanding”.

“Just imagine if it’s a series of events that happened to them at a young age, largely out of their control, and they ended up with nowhere stable to live. I think what you’d hope for is as soft a landing as possible and as much support as possible.”
