## Alfie the Cavapoo is enjoying new-found celebrity status in York

Alfie the Cavapoo, owned by Wayne Chadwick from YO1 and York Roast Co, on Bonfire Night. *(Image: Wayne Chadwick)*

**This is the second column for Wayne's World written by Wayne Chadwick, managing director of YO1 Radio and owner of The York Roast Co.**

You never quite know what’s around the corner when it comes to writing a column and including a young dog named Alfie within it.

In my first piece for The Press, I introduced you to my Cavapoo after he left a marked impression on the newspaper’s new editor.

But people seem to adore Alfie, and his built-in cuteness certainly helps with forgiving the occasional “faux pas”.

Following his inaugural appearance in The Press, I have been stopped by numerous people asking, “how is Alfie?”

Indeed, wherever I go, and just like Velcro, he is at my side – although now usually walking in front of me, head held high, due to his new-found celebrity status.

Alfie loves being centre stage so much so that when he walks into YO1 Radio’s studios, ahead of me presenting my show, he is always greeted by the team with cuddles and enthusiasm.

He’s a real showstopper.

Only when the excitement (and Alfie!) calms down might someone say, “oh, hi Wayne".

That’s where we are but in truth, he’s my little mate and I’m so proud of him.

My partner and I were a little worried about Alfie on the run up to Bonfire Night.

It would be his first experience of fireworks being only 10 months old.

On the fourth of November we heard a few fireworks in the distance.

Alfie barked and was clearly sensitive to the periodic bangs and cracks.

We decided to buy him a pair of dog earmuffs ready for the following day so, on Bonfire Night itself, we were ready.

Alfie had other ideas, though.

He wore them for literally five minutes and then pawed them off.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21695843.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Alfie the Cavapoo watching the fireworks on Bonfire Night. *(Image: Wayne Chadwick)*

He was having none of it and was clearly more interested in working out where the noises were coming from.

Fast forward to Saturday when bonfire events were in full flow all over North Yorkshire.

The sky was lit up, and the atmosphere was full of smoke from rockets and the like.

We closed our patio door curtains and turned up the TV louder than usual in an attempt.

To reduce the stress.

Not, again, Alfie was having none of it as he bypassed the curtains and placed himself with his nose pressed firmly against the patio window.

We then decided to open the curtains fully to improve his view.

He sat there simply mesmerised by the colours and configurations of the numerous rockets that climbed into the sky and cascaded down in a flurry of colour.

The noise didn’t seem to bother him and that was a massive relief to us being more than aware of the stress this annual event can cause.

Bonfire night.

Done.

Those who know me well are often subject to my “obtuse” humour.

For an older guy I have a somewhat childish sense of humour - often joking or pranking people but always laughing with them and not at them.

I had a fabulous relationship with my late nan who, sadly, lost her mobility at around the age of 50 due to a stroke which left her paralysed down one side.

It didn’t affect her mental ability, but her life did become more constrained spending many hours either listening to the radio or watching TV whilst grandad was at work.

We would often joke around together and, at just 11 years old, I would play pranks on her which she never minded.

In fact, she loved the attention and always seemed to enjoy it when I was on school holidays as we all lived in the same house so my mother could care for her when my grandad was at work.

We’d sing together and record it on my cassette tape recorder.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21695893.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Wayne Chadwick, managing director and chairman of YO1 Radio and owner of The York Roast Co *(Image: Wayne Chadwick)*

I’d take her out in her wheelchair and at times race along with nan giggling away.

We had our own shared vision of the world, and I would often perform little chores for her including cleaning her false teeth.

I’d take them away on a saucer to the bathroom and give them a good scrub before returning them.

One day I decided to up my game, tying some white cotton around nan’s bottom set of teeth, reeling out the cotton and hanging it over the central ceiling light.

My nan’s vision wasn’t the best, so she didn’t notice and I do remember how grateful she was for me cleaning her dentures.

She called my mother to sing my praises as a dutiful grandson.

Glancing from the living room door, mum saw my nan smiling and showing off her white piano key teeth.

With a rapid tug of my wrist the bottom set of dentures were swinging from the ceiling light and the look of surprise on both my nan and my mother’s face was priceless.

At this stage I wasn’t sure if I was in trouble but almost instantaneously they both burst into raptures of laughter.

It’s fair to say that my grandad was somewhat less impressed when he returned from work, but nan soon smoothed that out for me.

After all we had a special bond and that same “obtuse” humour.

It must be a DNA thing.
