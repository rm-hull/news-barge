LIV Golf filed for bankruptcy in September

**LIV Golf - backed by more than $5bn (£3.8bn) from Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund - paid millions to players and executives in the weeks before filing for bankruptcy, while leaving a trail of unpaid bills from UK suppliers.**

BBC Sport has seen invoices from five small businesses totalling hundreds of thousands of pounds, all of which were overdue before LIV made its final payments to players.

Global Infusion Group,, external a Buckinghamshire company that provided catering at July's LIV Golf UK event, is still owed £321,671, due on 7 August.

Suffolk-based BD365 Clean, external provided 108 cleaning staff and is owed £75,225, most of which was due on 20 July and 13 August.

"It just feels completely immoral," said Bonnie May, chief executive of Global Infusion Group.

"It really matters to a business of our scale. The impact has been huge, both personally and professionally."

Only $2m (£1.5m) has been earmarked so far by the Saudi owners to pay unsecured creditors, leaving many smaller companies unsure if they will receive anything at all.

Yet court filings reveal LIV made 59 payments to golfers on 31 July, amounting to $16.6m (£12.6m) and matching prize money for the same LIV Golf UK event.

A further $34.2m (£25.9m) went to players between 17 August and 3 September following tournaments in New York and Indianapolis, including $6.4m (£4.9m) to 2026 champion Jon Rahm just five days before the bankruptcy filing.

LIV Golf's bankruptcy and its debt to British businesses

## 'How can they sleep at night?'

Four senior LIV executives also received retention bonuses totalling nearly $3m (£2.3m) in August.

"It's sickening," said Krissi Foskett, owner of BD365 Clean, who described the unpaid bills as "almost an extinction-level event" for her team.

"How can they sleep at night, knowing they are potentially putting companies out of business?"

Meanwhile, public records list more than £3,000 owed to Goodysphere,, external a family-run Yorkshire firm that provides a sensory bus designed to help autistic children who may find busy events overwhelming.

LIV Golf did not respond to our request for comment.

However, in August, LIV chief executive Scott O'Neil said he hoped they would be able to pay their suppliers.

"What I would say to them is we're doing everything we can to make sure we can do right by them and the work they committed."

## 'It's Saudi money, that’s safe as houses'

The controversial breakaway league had been owned and bankrolled by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund (PIF) since its launch season in 2022.

But in April, the fund announced it would end its backing after the 2026 season, putting LIV's future in doubt.

"Our understanding was that the funding would take LIV through 2026," said Global Infusion Group's May, whose company had about 60 staff catering for players and broadcasters.

"We weren't aware the funding had already stopped and there wasn't enough to actually fulfil their obligations."

PIF's multi-billion-dollar investment helped it to lure some of golf's biggest names and disrupt the PGA Tour's dominance of the sport.

"It's Saudi money, that's safe as houses, we thought," said Foskett.

"Their head honchos came along and enjoyed hospitality. We were there cleaning up at one o'clock in the morning then back in at five in the morning, only to be treated like this."

PIF remains the majority owner of Newcastle United, while Saudi Arabia will host the 2034 Fifa World Cup.

"I'm sure the country won't want to be saddled with the characterisation that it doesn't pay its bills," said professor Simon Chadwick, who is an expert in the business and politics of sport across Africa, Europe and Asia.

"I do find it surprising that they would simply walk away. I think they would want to engage in some kind of remedial action, particularly in terms of managing their image and reputation."

Filmmaker Joe Bentley spent eight days from April working on a video for LIV's YouTube channel that is still online.

He is owed nearly £6,000, with £2,226 due on 31 July.

The video was part of the 'LIV Back' series, set up to "focus on the impact LIV players and teams are making off the course".

He filmed at two schools taking part in 'Little Sticks', a LIV outreach programme which aimed to provide golf equipment and training in areas of deprivation.

"You feel massively deflated, a bit cheated and undervalued," said Bentley.

"Everyone I dealt with at LIV was lovely. The first email I sent chasing was to a production coordinator or a manager and even she was appalled that we hadn't been paid."

Gateshead Council is also owed £1,912, due on 22 August, relating to 'Little Sticks'.

"Transformation is not a short-term process," said Professor Chadwick.

"If you're going to embark on investment programmes in community sport, grassroots sport, you've got to make a long-term commitment and those changes have got to take place."

## What happens next?

"The contacts we had sadly all lost their jobs," said Giles Mitchell, whose company, Office Pantry, supplied food to LIV's office on London's South Bank.

"We didn't hear anything from them and then their emails started bouncing back."

There was hope the competition could survive in some form with new investment, but in November LIV announced it was winding down operations.

In the bankruptcy filings, LIV estimated it had between $500m and $1bn (£380m-£760m) in liabilities and at least 1,000 creditors.

The largest 30 unsecured creditors alone were listed with claims of more than $64m (£49m).

"I think the message is loud and clear to somebody like me that, no, we're not going to get paid for any of this," said May.

"If anything did come our way, I'm thinking it's going to be pennies in the pound, nothing which will have any impact, or even cover our legal fees."

## Who else is owed money?

Fourteen golfers were among the published creditors with the 30 largest unsecured claims.

Rahm topped the list with a claim of $7.5m (£5.7m), which he agreed to give up as part of his deal to leave the league.

Bryson DeChambeau ($5.7m/£4.3m), Dustin Johnson ($5.5m/£4.2m), Cameron Smith ($4.8m/£3.6m) and Tyrrell Hatton ($3.4m/£2.6m) also featured.

IMG Media, the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, and British golf YouTuber Rick Shiels were each listed with claims of more than $1m (£760,000) too.

Meanwhile, BBC Sport understands the R&A is owed about £500,000 for advice on sustainable maintenance of courses used by LIV.

A hearing is scheduled for January to consider the wind-down plan, but creditors may have to wait longer to find out how much they will get back.

"I've had nights just sat there in tears, thinking, what is the point," said BD365 Clean's Foskett.

"How much comes in will honestly depend on whether or not the business will still be going at the end of January.

"I don't want to throw the towel in."
