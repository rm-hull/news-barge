Researchers have uncovered an unexpected lead suggesting that a blood test designed to detect a toxic protein linked to Alzheimer’s disease might eventually assist in diagnosing a distinct neurological condition in athletes and others exposed to repeated head trauma.

Currently, physicians rely on evaluating symptoms such as mood swings and memory loss to determine whether a patient might have chronic traumatic encephalopathy, or CTE, a disorder associated with concussions.

However, because several brain diseases produce similar symptoms, a definitive CTE diagnosis can currently only be confirmed by examining brain tissue after death.

Two small studies indicate a potential breakthrough, demonstrating that an experimental blood test engineered to track abnormal tau protein in Alzheimer’s patients also appears to detect high concentrations of that protein in individuals with severe CTE.

While tau normally helps support nerve cells, it forms destructive tangles inside neurons in Alzheimer’s disease.

To monitor these tangles, scientists developed a blood test to measure a specific tau fragment known as eMTBR-tau243.

Researchers at Washington University in St. Louis unexpectedly identified elevated levels of this biomarker during an autopsy study of a patient who did not have Alzheimer’s, but was diagnosed with CTE instead.

That discovery prompted the team to analyze archived blood and brain samples from 11 deceased individuals confirmed to have had CTE.

Compared to stored samples from healthy individuals, blood levels of eMTBR-tau243 were elevated in patients with advanced CTE, according to study co-author Chihiro Sato, a WashU assistant neurology professor.

However, as the team reported at a dementia conference in Japan, the biomarker failed to reliably spot early-stage CTE.

In a separate study, researchers at Boston University and Arizona’s Banner Health examined preserved blood from 21 former amateur and professional football players who had donated their brains for post-mortem research.

Autopsies determined that 16 had died with advanced CTE, two had earlier-stage CTE, and three did not have the disease.

The blood biomarker levels were significantly higher in athletes diagnosed with advanced CTE, researchers reported at a scientific meeting in Boston.

The highest biomarker readings occurred in five participants who suffered from both CTE and Alzheimer’s disease.

Both studies were published in the medical journal JAMA.

The findings remain preliminary and will require far larger studies to confirm a definitive link, cautioned Northwestern University neuropathologist Dr. Rudolph Castellani, who was not involved in the research.

Abnormal tau is present in several brain disorders that can overlap in older adults, and its structure in CTE differs from the form seen in Alzheimer’s, noted Drs. Günter Höglinger and Carla Palleis of Germany’s Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich in an accompanying editorial.

They emphasized that while extensive research remains necessary, the combined studies "represent an important step" toward detecting CTE in living patients.
