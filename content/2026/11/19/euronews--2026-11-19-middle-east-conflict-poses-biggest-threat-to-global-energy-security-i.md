## Disruption to oil supplies through the Strait of Hormuz is driving up costs for households, while the agency says the longer the crisis continues, the harder it will be for global energy markets to recover.

The Middle East conflict has triggered the largest threat to global energy security in history, the International Energy Agency (IEA) has warned, as disrupted oil supplies push up prices and leave consumers increasingly exposed to the fallout.

The comments come following the US and Israeli bombing of Iran, which began in February.

The IEA said in its flagship annual World Energy Outlook report that consumers were increasingly footing the bill as the world struggles to find new solutions to absorb the impact of the conflict on energy supplies.

The agency also warned that, just like previous energy shocks, the crisis will certainly leave "lasting marks".

The IEA made the remarks as Gulf countries continue to struggle with exporting oil since Iran began blockading the Strait of Hormuz and targeting tankers in the area, following the US and Israeli strikes.

Despite attempts by the United States to escort vessels through the Strait – which carried about one-fifth of global oil shipments before the crisis began – supply lines are still severely disrupted.

"The Strait of Hormuz crisis has created the largest threat to energy security in history, one that dwarfs the scale of the oil shocks of the 1970s," IEA Executive Director Fatih Birol wrote in his foreword to the report.

Birol also highlighted the efforts countries had taken to protect households, with producing nations bringing more supplies into the market and using alternative routes, as well as China buying less from international markets.

"Governments have intervened to try and provide some shelter for consumers, but they have nonetheless faced higher prices and, in some cases, shortfalls of supply," he wrote.

The report also mentioned that "increasingly it is falling to consumers – and high prices – to absorb the impact as other buffers are run down".

Back in July, Birol said the oil market was benefiting from "several cushioning factors", something that isn’t in place now, meaning the outlook is far bleaker.

### No way back?

The IEA's report comes as leaders meet for the COP31 climate talks in Turkey.

There, governments are under significant pressure to tackle climate change after a year of searing heatwaves and wildfires in Europe, as well as deadly floods in Nepal.

The IEA uses three scenarios for its energy outlook. These are a cautious one, which takes into account policies that are currently in place, a middle scenario, which looks at "stated" government policies, including measures that have yet to be adopted, and a third, which considers a world that reaches net zero emissions by 2050.

Climate scientist Bill Hare, founder of the Climate Analytics think tank, said the world had to commit to the most interventionist scenario outlined by the IEA.

"If we don't get on the net-zero train the IEA has outlined, then we are condemning the world to irreversible and permanent warming of 2.5C or above," he told the AFP.

In this particular report, the IEA also added two scenarios based on what might happen if the Middle East crisis persists.

"While the energy sector and the global economy have shown considerable resilience, the longer the crisis lasts, the deeper the effects will be," Birol said in a press release, adding that he doesn't “see any credible pathway for the energy world to return to the way it was before."
