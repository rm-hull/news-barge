Young people around the world are struggling to achieve the traditional milestones of adulthood, from financial independence and stable employment to marriage and parenthood, according to new research by the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA).

Despite being better educated and more connected to information than previous generations, millions are finding it harder to secure the jobs, housing and financial stability needed to build the lives they want.

The pressures are particularly acute in sub-Saharan Africa, where limited economic opportunities and rapid population growth are presenting governments with mounting challenges, according to UNFPA’s the *State of World Population* report, published on Thursday.

Young people face significant barriers to entering the workforce, with more than a third of Senegal’s young people unemployed in 2024, and 30 per cent in Mali.

Globally, one in five young people are not in employment, education or training, while four in five young adults in low-income countries do not have a secure paid job.

“The gap is not in young people's ambition, it is in their opportunity,” said Diene Keita, UNFPA's executive director, adding that today's young people are better educated than previous generations, have grown up with more access to information and have been taught that they have human rights.

“They can expect to live longer, thanks to decades of progress in public health, economic development, and breathtaking advances in technology,” Ms Keita told a press briefing. “But all these measures, they should be the most successful generation of young adults in history, but many are not yet seeing the gains they were promised.”

The report, which draws on a survey of more than 100,000 young adults across 73 countries and territories, challenges the idea that younger generations are increasingly rejecting marriage and parenthood.

More than 90 per cent of respondents said they wanted at least one child, while 70 per cent said marriage was their ideal relationship arrangement. Financial insecurity was the most commonly reported obstacle to finding a partner, cited by more than half, with almost three quarter saying it was a barrier to having children.

Nearly nine in 10 respondents said financial security was a precondition for feeling ready to become a parent.

The report highlights the particular challenges facing girls in sub-Saharan Africa, where poverty, child marriage and adolescent pregnancy continue to restrict access to education.

In Benin, only about one in three girls completes the first cycle of secondary education, with financial difficulties, early marriage and pregnancy among the main reasons. In Chad, secondary school enrolment rate is just 41 per cent, while over 60 per cent of women were married before the age of 18.

The report also points to examples of progress, however, with Nigeria girls' secondary school completion rates rising by a quarter between 2018 and 2024. Child marriage is also falling following investment in free schooling, school meals and safe spaces.

The challenges facing young people also extend to conflict, with the number living within 50km of an active conflict more than doubling since 1990, to 340 million.

Cuts to international aid are also risking progress in healthcare and education, while UNFPA warned there could be as many as 22 million additional preventable deaths by 2030 if the reductions continue.
