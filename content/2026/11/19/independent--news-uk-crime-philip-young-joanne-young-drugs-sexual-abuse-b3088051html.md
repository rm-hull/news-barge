Offenders who drug and rape victims are forming networks and swapping advice on how to sedate people, according to police, who fear the true number of attacks is “significantly” higher than the cases on record.

The warning follows the conviction of Philip Young, who drugged and raped his wife Joanne Young over a 13-year period at their family home in Swindon, Wiltshire.

Young advertised his wife for sex on sites including Craigslist, Grindr and Fab Swingers.

His offending came to light after a report that he had uploaded bestiality images to the Kik messaging site led police to examine his devices, on which they found intimate images of his wife.

Wiltshire Police, which investigated the former Tory councillor, said the full extent of drug-rape offending was unknown because victims might not realise they had been targeted.

A spokesman for the force said: “While cases involving organised drug-facilitated sexual assault remain relatively rare compared to other forms of sexual offending, intelligence and other recent cases would suggest this may be a significantly under-reported crime type.

“Victims are often unaware they have been drugged, may have fragmented memories of events, or may initially attribute symptoms to alcohol consumption. These factors make detection and reporting particularly challenging.”

James Foster, specialist CPS prosecutor, said: “The very nature of this offending is that the victim will not recall, will not necessarily have a memory, and the perpetrator is usually the husband or partner, or someone trusted by them, which is what makes the offending so egregious.

“And it happened behind closed doors in this case, it occurred in the family home.

“So, one of the challenges in these cases is that once the discovery has been made it is a unique and distressing set of circumstances where police know more about what’s happened to a victim than they do.”

The police spokesman said offenders were known to share information with each other, although there was no evidence that Young had done so.

He said: “There is growing recognition that offenders may work together to target vulnerable individuals, administer substances without their knowledge, and exploit the resulting incapacity.

“The covert nature of this offending means the true scale is difficult to quantify, and it is likely that some incidents are never identified as crimes.

“To expose more cases, it is vital to raise public awareness of the warning signs of drug-facilitated sexual assault, encourage prompt reporting, and ensure victims are supported and believed when they come forward.

![Specialist CPS prosecutor James Foster fears drug-rape cases could be more widespread](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/15/01M7T78GXH44J1AEDPWM2F1ZST.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“Close partnership working between police, healthcare professionals, licensed venues, forensic services and safeguarding agencies is also essential to identify patterns, link incidents and disrupt organised offenders.”

He also urged potential victims to come forward, saying: “We would encourage anyone who believes they may have been a victim of drug-facilitated sexual assault, regardless of how much time has passed, to contact police.

“Even where a victim has limited recollection of events, information they provide may help investigators identify linked offences, corroborate intelligence, and protect others from harm.

“Individuals can report directly to police or access support through specialist sexual assault services, where they can receive advice, safeguarding and support throughout the process.

“Every report matters. What may appear to be an isolated incident can, when combined with other information, help reveal a wider pattern of offending and enable law enforcement to bring perpetrators to justice.”
