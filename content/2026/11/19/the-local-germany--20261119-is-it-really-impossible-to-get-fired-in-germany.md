Alongside finding an affordable flat (and getting the rental contract in your own name), securing an *unbefristeter Arbeitsvertrag,* or unlimited employment contract, is seen by many international residents as the holy grail of building a life in Germany.

It certainly offers a sense of stability. However, debates about workers' rights in Germany often suggest that it is virtually impossible to lose your job.

On Reddit, threads with titles like 'How likely is it to get fired in Germany?' and 'Impossible to get fired… a myth?', regularly pop up, while it has even become a hot topic among German politicians.

So can you easily lose your job in Germany?

While a "hire and fire" culture, as seen in some other countries such as the United States, is not possible in Germany, the truth is a little more complicated.

Nick Schäfer, a HR interim manager in Frankfurt who recently launched a job support platform for English speakers in Germany called Joblumi, told The Local the idea that German employees are 'unfireable' is "a bit of a misconception, but it’s nuanced".

"Germany is always in the top five [countries with] the most complex labour law lists, so there’s no easy answer to any question around terminations," he added.

But while Germany does have strong protections against unfair dismissal, that doesn't mean employees cannot lose their jobs.

"It’s 100 percent possible to get fired in Germany. However, it’s comparatively difficult to fire someone once they have passed the six-month probation period," Schäfer said.

Before enjoying the full protection offered by German employment law, employees generally have to get through the first six months of their employment.

This is known as the *Probezeit,* or probation period. It is usually agreed for six months, although employers can set a shorter period.

During an agreed probation period, both the employee or employer can terminate the employment contract with just two weeks' notice. Employers are also not generally required to provide a reason for doing so.

**What about after the probation period?**

Once you've completed the first six months, you may get an unlimited contract, which means you'll likely benefit from Germany's *Kündigungsschutzgesetz* (Dismissal Protection Act).

Getting passed this hurdle is a big relief for internationals in Germany. "Expats who pass probation appreciate the labour laws from my experience because it gives a sense of security," said Schäfer.

But this protection depends on the size of the company you work for. The law generally applies to businesses with more than 10 employees. Smaller businesses are generally not covered by the dismissal protection provisions, although other employment laws and protections still apply.

Another factor is the type of contract you have. Some employers offer fixed-term contracts (*befristete Arbeitsverträge*), which automatically expire on the agreed date unless renewed. This can make employment less secure, as there is no guarantee of an extension. Temporary agency workers and freelancers also have different employment arrangements and levels of protection.

If the legislation does apply (as it would in most unlimited contracts) an employer must have a legally recognised reason to dismiss you. These fall into three main categories:

* Personal reasons (*personenbedingte Kündigung*): When an employee is no longer able to fulfil the requirements of their job. This could include losing a professional qualification or, under certain circumstances, long-term illness.* Conduct or behavioural reasons (* verhaltensbedingte Kündigung*): When an employee breaches their contractual obligations. Examples could include repeatedly failing to show up for work, being late, refusing to carry out reasonable instructions or serious misconduct.* Operational reasons (* betriebsbedingte Kündigung*): When a company reduces its workforce for business reasons, such as restructuring.

In reality, Schäfer said that it can be difficult to terminate a contract for behavioural or personal reasons.

"Operational reasons are the only real valid option in 90 percent of cases," he said.

**Agreeing severance terms**

Schäfer said that letting someone go often comes down to the severance package agreed. In Germany, there is no general statutory right to severance pay (*Abfindung*). It is usually up for negotiation or is written into a collective agreement.

"Firing someone is only a question of money," he said. "If the standard legal way doesn’t work, a mutual agreement always works.

"Everyone has a 'number' for which they would agree to separate, so it’s about finding that number. Once the option of separation comes up, most employees don’t even want to continue working at that employer, so the threat of reinstatement after an unsuccessful termination is low."

He said that mutual agreements might typically involve a severance payout of 0.5-1.5 monthly gross salaries per year of employment.

Depending on your situation you should seek advice from your works council or union (if there is one) or a specialist solicitor.

A claim for unfair dismissal generally has to be lodged with the Labour Court (*Arbeitsgericht*) within three weeks of receiving the written notice.

You should also seek out the help of an expert if you want to discuss redundancy package details.
