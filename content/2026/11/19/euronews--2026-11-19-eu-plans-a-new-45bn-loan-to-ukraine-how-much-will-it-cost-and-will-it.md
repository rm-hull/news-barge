## Euronews breaks down the European Commission's new proposal to issue a €45 billion support loan to Ukraine. Here's what you need to know.

The European Union is "as committed as ever to support Ukraine in war and in peace", Ursula von der Leyen said as she unveiled a €45 billion support loan to help the country address its growing wartime budget deficit.

"We think this is the best way to give Ukraine a strong hand at the negotiation table," the European Commission president said on Wednesday.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy publicly thanked her, saying "strong support for Ukraine is the best signal to Russia that this war must end".

The loan still has to be greenlit by EU leaders, but the urgency is growing. Moscow's non-stop airstrikes have multiplied costs and worsened Kyiv's dependency on foreign aid.

But governments are also wary of the optics of approving fresh cash for Ukraine at a time of high energy bills, tight budgets and popular discontent. After all, any new pledges will fall on European taxpayers.

Here is everything you need to know about the new loan.

## Why now?

In December last year, EU leaders agreed on a €90 billion loan to fulfil Ukraine's military and financial needs for 2026 and 2027 combined.

The €90 billion loan, **which is still ongoing**, was designed under the assumption that Russia's war would end sometime in 2026.

Instead, the opposite has happened: Moscow has escalated its attacks to make Ukraine's winter insufferable and further cripple the country's economy.

The relentless attacks have disrupted business activity, impaired the energy system and diminished Kyiv's capacity to raise public revenue. The blockade in the Black Sea, a vital route for Ukrainian farmers, has further exacerbated the pain.

The devastation has drilled a **€70 billion** hole in Ukraine's 2027 budget. In their October summit, leaders heard from Zelenskyy and agreed to address the situation.

## Why €45 billion?

This is the division of labour between the EU and its allies.

Last year, the bloc committed to cover two-thirds of Ukraine's estimated €135 billion needs. This produced the €90 billion loan.

Now, it repeats the same formula: the €45 billion will cover two-thirds of the €70 billion shortfall. Other allies, such as the UK, Norway, Canada, Japan and Australia, are expected to supply the remaining €25 billion.

In its proposal, the Commission offers two ways for allies to chip in: a €5 billion top-up through AAA-rated guarantees or an extension of a previous G7 loan backed by the windfall profits stemming from the immobilised Russian assets.

There is little hope that the US will contribute financially, despite the Democratic Party's sweeping victory in the midterm elections.

"It would not be wise to budget on hope alone," a senior Commission official quipped.

## How will it be raised?

The €45 billion will be raised through joint debt, a straightforward option.

The Commission, which is triple-rated, will issue bonds of different maturities to the markets and gradually borrow the money on behalf of all member states.

This is the same method as in the €90 billion loan.

Combined, the EU will raise €135 billion for Ukraine in 2026 and 2027.

## How much will it cost?

Joint debt is always costly – even more so today.

Borrowing costs have soared worldwide in reaction to energy turmoil, geopolitical volatility, high debt concerns and the all-consuming AI race. From Brussels to Washington, public accounting is under strain.

Commission officials estimate an annual bill of roughly €6 billion in interest payments: €3.5 billion from the €90 billion loan (up from €3 billion when it was set up) and somewhere between €2 billion and €2.5 billion from the €45 billion loan.

Member states will subsidise the interest to avoid burdening Ukraine.

As of now, the plan is to pay the annual payments through the €100 billion special envelope that Brussels has allocated for Ukraine in the next seven-year budget. But this will reduce the allocation by €42 billion and slash fungible support for Kyiv.

An alternative plan would be to cover the payments through a separate financial instrument to keep the €100 billion envelope intact.

The question adds yet another link between Ukraine and the budget, which are rapidly becoming one single, politically explosive package. Both depend on unanimity

## What about those countries that want an opt-out?

In the lead-up to the presentation, **four member states** – Slovakia, the Czech Republic, Hungary and Bulgaria – indicated they will not be part of the new scheme.

"I say a clear no to war, weapons and war loans, and a yes to peace," Slovak Prime Minister Robert Fico said in a video message.

Slovakia, the Czech Republic and Hungary are already exempted from the €90 billion and want to continue their opt-out policy. Bulgarian Prime Minister Rumen Radev, a vocal critic of weapon deliveries to Ukraine, wants to join the club.

The attitude **has infuriated** German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, who argues that "supporting Ukraine is a matter of our indivisible European security, and so must be the burden sharing for that support".

Merz has threatened to veto the next EU budget, which contains billions in cohesion funds that Slovakia, the Czech Republic, Hungary and Bulgaria depend on, if the new loan is not backed by all 27 member states.

"I support the Chancellor," von der Leyen said, taking Merz's side. "I'm very confident we will agree by 27."

The Commission has offered the four countries the option to opt in to the loan's budget strand (€20 billion) and opt out of its military strand (€25 billion). But this is unlikely to sway them, as their opposition is intrinsically political.

## Will it ever be repaid?

The EU will ask Ukraine to repay either the €90 billion or the €45 billion loans only when Russia agrees to provide war reparations as part of a future peace deal.

Until that day comes, it will roll over the loans indefinitely.

Moscow has repeatedly dismissed such a thing, raising the odds that the EU will have to assume responsibility one way or another.

Brussels says it "reserves the right" to use the immobilised Russian assets, worth €210 billion, to repay the aid provided to Ukraine. But this will deprive Kyiv of a much-needed cash injection to rebuild the country after the war ends.

Ukraine, which is already saddled with debts from other partners and institutions, is not expected to pay out of pocket in any case.

"There can always be a political decision to waive the debt," a senior official said.

"De facto, the EU has become the lender of last resort for Ukraine and is likely to be there for some time."
