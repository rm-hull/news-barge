Danish lawmakers have unanimously backed a security agreement with President Donald Trump covering Greenland, in hopes of settling a standoff over the Arctic island's future that has rattled transatlantic relations.

Thursday's vote in Copenhagen saw 109 lawmakers back the deal, with none voting against, according to the parliamentary tally.

The U.S., Denmark and Greenland signed the agreement in September.

![President Donald Trump (L) shakes hands with Greenland's Prime Minister Jens-Frederik Nielsen (R) as Denmark's Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen looks on during a signing ceremony for a trilateral security agreement between the United States, Denmark and Greenland on the sidelines of the 81st United Nations General Assembly in New York on September 22, 2026](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/11/2296049520..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

It was designed to ease tensions after Trump repeatedly threatened to take control of the mineral-rich island, a self-governing Danish territory home to 57,000 people.

Under the deal, the U.S. military's role in Greenland is expanded, including permission to establish a "Golden Dome" missile defense system. At the same time, the agreement reaffirms Danish sovereignty and Greenlanders' right to self-determination.

The deal is not subject to a U.S. Senate vote. It is an executive agreement, and after Thursday's vote in Copenhagen, it will enter into force once the Danish Foreign Ministry informs the U.S. in a diplomatic note that procedures are complete. Greenland's parliament ratified the deal this month.

![Foreign Minister Lars Lokke Rasmussen, seated to the right, during the second legislative process and vote on the security agreement between Denmark, Greenland, and the USA in the Parliament Hall at Christiansborg in Copenhagen](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/11/2026-11-19T095134Z_1328605971_RC2XQOAN3LKK_RTRMADP_3_USA-TRUMP-GREENLAND-DENMARK.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Last week it passed a first reading in Denmark's parliament with backing from all parties. Lawmakers expressed relief and hope that the deal will normalize the situation in Greenland. "However, there are many indications that this is not an end, but rather a beginning," said Mikkel Vedby Rasmussen, a political science professor at the University of Copenhagen.

"With the treaty in hand, the U.S. will be able to demand greater influence on decisions that have security policy implications and on part of the extraction of strategic resources," he said. Other experts say the new agreement does not add much U.S. military access to Greenland.

Trump's refusal to rule out military or economic force to acquire the island triggered a diplomatic crisis within NATO in January. That crisis forced Denmark and Greenland into months of intensive negotiations.

The shortest route from Europe to North America runs via strategically located Greenland, where the U.S. already has its northernmost military base.
