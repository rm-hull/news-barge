# How schools should educate if our children are to flourish in age of AI: Matt Bromley

## At a time when schools are under relentless pressure to improve outcomes, asking pupils to contemplate a painting, wrestle with an ethical dilemma, or find meaning in music might seem an indulgence.

And yet, as AI begins to reshape the world of work and the nature of knowledge itself, such pursuits may prove among the most important things we can teach.

A new “human flourishing” framework, being trialled in schools in England, offers a welcome challenge to our narrow conception of education. Alongside traditional subjects, it places greater emphasis on ethical reasoning, aesthetic appreciation, problem-solving, and developing a sense of agency and purpose.

For too long, education has been synonymous with examination performance, as though the purpose of school were simply to equip young people to pass tests.

Knowledge opens doors and good qualifications can transform lives, but they are not all that matters.

What happens when a young person leaves school? They must navigate relationships, manage money, cope with uncertainty, make moral choices, contribute to their community, and find their place in a changing world. They must learn not merely how to earn a living, but how to live a good life.

And increasingly, they must do so in a world in which machines can write essays, generate images, analyse data, and perform tasks that once required years of human training. If education is reduced to the acquisition and reproduction of information, AI presents an uncomfortable question: what, precisely, are we preparing children for?

The answer may lie in a broader understanding of the curriculum, one that educates the head, hand, and heart…

The head represents the knowledge and understanding that enable us to think clearly. Children need a rich, ambitious academic curriculum, encompassing literature, maths, science, history, geography, and the arts. They need the intellectual foundations to question what they encounter, distinguish evidence from assertion, and recognise when an AI-generated answer is plausible but wrong. Without knowledge, critical thinking has little to work with.

The hand represents the opportunity to put learning into practice. Young people need to make, build, perform, collaborate, and solve real problems. They need experience of the workplace and wider community, to develop practical competence alongside intellectual understanding. In an AI-driven economy, the capacity to turn ideas into action will be vital. So, too, will the human skills required to work alongside others.

Then there is the heart: the moral purpose of education. We must help children develop empathy, resilience, curiosity, self-worth, and a sense of responsibility towards others. They need opportunities to encounter beauty, confront difficult questions, and discover what they value. Technology may help us calculate what’s possible but cannot decide what’s worthwhile.

Crucially, a broader curriculum is also an issue of fairness. Affluent families can provide cultural experiences, practical opportunities, and personal guidance. But for disadvantaged children, school may be the only place to encounter a musical instrument, visit a theatre, debate an ethical question, or discover an unfamiliar career. To deny them these experiences in the name of raising standards is to confuse narrowing education with improving it.

Schools need the time, resources, and professional freedom to offer a genuinely rounded education, with assessment that values more than what can be measured in an examination hall.

We cannot predict every job our children will do, every technology they will encounter, or every difficulty they will face. But we can give them the knowledge to understand the world, the skills to shape it, and the character to make it better.
