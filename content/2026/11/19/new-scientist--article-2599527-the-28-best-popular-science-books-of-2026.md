*New Scientist*writers have picked their favourite popular science reads of the year

It’s the perfect time to dive into the year’s best non-fiction, whether you’re looking for gift-giving inspiration or a brilliant read for yourself. To help, here are the best popular science reads of 2026 according to *New Scientist*staff and writers. You’ll find the big names of science writing here, of course: Carlo Rovelli, Xand Van Tulleken, Maggie Aderin, Jim Al-Khalili. But there are also plenty of lesser-known gems, whether you’re interested in the power of heat to boost your health, a history of plants, why exactly humans sleep so little, or how to deal with the generally overwhelming nature of life. And if you think we’ve missed anything, do come and recommend your own favourite popular science reads here.**Alison Flood**

![The cover of On Time by Jim Al-Khalili](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321109445.jpg?w=840)

## ***On Time*by Jim Al-Khalili** The nature of time is a subject that has perplexed us for centuries. What Jim Al-Khalili does so expertly here is to break down the topic into a series of separate mysteries, explain each one and then build back up again to show how they interrelate and inform each other. Why do we feel time flowing, why does that flow feel continuous like a river, and what exactly is the difference between the past and the future? Al-Khalili tackles all this and much more, and I finished the book feeling like I understood more or less everything about time I would ever need to. Admittedly, that probably isn’t the case, but the fact that Al-Khalili created that illusion is itself a testament to this brilliant book.**Joshua Howgego**

![The cover of Hotwired by Bill Gifford](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321109446.jpg?w=840)

## ***Hotwired*by Bill Gifford**

*Hotwired*:* How the hidden power of heat makes us stronger*explores how saunas and other forms of heat exposure can boost health and athletic performance, and possibly prepare us for a hotter world. Gifford once hated being hot, having grown up in “steamy, swampy” Washington DC, but his interest was piqued by studies from Finland showing that frequent sauna use reduces the risk of heart attacks, stroke and Alzheimer’s disease. Gifford brings us on his personal journey of learning to love heat. He doesn’t do anything in half measures, even signing himself up for one of the world’s most gruelling bike rides under the ferocious Texan sun. The rigorous science mixed with personal stories, humour and tales of tragedy and triumph make this a highly engaging read.**Alice Klein**

![The cover of The Edge of Space-Time](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321114139.jpg?w=840)

## ***The Edge of Space-Time*by Chanda Prescod-Weinstein**

This is a stunningly rich and interdisciplinary book that invites the reader to understand our universe from the ground up, challenging us to think creatively and with urgency. Prescod-Weinstein doesn’t shy away from bringing anthropology, history and politics into the story of the cosmos, which enriches and expands it past more conventional approaches. And she has fun, too, as her book is replete with pop culture references, from *Star Trek*to Missy Elliot. Reading* The Edge of Space-Time*requires time and focus, and generously rewards both.**Karmela Padavic-Callaghan**

![The cover of The Beginning Comes After the End](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113480.jpg?w=840)

## ***The Beginning Comes After the End*by Rebecca Solnit**

The world desperately needs us to change our attitudes to ecology, nature, food, energy, consumption and race. As Rebecca Solnit reminds us in this slender, hopeful and galvanising book, we’ve already come a long way. I was especially pleased to see the acknowledgement that symbiosis is a vital process in ecology, and a discussion of the growing understanding that we are part of, not separate from, nature. These are key points that Solnit and I chatted about when I interviewed her for the *New Scientist*podcast. “There is no going back,” Solnit writes, “though how we go forward is the work.”**Rowan Hooper**

![The cover of On the Equality of All Things](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113880.jpg?w=840)

## ***On the Equality of All Things*by Carlo Rovelli** The theoretical physicist sets out here to deliver a theory of everything, from space-time to ethics and consciousness. Based on a series of lectures, the book is rooted in quantum physics and divided into themed chapters that progressively expand Rovelli’s frame. He writes not just about the nitty-gritty of doing physics, but the character of science itself, what it can offer and how we should adjust our philosophical views of it – and the world. It’s an impassioned book well suited to readers who may know a little bit about physics, but crave answers to the biggest questions about physical existence.**Karmela Padavic-Callaghan**

![The cover of Make Me Well](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321114150-1.jpg?w=840)

## ***Make Me Well*by Xand Van Tulleken**

Knowing how to care for ourselves feels harder than ever, thanks to the burgeoning wellness industry. Navigating the sea of supplements, wearable health monitors and at-home health tests is nearly impossible – even I don’t have the time to dig through every claim, and I am a full-time health journalist! The good news is that doctor, writer and TV presenter Xand Van Tulleken has. He takes on the wellness industry in his latest book *Make Me Well*:* One doctor’s quest to understand the confusing world of wellness.*It is a comprehensive, entertaining and informative read that will make you think twice before you buy into the latest wellness trend.**Grace Wade**

## ***The Sea-King and the Sorceress*by Neil Price**

Neil Price is unmatched at conveying the humanity, history and, frankly, weirdness of the Vikings. *The Sea-King and the Sorceress*almost doubles as a travelogue, taking us across – and, at times, well beyond – the medieval Norse world to visit the final resting places of an array of different people, not all of whom were professional raiders. Each person’s grave contains tantalising clues about the world in which they lived, including what they valued and who, in turn, cherished them, and why. Surprises and rich detail abound; it will make you think about the Viking Age in an entirely new way.**Kelsey Hayes**

![The cover of In Search of Now](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113485.jpg?w=840)

## ***In Search of Now*by Jo Marchant** We live in the moment, the “now”, yet according to physics, there is no such thing. Similarly, neuroscience tells us that “now” is just a construct, an illusion put together by the brain to help us get through the world. So why is now so central to our very existence? That is the subject of this enthralling and enlightening read by my colleague Jo Marchant. There are plenty of books on neuroscience, many on cosmology and even quite a few on quantum physics. But I don’t know of many that combine deep thinking from all these fields, or many writers who could manage it with such clarity. Yet that’s what Jo achieves in this fascinating book.**Rowan Hooper**

![The cover of Alive](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113483.jpg?w=840)

## ***Alive*by Melanie Challenger** What is life, asks Melanie Challenger. She pushes an answer: life is matter that pursues goals, that has agency. A thunderstorm may be violently active, but it isn’t trying to achieve anything. In contrast, animals seek out comfort and pleasure, they try to mate, they avoid dangerous predators. Even a single-celled bacterium makes efforts to feed itself and to maintain its own internal chemistry. This may seem obvious, but different ideas – notably that living organisms are machines for propagating their genes – have seized many biologists’ minds. Challenger seeks to reclaim life’s active nature.**Michael Marshall**

![The cover of How Flowers Made Our World](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113661.jpg?w=840)

## ***How Flowers Made Our World*by David George Haskell** and***Green Kingdom*by Ben Dark**

What a treat it is to have two great books about plants to recommend at the end of 2026. Either of them is worthy of your time. *How Flowers Made Our World* by biologist David George Haskell is a lyrical contemplation of flowering plants, the angiosperms. The book’s central theme is their staggering importance to humanity and most other land-based life. But, as a whole, it is an assemblage of loosely linked essays, as reviewer Michael Marshall noted, each exploring different aspects of flowering plant biology. I found it beautifully written.

![The cover of Green Kingdom](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113855.jpg?w=840)

*Green Kingdom*, by gardener and historian Ben Dark, is more structured, exploring key evolutionary splits and groupings in the family tree of all land plants (not just angiosperms). At the same time, he deftly weaves in an extensive yet entertaining history of western botany, from* Theophrastus* to the molecular revolution. Almost inevitably, it skews rather white and male, but I found it very absorbing and interesting anyway.

Both books cover Carl Linnaeus and Charles Darwin, both books consider the profound significance of seagrasses and orchids, and both books do justice to the marvellous plant kingdom, but they do it in very different ways. Which one you pick is up to you. **Penny Sarchet**

![The cover of Overwhelmed](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113696.jpg?w=840)

## ***Overwhelmed*by Claudia Hammond** Our lives can often feel like a series of never-ending burdens. Rather than suggesting a one-size-fits-all approach to redressing that, broadcaster and author Claudia Hammond offers an evidence-based toolkit of psychological strategies for dealing with the diverse challenges we face – from the never-ending to-do list to imposter syndrome and perfectionism. Her suggestions are refreshingly realistic, and her writing is full of humour and humility. I thought I’d read everything there was to know about stress, but I was wrong. There is plenty of new and exciting material here for anyone who would like a little more resilience.**David Robson**

![The cover of Togetherness](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113850.jpg?w=840)

## ***Togetherness*by Rowan Hooper**

This year, my friend and *New Scientist*colleague Rowan Hooper delivered a true big-ideas book in the form of* Togetherness**,*an exploration of symbiosis. I took from it that symbiosis isn’t a heart-warming and occasional freak occurrence of evolution, but instead a central organising principle of biology, one that occurs at every scale, from ecospheres to inside our cells, like zooming in on an infinitely repeating fractal. It’s an invigorating thesis that Rowan argues for and explores entertainingly, and one that has really stuck with me, not least in terms of how we account for symbiosis in our efforts to stop destroying the natural world so extensively.**Penny Sarchet**

![The cover of The AGI Chronicles](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113701-1.jpg?w=840)

## ***The AGI Chronicles*by Kevin Roose**

Whether you like it or loathe it, it is hard not to have an opinion on artificial intelligence – but with discourse this heated, wouldn’t it be better to have an informed opinion? If you don’t know your transformers from your tokens (that’s the T in ChatGPT and the unit of data processing used by AI, respectively), then *The AGI Chronicles*by Kevin Roose is an excellent way to get caught up. Roose is a bit too much of an AI booster for my tastes, but he mostly moderates it well throughout this guide to the rapid progress in AI over the past 10 years or so.**Jacob Aron**

![The cover of Beyond Inheritance](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321114033.jpg?w=840)

## ***Beyond Inheritance*by Roxanne Khamsi** You are diverging. The cells in your body have been mutating ever since you were conceived, with different cell lines accumulating different mutations and becoming less alike. Sometimes, the result is cancer. But, as Roxanne Khamsi describes here, this divergence can lead to many other consequences, such as evolutionary battles between competing cell lines that cause many conditions, or affect how we respond to drugs. This could even be the root cause of ageing, meaning trying to slow it is a battle against an unstoppable force. Everyone with an interest in health should read this book – as Khamsi writes, medicine needs to take on board the fact that each of our cells is a bit different.**Michael Le Page**

![The cover of A Brief History of the Universe](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113509.jpg?w=840)

## ***A Brief History of the Universe*by Sarah Alam Malik**

For a non-physicist like me, this was a friendly and mind-expanding guide to the discoveries through history that led to a deeper understanding of the universe, and I thoroughly recommend it. Malik, a particle physicist, nods to the late Stephen Hawking’s take on the topic, *A Brief History of Time*, in her title. I found Hawking’s explanation a little harder to digest, but fans can check out Graham Farmelo’s look at the man behind the book,* Hawking*, also published this year.**Alison Flood**

![The cover of In Defense of Sunlight](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113927-1.jpg?w=840)

## ***In Defense of Sunlight*by Rowan Jacobsen** We have been taught to avoid exposure to the sun for fear of skin cancer, but are we missing out on some surprising health benefits? In this enlightening book, Rowan Jacobsen examines the vital role that solar rays play in our biology, from lowering our blood pressure to soothing the immune system in conditions such as multiple sclerosis. It may even boost the activity of our mitochondria and enhance our metabolism. Perhaps most surprisingly, very few of these effects come from vitamin D. These are bold claims, but Jacobsen presents a compelling case and explores the complex scientific evidence with nuance.**David Robson**

![The cover of Signals](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113659.jpg?w=840)

## ***Signals*by Saira Hameed**

As someone who has routinely described herself as “hormonal”, *Signals: The inside story of our hormones*was a breath of fresh air for me. Each chapter follows one of Hameed’s patients from her endocrinology clinic at  Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust in London, using their cases to anchor us while she takes us on a journey through different hormones and endocrine systems. While I did catch myself making various self-diagnoses based on the ailments encountered in each chapter, this book genuinely changed the way I see hormones. In fact, since finishing it back in July, I haven’t once declared that I was “feeling hormonal”.**Sophie Berdugo**

![The cover of The Sleepless Ape](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113687.jpg?w=840)

## ***The Sleepless Ape*by David Samson** Why do we sleep so little? This is a question that many of us ask after a restless night, but it’s actually a crucial biological paradox. Humans sleep less than any other primate, so how did we evolve to need so little of it? In this fascinating book, evolutionary anthropologist David Samson delves into our deep past to unpick the conundrum of human sleep. In the process, he takes us on a journey that encompasses sleeping in chimpanzee nests and living with the Hadza, a hunter-gatherer society in Tanzania – and offers some intriguing tips for how to get a better night’s rest.**Alison George**

![The cover of James Webb's Ancient Cosmos](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321114160.jpg?w=840)

## ***James Webb’s Ancient Cosmos*by Maggie Aderin and Sarah Wild**

The James Webb Space Telescope launched on Christmas Day 2021, and has proved to be a tremendous Christmas gift for the picture desk here at *New Scientist*. We have eagerly awaited each and every new image beamed back to Earth. This stunning book brings together more than 200 of these otherworldly shots, cleverly curated not in chronological order of when they were captured, but according to how far back in time they allow us to look. Among them are some of the telescope’s most iconic photos, including shots of Earendel, the most distant star ever observed, and the resplendent Cosmic Cliffs in the Carina Nebula. Alongside these are lesser-seen delights, such as the sparkling galaxy NGC 1365, which almost looks like it’s winking back at you.**Tim Boddy**

Space scientist Maggie Aderin tells Karmela Padavic-Callaghan about these stunning photographs from the James Webb Space Telescope, which feature in her new book

![The cover of A World Appears](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113857-1.jpg?w=840)

## ***A World Appears*by Michael Pollan** I wasn’t especially looking forward to reading a book about consciousness, because what is there to say on a subject where we know so little? But Michael Pollan goes well beyond the “hard problem” (recognition of just how difficult it is to solve the mystery of consciousness) to write a beautiful book about the mind. Not only does he explore cutting-edge science, from fascinating evidence of plant consciousness to the few who study the unconscious, but he incorporates insights from fiction writers and older psychologists too. You may know less at the end than when you started, but the journey is thought-provoking and compelling.**Olivia Goldhill**

![The cover of End Times Facism](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113877.jpg?w=840)

## ***End Times Fascism*by Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor**“End times fascism” is the term used by Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor to describe the conglomeration of vested interests that are opposed to action on the crises of climate and nature that threaten to destabilise civilisation. Indeed, those vested interests – religious extremists, tech bro entrepreneurs and nationalist politicians – actively want to bring the crises on. Fascism, here, isn’t seen as the rise of nationalism that overwhelmed Europe in the 20th century, or isn’t just that anyway: it is the natural end point of capitalism. “Fascism is capitalism with bared teeth,” they write. Economists have failed because they don’t take ecology into account, and assume that we can just keep exploiting the planet. Klein and Taylor point to a way out – and remind us that people have always resisted and fought fascists.** Rowan Hooper**

![The cover of The Story of Birds](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113846.jpg?w=840)

## ***The Story of Birds*by Steve Brusatte**

Most of the dinosaurs have been extinct for millions of years, but one group has survived to the present day. We call them birds. Palaeontologist Steve Brusatte’s *The Story of Birds*does what it promises: it recounts how birds first evolved, how they took to the skies, how some of them survived the mass extinction 66 million years ago that took out all the other dinosaurs, and how modern birds came to be so widespread and diverse. Some of the story will be familiar to anyone with an interest in palaeontology, but Brusatte still manages to pack in a few surprises.**Michael Marshall**

![The cover of Darwin and the Queer Origins of Life](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321114038.jpg?w=840)

## ***Darwin and the Queer Origins of Life*by Ross Brooks** This is a meticulously researched history book that pulls back the curtain on how ideas about biological sex and sexual reproduction arose through the work of Charles Darwin and his contemporaries. Brooks scours Darwin’s notebooks and letters to show that the great naturalist not only knew more about the queerness of the natural world than modern readers may assume, but also struggled to marry it with the mores of his time, cutting corners in his own writing in ways that still reverberate today.**Karmela Padavic-Callaghan**

![The cover of Should I Say Something?](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321114030.jpg?w=840)

## ***Should I Say Something?*by Vanessa Bohns** Unless you are an extreme extrovert, conversation can often feel like a minefield of potential faux pas, from whether to tell someone they have something between their teeth to declaring our feelings for the loves of our lives. Social psychologist Vanessa Bohns examines various social dilemmas centred on the question of when it is and isn’t appropriate to share what we are thinking, and – if we do – how we can deliver those truth bombs without risking an explosion. I love Bohns’s writing: she comes across as the friend we’d always turn to when we need advice.**David Robson**

![The cover of Beyond Belief](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113963.jpg?w=840)

## ***Beyond Belief*by Helen Pearson** This is a book about evidence-based policy, which talks a lot about randomised trials and systematic reviews. It ought to be dry and hard-going, but in fact it’s one of the most absorbing and readable books I’ve tackled this year. Journalist Helen Pearson shows how evidence-based approaches have revolutionised medicine by giving us hard evidence about which treatments work and which don’t. Then she tackles a much gnarlier problem: can we use the same tools, like randomised controlled trials, to evaluate social policies, conservation programmes and parenting styles? This turns out to be truly difficult, because of pesky things called human beings.**Michael Marshall**

![The cover of How To Talk To AI](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113951.jpg?w=840)

## ***How to Talk to AI*by Jamie Bartlett**

In the early days of 2026, I sat down with Jamie Bartlett’s latest book, *How to Talk to AI*, without having examined my instinctive distrust of chatbots and their kin terribly deeply. Now that I have read this handy primer to human-AI relations, that distrust has persisted, but it’s grounded in a deeper understanding of what makes this new suite of technologies so appealing – and so dangerous. In his book, Bartlett dispenses practical advice on communicating with AIs and reveals how and why things can go wrong. A lot has happened since I first picked up this book: titanic mathematical problems have been toppled, escaped agents have instigated hacks, voters have been bombarded with slop and fakery. The promise and the peril of the past few months means I was all the more grateful to have read* How to Talk to AI*. It is vital that we all know how best to interact with technologies that now play a huge role in our lives, whether we like it or not.**Bethan Ackerley**

![The cover of Art Cure](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI321113956-1.jpg?w=840)

## ***Art Cure*by Daisy Fancourt** I read this back in January with the New Scientist Book Club, but if the number of people I’ve told about it is anything to go by, it remains one of my top popular science reads of the year. Daisy Fancourt is a professor of psychobiology and epidemiology, and she’s here to tell us about “how the arts transform our health”, from helping with pain relief to improving our mental health. There is tons of science and plenty of case studies, and I found it all extraordinary, inspiring and convincing. Although I’m yet to join a choir (an activity Fancourt is very much in favour of), I’ve definitely experienced more live art this year as a result of reading it, and each time it has left me feeling better about the world. Bring on communal singing in 2027.**Alison Flood**
