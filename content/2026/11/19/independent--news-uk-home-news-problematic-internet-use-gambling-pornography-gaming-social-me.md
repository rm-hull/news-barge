Problematic internet use is a “growing global public health challenge of considerable magnitude”, health experts have warned.

The experts called upon the government to take action by regulating addictive design features that keep people online, such as infinite scrolling and autoplay.

The potential for online platforms to become addictive is one of the primary concerns identified by an expert commission.

They also stated that young people are at particular risk from addictive content.

## Examples of problematic internet use

Problematic internet use includes:

* Gambling disorder
* Problematic online gaming;
* Compulsive online shopping; compulsive health searching – also known as cyberchondria;
* Compulsive sexual behaviour disorder, including problematic usage of pornography;
* Problematic social media use
* Toxic interpersonal behaviours such as cyberbullying.

Led by University of Hertfordshire academics, the experts highlighted addictive behaviours tied to social media, gaming and pornography, warning that overexposure to video streaming, online shopping and health research can also cause people to "lose control".

They also highlighted other concerns linked to problematic internet use including links between social media and mental health; underage use of pornography and “the potentially damaging effects of AI chatbots on young people’s social and emotional development”.

They said evidence in the field is “incomplete” but added: “There is enough evidence for the precautionary principle to apply.”

Writing in the *Lancet Psychiatry*, the authors said that data on prevalence rates for problematic internet use (PIU) is “inconsistent” but highlighted estimates which suggest that before the Covid-19 pandemic it was around 7% of people and afterwards it rose to 10%.

“Behaviours such as gambling, gaming, and use of pornography long predate the internet, but digital platforms have facilitated harm by increasing accessibility and impact via the use of engagement-optimising design features,” they wrote.

The authors added: “Technological features that appear benign but that facilitate online engagement, such as infinite scroll, automatic alerts, or autoplay functions, probably exacerbate risk in some individuals.”

They said that the efficacy of these features are enhanced by AI which “outpace public health responses”.

## The impact on young people

And they warned that younger people appear to be at higher risk of PIU.

Naomi Fineberg, professor of psychiatry at the University of Hertfordshire, who is leading the Lancet Psychiatry Commission on global action on problematic usage of the internet, said: “Our main concerns are around the addictive potential of interactive platforms and their effects on people and society.

“That includes not only use of social media, gaming and pornography but also many other everyday behaviours that you can lose control of if you are vulnerable or overexposed, such as video streaming, online shopping and health research.”

Prof Fineberg added: “Safety-by-design is our key message. The tech companies who understand the addictive ingredients of the platforms they use need to review their models and design safer platforms to protect all vulnerable people against internet-related harms.

“We have a younger generation who may be unable to understand the full consequences of engaging with these potentially harmful systems.

“And, because of their still-developing brains, they are particularly vulnerable to potentially addictive online material.

“The evidence is incomplete, as it often is in science. But we believe there is enough evidence for the precautionary principle to apply.

“Based on the evidence of harms that we have, it is reasonable in our view to call for governments and society to act.”
