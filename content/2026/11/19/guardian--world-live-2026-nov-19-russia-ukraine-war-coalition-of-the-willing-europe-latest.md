'God, protect us from such friends,' Poland's Tusk says after Trump's 'he's my friend' comments to Putin

Poland’s prime minister Donald Tusk – one of Europe’s five negotiators – does not seem to be impressed by what he saw in Shenzhen.

“God, protect us from such friends,” he said on X in an apparent reference to Trump’s enthusiastic greeting with Putin.

Poland’s foreign minister Radosław Sikorski was similarly alarmed, telling reporters:

“Indeed, it is difficult to judge, by their fruits we shall know them, but the optics of this hugging Putin, calling him a friend, causes us some concern.

Because the question arises – who is he more of a friend with, Putin or our president? And what will he possibly do if push comes to shove, which friend will he choose?”

France’s foreign minister Jean-Noël Barrot had little time for Trump’s summit with Xi and Putin, telling French media that he did not expect anything important to come out of that meeting.

“These three leaders fundamentally disagree on everything, so they cannot agree on anything,” Barrot told French broadcaster RTL, quoted by AP.

Putin, Xi and Trump Photograph: Doug Mills/AFP/Getty Images

“And as for this photograph that has generated so much discussion, it isn’t quite a photograph of the world’s three greatest powers, because the world’s three greatest powers are the United States, of course, China and the European Union.”

“These three countries are meeting because they have something in common: They all border the Pacific,” Barrot said. He added: “These three leaders agree on nothing, so, in a sense, nothing is going to come out of this meeting.”

For what it’s worth, the China-US-Russia ‘peace declaration’ issued after their meeting,and published by Chinese state media, includes absolutely no mention of Ukraine or Europe whatsoever.

There is only one, rather generic, line that could be seen as close to these issues:

“The three heads of state exchanged in-depth views on the current international situation and pledged to maintain strategic stability and international security.”

(Side eye at Putin.)

The document also includes a commitment to promote UN reform, increase the production and supply of refined petroleum products, as well as the leaders’ criticism of the international criminal court and declaration of intent to work on reducing nuclear risks and preventing proliferation.

Ireland’s EU minister Thomas Byrne said the main areas to be discussed on Friday “will have very significant benefits” for all parties, with the sanitary and phytosanitary agreement set to resolve “one of the biggest problems out of Brexit.”

But he was cautious talking about future relationship with the EU, saying that while he had “no doubt that the EU and Britain will want to go further on that path, we have seen how complex even dealing with the issues for tomorrow is and how long it takes.”

Sweden’s Jessica Rosencrantz stressed it is “up to the citizens of the UK to decide how close you want to work with the EU.”

But, again, asked if Britain could rejoin the bloc and keep its historical opt-outs and rebates, she dismissed it as “a highly speculative question.”

“Let’s remember how the discussion was at the time of Brexit. In that light, I think it is amazing that we now can have a big summit together, with discussions on how we can come closer together when it comes to trade and mobility,” she said.

Poland’s EU minister Ignacy Niemczycki said Warsaw’s approach “has always been very pragmatic,” as he stressed that te EU after Brexit is “weaker.”

“I would like us, wherever possible, to work out such mechanisms so it works for both of us. … This is a country that thinks similarly to us in Europe on many issues and there is no reason for us to treat each other like enemies. We are friends and let’s find the right formula for that.

In a rather cheeky comment, he added:

“I would encourage everyone to think seriously about the benefits of being in the EU, … and I think the people of the UK see it [too], now that they are outside the union.”

Meanwhile, foreign ministers arriving for the EU foreign affairs council meeting in Brussels are heroically trying to avoid saying anything remotely interesting or controversial in response to reporters’ questions on Trump’s meetings with Xi and Putin.

Clearly, they would prefer to leave the talking to their bosses who are expected in Berlin later today.

And so, Spanish EU minister Fernando Sampedro merely said that Spain and the EU remained “strongly committed” to supporting Ukraine.

Asked specifically about his views on Trump’s comments on “his friend” Putin, he only sheepishly smiled and once again repeated his previous lines.

Sweden’s EU minister Jessica Rosencrantz said that “we don’t really know the details of this meeting, neither the agenda nor the specific issues" the leaders were planning to discuss.

“But the general principle is there should be no talks about European security without Europe;there should be no talks about Ukraine without Ukraine. That is our general attitude when it comes to issues about European security or Ukrainian security,” she said.

(For what it’s worth, her boss, Sweden’s foreign minister Maria Malmer Stenergard, was a bit more outspoken when she said early this morning that it was “worrying if we have great powers who think they should decide how the world should be governed.”)

Sweden's EU minister Jessica Rosencrantz arrives for a FAC meeting in Brussels, Belgium. Photograph: Olivier Hoslet/EPA

Ireland’s EU minister Thomas Byrne struck a similar note to his Swedish counterpart, saying “nothing will be decided about Europe without Europe.”

“The only way peace can work is if Russia decides to stop its invasion.”

Meanwhile, Poland’s Europe minister Ignacy Niemczycki dismissed any comparisons between the Shenzhen meeting to the Yalta conference in 1945, saying “Europe is still very strong,” and so “the situations is very different than historically.”

“Even if some decisions were to be made, their implementation would simply be impossible without Europe’s participation,” he said.

Danish parliament votes to ratify Greenland security deal with US

Meanwhile, Denmark’s parliament unanimously ratified a security agreement with US president Donald Trump covering Greenland, hoping to end a standoff over ⁠the Arctic island’s ⁠future that ​has rattled transatlantic relations, Reuters reported.

The US, Denmark and Greenland signed the agreement in September to resolve tensions after Trump repeatedly threatened to ⁠take control of the mineral-rich island, a self-governing Danish territory that is home to 57,000 people.

Crimea bridge has been attacked again, says Russian-installed governor

Meanwhile, a key bridge connecting annexed Crimea to Russia has been attacked again last night, a Russian-appointed regional governor has said, two days after Ukrainian strikes caused serious damage to it.

A drone view of a damaged Crimea Bridge in the Kerch Strait, Crimea. Photograph: Ukraine’s President Volodymyr Zelenskiy/Telegram/Reuters

“Following a new attack on the Crimean Bridge, traffic has been temporarily halted. All relevant services are on site and at work. An assessment of the damage is under way,” Sergei Aksyonov, the Kremlin-appointed governor of the Russian-occupied Crimea, wrote on Telegram.

The Russian transport ministry said on Thursday that no one was wounded in the attack and that train services across it had been halted.

Wednesday’s ‌attack is third this week on the highly symbolic bridge that links Russia with Crimea, which ​it seized from Ukraine in 2014. A 227-metre section of the road spanning the Kerch strait was destroyed this week, but the rail corridor appears to be operable despite sustaining some damage.

'We are going to be discussing the war between Russia and Ukraine,' Trump says

On Ukraine, Trump says at the beginning of the trilateral meeting:

“We are going to be discussing the war between Russia and Ukraine, and we already have. President Putin and I have had a long discussion today for quite a while. That’s what pretty much what the discussion was about.

And I think things are happening.

I think that he and – I hope - the other side would like to see an end to that war.”

God knows what that’s supposed to mean in practice.

Morning opening: Ukraine's allies meet in Berlin, anxious to hear about Trump's meeting with Putin, Xi

Jakub Krupa

Several European leaders from the Coalition of the Willing are meeting in Berlin today, just hours after US president Donald Trump, Russia president Vladimir Putin and China president Xi Jinping are due to meet in Shenhzen.

The trilateral meeting is due to get under way any moment now, though the tone of Trump’s earlier talks with Putin will already be worrying for Europeans.

Loop: Trump and Putin shake hands

“Who is he? He’s my friend!” said Trump, absolutely beaming, as soon as he saw Putin.

These images will send a shiver down the spine of Europe’s top leaders as they prepare to leave for Berlin to attend the “coalition of the willing”’s latest coordination meeting.

Police officers provide security ahead the summit of the so-called Coalition of the Willing group of pro-Ukrainian countries in Berlin. Photograph: Fabrizio Bensch/Reuters

Europe’s newly appointed negotiating team of Andy Burnham, Emmanuel Macron, Giorgia Meloni, Friedrich Merz and Donald Tusk plus the EU’s Ursula von der Leyen and António Costa will be joined by several other leaders for a meeting with US peace envoys, Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, where they are expected to get a read out from the meeting in China.

Ukraine’s Volodymyr Zelenskyy and Nato’s secretary general Mark Rutte are expected to attend, as are several other key European leaders, including Finland’s Alexander Stubb, Denmark’s Mette Frederiksen, Sweden’s Ulf Kristersson, Norway’s Jonas Gahr Støre, and Ireland’s Micheál Martin (and many others).
