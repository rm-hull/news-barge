The birth of a rare greater one-horned rhinoceros at a UK zoo has been revealed in newly-released footage.

CCTV cameras show 19-year-old mother Asha delivering her calf inside their enclosure at Chester Zoo a few days ago.

It further records the newborn taking its initial tentative steps within an hour of being born.

Asha’s pregnancy lasted 15 months, though the sex of the youngster is yet to be disclosed.

![The video shows 19-year-old mother Asha giving birth to the calf in their enclosure at Chester Zoo a few days ago](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/15/01M7PFV9KK8W38HNW769N678G8.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Zookeepers confirmed that Asha and her newborn are in good health after staying indoors together during their first few days to keep warm.

The as-yet-unnamed calf, born on November 11, is the fourth born to Asha and father Beni, who died aged 22 earlier this year after a short and sudden illness.

“It’s always exciting to welcome a new calf and Asha has been a brilliant mum to all her youngsters,” said Tom Garland, assistant manager of the rhino team at Chester Zoo.

“Every birth is important for the future of this species and it’s wonderful to see another healthy arrival.”

Mr Garland added: “Beni was a lovely rhino. He was calm and approachable, helped train animal care specialists and even took part in animal behaviour studies.

“It’s lovely to know that his legacy lives on through this calf.”

![The as-yet-unnamed calf, born on November 11, is the fourth born to Asha and father Beni, who died aged 22 earlier this year](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/15/01M7PFTYBGRWKEWV5XWAWHRWV6.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The calf and its mother are continuing to bond and have begun to explore a secluded paddock before they move into the main paddock at a later date.

Greater one-horned rhinoceros are native to India and Nepal and are classed as vulnerable to extinction.

The species was driven to near-extinction in the early 1900s by habitat loss and trophy hunting, as well as poaching for their horns.

There are now around 4,000 in the wild.

The new calf is part of a conservation breeding programme across Europe that helps to maintain a healthy and genetically diverse population of the species in human care, Chester Zoo said.

Emma Evison, team manager for rhinos, said: “Asha was pregnant for about 15 months, so it’s a long wait.

![The new calf is part of a conservation breeding programme across Europe that helps to maintain a healthy and genetically diverse population of the species in human care](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/15/01M7PFV17MG4QWZRXJRQHPSGJV.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“We had our fingers crossed when she showed signs of labour and we knew we were likely to welcome the calf very soon.

“It was a big moment for us – and for the conservation breeding programme – when the calf arrived.

“Any birth can be risky, even for an experienced mum, but fortunately everything went smoothly. The calf was up and about within an hour and feeding shortly afterwards. Asha knows exactly what she’s doing.”

Greater one-horned rhino calves weigh roughly 45kg at birth, about the size of a big adult dog, but the largest adults can weigh more than two tonnes – as much as a car.

Earlier this year, a British wildlife park celebrated the birth of another rare rhino whose species was classified as “near threatened”.

A male southern white rhino calf was born at Knowsley Safari in Merseyside and represents a valuable contribution to international efforts to protect the future of the animals.

The southern white rhino’s cousin, the northern white rhino, is now functionally extinct, with only two females remaining globally.
