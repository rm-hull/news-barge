Terrorists could be granted early release under government efforts to reduce overcrowding behind bars.

More than 200 such criminals are understood to be included in the scheme. It is understood that participants on a Metropolitan Police counter terrorism training course were told 267 offenders could be let out of jail early.

However, the Ministry of Justice (MoJ) insisted the "vast majority of terrorism offences are not eligible for release changes", adding: "We do not recognise this number".

Government officials believe the number eligible for early release is smaller than suggested, though they have not yet provided an estimated figure of how many could be in scope.

The MoJ previously stated terrorists would not qualify for early release. However, terrorism offences were not specifically excluded under the Sentencing Act, meaning some could be eligible.

These are likely to be offenders jailed for two years or under for lower level crimes under terrorism laws.

Terrorists can be handed extended sentences requiring them to serve two-thirds of their punishment in custody and face the Parole Board before walking free, which the government maintains would block them from being eligible for early release.

![More than 200 criminals currently being held for terrorism offences are said to have been included in the early release scheme](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/15/2299459216..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Record numbers of terrorists are behind bars in Britain, according to the latest figures.

As of the end of June, there were 275 criminals in prison for terrorism, the highest number since records began in 2010 (126).

The number is up 7 per cent year-on-year (from 257) and has surpassed the peak recorded at the end of March (273).

The majority, 57 per cent (157), held Islamist-extremist views and 29 per cent (80) had extreme right-wing beliefs, according to Home Office data.

Some 38 (14 per cent) were classed as holding “other” ideologies – a category which includes left-wing extremist, dissident Irish extremist, domestic extremist and terrorists where a specific ideology has not been identified or their views are not known.

No breakdown of how many terrorists fall into each category is provided.

But MoJ data obtained under freedom of information laws by PA revealed the breakdown of the 34 terrorists behind bars as of March who were in this category. Of these, four were classed as “far-left or anarchist”, eight held multiple ideologies, 10 had “no ideology” and 12 were designated as “other”.

Thousands of prisoners are being freed from jails across England and Wales as part of government measures which came into force last month.

![Terrorists can be handed extended sentences requiring them to serve two-thirds of their punishment in custody and face the Parole Board before walking free, which the government maintains would block them from being eligible for early release](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/29/13/2299966337..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The changes mean criminals serving fixed-term sentences will serve a third of their time behind bars before being released, rather than half.

The government says it has been forced to act to free up space in overcrowded jails and stop the justice system from collapsing.

Rapists, prisoners guilty of manslaughter and some child sex offenders are excluded from the policy, and it does not apply to criminals such as murderers serving life sentences.

A fourth wave of about 550 prisoners – who are serving sentences of more than 1,162 days and less than 1,562 days (roughly four years and three months) – are due to be freed early on 8 December.

The MoJ said: “We do not recognise this number. Within weeks of coming into office, the Prime Minister and Justice Secretary took action to keep more of the most serious criminals locked up, and the vast majority of terrorism offences are not eligible for release changes.

“We work closely with police and the Security Service to manage terrorist offenders’ risk to the public when they are eventually released from prison, including through stringent licence conditions.

“They face tagging and restrictions on their internet use, movements and contact with others. If they break those conditions they can be brought back to prison.”

The Metropolitan Police has been contacted for comment.
