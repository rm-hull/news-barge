## New EU guidelines urge citizens to manage the first 72 hours of an emergency independently. Commissioner Hadja Lahbib said it “has become an internationally recognised benchmark for household emergency preparedness.” Yet a University of Latvia survey found that only 8% feel prepared.

Food, water, and cash. Grab-and-go bags stocked with documents and medicines. Evacuation plans and practical skills, such as cooking without electricity and first aid.

This is how the European Commission’s new crisis guidelines advise citizens to be self-sufficient during the first 72 hours when cyber or drone attacks sabotage critical infrastructure and natural disasters disrupt transport and communication networks.

But few people are ready for a crisis breaking out today. The University of Latvia’s 2026 Baltic Individual Crisis Preparedness Barometer, which surveyed residents in 10 European cities across 8 Northern and Eastern EU countries (plus Iceland and Norway), found that just 8% feel quite or completely prepared. 44% plan to improve their preparedness within six months.

### Preparedness today

Self-sufficiency during the first 72 hours of a crisis is crucial, as “it reduces the pressure on emergency services and allows them to focus on those most in need,” Hadja Lahbib, EU Commissioner for Equality, Preparedness and Crisis Management, told Euronews.

Yet the Baltic Individual Crisis Preparedness Barometer shows that at least half of residents in every surveyed city feel unprepared today. Dr. Ieva Birka, the Barometer's lead researcher, is not surprised. What surprises her is that nothing is changing.

“Preparedness has never been talked about more: there are national brochures, EU guidance and constant media coverage. Yet households’ actual material preparedness is not moving”, Birka explained.

Across the cities surveyed, only 31% have enough drinking water at home and 25% at work to last 72 hours or more. 50% think they have enough cash, and 71% believe they have enough food. While 73% have first-aid supplies, only 38% have a battery-powered AM/FM radio.

Differences between cities largely reflect how close people feel an attack is, Birka said. Vilnius is the only capital where the unprepared are a minority, as 67% consider an attack a major threat. But “feeling closer to the threat doesn’t mean being stocked up. Even in Vilnius, only about a quarter of respondents had three days’ worth of water,” she added.

### Many people can’t afford

“Being prepared does not have to be expensive”, said Lahbib, suggesting that simple steps, including strong social networks and essentials such as food, water and a torch, can be real game-changers.

But many people can’t afford to be prepared. A family of four needs 36 litres of water for three days, non-perishable food, a battery-powered radio, first-aid supplies, medicines and cash. These essentials cost about €100–150 in Baltic capitals, while a fully stocked crisis kit with sleeping bags and tents can exceed €300, Birka explained. If heating fails, “unless you have a fireplace, the alternatives are generators or portable power stations, which cost hundreds of euros more”, she added.

About 30% of respondents say they need financial help to prepare. “Support should be targeted rather than universal,” Birka said, suggesting vouchers or ready-made kits for low-income households, distributed through municipalities and social services.

Asked whether the EU could fund preparedness, Lahbib said the Commission has launched “a behavioural study to better understand preparedness levels among low-income groups”. The results are expected next year.

### When your preparedness depends on others

Money is not the only constraint. A 2026 European Disability Forum report, citing UN data, finds that 91% of people with disabilities in Europe lack a personal disaster plan, and 43% can’t evacuate without help. Accessible early warning systems cut the number to 22%, yet just 10% receive risk information in accessible formats like braille or sign language.

While the survey doesn’t include data specifically on people with disabilities or those who rely on assistive devices, Birka thinks “it's important to remember that for these groups, self-sufficiency depends on other people, so carers need to be prepared themselves.”

The Commission urges inclusive, accessible evacuations, transport, and shelters that meet diverse needs, with people with disabilities involved in planning. First-aid responders should be trained to communicate effectively, identify needs, and provide targeted support.

Birka wants these measures included in national and municipal emergency plans, not as "an optional extra”. She advises on personal emergency plans and support networks, voluntary registers of people who need assistance, backup power for medical and assistive devices, and accessible evacuation.

### Preparedness is also psychological

For others, the issue is psychological. In Vilnius, where people feel the threat most heavily, 42% say the main obstacle is the psychological difficulty of thinking about it.

“More leaflets won’t close the gap,” Birka said, stressing that simply giving citizens more information about preparedness won't solve the problem. “People want more direct reassurance from governments that there is a plan, that crises will be handled, and that if individuals do their share in preparing, the country, civil protection and emergency services, and the army will do theirs”, she explained.

Practical help is what makes the difference. For Birka, this includes concrete action plans and raising awareness in their own neighbourhoods. Formats should suit the audience, from short videos for younger generations to printed materials for older audiences.

### Same guidelines, different levels of preparedness

The guidelines are non-binding. “Member States have been involved throughout,” Lahbib said, but “the EU cannot guarantee that citizens are ‘equally prepared’. That is the responsibility of each Member State.”

This could leave Europeans unevenly prepared and widen the existing gap between member states. Birka argued that consistently tracking progress across countries and over time can show whether national governments are taking the EU’s advice seriously.

The lead researcher urged the EU to adopt shared indicators and conduct regular, comparable surveys with clear, measurable targets, for example, tracking how many households have enough water for three days. “Shared standards for measuring and reporting would make progress visible and create healthy pressure to act”, said Birka.
