A powerful explosion rocked Saudi Arabia's capital Riyadh early on Thursday, with residents reporting smoke near the city's international airport as the Saudi-led military coalition said it had dealt with a missile threat launched by Yemen's Houthi rebels.

The blast was heard across parts of the capital, with one resident in northern Riyadh describing it as "extremely loud", according to Agence France-Presse (AFP).

An AFP journalist reported that the force of the explosion caused doors inside her home to swing open and briefly knocked out the lights.

The precise location of the blast was not immediately known. However, residents told AFP they had seen smoke rising near King Khalid International Airport, in the north-east of the city.

One resident told the news agency: "Hearing a blast without receiving a warning is very frightening."

The Saudi-led coalition subsequently confirmed that it had dealt with a missile threat launched by the Houthis towards Riyadh.

"Initial assessment: debris fell and material damage is being assessed," coalition spokesperson Major General Turki Al-Maliki said in a statement posted on X, according to Arab News.

The coalition added that the extent of the damage was still being assessed. There was no immediate information about casualties.

Saudi Arabia's Civil Defence authority had earlier issued a warning about potential danger in the capital before subsequently informing residents that the threat had passed, Reuters reported.

The explosion came amid a renewed escalation in hostilities between Saudi Arabia and Yemen's Iran-aligned Houthi movement, which has stepped up missile and drone attacks against the kingdom's cities and critical infrastructure.

The latest incident follows weeks of escalating attacks by the Houthis, who have increasingly targeted Saudi cities, airports, ports and energy infrastructure.

The group has intensified its operations as the wider conflict involving Iran, the US and Israel has spread across the region. Saudi Arabia, a longstanding US security partner, has responded with air strikes against Houthi targets in Yemen.

Riyadh has been repeatedly targeted in recent months. In October, a Houthi missile attack on King Khalid International Airport killed at least 12 people and injured more than 300, according to Saudi authorities. The death toll subsequently rose following the death of an injured Indian national.

![Mourners carry coffins during the funeral of people killed in recent airstrikes on al-Shariya village, northeast of Sanaa](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/03/download.-3..jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

A further projectile struck the airport's passenger terminal on 11 October, killing one person and injuring 11 others, according to Saudi authorities.

The Houthis have also targeted Saudi oil infrastructure. In September, a large plume of black smoke was seen rising near King Khalid International Airport after an attack on a fuel storage facility. The group claimed responsibility for missile and drone operations targeting what it described as sensitive sites in Riyadh.

The latest escalation has also raised concerns over regional shipping routes, particularly the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, a strategically important passage connecting the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden.

The Houthis have made territorial advances along Yemen's Red Sea coast, moving towards the strait, which is an important route for international trade and energy shipments.

Saudi Arabia was preparing for a renewed military campaign against the Houthis that could last up to six months and extend into 2027, reported Reuters, citing officials briefed on the kingdom's plans. Riyadh wanted its Yemeni allies to make battlefield gains before negotiations, according to the report.

The latest explosion was reported shortly after Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman met US Central Command chief Admiral Brad Cooper in Riyadh.

According to the Saudi Press Agency, the two discussed military relations and developments in the region. Mr Cooper also met Saudi defence minister Prince Khalid bin Salman to discuss defence cooperation and regional security.

The renewed fighting has effectively ended a United Nations-brokered truce established in 2022, which had largely halted attacks between the Saudi-led coalition and the Houthis.
