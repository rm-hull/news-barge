**Warning: This story discusses sexual violence and abuse**

When Joanne Young awoke tied to her bed, she had no idea about the horror that had been unfolding while she was unconscious.

After hearing her then-husband Philip Young calling for someone else to "get down", she quickly realised another person was in the bedroom with them.

Unbeknown to Joanne, Young had advertised online for other men to abuse her. He then drugged her and invited a series of strangers into their bedroom to sexually assault her.

This had been going on for 13 years.

On Thursday, four men were convicted of a number of offences including raping and assaulting her. Her ex-husband had already pleaded guilty to a number of rape charges.

Young, a former Conservative councillor, and another man, Dean Hamilton, pleaded guilty to rape and sexual assault at a previous hearing.

The group came from a variety of backgrounds. One of the men worked on Ministry of Defence nuclear defence projects while another drove Red Cross trucks to Romania in the 1980s.

Speaking outside court after the verdicts earlier, Joanne said "silence is not an option if we are serious about preventing abuse" - you can read her statement in full here.

Her case has striking comparisons with the repeated rape of Gisèle Pelicot in France, with experts saying the crime was "deeply abusive".

Online, Young portrayed the abuse as a shared sexual kink known as "cuckolding". The photographs used in the adverts posted on sites like Craigslist and Grindr showed Joanne inanimate and tied to the bed.

James Foster, a specialist prosecutor with the Crown Prosecution Service (CPS), said Young "would drug her so that he could then sexually abuse her and record that sexual abuse and also arrange for other men to attend the address to sexually abuse her".

One of the men who answered the advert was Dean Hamilton, 46, who admitted to raping Joanne and abusing her in 2015.

The other men who were convicted of sexually assaulting Joanne were Connor Sanderson-Doyle, 32, Richard Wilkins, 62, Norman Macksoni, 48 and Mohammed Hassan, 38 - the man Young told to "get down" when Joanne woke.

They told the court they believed they had the consent of both Joanne and Young and were told Joanne was pretending to be asleep - when in fact she was heavily sedated.

"But not once during any of this abuse did they ask Joanne Young what she wanted or show any interest in what her wishes were," Foster said.

In a statement read out in court, one of the co-offenders called Young a monster

The law states the sexual consent can only be given if the person involved "agrees by choice ... and has the freedom and capacity to make that choice".

Investigators are told to establish "what steps, if any, the suspect took to obtain the complainant's consent".

Professor Marianne Hester, emeritus professor at the University of Bristol, said Joanne "clearly" had no capacity to say no, adding a "women cannot consent if they are asleep".

"So it's the same if they are completely drugged. They cannot make the decision to agree to sex," she added.

Hester, who has researched gender-based violence for years, said those cases raise broader questions about attitudes towards women and sex.

"We have to question the men, the way men think it's okay to do sex and how they keep normalising, we keep normalising all these very abusive acts within sex and that's really problematic," she said.

Hester added: "We know that investigations have now started on certain websites and that individuals have been identified in a number of different countries who have been up to exactly the same kind of thing.

"So I think that there are potentially quite a lot more cases in the pipeline."

The case has also reignited questions about the role of websites used to facilitate abuse.

Lisa Sugiura, Professor of Cyber Crime and Gender at the University of Portsmouth, argued online platforms have a responsibility to address content that normalises violence against women.

"Often these sites are accompanied by image-based abuse and deeply violent language and rhetoric about women anyway," she said.

"You think, how are these forums allowed to act with impunity without oversight and accountability?

"Before we even get to the question of whether we should simply accept the word of a man claiming to speak on behalf of himself and his wife."

Prof Hester said this case could be "a lot more cases in the pipeline"

Data shows hundreds of similar potential crimes are currently being investigated across the UK.

The National Crime Agency said there was an "urgent need" to deal with the problem as an international issue.

NCA investigators have identified more than 270 individuals linked to one online forum and successor groups.

The CPS describes this type of offending as an emerging challenge for investigators.

"This particular type of offending, where a husband is drugging and abusing his wife and arranging for other men to abuse her, does seem to be a new type of offending," said Foster.

A spokesman for Wiltshire Police explained the extent of drug-rape offences was unknown because victims might not know they had been targeted.

He said: "While cases involving organised drug-facilitated sexual assault remain relatively rare compared to other forms of sexual offending, intelligence and other recent cases would suggest this may be a significantly under-reported crime type.

"Victims are often unaware they have been drugged, may have fragmented memories of events, or may initially attribute symptoms to alcohol consumption. These factors make detection and reporting particularly challenging."

Sugiura disagrees the offending itself is new.

She believes technology has made it easier to facilitate and conceal abuse that may have existed for years.

"The use of technology enables anonymity, it enables that division of identity, the protection of the respectable face offline, and these heinous deeds online," she said, adding offenders often operate within wider online communities where misogynistic attitudes are encouraged and abusive material is shared.

"These are not always isolated incidents," she said.

"They can be part of larger networks where people are swapping tips, encouraging one another and sharing material that is deeply abusive towards women."

"I think that there are potentially quite a lot more cases in the pipeline," she added.
