When we look at the defining moments of prime ministers over the last decade, most of them are pretty bleak.

For Boris Johnson, it was lockdown rule-breaking in Downing Street with Partygate; for Liz Truss it was the ill-fated mini-budget. For Rishi Sunak, it was skipping the D-Day commemorations early in a catastrophic general election campaign and, for Keir Starmer, it was the Lord Peter Mandelson scandal.

Not just career-ending – but reputation ending, too – for political careers which at one point seemed glittering.

So, if we ask whether the UK-EU summit on Friday will be Andy Burnham’s defining Brexit moment, we cannot ignore the disasters of the last decade.

![Prime minister Andy Burnham with European Commission president Ursula von der Leyen (Stefan Rousseau/PA)](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/17/f690ad599165109537114109e4778e54Y29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzk1MDIwOTM2-2.86468843.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The simple answer is that it could be – but it should not be. And, if it is, then it will be a crushing disappointment.

Mr Burnham will unveil a deal which ends food and drink border checks, will bring the UK into the EU energy market and, hopefully, resolve the youth mobility scheme fiasco, although there is still a wait and see on that last one.

If it is all delivered, the deal will go some way in undoing some of the harms of Brexit and improve economic growth.

But it will be a long way from resolving the catastrophe which was leaving the European Union.

In a world where Donald Trump sharing a platform with President Xi Jinping of China and Vladimir Putin has underlined the importance of massive power blocks, the absence of Europe from the table was noticeable.

The departure of the UK from the EU was part of the degradation of European nations being seen as a voice worth listening to – and it is no coincidence that Trump allied himself with Brexiteers like Nigel Farage and Putin’s social media fake news bots who pushed support for Brexit.

What Mr Burnham and European Commission president Ursula von der Leyen are about to unveil is, essentially, tinkering – defined by the restrictive red lines of Keir Starmer’s Labour election manifesto.

The big decisions on the customs union, single market, free movement or rejoin completely option will not be contemplated, as the world hurtles into a greater economic crisis and war.

So if this is Mr Burnham’s defining moment, it will be a failure. It will mean he has failed to persuade voters in either a later general election or referendum to go for something far more radical.

We all know what Mr Burnham wants to be as his defining Brexit moment. That will be him being the prime minister who restored the British gold star on the EU flag and takes his seat as the PM of a member nation of the EU.

His desire to rejoin the EU may have slightly faltered during his attempts to win the Makerfield by-election in a Leave-voting seat. But it has always been his goal.

As Mr Burnham has put it, Brexit is responsible for “a decade of division and low economic growth”. He wants to be the prime minister who ended that.

There is a long way to go and many political challenges to be overcome before we know whether Friday will, or will not, be Mr Burnham’s biggest Brexit moment. But for the good of the country, and Europe as a whole, we should all hope that it is not.

In the end, what Mr Burnham wants is for his place in history not to be someone who merely tinkered with a historic mistake.
