Plans by the U.S. Army to detonate roughly 5 tons of unstable bomb-making chemicals that pose an explosion risk if moved have raised fears among southeast Iowa residents over potential lead dust contamination and health risks.

Scheduled for Friday, the blast at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant has prompted officials to recommend that nearby Middletown residents evacuate that morning. Those unable to leave are advised to remain indoors with windows and doors securely closed.

"The detonation will result in an air plume with some lead-containing particles which will disperse in the air and deposit on the ground," an advisory states. "Lead is toxic to humans and animals. The goal of this advisory is to provide the public with actionable information out of an abundance of caution to minimize lead exposure."

Maps released by officials show lead dust potentially drifting northward. The Army will update these projections as weather patterns shift, and several school districts situated along the expected path of the plume have already canceled classes for Friday.

Middletown has a population of nearly 400, though thousands more residents fall within the trajectory of the plume.

Vibrations from the two detonations will be brief but could shatter older windows in town. Officials said the resulting airborne dust cloud is expected to linger for no longer than an hour, contingent on wind speed.

Though the Army advises washing cars, windowsills, and outdoor surfaces, residents have turned to social media to express anxiety and question if those measures will prove sufficient.

## Surrounding communities get late notice

Residents of Middletown first learned in March that dozens of lead azide barrels required disposal, after a temporary pause in operations at the plant for safety review revealed the degraded material.

"The safety of our people and our neighbors is non‑negotiable," Lt. Col. Daniel Nosse, commander of the plant, said at the time, acknowledging the volatility of the chemical compound used in munitions production.

Additional details were presented during an October public meeting in Middletown, warning of an unscheduled November blast. On Nov. 13, the Environmental Protection Agency granted an emergency permit, clearing the path for Friday's operation.

The permit required the military to calculate potential air and dust dispersion rates and give five days' advance warning, leading to Sunday's advisory issued to communities up to 100 miles (160 kilometers) away.

A few miles northwest, Danville Community School District will be closed Friday, its superintendent announced Tuesday. Pat Wallace said he initially planned to stay open, but lingering uncertainties made cancellation "the most responsible decision."

"I am not suggesting that the detonation will result in unsafe conditions," he wrote. "The reality is that we simply do not know exactly what conditions will look like Friday afternoon."

In Mediapolis, under 20 miles (32 kilometers) northeast, municipal officials were approached late last month regarding soil, water, and air testing. However, they only learned of their risk on Sunday when an initial map placed them directly in the dust path. Local schools and the public library are closing Friday, while the fire department plans to spray down playgrounds that might collect lead particles.

An updated map later showed the town of nearly 2,000 residents outside the plume's trajectory.

"This is probably a first for me. I believe it’s a first for a lot of our citizens," Deputy City Clerk Amy Stucker said Wednesday. "The biggest issue we’re having is the lack of information."

The office of Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds was first informed of the advisory on Monday, her staff stated.

Word of the upcoming explosions also prompted environmental and public health officials in Illinois to urge the U.S. military to conduct thorough environmental testing in their state following the blasts.

## Long-term storage leaves lead azide vulnerable

Stored at the facility for over 20 years inside earth-covered containers, the lead azide barrels sat immersed in ethanol and water to remain stable, according to Justine Barati, public affairs specialist at the Army Sustainment Command.

A routine inspection earlier this year revealed that partial water evaporation caused the lead azide to crystallize. This increased its sensitivity to shock and friction, "creating a highly unstable and dangerous situation," according to an emergency permit approved last week by the EPA.

Relocating the material posed an unacceptable hazard of accidental explosion, officials noted, a risk that Barati said rises during cold weather.

"We’re really concerned about the weather situation," Barati said on moving forward with Friday’s detonation. "We’re really concerned that it will detonate on its own."

The military assembled a panel of specialists in munitions chemistry, engineering, and environmental safety to formulate the detonation strategy.

The barrels are encased in concrete on all sides beneath soil, surrounded by sand barriers stacked two stories high.

During the blast, soil will be saturated with water and the air misted to help retain most of the lead on Army land, Barati said.

Patrick O’Shaughnessy, a professor at the University of Iowa’s Department of Occupational and Environmental Health, said while efforts by the Army and EPA to curb dust are encouraging, it remains uncertain how much lead will enter the atmosphere and land on surfaces.

As a neurological toxicant, lead can impair brain development and function, particularly in young children, O’Shaughnessy noted, highlighting risks for toddlers who frequently place objects in their mouths.

EPA documents show that chemical neutralization and decomposition were evaluated, but officials concluded that any plan requiring physical handling of the drums would heighten the chance of an accidental, fatal explosion.
