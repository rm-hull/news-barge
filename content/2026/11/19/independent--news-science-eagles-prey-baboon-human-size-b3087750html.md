Scientists have discovered that large baboons in the Sahara are being preyed upon by eagles in the wild, a puzzling finding that could reshape our understanding of the threats faced by early human ancestors in Africa.

Baboons in the Ennedi plateau of Northern Chad live extraordinary isolated lives in the middle of the Sahara.

The extensive sandstone-dominated landform extends across a 250km arc to the border of Sudan, with dry canyons, water-filled pools, and elevations reaching up to 1450m.

This landscape is home to olive baboons (*Papio anubis*) and two species of eagle, the golden eagle (* Aquila chrysaetos*) and Verreaux's eagle (* A. verreauxii*).

“Baboons are some of the best studied monkeys in Africa, but our very first observations from Ennedi upended all sorts of notions,” said Catherine Hobaiter, an author of the study published in the *American Journal of Biological Anthropology.*

“Some of the baboons there are so remote they rarely, if ever, see people, and so after a couple of days they ended up following us around – we were probably the most interesting thing they’d seen for a while!” Dr Hobaiter said.

Here, analysing skeletal remains of prey in the eagles’ nests, scientists found something peculiar.

![Speculative face-­ off between an adult male baboon and Verreaux's eagle](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/9/30/Speculative-face-off-between-an-adult-male-baboon-and-Verreauxs-eagle.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Researchers found primate skulls in the nests bearing talon punctures and other signature marks and scrapes of eagle predation.

While eagles have hunted primates for millions of years, humans and ancient human ancestors were thought to have escaped these predators for the past 2.5 million years.

So far, no primate over 13kg has been confirmed to be killed by eagles.

So scientists have held that our body sizes could just be too heavy for even the giant eagles of the past to lift.

But in the eagle nests of the Ennedi plateau, researchers could calculate that some of the baboon prey likely weighed as much as a human child.

![Young baboons in the cliffs of Ennedi](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/9/35/Young-baboons-in-the-cliffs-of-Ennedi.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

One individual baboon likely weighed over 26kg, well within the range of prehistoric human species.

“We saw the eagles while we were searching for the baboons, but we never imagined that they might be a threat to them. Then we found the first skull with a puncture mark, and then another, and another,” Dr Hobaiter said.

The eagles seemed relaxed around other big birds of prey, but as soon as a Verreaux's eagle came into view, they alarm called and dropped down to safety.

“One of the most exciting observations was seeing the baboons respond when a pair of Verreaux’s eagles swept overhead – primates are very specific about only responding to eagles that are a genuine threat,” she said.

![A) Eagle baboon encounter B) Bone markings associated with eagle predation](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/9/33/Low-Res_4-EaglePredation_Fig5.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The fact that human ancestors survived large eagles of their time suggests that they may have also had defence strategies quite different to those for land predators.

Typically, modern primates respond to aerial predators by “mobbing”, in which a large adult would charge at the attacker to drive them off.

For this strategy to have worked, even ancient human ancestors may have also moved in large, often mixed groups, which likely also included other primate species, researchers say.

“I had never imagined that these predators would target primates of this size until the research team stumbled upon evidence. This discovery leads us to explore further the mystery that surrounds the gueltas of the Sahara Desert,” said Didier Akpona, another author of the study.
