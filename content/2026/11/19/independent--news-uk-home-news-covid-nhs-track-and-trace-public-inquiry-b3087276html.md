Campaigners have branded the UK’s Covid-19 test and trace mechanism an "abject failure", ahead of a major new report into pandemic response measures.

Baroness Heather Hallett, chairwoman of the UK Covid-19 Public Inquiry, is set to release her latest findings on Thursday, evaluating the strategies deployed to curb the spread of the virus.

Ahead of the report’s publication, the Covid-19 Bereaved Families for Justice UK group stated: "The UK’s test and trace system was an abject failure that cost lives.

"Thursday’s report must be honest about those failures and give bereaved families the truth about why their loved ones were left unprotected.

"It must hold those responsible to account and set out what needs to change so we are prepared for the next crisis."

When the virus first emerged in January 2020, health officials insisted the danger to the UK public was "very low", adding later that month that "UK public health measures are world-leading and our excellent NHS is well prepared to manage and treat new diseases".

![A member of NHS Track and Trace gives the thumbs up to a driver taking a Covid-19 test](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/18/15/01M7PFTRF6GMJGSAS25JMWR0W7.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

However, as international concern grew, the World Health Organisation (WHO) designated the crisis a "public health emergency of international concern", prompting UK authorities to upgrade the national risk assessment from low to moderate.

In response, the Government, Public Health England (PHE) and the NHS released guidance, expanded laboratory testing capacity, and outlined plans for a national surveillance system to "prepare for and prevent wider transmission of the virus".

By March 2020, as the threat level was raised from moderate to high, ministers declared a transition from the "contain" strategy to the "delay" phase.

Consequently, targeted contact tracing was halted, with officials arguing that community transmission made the approach "no longer the most useful strategy".

As of February 2020, PHE had the capacity to handle only five new infections per week alongside the isolation of 800 contacts, forcing active tracing to be restricted to high-risk environments such as hospitals and care homes.

When the WHO formally declared a global pandemic, the UK introduced initial social distancing rules and advice for symptomatic individuals to remain at home.

While the WHO exhorted nations to "test, test, test", UK health authorities restricted available testing to hospitalised patients and medical staff.

Educational establishments, hospitality venues, and leisure facilities were ordered to close, alongside the implementation of shielding advice for high-risk individuals.

On 23 March 2020, the initial national lockdown came into force, with then prime minister Boris Johnson telling the public: "From this evening I must give the British people a very simple instruction – you must stay at home."

Shortly afterwards, ministers unveiled a "five pillar" plan to expand testing capacity, with former health secretary Matt Hancock promising 100,000 daily tests by May 2020, supported by new "lighthouse labs".

In April 2020, Mr Hancock announced a national test, track and trace scheme in England, involving mass recruitment of contact tracers and the creation of a mobile application.

Baroness Dido Harding was placed in charge of NHS Test and Trace, launched in May to ensure symptomatic individuals could obtain a test and instruct their recent contacts to self-isolate upon a positive result.

Testing infrastructure was gradually expanded to include drive-through sites and home delivery test kits.

Initial tracing relied on call centres, while the smartphone app suffered technical delays during development before its eventual rollout in September 2020.

The NHS Covid-19 app was subsequently downloaded by millions, offering exposure notifications and public health guidance.

![Then prime minister Boris Johnson told the public to ‘stay at home’ (PA)](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/07/18/17/936f55c866bca53628fefc8af672ed35Y29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzg0NDc2NDM3-2.82092621.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Despite criticism over its operational glitches, research published in Nature Communications estimated that the app’s contact tracing feature prevented one million infections and preserved nearly 10,000 lives.

By September 2020, self-isolation became a legal requirement in England for individuals who tested positive or were alerted by NHS Test and Trace.

A £500 Test and Trace Support Payment was introduced to assist low-income workers needing to isolate.

However, Lady Harding previously testified to the inquiry that additional financial support for self-isolation could have saved further lives, noting that former prime minister Rishi Sunak – serving as chancellor at the time – refused to raise isolation payments.

The inquiry also examined evidence indicating former prime minister Boris Johnson preferred a punitive stance toward those violating isolation rules.

A January 2021 entry in the diary of Lord Patrick Vallance, the Government’s then-chief scientific adviser, recorded: "PM says: ‘We haven’t been ruthless enough. We need to force more isolation. I favour a more authoritarian approach’."

In October 2021, a report by the Public Accounts Committee found NHS Test and Trace had "not achieved its main objective to help break chains of Covid-19 transmission and enable people to return towards a more normal way of life".

It highlighted that although 691 million lateral flow tests had been distributed by June 2021, results were logged for only 14% of them.

Amid scrutiny of its performance, Public Health England was subsequently dismantled.

![Lord Patrick Vallance wrote in his diary that the PM felt ‘we haven’t been ruthless enough’ (Jordan Pettitt/PA)](https://static.independent.co.uk/2025/09/25/11/9d6019ed28d9ef77ee4566aade2fd18dY29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzU4ODc5NjE0-2.80265689.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Its functions were initially subsumed into the National Institute for Health Protection alongside NHS Test and Trace and the Joint Biosecurity Centre, before being restructured into the UK Health Security Agency and the Office for Health Improvement and Disparities.

The inquiry's report may also address former MP Owen Paterson’s involvement with Randox, a firm awarded contracts for Covid testing kits. Mr Paterson resigned from Parliament in 2021 following a finding that he breached advocacy rules by lobbying ministers on behalf of the company.

Clare Fletcher, a solicitor representing over 7,000 families with Covid-19 Bereaved Families for Justice UK, stated: "Test and trace was a shambolic and chaotic system that led to avoidable loss of many lives despite the eyewatering sums of taxpayers’ money that were injected into the system.

"Our clients hope the chair confirms what many believe – it was fatally flawed and made many private firms and contractors rich, while too many innocent lives were lost.

"We call on the chair to recognise the true extent of the impact this had on so many families and agree to binding recommendations to ensure the same tragedies never happen again."
