**Micael Johansson, President of the Aerospace, Security and Defence Industries Association of Europe (ASD) & David Lockwood President of the ADS, the UK trade association for the aerospace, defence, security and space sectors**

## 'Europe’s security environment will not wait for perfect alignment. Our leaders should seize the opportunities within reach. Our industry needs them to do so,' write Micael Johansson and David Lockwood, senior representatives of the European aerospace, security and defence industries, in an op-ed.

When EU and UK leaders meet at their summit tomorrow, the temptation will be to take comfort in improved relations. Amid war in Europe, intensifying great-power competition and mounting pressure on industrial capacity, better relations must now translate into very concrete results.

The EU–UK Security and Defence Partnership agreed last year was an important step, recognising that Europe’s security challenges are best addressed through closer EU–UK cooperation. Political recognition is useful. The task now is to build on it with greater urgency.

## The EU and the UK share decades of industrial integration

In modern Europe, power begins as much in factories, laboratories and assembly lines as in ministries. EU Member States and the UK share decades of industrial integration: common standards; integrated supply chains; research, operational and regulatory cooperation; and long-established relationships of trust.

This applies to civil aeronautics and defence alike. Aerospace, defence and security companies on both sides of the Channel form an industrial ecosystem spanning flight technologies, ground and naval platforms, advanced manufacturing, electronics, cybersecurity, artificial intelligence and digital technologies. Decarbonising European aviation requires a strong, integrated European research and industrial base. Every major European aircraft carries UK technology, while UK aerospace depends on EU supply chains and access to the EU Single Market.

UK industry is woven into the fabric of European capability, just as EU industry is embedded in the UK. Geography, capability and integration make the UK the EU’s closest and most natural industrial partner.

## Both sides must make pragmatic decisions

A more integrated EU–UK approach would immediately deliver speed, scale and efficiency at a time when these qualities are increasingly decisive. It could strengthen security of supply, reduce duplication, enhance interoperability, support collaborative research and long-term capability development. This requires closer industrial planning, research and innovation cooperation, pragmatic alignment on standards and certification, and common principles for applying open modular system architecture of platforms and solutions.

This is not about revisiting past political debates or one side accommodating the other. It is about aligning Europe’s aerospace, defence and security capacity with a more demanding strategic environment.

The responsibility is shared. The EU has ambitious goals for aerospace innovation, defence readiness and industrial capacity. The UK has signalled its commitment to European security and closer cooperation. Both sides must make pragmatic decisions if those ambitions are to translate into tangible outcomes.

## UK participation in selected EU defence initiatives could be enabled

A dedicated industrial cooperation framework could enable UK participation in selected EU defence initiatives — under fair conditions and with an appropriate UK financial contribution — where this strengthens readiness, industrial capacity, supply-chain resilience and interoperability.

This should include possible UK participation in relevant European Competitiveness Fund activities, under mutually agreed conditions and with an appropriate financial contribution. Such participation would build on the longstanding integration of the UK within Europe’s aerospace, defence and security industrial base and support closer EU–UK industrial cooperation.

A further step would be for the UK to sign an Administrative Arrangement with the European Defence Agency (EDA). An established mechanism already exists to support cooperation with third countries, including Norway, Switzerland, Ukraine and the United States. Extending it to the UK would enable participation in selected EDA activities and programmes, strengthen dialogue within the EU-UK Security and Defence Partnership, and support interoperability.

Should the European Security Council be established and include the UK, it could provide a useful platform for closer cooperation, particularly if it integrates industrial and technological cooperation alongside the broader security agenda.

## The UK should provide access to UK programmes and procurement

The UK, in turn, should provide reciprocal access for EU industry to UK programmes and procurement; sustain regulatory alignment in airworthiness certification, export controls and defence-relevant standards.

The EU and the UK do not need to agree on everything to make progress. Practical cooperation can build trust and momentum and demonstrate the value of working together. Pursuing perfect alignment from the outset risks delaying vital outcomes that would benefit both sides today.

That will require strategic maturity from both sides: a focus on effectiveness and existing strengths and a political choice acknowledging the relationship’s unique nature. Europe can choose to develop its industrial base while reducing its dependency on non-European suppliers, or to invest time in narrower constructions that may deliver more slowly.

## The EU and the UK should move from agreement to delivery

A coherent EU–UK industrial partnership would strengthen competitiveness, innovation, technological leadership and Europe’s capacity to operate at scale in civil aviation and defence alike.

It would also strengthen NATO’s European pillar by better aligning industrial capability with operational needs. More balanced burden-sharing requires a strong European industry capable of delivering the full spectrum of capabilities needed for transatlantic security, drawing on strengths on both sides of the Channel.

It would demonstrate that the EU-UK partnership is more than a diplomatic formality, and that shared interests can be translated into practical benefits for the whole European community.

The foundations are in place. The strategic rationale is clear. The opportunity to build on this uniquely integrated relationship is real. What remains is the political choice.

The forthcoming summit should be the moment when the EU and the UK move from agreement to delivery. Europe’s security environment will not wait for perfect alignment. Our leaders should seize the opportunities within reach. Our industry needs them to do so.

*ASD is the Aerospace, Security and Defence Industries Association of Europe. Its overall representation adds up to more than 4,000 companies, accounting for 99% of industry’s total turnover and 94% of its total employment in Europe.*

*ADS is the UK trade association for the aerospace, defence, security and space sectors, representing almost 1,500 businesses. It enables prosperity, clean growth, and aims to secure advanced manufacturing’s collective advantage. ADS is a member of ASD.*
