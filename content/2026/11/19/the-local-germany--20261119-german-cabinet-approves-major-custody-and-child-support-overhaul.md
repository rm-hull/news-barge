On Wednesday, the Federal Cabinet signed off two draft bills: One – from Justice Minister Stefanie Hubig – reforms custody and contact rights. The other – from Family Minister Karin Prien – tightens the rules on state-backed child support.

The proposals, which are not yet law, would change how separated families deal with both contact between parents and children and money.

**What's changing on custody**

The Justice Ministry calls the draft the biggest reform of child law (*Kindschaftsrecht*) since 1998, and the bill runs to almost 200 pages.

The headline change is about domestic violence (*häusliche Gewalt*). Family courts already have to take this into account when deciding on custody (* Sorgerecht*) and contact (* Umgangsrecht*).

But there are currently no explicit rules specifying what happens when one parent is violent towards the other, rather than towards the child.

Under the draft, contact with the child could expressly be ruled out in that situation.

"Where necessary to protect the affected parent, visitation with the perpetrator of violence may be completely excluded," according to Hubig.

The ministry also says it wants to make clear that children have a right to be protected from witnessing attacks on people close to them.

In addition, an earlier draft of the bill would have allowed separated parents to transfer custody in full without visiting a family court. That proposal has been dropped; parents will still need a court to change custody, although smaller agreements on individual custody powers remain possible.

The draft covers physical, sexual, psychological and economic violence within the family. Assuming the bill passes, courts would then get a list of criteria to help them decide cases in future.**More say for teenagers**

Under the proposed changes, teenagers aged 14 and over would also get a bigger voice in decisions about their own lives.

Agreements on custody or contact would need the teenager's consent. They would also get their own legal right to stay in touch with grandparents, siblings and other close relatives, as long as that's in their best interests.

There's a change for day-to-day parenting, too. When the child is with them, a parent who lives apart from the other would be able to make routine decisions without checking first.

The Justice Ministry expects implementation of the bill in the second half of 2027 at the earliest. Until then, current rules apply.

**Changes to child support payments**

In Germany, if one parent stops paying child support, the state often steps in with an "advance maintenance payment" (*Unterhaltsvorschuss*). The authorities then try to claw the money back from the parent who didn't pay.

It doesn't always work. In 2024, federal and state governments reportedly paid out about €3.2 billion and recovered only around 17 percent. Spending on advance maintenance payments has nearly quadrupled in ten years, according to the German Press Agency.

Originally, Family Minister Karin Prien intended to make savings by stopping advance maintenance payments at 16 instead of 18. After protests, Prien changed her plans. The approved bill instead focuses on an income limit and tougher enforcement.

From 2029, single parents with a taxable income above €40,000 a year would lose their entitlement to advance maintenance payments. The limit rises by €10,000 for each additional child. This is taxable income, not gross pay. The ministry says it roughly equals a gross monthly income of around €4,500 or more.

Parents who owe maintenance would have to give the authorities details of their finances whenever asked. Failing to do so could mean a fine and potentially a driving ban.

Some income, such as a Christmas bonus, could also be garnished more heavily.

Prien says the aim is to keep supporting single parents who rely on the payment while holding those who don't pay more accountable.
