North Yorkshire councillors have narrowly rejected a bid to scrap new home-to-school transport rules amid claims the policy has been a 'shambles'.

Members of North Yorkshire Council voted 43 to 40 on Wednesday against a motion to restore free school transport eligibility based on school catchment areas, rather than the new policy of providing transport to a child’s nearest eligible school.

The motion was proposed by Liberal Democrat councillor Felicity Cunliffe-Lister, who warned that the policy change would be a ‘Tory legacy that will continue to haunt us’ if not overturned.

She told the full council meeting at County Hall in Northallerton:

> “Many of us will remember Thatcher the Milk Snatcher — if this council votes to continue to deprive children of their access to school and divide communities in doing so, we know many will find it hard to forgive or forget.
>
> “The current policy, without the inclusion of catchment, will continue to deliver long-term hardship and inequity.”

But Independent councillor Caroline Goodrick, who chaired the children and families overview and scrutiny committee which recommended rejecting the motion, urged members to consider the financial implications.

She said:

> “In 10 years, the home-to-school travel budget has risen from £25m to a staggering £54m.
>
> “These costs are borne by our council tax residents, whether they have children in education or not.
>
> “This isn’t our money we would be spending on a more generous policy, but theirs. The financial consequences of the motion would impact the projected £4m savings.
>
> “In the current economic climate, that is neither equitable nor sustainable for our council taxpayers.”

Later in the meeting, councillors voted to accept a number of changes recommended by officers in a post-implementation review of the policy.

Conservative executive member for children and families Janet Sanderson said opponents of the policy had been unable to come up with a costed alternative.

Pointing to a provisional £65,000 underspend in the school transport budget reported in the council’s second-quarter financial figures, she said critics had been unable to suggest alterative savings.

> “The majority of services in the children and young people’s services directorate are statutory functions.
>
> "Would you have us cut the support for those families who require early help, our children in care, or our young people leaving care?
>
> “Perhaps we could forget about the excellent work to support those children on the edge of care, those children in danger of entering the youth justice system.”

However, other councillors remained strongly opposed to the policy.

Green Party councillor Andy Brown said:

> “Whatever your views on the overall policy, you’ve got to admit it’s been a shambles and a really sorry story. It’s been handled so badly from start to finish.
>
> “It’s been a sorry tale of heavy-handed decision-making, followed by a lack of flexibility.
>
> “We’re meant to be here to help our residents. We set out to reduce costs, but achieved few real savings, instead shifting most of the burden onto parents and causing immense stress to many decent families trying to do the best for their children.”

The meeting had earlier heard from Swaledale parent Charlotte Fowler, a member of the School Transport Action Group, which was formed to fight the new rules.

She said the council’s own review acknowledged that removing children from the transport system had not yet translated into equivalent savings because vehicle numbers had not fallen at the same rate.

She added:

> “You say savings are on track, where’s the evidence? We’re told money has been banked, but where are the accounts to prove it?
>
> “Why are you being so sheepish about the numbers? What are you trying to hide?
>
> “Meanwhile, families are under enormous stress. Rural communities are being hollowed out. Money is leaving the county. Schools are losing pupils and losing funding.”
