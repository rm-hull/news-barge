The U.S. State Department has expressed support for Colombia’s plans to resume aerial spraying of coca crops, defending the controversial strategy despite warnings from American lawmakers about potential risks to human health and the Amazon rainforest.

In a letter to lawmakers seen by The Associated Press, the State Department said it supported Colombia’s renewed efforts to combat drug trafficking, including the proposed return to aerial eradication after an 11-year hiatus.

The decades-old U.S.-backed strategy was suspended in 2015 after concerns about the health effects of glyphosate, the herbicide previously used to destroy coca crops, the raw material for cocaine. Critics say spraying can damage legal crops, threaten rural communities and push farmers deeper into the rainforest, accelerating deforestation.

The State Department was responding to an Oct. 29 letter to Secretary of State Marco Rubio from 19 Democratic lawmakers led by U.S. Rep. Mark Pocan, D-Wis. The AP confirmed Thursday that two of the signatories received the department’s Nov. 18 response.

Other signatories included Reps. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-N.Y.), Rashida Tlaib (D-Mich.), Jim McGovern (D-Mass.) and Pramila Jayapal (D-Wash.). The lawmakers urged Washington to oppose the program and asked whether the Trump administration was pressuring Colombia to restart spraying or providing financial or logistical support.

The State Department said aerial spraying, alongside manual eradication and drug interdiction, offered significant potential to reduce coca cultivation and cocaine production.

Colombia is preparing a short-term pilot program to assess the viability of aerial eradication, the letter said, adding that the South American country would provide the personnel, aircraft fuel and herbicide needed for the operations. It also stressed that Colombia would be responsible for selecting the herbicide, in accordance with its own laws and regulatory procedures.

Pocan criticized the State Department’s response, saying it also indicated that Washington planned to provide technical assistance and aircraft for the pilot program.

“Aerial fumigation was not effective in the past at reducing coca cultivation over the long-term, and the hazards to human health and the environment are too significant to justify restarting the program,” he told AP. “I will continue to oppose this harmful program.”

In response to AP questions about the letter, the State Department said the Trump administration welcomed Colombia’s increased efforts to reduce cocaine production, which it said had reached record levels following the previous government’s failed counternarcotics policies.

“We expect Colombia to lead these efforts and demonstrate results, with the United States prepared to support its chosen approach,” the department said.

Colombia’s presidency and Environment Ministry did not reply to requests to comment.

Colombia’s controversial spraying pilot

Colombian President Abelardo de la Espriella’s government plans to test aerial spraying on up to 1,000 hectares (2,471 acres) of coca crops in Putumayo, an Amazonian department bordering Ecuador and Peru.

The program plans to use glufosinate ammonium instead of glyphosate, which was suspended after the World Health Organization’s cancer research agency classified it as probably carcinogenic to humans. Glufosinate ammonium has also faced scrutiny over potential health risks and is no longer approved for use in the European Union.

Colombian authorities argue that aerial spraying offers a faster and safer alternative to sending security forces into remote areas controlled by armed groups, where coca fields can be protected by land mines and other threats.

A court blocked the pilot in September. The government appealed, but a regional court kept the pilot on hold on Nov. 3, citing unresolved questions about the herbicide’s effects on health and the environment. The court gave the plaintiffs four months to file a separate lawsuit. If they miss that deadline, the temporary order expires.

Colombia’s environmental regulator opened a review of an environmental impact study by police on Nov. 4. The pilot requires the regulator’s approval of an environmental management plan and a formal opinion on the potential health risks from Colombia’s National Institute of Health.

Andrew Miller, advocacy director at Amazon Watch, an environmental organization that works with Indigenous communities, warned that the program could undermine cooperation with local people.

“This obsolete fumigation program is likely to alienate the very Amazonian communities the Colombian government should be coordinating with,” he said.

The State Department’s response comes as Colombia and the United States discuss expanding security cooperation against drug trafficking. Colombian Foreign Minister Omar Bula said Wednesday that the government would seek congressional approval for potential joint military operations with Washington, following talks with U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth in Bogota.

Colombia remains the world’s largest cocaine producer, with coca cultivation reaching a record 261,000 hectares in 2024, according to the latest available United Nations figures.
