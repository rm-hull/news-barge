As the cost of healthcare continues to rise in the U.S., a new report reveals 1 in 10 Americans, or 16 million, were uninsured last year.

Though unemployed workers were most likely to be uninsured, more than 10 million uninsured adults \ had full-time jobs, the Center for Economic and Policy Research said Wednesday.

“The data are clear that even before the full implementation of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, many millions of full-time workers fell through the cracks of our fragmented healthcare system,” Senior Fellow John Schmitt said in a statement.

“Providing coverage for every worker and their families is an urgent priority. But absent quick action, we are now on a course that will make matters even worse in 2027,” he said.

## The data

![Millions of Americans had no health insurance last year, many of whom had full-time jobs](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/20/57/GettyImages-1235239470.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Analyzing annual Census Bureau survey data showed Hispanic workers were most likely to be uninsured followed by Black workers and white workers. Men were also more likely than women to be uninsured - especially those aged 18-25.

Wages and education were major predictors of insurance coverage, as well. Nearly one in five workers in the bottom fifth of pay had no health insurance and close to a third of those with less than a high school degree were without coverage.

There are also disproportionate impacts for immigrants and people who are unmarried without children.

“Roughly similar shares of workers with and without a disability report having had no health insurance in 2025: 9.4 percent of workers with a disability and 10.4 percent of workers without a disability,” the report noted.

The assessment included workers aged 18-64 because those 65 and older are overwhelmingly eligible for Medicare, the government insurance program for seniors.

## Costly problems

The findings underscore the barriers to accessing life-saving care.

Most American workers - some 165 million - are on their employer’s insurance plans, but not everyone has that option.

The other options are government-funded and private insurance plans, but not everyone meets eligibility requirements.

For example, Medicaid is available in all states - but not everyone can get it due to certain limitations, such as how much money they make and whether they live in a state with Medicaid expansion, which extends eligibility.

![Health insurance costs are expected to jump next year, as the price of prescription drugs and medical care rise](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/20/2303028313..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Unfortunately, health insurance is growing costlier for employers and workers alike.

Workers are paying hundreds more toward premiums - payments to keep coverage active - this year than last year, according to an October survey from nonpartisan research group KFF.

The costs were particularly pronounced for employees at businesses with between 10 and 99 workers, who take on a larger share of insurance costs than those at bigger companies.

Around 8 in 10 Americans say their health care costs are higher, another KFF poll found in March.

And it’s only expected to be less affordable next year, with costs going up due to the increased costs of hospital care and prescription drugs.

“Healthcare costs are going up faster than they have in years, and open enrollment is when the healthcare affordability crisis is really going to hit home for people,” Larry Levitt, executive vice president for health policy at KFF, told NBC News in September.
