A dispute over access rights has left a Welsh glamping site owner embroiled in a court battle costing more than £135,000, after she attempted to prevent her neighbours from parking their car in their own garden.

The feud was triggered by an omitted letter "B" on a 60-year-old map.

It disturbed the peace at Carol Esteban’s farm glampsite on the edge of the Brecon Beacons national park, sparking a dispute with neighbours Neil and Nadine Gould over the shared driveway serving both properties.

Relations worsened following mutual allegations of trespass, "shouting and harassing one another" and deliberate obstruction of the lane.

The matter reached court after Ms Esteban — who advertises "blissful staycations" featuring glamping pods with individual hot tubs set in 60 acres of ancient woodland — informed the couple that their right to use the track terminated directly opposite their front door, preventing access to the parking space beside their home just a few feet further on.

Following a row in which Ms Esteban blocked the track north of their property with "boulders", they sued and successfully secured a High Court ruling last July entitling them to drive far enough along the track to access their hardstanding parking area.

![Carol Esteban outside London's Appeal Court](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/10/20/CarolEsteban2-fd701e45.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&auto=webp)

However, the litigation is ongoing, with Ms Esteban challenging the judgment — together with the £135,000 costs bill imposed upon her — at the Court of Appeal in London.

Judge Milwyn Jarman KC, in his ruling on the case in 2025, explained how the Goulds' detached cottage, Cwmtawe Villa, Caerbont, Abercrave, near Swansea, is completely surrounded by land attached to Cwmtawe Farm, where Ms Esteban runs her glamping site.

The properties were once all held by the same owner, but the house was sold off in 1949, with documents relating to that transaction now lost.

A 1964 coveyancing document however states that the owners of Cwmtawe Villa enjoy an "easement", granting them the right to use a track owned by the farm to access their house on foot or with vehicles.

However, a missing letter on a map attached to the document sparked the neighbours' fight over how far along the track the easement extends.

The judge explained that while the map shows the start of the right of way marked "point A in a circled capital letter in black near to the junction of the track with the main road. There is no point marked B" to show where it ends.

![Cwmtawe Villa, with the disputed car parking space north of the house on the left](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/10/48/Cwmtawe-Villa1-12387995.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&auto=webp)

Ms Esteban and her lawyers say the location of point "B", and the end of the right of way, was proved by a later map to be exactly outside the Goulds' front door, meaning they would not be legally allowed to drive the few feet further on they would need in order to turn onto the hardstanding they installed to park their car on just to the north of their house.

The glampsite boss argued that a thick hedge had surrounded the villa at the time the right of way was granted and that it would have made no sense for the right of way to entitle the owners to go any further than the gap in the hedge by the front door.

But Judge Jarman, rejecting her argument, said Ms Esteban's assertion "has the potential to lead to impracticability, if not absurdity.

"It would mean that those entitled to use the right of way could drive a vehicle up to a point adjacent to the front door of the villa, but no further and not onto the villa. They could not park on the track for any length of time, or on the farm land.

"Assuming a right so interpreted would allow them to turn the vehicle around rather than reverse down the track, they would then have to drive off and find somewhere else to park.

"In my judgment, the claimants are entitled to a declaration that their vehicular right of way extends to the space at the north side of the villa, where they presently park their vehicles."

Appealing the ruling, Daniel Bromilow, for Ms Esteban, told Lord Justice Miles, Lord Justice Fancourt and Lord Justice Butcher that the High Court judge "did not base his decision by construing the 1949 conveyance and the conveyance plan, but simply determined what he thought the parties should have agreed to in relation to the right of way.

![Glamping pods at Cwmtawe Farm, Caerbont](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/19/10/34/EstebanGlamping1-c3999ff2.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&auto=webp)

"When interpreting a contract, a judge should avoid re-writing it in an attempt to assist an unwise party or to penalise an astute party," he said.

"There was no evidence that the track shown on the conveyance plan extended beyond the front door of Cwmtawe and no evidence on which the judge could have concluded that the lost 1949 conveyance provided for a right of way over that extent of the adjoining land.

"The judge was wrong to find that the right of way which exists for the benefit of Cwmtawe extended beyond the location of the small opening in the hedge at Cwmtawe leading to the front door of the house."

He also argued against Ms Esteban being handed her neighbours' costs bill for the case, saying that she had "succeeded to a significant extent on her counterclaim," including winning a finding that a new waterpipe had been installed unlawfully on her land by the couple, as well as having "successfully resisted substantial elements" of their case.

However Owen Curry, for Mr and Mrs Gould, urged the appeal judges to uphold the decision.

He said: "The appeal concerns the scope and extent of express an easement, with or without vehicles, leading across Ms Esteban's land to Mr and Mrs Gould's house, Cwmtawe.

"The dispute on appeal is whether the easement stops in line with the front door of Cwmtawe or whether it permits them to reach parking spaces at the side of their house, which involves travelling a few feet past the line of the front door.

"If it does not, then the easement, despite expressly including vehicles - a fact that was in dispute at trial but not on appeal - will be of little to no practical utility, save as a footpath.

"The construction preferred by the judge at trial gives practical and commonsense effect to what the grantor of the easement must have intended. The construction advanced by the appellant on appeal is not supported by the words of the grant or by a commonsense approach.

"The layout of the land shows there is not, and has never been, any obvious physical feature which would stop them driving onto their parking spaces, or, indeed, stop them walking a little past their front door before turning onto their land.

"The learned judge reached the right conclusion in this case, and the appeal should be dismissed. The construction of the easement advanced by the appellant makes no sense and ignores the plain words of the grant."

The judges have now reserved their decision on the appeal, to be given at a later date.
