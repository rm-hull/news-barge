# Parents need to get a grip and teach their young children the basics: Jayne Dowle

## In his long-awaited report into the crisis of NEETS - young people not in education, employment or training - Alan Milburn pulls no punches. He stops short of actually blaming the parents but delivers a clear recommendation that mothers and fathers must do their job.

The former Labour Cabinet minister, who resigned as Health Secretary in Tony Blair’s government in 2003 saying he wished to spend more time with his young family, believes that if a child is allowed a strong start in life they are far less likely to lose their way.

“Change should begin before a young person becomes NEET,” Milburn argues, in his report, ‘From a Welfare State to a Working State: A new settlement for the next generation’.

He fears that far too many children are starting school lacking basic skills they should have already learned at home.

He is absolutely right. It is shocking that more than a third (37 per cent) of children go into reception class unable to use the toilet, eat with cutlery, communicate clearly or follow simple instructions, according to a recent report by early years charity Kindred Squared.

By 2036, Milburn wants at least 90 per cent of children to be school-ready at the age of five. This means that - bluntly - parents need to get a grip on the basics.

I was thinking about this when I was food shopping the other evening. Surely is it never acceptable to walk into a supermarket, abdicate all parental responsibility, and simply let your under-10s loose to race up and down the aisles whilst you fill your trolley. I see it more and more, and it makes me wonder what parameters of behaviour some modern parents consider acceptable.

I’m a mother of two - my son and daughter are in their early twenties now - so I get that it can be very trying shopping with kids. However, if I ever had to take mine along, they knew to walk next to me, not scream for sweets, not pull items off shelves and generally control themselves.

On this occasion, a brother and sister in primary school uniform had picked up long wooden Christmas decorations and were chasing each other all over the store in a mock sword fight. I looked around for their parents, but they were nowhere to be seen.

The other weekend, I saw a dad with a gaggle of seven and eight-year-olds in sports kit running around the drinks section passing a rugby ball to each other. Bottles were clanking, people were tutting. The dad never even raised his voice; my son was certainly a handful at that age, but would he have even thought to do that? No.

If this is the way some children are allowed to behave in public, what is going on home? No wonder my friends who work in early years and primary education despair.

Lack of parental input in toilet-training is one of their biggest gripes. I know I was old-school, but I got mine out of nappies as soon as possible. Not because of some strict parenting approach, but because nappies were messy, expensive and time-consuming and I wanted my two to feel independent and grown-up.

My very laidback daughter was particularly reluctant but finally managed it after a humiliating public accident at the age of three. So again, I understand this aspect of parenting is not easy and requires patience and persistence.

However, when I speak with parents of young children these days it’s rare to find one who has the same determination to dispense with the whole grim business and support and encourage their under-fives to use a loo. Why? I don’t want to judge, but is it because trailing backwards and forwards to the bathroom interrupts parental screen time?

In his report, Milburn states that whilst technology has brought huge benefits, “the amount of childhood lived through a screen raises legitimate concerns about sleep, relationships, physical activity, attention and wellbeing”. What about the parents though? If they are constantly on their phones, what example does this set to their children?

Which is where the key point of Milburn’s recommendations comes in. Parents, he argues, “remain the first and most important influence on a child's development”. As such, we are “the first and most important source of nurture, security, encouragement and expectation”, with a vital role from the moment of birth.

Public policy should “make good parenting easier”, Milburn lays down. But parents should not shrug off their responsibilities to school or other services.

In his recommendations, the state's role would be clearer and more proportionate, providing information and accessible universal services, and intervening more intensively where serious difficulty emerges.

Support should be built through the new Best Start Family Hubs - 200 of these one-stop shops for advice, services, classes and activities opened earlier this year, with 1,000 planned by 2028 - and concentrated most strongly where developmental disadvantage is greatest.

In return, parents are expected to engage with their child's development, attendance and education.

In other words, recognise your responsibilities, be involved and stay involved, however tough the parenting terrain.
