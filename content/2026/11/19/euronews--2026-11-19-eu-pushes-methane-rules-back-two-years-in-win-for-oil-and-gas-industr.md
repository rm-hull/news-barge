## MEPs and EU governments have agreed to postpone key methane emissions requirements by two years amid pressure from the fossil fuel industry and mounting concerns over energy security and affordability.

EU methane emissions rules will be delayed by two years, until 2029, under a deal struck between MEPs and EU governments, in what critics have decried as another concession to the oil and gas industry at the expense of the bloc's climate ambitions.

The EU's methane rules, first adopted in 2024, require foreign suppliers to provide information about methane emissions associated with the oil, gas and coal they sell to European buyers.

The delay marks another retreat from the EU's climate commitments as governments prioritise energy security, industrial competitiveness and affordability over the swift implementation of green legislation.

EU countries have cited high and volatile oil and gas prices, coupled with potential supply disruptions caused by the US-Israeli war against Iran, to justify delaying the rules.

The political battle over the delay has exposed a difficult relationship between the EU's centre-right and its traditional pro-climate allies.

In October, the European People's Party (EPP), the Parliament's largest political group, pushed through a three-year postponement, winning support from far-right lawmakers after negotiations with the Socialists and Liberals broke down. The proposal passed with 341 votes in favour and 225 against.

The Greens accused conservatives of joining forces with the far right to weaken climate rules and protect the interests of the fossil fuel industry. The EPP, however, argued that the original timetable was unrealistic and risked putting European producers and importers at a disadvantage.

Thursday's compromise reduces Parliament's demand for a three-year delay to two years, but still exceeds the Commission's initial offer and the one-year delay backed by EU governments.

MEP Dimitris Tsiodras (EPP/Greece), the Parliament’s negotiator on the Environment Committee, said the EPP's priority was to ensure energy security, affordability and industrial competitiveness alongside methane reduction commitments.

"We are not suspending the methane regulation but ensuring its effective implementation and that all compliance requirements can be met in practice”, Tsiodras reacted.

Czech First Deputy Prime Minister and Industry and Trade Minister Karel Havlíček said the deal would prevent further increases in oil and gas prices.

"It can be done. When we unite and are determined, we can change even what seemed impossible. The Czech Republic, which won 19 other countries over to its side, achieved a major success in the EU," Havlíček said on X.

## Macron's intervention and US pressure

The push to delay the rules has also gathered momentum outside Parliament.

French President Emmanuel Macron wrote to Commission President Ursula von der Leyen in September calling for a delay of the methane rules, arguing that the EU should not weaken its own production capacity at a time of mounting energy insecurity.

Paris had previously backed stronger action to curb methane emissions, but joined governments and industry groups arguing that the rules could make it harder to secure oil and gas supplies during a period of market disruption.

The United States, Europe's largest supplier of liquefied natural gas (LNG), has also repeatedly criticised the EU legislation, warning that its requirements could create additional costs and complicate exports to the EU market.

The EU also relies on the US for between a third and half of its diesel imports (around 180,000 barrels per day).

The pressure comes as the US-Israel war against Iran has disrupted shipping through the critical Strait of Hormuz and has intensified concerns about the bloc's dependence on imported fossil fuels.

But the argument that delaying methane rules will protect supplies and lower prices remains disputed. The rules don't ban imports of fossil fuels that fail to meet EU standards.

Its initial requirements focus on monitoring and reporting emissions, allowing the bloc to obtain information about pollution generated during the production of imported energy.

Environmental groups and experts have questioned whether postponing those obligations will make any meaningful difference to energy prices or supply availability.

## Another test for the Green Deal

Methane is responsible for around 30% of global warming and is significantly more potent than CO2 over the short term. Cutting emissions from fossil fuel production is considered one of the fastest ways to slow global warming.

Under the deal struck overnight on Thursday by EU governments and the Parliament, importers of crude oil, natural gas and coal will have until 1 January 2029 to comply with monitoring, reporting and verification requirements, instead of the original January 2027 deadline.

The agreement also pushes back methane-intensity reporting obligations for both European producers and importers by two years, to August 2030.

The provisional deal still needs formal backing from the Parliament and the Council, due by the end of the year.

François-Régis Mouton de Lostalot, managing director at the lobby group the International Association of Oil and Gas Producers (IOGP) Europe, described the delay as a "certain improvement" building on the Commission's proposal.

"But without a review clause, Europe has no margin for error. The Commission and Member States must lead a coordinated effort from day one, with clear milestones and responsibilities, to make the compliance framework fully operational before the obligations apply," Mouton de Lostalot said.

EU lawmakers and climate scientists have previously identified methane as a "low-hanging fruit" because it delivers fast, low-cost reductions in near-term global warming using existing technology.

Inger Andersen, executive director of the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP), said methane is the second largest contributor to global heating behind CO2 and warned that cutting its emissions "is our emergency brake to slow warming".

“With the world on course to exceed 1.5°C of warming, we must limit how high temperatures rise and how long they stay high, and then bring them down,” said Andersen.

Environmental activists argue the extended delay will weaken climate goals, criticising Brussels for its strike in softening other parts of its climate agenda, including the EU's carbon market, industrial decarbonisation rules and CO2 emissions in cars.
