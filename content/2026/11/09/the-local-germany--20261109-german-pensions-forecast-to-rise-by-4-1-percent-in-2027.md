More than 21 million people receiving Germany’s statutory pension could see their payments rise by about 4.1 percent from July 1st, 2027 – an extra €41 a month on a €1,000 pension.

But the forecast is not yet final, and the good news comes with a catch. Contributions are also expected to rise, intensifying debate over who should bear the growing cost of Germany’s ageing population.

**Where does the figure come from?**

Each year, the government publishes its *Rentenversicherungsbericht*, or Pension Insurance Report, setting out how the statutory pension system is expected to develop. The figures in the latest edition of the report were first reported by the* Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung*.

The final adjustment will be calculated when more data become available in March 2027. The confirmed rate will then take effect on July 1st next year.

The increase introduced in 2026 was initially estimated at 3.7 percent but eventually came to 4.24 percent. The current 4.1 percent projection is already below an earlier estimate of 4.4 percent.**Why are pensions expected to rise?**

Annual pension adjustments in Germany are closely linked to wage trends: when wages rise, pensions generally rise too.

The calculation also includes the pension value, the pension level and a “sustainability factor” reflecting the balance between contributors and pensioners.

In addition, a safety net keeps the pension level – the relationship between a standard pension and average earnings – from falling below 48 percent until 2031.

That is good news for retirees, especially after several years of sizeable increases. But there is another side to the story.

The statutory pension contribution rate is expected to remain at 18.6 percent of gross income in 2027.

But the latest draft report projects an increase to 19.7 percent in 2028, followed by further rises to 21 percent by 2040.

An earlier official spring estimate put the possible 2028 rate slightly higher, at 19.9 percent.

Either way, employees would face larger payroll deductions, while employers – who share the cost – would pay more for labour.

**The pension system’s difficult balancing act**

This creates an apparent contradiction: if pensions are rising, why do contributions have to rise as well?

Germany’s current workers largely finance today’s pensioners. As the population ages and baby boomers retire, the number of recipients grows relative to the number of contributors. Protecting pensioners’ income therefore makes the system more expensive even when wages and payments into the fund are rising.

That tension is becoming political. Critics argue that maintaining the 48 percent pension level shifts too much of the bill to younger generations, who may pay more while eventually receiving less favourable benefits themselves.
