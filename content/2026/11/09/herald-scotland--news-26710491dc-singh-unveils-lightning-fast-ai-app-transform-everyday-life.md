At 6:40 on a wet weekday morning, a private-hire driver named Marcus sits in a parked car outside a train station, watching two apps at once.

One shows a surge zone pulsing across the east side of the city. The other shows a delivery queue that has just thinned out. He has been awake since five. He cannot know whether the next forty minutes will repay the fuel he is about to burn, so he does what most drivers do. He makes an educated guess.

Marcus is a hypothetical driver, but his morning will be familiar to many who work behind the wheel.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21687221.jpg?type=mds-article-620)DC Singh *(Image: Supplied)*

It is a small decision, repeated millions of times a day, and it captures one of the quieter imbalances of the modern economy. The platforms that dispatch drivers and couriers run on some of the most sophisticated prediction engines ever built for commerce. The people who feed those engines with their time, fuel and miles mostly rely on instinct.

His venture is developing an AI-powered application designed to give drivers, delivery partners and other platform workers the kind of analytical support that large organisations take for granted: a clearer view of what they earn, what it costs them, and where their time is best spent. The product is in development, and DC Singh is careful not to oversell it.

But the thesis behind it is simple enough to fit on a business card. If artificial intelligence is going to reshape work, the people doing the work should share in the upside.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21687222.jpg?type=mds-article-620)DC Singh *(Image: Supplied)*

**The Asymtimetry Problem**

Platform work has plenty in its favour. It is accessible, flexible and quick to start. For some people it is a primary income, for others a bridge between jobs, and for others a way to earn around family or study. That is why it has grown so widely.

It also carries well-documented frictions. Earnings can swing from hour to hour and week to week. Workers often juggle several apps, each with its own pricing logic and incentives. Costs such as fuel, maintenance, insurance, depreciation and waiting time are easy to underestimate and hard to track. And many workers, particularly the self-employed, must manage tax and benefits with no human resources department to lean on.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21687223.jpg?type=mds-article-620)DC Singh *(Image: Supplied)*

The available evidence points the same way. The International Labour Organization's 2021 flagship report found that taxi workers on app-based platforms averaged 65 hours a week, and delivery workers 59. In the UK, Oxford University's Fairwork project assessed 12 platforms in 2023 and found that only two could evidence that workers earned at least the minimum wage after costs such as fuel, maintenance, insurance and time spent waiting between jobs. And in the United States, Pew Research Center found that 23% of current and recent gig platform workers described the income as essential for meeting basic needs, with another 35% calling it important.

None of this reflects a lack of effort. Most drivers and couriers work with real discipline. The issue is structural. Their judgment, however seasoned, is being measured against systems with far more data and computing power, and the intelligence those systems produce flows mainly in one direction.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21320864.jpg?type=mds-article-620)DC Singh *(Image: Supplied)*

"The sophistication inside these systems was always a decision about who it should serve. A driver deserves to see their own week with the same clarity the platform does. That is not an unreasonable thing to build," DC Singh says.

DC Singh's argument is that this symmetry was designed, not destined, and that the same class of technology can be pointed somewhere else. It is a modest claim with an ambitious implication. The tools exist. The question is who they are built for.

**A Product Built Around The Worker**

The concept is straightforward. DC Singh is working toward an application that brings a worker's own earnings and activity together in one place, across the different platforms they use, so a driver can see what a week of work actually produced after costs.

From there, the ambition widens. The application is intended to translate patterns in demand into plain-language guidance, such as which times and areas have historically been more worthwhile, and where the cost of repositioning outweighs the reward. It is designed to handle the less glamorous side of platform work too: expense tracking, mileage records and earnings summaries that make tax season less stressful and show workers what they are genuinely keeping.

There is a longer-term layer as well. Information about benefits, protections and support schemes is often scattered and hard to interpret. Organised into guidance a worker can act on, it becomes part of a larger picture. A person who understands their numbers can plan around them, whether the goal is a deposit, a safety buffer or a child's education.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21687224.jpg?type=mds-article-620)DC Singh *(Image: Supplied)*DC Singh frames all of this as aims rather than accomplishments, and he applies one filter to every feature under consideration. "Does this leave the driver better informed at the end of the week? If the answer is no, it does not belong in the product," he says.**Shared Prosperity Is A Business Model, Not A Slogan**

Mission statements about shared prosperity are easy to dismiss. DC Singh treats his as a commercial argument.

The logic runs like this. Workers who earn more reliably and understand their finances tend to stay engaged, plan further ahead and build steadier lives. A product that earns their trust can build a durable relationship with a large and underserved user base. And a company whose success depends on the success of its users has an incentive structure that points in the right direction.

It is closer to the economics of a good professional service than to the economics of an extractive one. The provider does well when the client does well. DC Singh believes that alignment is both the ethical foundation of the business and its competitive advantage, because trust, once earned, is difficult for a rival to copy.

"Shared prosperity only means something if it shows up in the business model. If our users do not do better, we have not built anything worth backing," he says.

There is a broader argument too, and it is one investors and policymakers are increasingly willing to hear. As AI becomes more capable, public confidence in it will depend partly on whether ordinary people can see a tangible benefit in their daily lives. Technology that visibly improves working conditions helps show

that this wave of innovation can be inclusive. DC Singh sees his venture as one contribution to that argument.

**The Market Math**

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21687225.jpg?type=mds-article-620)DC Singh *(Image: Supplied)*

The opportunity is easier to describe than to inflate, and DC Singh prefers it that way.

Platform-based work spans taxis, private hire, food delivery, parcel courier services and a growing range of on-demand tasks, and the numbers are considerable. The World Bank's 2023 estimate puts the global total of online gig workers between 154 million and 435 million, or 4.4% to 12.5% of the world's labour force. The breadth of that range is itself a finding: this is a workforce that is still poorly measured, let alone well served. The ILO counted more than 777 digital labour platforms in 2020, up from 142 in 2010, with taxi and delivery platforms growing almost tenfold over the period.

National surveys tell a similar story. Research backed by the Trades Union Congress found that 9.6% of UK working-age adults, close to one in ten, worked through a platform at least weekly in 2019, roughly double the share in 2016. In the United States, Pew Research Center found that 16% of adults have earned money through an online gig platform. Platform work is smartphone-based by design, so the distribution channel for a mobile-first application already exists in workers' pockets.

What does not yet exist at scale is software built for them. Most of the technology in a driver's day is designed to dispatch, monitor and rate. Relatively little is designed to advise. The intelligence on the other side is anything but thin: Uber alone reported 13.6 billion trips in 2025, up 20% on the year before, and 202 million monthly active platform consumers in the fourth quarter. That gap is the space DC Singh intends to occupy.

For prospective investors, the pitch rests on four elements: a large and clearly defined user group, a genuine unmet need, a founder with a coherent point of view, and a technology foundation in applied AI that is maturing quickly. The underlying demand is not speculative. People who work on platforms already want better visibility into their earnings. The commercial task is to deliver it well and to build a revenue model that works for the people it serves.

**Trust Is The Product**

If one theme runs through DC Singh's thinking, it is trust. A product aimed at workers who manage tight budgets cannot afford to be careless with their confidence.

That has practical consequences. Worker data should be handled with care, with plain explanations of what is collected, why, and who can see it. Recommendations should be understandable rather than opaque, so a driver can see the reasoning behind a suggestion and decide whether to follow it. And the application should be honest about uncertainty. Demand forecasts are estimates, not guarantees, and users should be told so.

DC Singh regards these commitments as foundational. A tool that quietly serves other interests, or promises more than it can deliver, would defeat the purpose for which it was built. For a company trying to reach workers who have often felt unseen by the systems around them, credibility is the asset that matters most, and it is earned slowly.

He is equally clear about limits. No application can resolve questions of pay rates, worker classification or platform policy. Those belong to companies, regulators and society. What a well-designed tool can do is

help individuals navigate the landscape as it exists today, and give them better information with which to take part in the conversations that shape it.

**The Risks, Stated Plainly**

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21687226.jpg?type=mds-article-620)DC Singh *(Image: Supplied)*

Early-stage ventures carry real risk, and this one is no exception. The model must be proven. The product must earn adoption among a sceptical and time-poor audience. Integrating data across multiple platforms is technically and contractually complex. Regulation of both AI and platform work is evolving, and the economics have to hold at scale.

DC Singh does not dispute any of this. His response is a posture rather than a promise: listen closely to the people the product is meant to serve, test ideas against real behaviour rather than assumptions, and stay honest about what the company can and cannot yet do. In a field prone to hype, that restraint is a feature. Artificial intelligence attracts grand promises, and many go unfulfilled. A founder who speaks about opportunity, effort and gradual improvement rather than disruption for its own sake is making a deliberate choice about the kind of company he wants to build.

"Trust with working people is not something you announce. You earn it one accurate number at a time, and you lose it the first time you overpromise," he says.

**What Cotimes Next**

The coming years will test whether the promise of artificial intelligence extends beyond boardrooms and research labs into the working lives of ordinary people. For the millions who earn a living through digital platforms, the answer will be measured in concrete terms: clearer earnings, smarter choices and a fairer share of the value they help create.

DC Singh's venture is at an early stage, with the hard work of development, validation and growth still ahead. But the proposition is clear and increasingly timely. Technology built with workers in mind can be good for them and good business at the same time.

Back outside the station, Marcus finally pulls away from the kerb. Somewhere down the line, a different kind of application might tell him what the next hour is likely to be worth, what it will cost him, and what he will take home. That is the future DC Singh is working toward: AI that serves the people who keep the city moving, and rewards the work they already do.
