## York City legend remembered as 'true people person'

Barry Swallow (centre) challenges for the ball at Old Trafford on March 29, 1975, in a Division Two fixture against Manchester United. *(Image: Newsquest)*

Former York City captain Barry Swallow, who led the Minstermen during one of the greatest periods in the club's history, has been remembered by his family as a hard-working "people person" who became as much a part of York life as the team he represented.

Swallow made 312 appearances and captained the side that won promotion in 1973/74 to the old Second Division, paving the way for the club to achieve the highest league position in its history the following year.

That 74/75 campaign contained an Easter fixture at Old Trafford against recently relegated Manchester United, a side which still boasted international players, and one which Yorkshire Evening Press reporter Malcolm Huntington described at the time as a 'dream' for City players and fans because only two years before, the Minstermen had escaped relegation from Division Three on goal average.

![Barry Swallow leads out York City.](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/20945883.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Barry Swallow (right) joined York from the Bantams at the age of 27 in 1969 *(Image: Newsquest)*

The captain had received treatment for a calf injury in the run up to the fixture to be played in front of 46,802 fans but was declared fit for a game which City ultimately lost 2-1 in what then York - and former Man Utd - manager Wilf McGuinness called a brave rearguard action against the then Second Division leaders.

Swallow was also part of a City side which equalled a Football League record by going 11 consecutive games without conceding a goal.

His performances also earned individual recognition, with the centre-half named in the PFA divisional Team of the Year in 1973/74 alongside teammate Barry Lyons.

In October 1998, The Football League named him as City’s 'Local Hero' and in 2000 he was named City’s Player of the Millennium.

Yet for his wife Elizabeth and children James and Jenifer, the achievements which made Swallow a hero to a generation of York supporters were worn lightly at home.

![Barry and Elizabeth Swallow](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21684337.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Barry heading a ball in the back garden with Elizabeth hanging out the washing *(Image: Newsquest)*

Towards the end of his playing career, Barry and his wife Elizabeth took on a pub in a York village and later combined this with the running of a hotel in Clifton.

Jenifer recalls walking through the city as a child with her father and hearing people call out to him.

She said: “Dad was a real people person – it was normal for him to walk down the street and say hi to everybody who recognised him and to have a chat, like this is just what you do.”

It was only as they grew older that Jenifer and brother James began to appreciate quite how much their father meant to the thousands who watched York City play.

Going to Bootham Crescent every Saturday and being surrounded by supporters was simply part of family life, their dad was always just "with the people, but never wallowing in it, he was just going to work – he loved it and loved them all".

Fans even had their own song for the tall centre-half: ‘Six foot two, eyes are blue, look out, Swallow's after you.’

![Barry Swallow, former York City captain](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21684340.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Barry Swallow played 312 times for the Minstermen *(Image: Newsquest)*

Son James also recalled when being dropped off as a boy outside the cub scout hut, youngsters waited hoping for Barry's autograph.

James said: “I remembered thinking, ‘this is a bit odd, what’s going on here?’

“Things like that did spark my attention, and at the time I didn’t really understand that he was as well-known to other people living in the city.

“And today, we still get people speaking to us who remember him from those days as a hero.”

The game ran through the family, with Barry's father Ernest having also been a professional footballer, playing full back for Doncaster Rovers.

Elizabeth, his wife of 56 years, said even when they met in the South Yorkshire town it was clear from his dedication and discipline that Barry was destined to follow in his dad’s footsteps.

Swallow turned professional with Doncaster Rovers in 1959 and made his football league debut the year after in a career that also took him to Crewe Alexandra, Barnsley and Bradford City.

A £3,000 transfer fee from the Bantams brought him to York in 1969, where he put down lasting roots.

Elizabeth said then manager Tom Johnston insisted they move over to the city. She described it as the best thing the couple ever did and they immediately loved York and all it had to offer.

That mindset and work ethic was evident off the pitch too.

While still playing for York, in 1975 Barry and Elizabeth became landlords at The Rose and Crown pub in Askham Richard, two weeks before James was born.

Their children said the couple transformed the business, introducing food including curries, chilli, lasagne and steak sandwiches at a time before gastro pubs when such dishes were far from commonplace on pub menus.

Barry brought the same exacting standards to hospitality that he demanded in football.

His daughter said she and James always had jobs to do in the family business and remembered immaculate beer pumps, Dad hefting beer barrels, sparkling pipes and plates piled high.

There was a determination that every customer should receive consistently great food and service and be greeted warmly.

Elizabeth said: “The pub just took off – everybody came because Barry had it.

“The players would come in, and the local prison officers too.

"We had Burns nights and darts and dominoes tournaments and all sorts.

“It was pretty run down when we got it and had never done any food, or anything like that.

“To start with, the food was an extension of our own home cooking.

"People still tell me how much they remember the steak pies, the curries and the chilli con carne.

“The kids would be helping in the kitchen too; they used to trim the fat and tissue off the kidneys before they went into those pies."

His care for the local community also led to Barry rounding up locals to take on the task of dredging the village pond outside the pub, which had become silted.

Working with Ray Bartram, the neighbouring farmer, and Dave Charlton, the local drainage specialist, and a team of village volunteers they dredged and refilled it.

![A village pond being dredged in Askham Richard, York](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21684336.jpg?type=mds-article-620)A large team including village volunteers came together to dredge the pond outside the Rose and Crown in Askham Richard *(Image: Contributed)*

But perhaps Barry’s greatest asset in the pub was the same quality supporters had encountered on the streets of York: he loved talking to people.

Jenifer remembered a pub with two sides, one for its traditional drinkers, and another with a family-friendly food operation, with her father equally comfortable talking to the man drinking alone at the bar or families coming in for a meal.

She said: "Everybody he spoke to about football and his career had their own anecdote or story, their own memory of him.

"Although he was quite a private man in himself, he liked to hear about their lives and perspectives."

The couple later also ran the Clifton Bridge Hotel for many years, combining the demands of two hospitality businesses with bringing up their children.

The hotel also offered meeting and office space used by young businesses – an early forerunner of the shared workspaces of today.

James described his parents as a formidable team, with his father often the public face of the operation and his mother the "engine room".

“Dad obviously was front of house,” he said.

“The two of them running those businesses, and working as a team together, it set a great example for us kids and we enjoyed contributing.”

Elizabeth said: “He was regimented about things, he hated if people were waiting too long for their tables, he had such high standards.”

Jenifer said: “It was 24-7 in the hotel, people might need you in the night, you’re up to do breakfasts and you’re closing the bar late in the evening.

“So, I don’t know when the sleeping happened.”

Family life nevertheless had to be carved out around businesses that rarely stopped.

One respite came on Wednesday afternoons, when the family would head to their caravan on the Yorkshire coast.

With country music playing in the car, they would meet the children's grandparents, have fish and chips and spend time on the beach before returning in time for school the next day.

![Barry Swallow and family on holiday at Primrose Valley](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21684335.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The Swallow family on holiday at Primrose Valley near Filey *(Image: Contributed)*

Barry Swallow's football story did not end when he hung up his boots.

He remained actively involved with York City, moving from the pitch to the boardroom and becoming a director, as well as stepping in as caretaker manager on more than one occasion.

Elizabeth remembered the transition as a natural one.

She said: “He was smart, commanding and respected, but above all brought to the board a footballer's understanding of the game.”

His family repeatedly returned to two particular footballing qualities; his ability to read a game and understand players.

The family saw these qualities extend beyond football.

Elizabeth said: “He was a great observer of people, and listened to them rather than judging.

"He would have no truck with negativity and was always ready to offer a ‘tactic talk’.

“He was a proud grandfather, father and husband and felt he had been a lucky man all his life.”

Barry died peacefully on October 20, having lived with dementia in his later years.

He is survived by Elizabeth, James and Jenifer and five grandchildren.

Donations in his memory are being made to Alzheimer’s Research UK, and tributes can be left at barryswallow.muchloved.com.
