Harry Boisseau, chief executive and co-founder of Citizen Ticket, declares that he fell into the sector “by chance”.

He now heads a business which sells to event organisers, festivals and venues, from Glastonbury and Burning Man down to small indie festivals selling fewer than 1,000 tickets, “and everything in between”.

Mr Boisseau says: “One thing led to another until we realised there was an opportunity to build a platform ourselves.”

He adds that a “deep, growing understanding of events is really the reason behind whatever success we've had”, along with “dogged perseverance”.

**What are the challenges facing the sector and market, and what could be done to overcome or address these?**

The cost of putting on events keeps rising, but organisers can't justify raising ticket prices to match. Events need scale to be properly profitable, and that's hard to achieve without eventually selling out to a multinational conglomerate. And even then, they'll only acquire the biggest, lowest-risk events and promoters. The knock-on effect is that live event content has become homogenised, because that's what sells tickets reliably. Fewer organisers are willing to push boundaries when it's a financial risk. Newness has a small, risky market; familiarity has a huge one.

One idea worth exploring with a body like Creative Scotland is a low or zero-interest loan scheme for event organisers, essentially backing people to take a punt on something new, without it wiping them out if it doesn't land. You get a small loan to put on an event; if it turns a profit, you've proven you can do it, and you unlock a bigger loan next time. Do that a few years running and you're building real scale, without having to sell to a big promoter to get there. It flips the usual funding model on its head: instead of judging ideas on merit before anything's happened, you're backing people once they've actually proven the market wants what they're doing, and growing with the ones who prove it works. It feels like it could genuinely help independents grow and scale on their own terms, rather than the only route to scale being getting bought up by a major player.
