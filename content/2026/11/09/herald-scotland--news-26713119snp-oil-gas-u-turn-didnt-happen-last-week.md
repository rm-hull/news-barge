The SNP is now worlds away from Nicola Sturgeon’s presumption against new oil and gas exploration in the North Sea.

On Thursday, John Swinney made clear that he opposes the UK Government’s plan to end new licences, prompting the inevitable accusations of yet another Scottish Government U-turn.

The comments drew praise from the most unlikely of places, including Reform UK’s Malcolm Offord.

Except there’s one problem with that narrative.

The SNP has changed its position, undoubtedly. But the First Minister did not stand in the chamber on Thursday and suddenly pull the brakes on a long-held position.

The facts are that the SNP, and Swinney, have been signalling for months that a blanket ban on new North Sea projects would not be supported.

Back in April, the SNP leader was already arguing that circumstances had changed and pointing to the consequences of importing oil and gas from overseas while Scotland continues to rely on fossil fuels.

He has repeatedly said new licensing should instead be weighted on climate compatibility targets, especially given the turmoil in the Middle East which has driven up energy prices.

His pre-election comments were hailed at the time as a change in position.

By September, there was even less room for doubt.

The Programme for Government, setting out Swinney’s legislative agenda for the coming years, explicitly backed an “evidence-led” and “case-by-case approach” to future oil and gas projects rather than an outright ban.

But the UK Government’s announcement this week forced the Scottish Government to go further.

Until now, phrases like “evidence-led”, “case-by-case” and “climate compatibility” gave Swinney plenty of room to manoeuvre without stating explicitly whether new oil and gas licences should still be issued.

The UK Government’s proposed ban changed that. Swinney’s answer is now clear. He believes new developments should remain possible, but only if they meet the tests the Scottish Government has set out.

At First Minister’s Questions, Swinney said parts of the Energy Independence Bill were supported by the Scottish Government, but he said: “We do not support the provisions of that Bill in relation to the position on granting of new licences for the exploration of new oil and gas fields.”

He again reiterated there had to be climate compatibility tests applied to proposals for new drilling.

The clarification matters. But it is not the same thing as adopting a new position.

There is also another fairly obvious reason why the SNP was unlikely to embrace the UK Government’s plans: Westminster is making the decision.

Regardless of the North Sea question, an SNP Government is always going to object to Westminster deciding what should happen to Scotland’s natural resources, so instructing SNP MPs to vote against the draft Energy Independence Bill is hardly a shock.

Last month, the First Minister said he “accepts and supports” the UK Government’s approval of Jackdaw, having previously been reluctant to give the field his explicit backing.

Rosebank is trickier. The largest untapped oil field in the North Sea remains under consideration by the UK Government and Swinney has repeatedly been pressed to say whether he supports it.

He still won’t give a simple yes or no, instead falling back on those climate compatibility tests.

And that is important. Opposing a blanket ban on new licences does not mean John Swinney has suddenly signed up to every new drilling project put in front of him.

None of that changes the fact that the SNP has travelled a considerable distance since the Sturgeon years. A presumption against new exploration has been replaced by a government now explicitly keeping the door open.

That change has been happening in plain sight for months. Thursday simply forced Swinney to be clearer about it.

It is easy to sit on the fence over new licences when the power to grant them remains at Westminster. The harder question for Swinney is just how far he is actually prepared to go in supporting new drilling.
