*(Image: Jeff J Mitchell)*This year marks the 250th anniversary of Adam Smith’s* The Wealth of Nations*. Last month, the British Academy, the Royal Society of Edinburgh and the University of Glasgow brought together economists and other scholars to reflect on Smith’s ideas and their relevance for today.

One of Smith’s insights was that economic prosperity should ultimately be judged by people’s ability to enjoy what he called the “necessaries, conveniences, and amusements of human life”.

Two and a half centuries later, that remains a useful way of thinking about economic progress. For most of us, the questions are straightforward. Is life becoming a little easier? Will wages stretch further, saving become more manageable, or buying a home feel more achievable?

Last month’s forecasts from the Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) suggest that the UK economy will continue to grow, but too slowly to deliver meaningful improvement in living standards.

One OBR projection captures the scale of the challenge. By 2029–30, real household disposable income per person, a measure of the purchasing power of household incomes after taxes and benefits are taken into account, is expected to be just 1% above its pre-pandemic level.

A decade with so little progress is a sobering prospect.

The Institute for Fiscal Studies predicts that household income growth during this Westminster Parliament will be the second weakest parliamentary performance since 1979.

Part of the explanation lies in the UK's longstanding productivity problem.

An economy can grow because more people work, because they work longer hours, or because they produce more in each hour. All can increase output, but sustained improvements in living standards depend heavily on the last of these: productivity.

Better equipment, skills, technology and working practices allow incomes to rise without requiring people continually to work more. Since the financial crisis, that source of progress has been disappointingly weak.

Britain is far from alone in facing pressures on living standards. Across high income economies, households have experienced a substantial increase in the cost of everyday essentials, particularly energy, food and housing.

OECD data shows that real wages remain below the levels seen in early 2021 – just before the post-pandemic inflation surge – in around two-thirds of their 38 member countries. Collectively these countries make up around 60% of global GDP.

But there is an important distinction between the cost-of-living shock and the UK's longer-term economic difficulties.

Higher inflation has been a global phenomenon. Britain's weak productivity performance, however, predates the recent inflation shock by more than a decade.

For Scotland, this reinforces a warning we made at the Scottish Fiscal Commission earlier this year.

Our February report on the challenges facing the incoming Scottish Parliament highlighted likely fragile living-standards growth over the next five years. Weak income growth was already an important part of the backdrop against which the new Parliament would have to make its choices.

Recent increases in energy costs add to these pressures. They also complicate the outlook for interest rates. Higher energy prices squeeze household budgets, but they can also make it harder for the Bank of England to bring inflation sustainably back to target, raising the prospect of an increase in borrowing costs.

There are limits to what governments, particularly devolved governments, can do about these immediate pressures. Neither Holyrood nor Westminster can determine global energy prices or international supply chains.

But governments can influence the conditions that support higher incomes over time.

Encouraging business investment, improving infrastructure, supporting better health and ensuring that people can develop and use their skills effectively all matter. So too does making better use of the research and innovation taking place in our universities.

None offers a quick solution. Improving productivity requires consistent policy over many years.

The immediate pressures on household budgets will eventually ease. But the more fundamental challenge is to restore the expectation that living standards will improve over time, and that today's young people can look forward to greater economic opportunities than their parents enjoyed.

Adam Smith understood that economic prosperity ultimately matters because of what it allows people to enjoy in their everyday lives. Two hundred and fifty years after *The Wealth of Nations*, that remains a useful test of economic success.

On current forecasts, it is one that Britain is struggling to meet.

*Graeme Roy is professor of economics at the University of Glasgow’s Adam Smith Business School.*
