Salmon farms in Scotland are to have their organic status withdrawn by the UK’s key certifying body due to "welfare and environmental" failings.

The Soil Association announced that it has decided to pull out of setting standards for organic salmon after an extensive review found that environmental and welfare outcomes were not meeting its “high bar”.

Chief executive of the Soil Association Helen Browning said: "When consumers see our logo on pack, they should be confident that organic principles are being safeguarded to the highest level possible within the constraints of a commercially viable system."

But, she noted, the evidence suggested that the charity could "no longer provide that assurance".

The Soil Association logo must now disappear from farmed salmon by 2028.

The charity, which has been developing and setting standards for organic salmon farming for more than two decades, has been reviewing its involvement for the last two years.

Browning outlined the reasoning behind the decision**:** “When we started working on salmon 20 years ago, we had an aspiration for an aquaculture system that could deliver the principles of organic production in the sea.

"We envisioned a multi-trophic, enriched environment which could provide the resources for a good life for salmon, with minimal negative impact on other species. However, progress along these lines has been negligible. While our standards ensure lower stocking rates and prohibit the most damaging chemical treatments, the evidence suggests that welfare and environmental outcomes are not sufficient for us to continue."

She added: “Furthermore, new welfare challenges like jellyfish blooms are emerging due to warming seas. The salmon industry is dynamic and profitable and may well be able to innovate to deal with these emerging challenges.

"But we were not confident that the issues were likely to be resolved within a suitable timeframe through our standards. There is a bigger research and development piece needed here which, as a charity, it is not our role to lead."

The decision comes in the wake of a campaign led by the Green Britain Foundation, Chris Packham, and other campaigners.

Dale Vince, founder of the Green Britain Foundation, said: "It takes courage to walk away from an industry this powerful. The Soil Association has done the right thing. It has restored the integrity of the organic label and deserves real credit for that."

The Soil Association decision leaves open the possibility that another certification body could step in to fill its role.

A spokesperson for Salmon Scotland said: “We have been assured by the UK Government that our farms will continue to be assessed against organic standards with no loss of certification. Consumers can therefore continue to buy Scottish organic salmon with confidence.”

Earlier this year, Chris Packham, Dale Vince, Guy Singh-Watson, creator of Riverford organic delivery, and Hugh Raven, chair of the Open Seas Trust and one of the architects of the Soil Association Organics standards, wrote a letter to the Soil Association in which they called for withdrawal of the certification.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21546073.jpg?type=mds-article-620)A still from The Perfect Picnic, featuring Chris Packham, part of Project Slingshot's anti salmon farm campaign *(Image: Project Slingshot)*

“Our concern," the letter said, "is not only environmental. It is reputational. We believe continued certification of salmon farms poses a growing reputational risk to the Soil Association and risks undermining public trust in the organic label ... Trust, once weakened, is difficult to rebuild

The letter included a list of criticisms of salmon farming, among them that it “continues to discharge waste directly into coastal waters, intensify sea lice pressures on wild fish, depend on feed derived in part from wild fisheries and carry risks of escape and genetic dilution of already fragile wild salmon populations”.

![Lice infested salmon at an organic salmon farm in Scotland](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/20712701.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Lice on salmon *(Image: Green Britain Foundation)*

Responding to today’s announcement, Dale Vince added: This is a huge win for our campaign. We went inside these farms and filmed what the organic logo was hiding. Then we dug into the data and the science. We built a file of evidence so strong the Soil Association could not look away and they acted.

The Green Britain Foundation is now calling for Defra “to end the inclusion of salmon as an organic product”, retailers to “stop using the Soil Association label on salmon packaging now” and RSPCA Assured to “stop certifying farmed salmon”.

Nick Measham, CEO of WildFish, said: “It is welcome news that the Soil Association has finally seen sense and decided to stop providing an organic fig leaf for the industrial salmon farming industry.

"In 2024, Soil Association Certification refused our request to disclose inspection reports for Scottish salmon farms, documents that could lift the lid on environmental and animal welfare problems routinely unfolding at supposedly ‘organic’ salmon farms.

"The Information Commissioner subsequently upheld our appeal, but the Soil Association is now challenging that decision in an attempt to prevent these documents from being disclosed.

"While we welcome today's announcement, the Soil Association must still be held accountable for its role in certifying these operations. We will continue pressing for the release of these inspection reports. The public deserves to know what was happening behind the organic label.”
