Civil servants will be trusted to report their own office attendance after the Scottish Government admitted it did not consider stricter methods of recording hybrid working.

From today, civil servants will be required to work from the office for at least two days per week, while senior officials must meet a 60% expectation.

It is a significant shift from the Scottish Government, after The Herald’s Out of Office investigation repeatedly revealed the previous unenforced target was repeatedly being missed.

The new attendance rules will be monitored through a self-reporting system, despite UK Government departments using methods including swipe-pass data, computer logins and desk booking systems to measure office occupancy, with most meeting the 60% requirement.

The Herald asked for evidence and briefing notes outlining the Scottish Government’s decision to introduce self-reporting for the hybrid working policy.

But officials said it holds no information showing that any alternative tracking methods were considered, while stating the Scottish Government had not undertaken work to determine the effectiveness or justification for self-reporting.

Officials also could not state how regularly the new attendance statistics will be made public, and said the new reporting measure will be used to "establish a baseline".

Instead, the Scottish Government said: “In terms of justification, you may be aware that the previous approach to monitoring policy compliance was carried out through the Aternity system.

“This approach did not provide a comprehensive picture of staff working patterns as it could not account for occasions where staff worked in in-person settings beyond a Scottish Government building and as a result this approach was discontinued.”

The Aternity method used IT logins by more than 9,000 core civil servants divided by the number of desks allocated to them.

The average monthly percentage in October 2025, when the hybrid working policy was first introduced for core civil servants, was 34.5% and dropped to 32.5% in January this year, when officials chose to stop recording the statistics.

Edinburgh’s Victoria Quay had an average attendance of 12% between October and January, while St Andrew’s House’s statistics sat around 22%. Saughton House was 8%.

An analysis of attendance between December and January found average core attendance of just 5% on Mondays and 3% on Fridays.

“The Scottish Government has not identified any separate documents, briefings, meeting notes or correspondence recording a further justification,” the freedom of information documents obtained by The Herald show.

Officials add that they had “not identified recorded information showing that any other tracking methods were considered”.

The Scottish Government then said it did not know how often the hybrid working data will be published, adding that times remained “under consideration”.

Justification for setting the new attendance data at 40% and 60% for senior staff was not recorded by the Scottish Government.

Officials said the threshold reflected the Scottish Government’s judgement that the existing policy needed to be strengthened to demonstrate the “clear, consistent standard of delivery the public and ministers are entitled to expect”.

The only document identified about the decision was an action from July ordering officials to develop an Oracle monitoring solution.

Separate documents obtained by The Herald reveal the changes were being discussed at the highest levels of government weeks before their introduction, with First Minister John Swinney seeking assurances in August that hybrid working arrangements were being “appropriately managed”.

The First Minister personally addressed civil servants on the announcement of the policy after it had previously been reported hybrid working expectations were causing anxiety in the workforce.

Core civil servants were told there would be repercussions if they failed to obey the new rules, which is a marked shift from the previous stance, where the Scottish Government said it could not enforce hybrid working over concerns of reaction from civil service trade unions.

The new announcement has, however, infuriated unions, with Allan Sampson, the national officer in Scotland for the FDA, stating the policy was “unworkable” and may not be able to be enforced by managers.

The Public and Commercial Services (PCS) union is also balloting staff at the Scottish Government and four of its agencies over the policy, which it claims is “unacceptable”.

The union is calling for the Scottish Government to scrap the plan, with a ballot on potential industrial action opening on Monday and lasting until December 14.

Ruby Gibson, the national officer for Scotland for the PCS union, said the Scottish Government has “provided no convincing evidence” for the 40% mandate.

She added: “Instead, the Permanent Secretary and employers have chosen to impose an arbitrary target and threaten disciplinary action against workers who do not comply.

“This is an unacceptable way to treat the people who helped keep Scotland’s public services running through the Covid-19 pandemic and have continued to deliver vital services in challenging circumstances ever since.”

A Scottish Government spokeswoman said: “Our hybrid working policy reflects the fact that in-person working is not limited to attendance at Scottish Government buildings, but can also include working with stakeholders and in communities. Building-access or IT data alone cannot therefore provide a complete picture.

“Under the revised in-person working policy, colleagues will record their working location through our existing time management system, with managers able to review information for their teams.”
