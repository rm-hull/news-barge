Senior medics at Scotland's flagship super-hospital have issued a desperate plea for help over "indefensible" conditions inside A&E, warning that chronic gridlock and 24-hour delays are costing lives.

In an extraordinary escalation, the entire Emergency Department consultant body at Glasgow's Queen Elizabeth University Hospital (QEUH) has warned that patient harm has become a "daily reality".

In a damning letter to Scotland's health watchdog, seen by The Herald, the senior doctors said staff are routinely forced to move patients out of resuscitation areas earlier than would normally be considered appropriate, and to identify patients for corridor care on wards, as the department buckles under sustained pressure.

![Picture of medics](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/16427391.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The consultants say their "day-to-day experience suggests that conditions have continued to deteriorate."

Healthcare Improvement Scotland (HIS) has raised the concerns with the Scottish Government after receiving the letter, which details a near-total collapse of confidence in health board leadership.

However, in a blow to the doctors, the Scottish Government has declined to get involved, saying it is an "operational matter" for the beleaguered health board.

The department's consultants first wrote to the watchdog in 2023, warning that patient safety was being seriously compromised, but HIS closed its probe after speaking only to senior NHSGGC executives.

HIS upheld two complaints about its handling and, in March 2024, apologised to the medics.

HIS then reviewed the emergency departments at the QEUH, Glasgow Royal Infirmary and the Royal Alexandra Hospital. In a damning report, published in March 2025, it made 41 recommendations, 30 of them for NHSGGC.

It found overcrowding and poor patient flow, and a lack of compassionate, respectful and positive leadership at all levels of the health board.

HIS is now carrying out a follow-up check on whether NHSGGC has delivered on those recommendations.

In a letter dated July 10, the consultants wrote to Eddie Docherty, HIS's director of quality assurance and regulation, to say that it had not.

They said: "More than a year has now passed since publication of the original HIS report and its recommendations, yet our day-to-day experience suggests that conditions have continued to deteriorate."

The consultants added: "The Emergency Department continues to experience sustained overcapacity and prolonged exit block.

"During periods of sustained overcapacity and inadequate patient flow, staff are forced to step down patients from resuscitation areas earlier than would ordinarily be considered appropriate and to identify patients suitable for corridor care on receiving wards, all in order to mitigate the consequences of systemic failures beyond our control."

![The Queen Elizabeth University Hospital has been mired in controversy from the start](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/11489728.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Patients' waits for admission from the Emergency Department can now exceed 24 hours the consultants say

The consultants say their daily operational handovers, which are distributed to NHSGGC executives, routinely use the terms 'unsafe department', 'gridlock', 'overcapacity', 'patient harm', 'poor patient flow', 'lack of escalation' and 'low staff morale'.

"Patient harm associated with prolonged delays, overcrowding and exit block is not only a theoretical risk but a daily reality for staff," they add. "Patients' waits for admission from the Emergency Department can now exceed 24 hours before definitive inpatient care can be provided.

"There is now validated evidence linking prolonged Emergency Department crowding and exit block with increased mortality.

"Delays to time-critical interventions, prolonged waits for assessment and treatment, and delays in providing definitive care have become increasingly common within the Emergency Department at Queen Elizabeth University Hospital. These experiences continue to cause moral injury and distress among clinicians and represent a significant patient safety issue."

Research by the Royal College of Emergency Medicine estimates that long A&E delays cause around 800 excess deaths a year in Scotland, calculating one avoidable death for every 72 patients waiting over eight hours for admission.

The most recent data from Public Health Scotland shows that in the first ten months of this year, 5,961 patients spent more than 12 hours in the QEUH emergency department—a 33% increase on the total for the whole of last year.

In the most recent week of published data, to October 25, 1,135 patients waited over four hours to be seen, admitted, or discharged, while 501 waited beyond eight hours and 223 were there for more than half a day.

The consultants say staff are working "under sustained pressure carrying the risk of system failure beyond their control".

They said: "These working conditions are unacceptable and continue to adversely affect staff morale, wellbeing, and the ability to deliver safe patient care."

A recent survey of the department's consultants gave their overall experience of working there a score of 3.5 out of 10, and confidence and trust in NHSGGC's board leadership 32 out of 100.

![Patients waiting](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/3863804.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Last week 501 A&E patients waited beyond eight hours and 223 were there for more than half a day.

After the HIS report, NHSGGC commissioned an independent review of culture and relationships in the department, led by Professor Tracy Myhill. The consultants said staff took part after being told a summary report would follow.

They said the review included a report from clinical psychologists on the damage done to staff by prolonged exposure to risk and stress, which they understand reached NHSGGC executives in December 2024.

The letter states: "However, despite repeated requests from our Clinical Director to executive management, the report remains withheld."

The letter was copied to NHSGGC chief executive Prof Jann Gardner, board chair Lesley Thomson, medical director Dr Scott Davidson, and chief operating officer Russell Coulthard, as well as senior HIS officials.

HIS's reply was emailed to the department's consultants, with Mr Docherty acknowledging their concerns. He wrote: "The issues you describe are significant, and I do not underestimate the frustration that can arise when improvements are not being realised at the pace that staff would reasonably expect."

But he set out the limits of the watchdog's role, saying responsibility for improvements rests with NHSGGC and its leadership teams. He wrote: "As such, our role is not to design, direct or implement service changes."

Mr Docherty said HIS met Scottish Government colleagues to raise the department's problems with them.

"While responsibility for addressing these matters remains with the Board, it is important that relevant national colleagues are sighted on the challenges being experienced and can consider them within their own review and oversight activity," he said.

Mr Docherty added: "We will continue to work with Scottish Government colleagues to ensure that appropriate oversight, challenge, and assurance are applied to this work as it progresses."

When asked for comment, a Scottish Government spokesperson told The Herald: "This would be for NHS GGC to respond to as it is an operational matter."

![Health Secretary Angela Constance has been urged to intervene](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/19062146.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Health Secretary Angela Constance has been urged to intervene *(Image: PA)*

Scottish Labour's health spokesperson Dame Jackie Baillie said Health Secretary Angela Constance "cannot look away from the crisis".

She told The Herald: "This is a horrifying warning about the state of our emergency departments from those who know best — the clinicians on the frontline.

"This is not the first time that chaotic and often incompetent management at the QEUH has compromised patient safety.

"The Health Secretary cannot look away from the crisis unfolding in A&E departments in Glasgow and across Scotland.

"It's time to listen to the clinicians and act to prevent unnecessary loss of life."

A spokesperson for Healthcare Improvement Scotland said it took the concerns seriously: "It is for NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde to implement the changes required and to address the issues identified. We expect the board to demonstrate progress in implementing the recommendations and responding to the concerns raised by staff."

A spokesperson for NHSGGC said: "We continue to work with our colleagues and teams to address the issues raised in their correspondence, as well as continuing to work with Healthcare Improvement Scotland as part of its independent progress review and our improvement work.

"A programme of improvement work is ongoing across the organisation to address patient flow, strengthen escalation arrangements, decrease hospital occupancy and provide more care through alternatives to hospital services.

"We continue to work with a range of staff groups to deliver these improvements and to support staff across our hospitals. NHSGGC staff and HIS are involved in this programme. This has included significant investment in staff within our emergency departments and in other services that support the department.

"NHSGGC recognises that, despite the work to date, further support is required in the Queen Elizabeth University Hospital. In recognising this, the continuation of our improvement work, including a refreshed whole system approach, is a priority.

"We recognise that some patients continue to wait too long within the Emergency Department, particularly when there are pressures on hospital capacity and delays in discharging patients who are clinically ready to leave, and improving this situation for both our patients and colleagues is really important to us.

"The QEUH emergency department, like those across Scotland, continues to be under pressure, which we know can create a difficult environment for both patients and staff.

"Patient safety remains our highest priority.

"We are extremely grateful to all our Emergency Department teams for continuing to provide care in challenging circumstances, and we are committed to working with all frontline clinicians, staff representatives, and colleagues across our whole system to address the concerns raised."
