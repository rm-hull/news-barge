Darcie Parker has now created a course about teaching deaf people how to drive

**When Darcie Parker turned up for her first day at university, she had no idea a case of freshers' flu would leave her profoundly deaf in both ears.**

The 22-year-old said she assumed not being able to hear was part of the illness, but was told by medics a week later that her hearing would not come back.

Darcie went on to complete her degree at The Academy of Contemporary Music in Birmingham, trained to become a teacher, and later passed her driving test following the challenging task of finding someone willing to teach her.

Now she has partnered with her driving instructor to create a course about teaching deaf people how to drive.

The first Driving Success course took place on Sunday, and she said staff were now making an online course as well as planning more classes.

![A selfie of a young woman with blonde hair and a nose ring in a driver's seat sat next to a man wearing sunglasses who is a driving instructor. ](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/fc69/live/494d80b0-e1cc-11f1-b8c8-67fa35f5afdb.jpg)

Dave Leverton, from Handley Driving School, made adaptations for Darcie

Darcie, from Dronfield in Derbyshire, said she went to university a year early, at the age of 17, and became deaf in the first few weeks.

"I just had freshers' flu, and it deteriorated my inner ear on both sides," she said.

Darcie added: "I assumed that me not being able to hear was just part of the illness and I didn't know it was a medical emergency, so I didn't get it checked until a week later and I still couldn't hear.

"And that's when they told me, because it's been so long, we can't get it back."

She said the condition was called sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSHL).

Darcie said it was not easy doing a music degree but she had to "find ways around things".

"So it didn't ruin in my life, it just changed how I had to do things," she added.

![A young woman and a man stood in front of a screen which says "Driving Success" in large letters.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/c53a/live/83b6cf90-e1cc-11f1-b8c8-67fa35f5afdb.jpg)

Darcie is a qualified primary school teacher with a specialism in music and sign language, and is currently supply teaching

Darcie said hearing problems ran in her family, and all the women on her mother's side were either deaf or severely hard of hearing.

After finishing her degree, she trained to be a teacher and wanted to learn to drive to get to work.

"I didn't think I was allowed to drive being deaf," she said.

However, after doing a bit of research, she found there was no legal barrier to driving when you are deaf.

The government says to drive a car or motorcycle, people do not need to tell, external the DVLA if they are deaf, and adjustments will be made, external for deaf people taking driving tests.

![A woman sat in front of a group of people with a screen behind her.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/e28c/live/8bb74da0-e1cc-11f1-b8c8-67fa35f5afdb.jpg)

Darcie says she does not wear hearing aids or have a cochlear implant but purely lip reads

However, Darcie said it was challenging finding someone who felt comfortable teaching her.

She said: "I reached out to God knows how many driving instructors, and they all said the same thing of 'I'm not confident I can accommodate that'.

"So when I contacted Dave, I didn't say anything and he was like, 'yeah, no worries. I can fit you in this day'.

"And then at the end I put, 'oh yeah, by the way, I'm deaf', and that was it - we had to work together because he'd never worked with a deaf person before."

She said they soon found ways of working around her hearing loss.

These included for the emergency stop when the instructor usually shouts "stop", he instead puts his hand out in front of him really quickly.

![A group of about 18 people holding certificates. ](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/2cd7/live/93da95f0-e1cc-11f1-b8c8-67fa35f5afdb.jpg)

Darcie said: "As long as it helps even one person, it's done its job"

Darcie said during the lessons, they realised they could help share this knowledge.

She said: "If I'm having this problem, I know that there's gonna be other people that are gonna need instructors that are deaf aware, can maybe sign a little bit.

"Me and Dave just looked at each other one day, and were like, 'we should make a course', and he goes, 'yeah, we should'.

"I created the course myself and I sent it to Dave, and he loved it."

Dave Leverton, from Handley Driving School, said he made adaptations for Darcie's lessons, including adding an extra mirror in a position where she could see him.

"[This] meant that we could use some verbal communication," she said.

"Then Darcie taught me things... simple sign language that we could manage on the move so that she knew where she was going and what she was going to do.

"It was a constant process of her teaching me so that I could teach her."

Franki Oliver, audiology manager at the Royal National Institute for Deaf People, said: "If you suddenly lose your hearing - either in one or both of your ears - getting treatment quickly is key. A sudden change in hearing is not always serious, but it could be a medical emergency.

"Sudden hearing loss can affect one or both ears, can happen instantly or over the course of a few weeks, and you might also have tinnitus or balance problems.

"Causes vary, and can include ear wax build-up, infection or physical injury. Recovery depends on the cause and how badly you're affected.

"Getting urgent treatment for sudden hearing loss increases your chance of recovering your hearing."
