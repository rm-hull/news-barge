A rare person-to-person transmission of E. coli has been detected at a California pre-school following a deadly outbreak of the bacteria.

An 18 month-old girl died and four others were infected after visiting the Brookshire Farms pumpkin patch in the city of San Luis Obispo last month.

However the sixth and most recent case had not visited the farm and is instead believed to have caught it at school, Department of Public Health announced on Saturday.

The unnamed patient has now developed hemolytic uremic syndrome, a complication associated with a specific type of E Coli infection called STEC.

![An E.Coli (picture) outbreak has left one toddler dead and led to a transmission at a California preschool](https://static.independent.co.uk/2024/06/27/14/7412c11261b1775be449c6329a0c31f8Y29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzE5NTgwODI2-2.24149995.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

​Hemolytic uremic syndrome can damage blood vessels and cause anemia, kidney failure and low platelet levels, according to the National Kidney Foundation. In the majority of cases, it usually requires medical care in the hospital and often requires dialysis treatment.

The patient is now recovering at home and is believed to have been infected via secondary transmission from one of the five other cases.

​San Luis Obispo County Health Officer Dr Christy Mulkerin says that officials are working with the preschool to keep families safe following the E. coli transmission.

​“Since the transmission is thought to have occurred at the school, families were notified, thorough cleaning protocols were initiated, and the affected classrooms will be closed on Monday,” Mulkerin said. “The preschool has been working collaboratively with Public Health.”

​The exposure period at Brookshire Farms is believed to have spanned October 16 to October 26, officials say. ​An investigation into the outbreak determined that all five people who tested positive for an STEC infection had visited Brookshire Farms on either October 17 or October 18.

​Three patients developed HUS, with a fourth being hospitalized and released.

![The outbreak has been linked to Brookshire Farms in San Luis Obispo](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/14/56/Screenshot-2026-11-17-at-14-37-57.png?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&trim=107%2C2%2C40%2C0&auto=webp)

​Testing on food, water and environmental samples at the farm did not show signs of the strain that had led to the five confirmed cases, although other strains were detected, the Department of Public Health says. ​Brookshire Farms has closed while the investigation is carried out.

“Although it’s frustrating to not be able to pinpoint the exact source of the infection through environmental lab testing, this is not unexpected, and bacterial contamination is often temporary and difficult to detect through available sampling methods,” Mulkerin said.

​Judy Wolf, the grandmother of the 18-month-old girl who died after contracting an STEC infection, told *The San Luis Obispo Tribune* that her granddaughter was hospitalized for 10 days before dying on November 4.

​“She was a loved child,” Wolf said. “We are inconsolable.”

​Officials say that E. coli infections can cause diarrhea, severe stomach cramps and vomiting. Symptoms usually appear within 3-5 days of infection, but can appear up to 10 days later.

*​The Independent* has contacted Brookshire Farms for comment.
