A major government-backed report has called for an “entirely new” benefit system to tackle the rising number of under-25s not in education, employment or training (Neet).

Alan Milburn’s review into young people and work has presented its findings, outlining how spending on welfare and employment support should be rebalanced to boost economic activity.

Speaking on Tuesday, he said: “A lost generation is not a fact of life. It is a choice. And I believe it is the wrong one.

“It is time for a new settlement that makes work not welfare the future for the next generation.”

Around one million young people are now Neet, according to the latest official statistics, with six in ten of them having never had a job.

There is a large focus on welfare reform in the former health minister’s report, which he stresses will not constitute “crude benefit cuts”.

This echoes the view shared by Andy Burnham in recent months, as the new prime minister looks to set himself apart from predecessor Sir Keir Starmer on the issue. The former Labour leader last year suffered a damaging rebellion of over 100 backbench MPs over his government’s proposals to cut welfare spending.

![Former health secretary Alan Milburn (Jeff Moore/PA)](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/11/23/2a566440602d74bdf2cb240a2c17aceaY29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzg5MjQ3NDE2-2.84769039.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

In his highly-anticipated report, Mr Milburn admits: “I am not saying this will be easy or that these changes will be without pain. They could mean some people get less financial support at the beginning of their benefit claim than under the current system.”

The wide-ranging recommendations would significantly reduce the generosity of the welfare system for only younger claimants, potentially opening the door to a showdown with campaigners and MPs wary about moving too quickly on reform.

Over the next ten years, the government would spend £5.8 billion a year on new employment schemes, the report explains – offset by £3.6 billion in direct welfare savings and £2.4 billion from higher tax receipts and lower benefit claims as more young people enter work.

By 2036/37, combined welfare cuts will reach £13.7 billion, it adds.

Responding to the report, James Taylor, executive director of strategy at Scope, welcomed increased employment support – but added that the charity is “deeply concerned” by proposals which would reduce financial support for disabled people.

Here’s what the new system would look like for young benefit claimants:

## Universal Credit scrapped for under-25s

The UK’s most-claimed benefit – Universal Credit – would be scrapped for all younger claimants under Mr Milburn’s wide-ranging proposals.

In its place would come the Youth Allowance, a means-tested benefit paid at the same rate as basic UC, but with no health top ups. A higher rate would be payable to those living independently.

![Alan Milburn, Prime Minister Andy Burnham and Education Secretary Lucy Powell during a visit to Alstom Transport in Derby in July (PA)](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/00/9d28fe439a2bad26e27f5446c4381cf6Y29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzk0ODcxMzg2-2.85437364.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The move would also remove the Work Capability Assessment for young people, which the report claims creates a “premature binary judgment about capacity for work”.

This assessment is the main pathway to claim the health-related element of Universal Credit, which is tied to a person’s ability to work.

Around 258,000 under-25s currently claim the top up.

Earlier this year, the monthly payment was cut by nearly half for new claimants, reducing it by £200, while freezing it for all others. Critics warn the measure is disproportionately hitting young disabled people moving on to the benefit for the first time.

The changes could leave many young disabled people to cover their essential costs with around £80 a week in basic entitlement, the Joseph Rowntree Foundation (JRF) has warned – the current standard Universal Credit rate for single under-25s.

## Pip also abolished for young claimants

Another major change would see the Personal Independence Payment (Pip) also removed for all under-25s, with two new benefits taking its place.

The first would be a new health and disability fund operated by the local Youth Opportunity Service, which can pay for extra costs or one-off payments to support a young person with ill health.

Alongside this, the new Additional Cost Allowance would provide non-means-tested, ongoing financial support for extra disability-related expenses.

![The number of young people not in employment, education or training is set to hit one million for the first time in more than a decade, the Resolution Foundation has warned (Gareth Fuller/PA)](https://static.independent.co.uk/2025/10/21/00/6e64df958d3695fc2f47746e4a3e6e00Y29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzYxMDQzNTY1-2.19368553.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

This is similar to the purpose of Pip now. However, the report makes clear that there would be a burden on the claimant to prove the costs that they are facing, rather than being given a flat-rate payment based on a health assessment.

For those with health conditions that will keep them out of work long-term, a new Severe and Complex Needs Insurance would pay a stable, long-term income worth more than any benefit currently being paid. Qualifying for this payment should have a “very high bar”, the report states.

Scope’s Mr Taylor commented: “Pip helps with the extra costs of disability, whether someone is in work or not. Taking that support away could make it harder, not easier, for young disabled people to get into work and live independently.”

“The Government must be ambitious about creating jobs and opportunities for young disabled people. But that ambition must not come at the cost of the vital financial support they need.”

## Benefit sanctions for not engaging

Those that do not engage with their opportunity support should see their new benefit entitlement cut or removed entirely, the report states.

This is not designed to be “punitive”, the authors write, but will prevent those who are able to participate but choose not to from claiming benefits.

It explains that “a young person who persistently refuses to engage, after attempts to understand why and after reasonable routes to re-engagement have been tried, should ultimately lose their Youth Allowance benefit.”

In a positive move for young people that do move into work, the Milburn review calls for a work allowance that will allow young people to work part-time before their benefits are reduced.

## Older adults and children could be next

Looking beyond the scope of young people, Mr Milburn’s report also presents ministers with options for how welfare could be reformed both for children and adults over the age of 25.

For older claimants, the benefit reforms outlined “could [also] be applied”, it states, adding that the government should “act with urgency” to bring forward proposals to reform the welfare system for over-25s.

Turning to child claimants, the report calls for changes to the Child Disability Living Allowance (DLA). This is a health-related benefit available from the age of 3 months to 16 years old, when most claimants (77 per cent) go on to claim Pip.

This “pipeline” is a principal reason for the growth in spending on disability benefits for young people, the report states, by “documenting what they cannot do and never asking what they could.”

To tackle this, Mr Milburn calls for Child DLA to be extended to 18, with the two extra years used to actively prepare claimants for work and adult life. Underpinning this is the assumption that the majority of these young people have barriers that can be addressed, rather than needing permanent cash.
