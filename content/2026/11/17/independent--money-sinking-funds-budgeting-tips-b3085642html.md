One challenge of personal budgeting is that your spending peaks several times a year, while your income usually stays the same.

If you haven’t prepared for these peaks, you’ll struggle to cover them.

Sinking funds help to spread your costs more evenly between each month.

You’ll need to do some planning for this type of saving, but the benefits make it well worth it.

This is exactly what they are and how to go about using them to manage your money across the year.

## What is a sinking fund?

A sinking fund is a pot of money you add to each month in anticipation of a future expense.

A typical example is Christmas (or any other festival you celebrate).

You know it’s coming every December and that you’ll need to spend more than you would in a typical month. So, you put a little of your income aside in each of the other months to cover it.

Depending on your lifestyle, you might have several sinking funds for different annual expenses. Other common examples are summer holidays and recurring car costs.

![You can have different saving pots for different upcoming expenses](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/11/54/iStock-2295232122.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

## Benefits of sinking funds

Sinking funds can help you avoid going into debt. For example, your Christmas sinking fund will cover the shortfall between your November earnings and your December spending, so you won’t need to use your credit card or overdraft, helping avoid fees or excess interest payments.

Similarly, they can keep your saving or investing plans on track. If you like to save or invest your excess income each month, sinking funds help you continue to do so by covering bigger expenses when they come along, rather than cutting the amount you put away.

While you can often pay for your car insurance and road tax in instalments, both are cheaper if paid in one go. So, paying out of a sinking fund created through the year *behind*will cost less than paying across the year* ahead* - plus you can earn interest on the pot across the course of the year you’ve saved it for.

Sinking funds might also help you stick to a budget.

With your summer holiday, you’ll decide in advance how much you can afford to spend, and you’ll have a pot to cover that exact amount. Without a sinking fund, your spending is open-ended, and you might lose control.

## What’s the difference: Sinking funds vs emergency fund

A sinking fund is different from an emergency fund.

Your emergency fund is your safety net to cover necessities when your income or spending changes unexpectedly - for example, if you were out of work for several months, you could use it to live on.

You might need to build up your emergency fund over several months, or even years. But once you have enough to cover several months of essential spending, you can stop saving (unless your spending increases).

Sinking funds are assigned to specific expenses, which might be necessities or luxuries.

You’ll add to it every month, but you’ll also intentionally spend it all periodically, then start over again.

## How to make sinking funds work for you

First, decide which expenses you’d like to cover with sinking funds. Keep this to a reasonable number; if you have more than three or four sinking funds, you might find the admin too arduous.

Then, set a target amount for each. This might be the total of your car’s insurance, road tax, MOT and servicing costs, or how much you’d like to spend on Christmas gifts. Divide that amount by 12 to calculate how much you need to pay in each month.

Choose a way to keep this money separate from your monthly spending money, which will stay in your current account (or other account you choose to spend from).

You could open separate savings accounts, or separate pots within one account (if your bank facilitates this). You could even use physical piggy banks.

Automate the payments into these pots or accounts, if you can, so you don’t need to remember them. Set the payment date soon after your usual payday so that they don’t take you by surprise later, when you might be low on funds.

Some people find it hard to resist dipping into their sinking funds. If you think you might struggle, you can put up barriers. For example, you could use a savings account that penalises withdrawals or requires notice.

It can take a while to see the benefits of this approach, and for the first few months you might miss the extra cash. But when a big expense arrives and your sinking fund covers it, you’ll hopefully be motivated to keep going.
