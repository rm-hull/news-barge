The government-backed, long-awaited report into young people and work has been released, with several of its key recommendations now revealed.

Led by former health minister Alan Milburn, the 250-page report lays out steps to tackle the rising number of young people not in education, employment or training (NEET) in the UK, which currently sits at around one million.

Sweeping changes to the benefit system and improved employment support are central to the proposals, which ministers will be expected to set into action during this parliament.

Publishing his report on Tuesday, Labour’s youth unemployment tsar said: “A lost generation is not a fact of life. It is a choice. And I believe it is the wrong one.

“Not just because it is a fiscal crisis or an economic crisis. But because it is a moral one.”

On welfare, Mr Milburn has recommended a wholesale replacement of the system for those aged under 25. This will not constitute “crude benefit cuts”, his report claims, although the far-reaching proposals will likely be met with some opposition from campaigners and backbench MPs concerned about moving too quickly on reform.

He also recommended that, over the next ten years, the government should spend £5.8bn a year on new employment schemes – offset by £3.6bn in direct welfare avings and £2.4bn from higher tax receipts and lower benefit claims as more young people enter work.

This makes the proposals virtually fiscally neutral, with a saving of just £200m a year. However, combined welfare cuts will reach £13.7bn by 2036/37, the report adds.

Outlining the need for his reforms, Mr Milburn writes that £25 is currently spent on youth benefits for every £1 on youth employment support, arguing that this should be rebalanced to £2 to £1.

Around 40,000 young people will be helped into work by 2036, the report claims, while relative poverty is reduced by 50,000 people.

Here are the key takeaways from the landmark report:

## ‘NEET zero’

Mr Milburn wants the government to commit to more than halving the rate of young people not in education, employment or training (NEETs) by 2036.

A drop from the current rate of 13 per cent to 5 per cent in 10 years from now would be “an effective rate of NEET Zero”, his report stated.

He said: “I believe we should set ourselves a new national ambition: every young person should be learning, earning or actively participating.”

He states that different young people will require different opportunities and levels of support and that a small group “will require secure long-term protection because conventional participation is not a realistic expectation”.

## Current benefit system abolished for young claimants

Both universal credit and the personal independence payment (PIP) should be replaced with new benefits for claimants under 25, Mr Milburn has said.

The changes would affect over 970,000 young people currently in receipt of at least one of the two in England and Wales.

A new “youth allowance” would replace universal credit entirely, including its health-related element. This would be paid at the same level as the basic rate, but end the work capability assessment needed to claim the health top-up.

In place of this, young claimants will be able to access a new, devolved health and disability fund, which can provide immediate payment for health-related costs without a long-term assessment.

For those who do have significant, ongoing disability-related extra costs, the new additional costs allowance will replace PIP to provide support towards these. At the same time, the “small minority” of young people who will never be able to work due to ill-health will be eligible for the severe and complex needs insurance.

The changes would be markedly less generous than current provision, adding extra hurdles – across four new benefits – to claim support on health grounds.

## Jobs for NEETs

Central to Mr Milburn’s recommendations is the creation of a new youth opportunity service that would bring together employment, career and health support for young people.

This would be a devolved initiative, with mayors and strategic authorities responsible for delivering the service in their areas. One should be established in every area by 2029, the report states.

In practice, the service will enable a young person to meet with a named adviser within 14 days of getting in touch. A participation plan would then be drawn up within four weeks, setting out “what the young person and the State will do and by when”.

After four months, a route into education, training or work should be established, and this will be a statutory duty for the strategic authority. If a young person is still NEET after six months, they are eligible for a grant.

At 12 months, the government will guarantee the young person a paid six-month job through the Jobs Guarantee, whether they are claiming benefits or not.

## Cash for jobs

Mr Milburn said he wants to see employers given more incentives to hire young people, while also committing to guarantees to do so.

To that end, bosses would be offered between £3,000 and £7,000 for hiring those who have been NEETs for six months.

The report also recommended lowering the rate of national insurance contributions for employees between 21 and 24 over a five-year period, with retail and hospitality prioritised for the tax cut.

If the policy were adopted by the government, it would be a partial U-turn on a tax hike introduced by Rachel Reeves when she was chancellor.

In return for the extra support, the government should also ask employers to do more on their part to solve the NEETs crisis and should also lead by example and hire more young people in the public sector.

## Education overhaul

Turning to education, the report calls for more support for parents to boost the number of children ready for school by age five from 75 per cent to 90 per cent, up from the current 63 per cent.

Mr Milburn also echoes calls from the prime minister to rebalance the curriculum to boost its focus on getting pupils ready for the future world of work. This comes alongside an ambition to double the number of young people achieving an apprenticeship over the decade, shifting priority away from universities.

Mr Milburn also argued that technical education should be on an equal footing with academia and further education and apprenticeships must become one of the main routes into skilled employment, with 270,000 under-25 apprenticeship starts a year by 2036 as well as a major expansion in further education for 18- to 24-year-olds.

## Health

The report warned that poor mental or physical health “should not become a one-way route out of education and work”. Young people should be able to access immediate advice and support rather than waiting for a diagnosis or assessment, it added.

Those deemed to have lower-level needs should have these addressed as soon as possible “and at the least intensive effective level”, with specialist clinical capacity “reserved for those who need it most”, it added.

He criticised a “cash for diagnosis” approach in some private providers carrying out ADHD and autism assessments, and said some young people are being failed when they get a diagnosis despite not meeting the appropriate threshold, “or when they should not get one”.

He urged the government to also take swift action on the private sector by restricting or cutting their NHS funding if they fail to meet required standards, and bringing in a new payment mechanism.
