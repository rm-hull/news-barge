The parents of a Treasury worker who took her own life last year have launched a policy that directs employers to treat staff in a kinder and more commonsense way, and are asking HR departments across the country to adopt it.

Chloe Moffat’s parents also said the government department plans to plant a memorial garden in her honour at its Horse Guards Road offices.

Moffat, a 26-year-old personal assistant, took her own life the day after she was called into a surprise disciplinary meeting about anonymous complaints alleging she had disclosed sensitive information at work, which she denied. A coroner found in August that the Treasury had “materially contributed” to her death.

She had been convinced she would lose her job, the coroner heard, despite that not being a likely outcome. Moffat’s managers declined to allow her to bring a colleague to the “informal” meeting, did not give her any advance notice of it and refused to tell her whether she was likely to lose her job. They also removed access to her director’s email and changed her work responsibilities.

She had worked at the Treasury for almost three years, loved her job and had an exemplary employment record, the coroner heard.

Her parents, Anne and David Moffat, have been working with the Global Kindness Institute, an NGO, to come up with “Chloe’s policy” for HR departments to adopt to prevent such tragic incidents from happening in future. They are calling on organisations across the country to help develop and take on the policy.

“All we want is for nobody else to go through this,” Anne Moffat said. “If it goes through HR departments, even people considering making allegations, everybody can hopefully follow the Chloe policy and ask if this is really necessary.

“So many people are so policy driven and so into disciplinaries and making people accountable when it may not be necessary. People aren’t being treated in a commonsense, human way.”

![Anne and David Moffat](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/c6f6ba73b00c4224800cad141f8361254605b93f/2381_335_3796_3038/master/3796.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

The policy calls on HR departments to consider the harms of disciplinary processes to those subjected to them, particularly when complaints are anonymous or unverified. It emphasises prioritising psychological safety and supporting those going through any disciplinary process, as well as minimising these processes in the first place by considering alternatives. It also calls on HR departments to provide clarity to those going through disciplinary processes, including letting people know if their role is safe.

David Moffat said: “It’s a powerful leadership standard. If it raises the profile of the process that Chloe went through and avoids it happening to anyone else, that is good enough for me.”

The Treasury’s response to the coroner’s prevention of future deaths report acknowledged the department’s role in her death and committed to safeguards. A review of the civil service by the Cabinet Office found that the conditions to which Moffat was subjected had been replicated in other disciplinary meetings.

Anne Moffat said: “If you think about what happened to Chloe, if HR had thought ‘we know that she was very well thought of, nothing else had ever happened, these were the first ever allegations, they were anonymous,’ was this process necessary?”

The prevention of future deaths report issued by the coroner last month found there was still a risk at the civil service due to its disciplinary procedures. The civil service has put in place multiple safeguards to address these issues. This guidance includes allowing staff to have a supporter in disciplinary meetings, giving proper notice of any such meeting, and informing staff if a dismissal is not likely. The Treasury is also hiring an on-site occupational therapist to support staff wellbeing.

The Moffats met James Bowler, the permanent secretary at the Treasury, at the end of last month. David said: “We felt listened to, and as part of their response to the coroner as well we felt reassured that change was being implemented. If the changes they have put forward to the coroner had already been in place, we certainly feel that Chloe would be with us today. It was a very emotional meeting.”

Anne added: “It is the first time we felt that the Treasury had been compassionate to us and it wasn’t defensive or legal speak. They are looking at how to remember Chloe, and looking at the possibility of a memorial garden within the building and marking the garden with a plaque.”

David said: “We feel that Chloe is finally being acknowledged and remembered.”

A Treasury spokesperson said: “The Treasury’s deepest sympathies continue to be with Chloe’s friends and family, and the department continues to mourn our colleague.

“Our response to the coroner’s prevention of future deaths report sets out the action we have taken following this tragic case. An independent review found staff acted in good faith and with a duty of care throughout.”
