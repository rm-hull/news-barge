Morning opening: Ukraine hits crucial bridge linking Russia and Crimea

Jakub Krupa

Ukraine has critically damaged the Kerch bridge linking occupied Crimea with Russia in an overnight missile attack, in what Ukrainian officials say is a symbolic blow to the Kremlin.

Gallen section of Kerch Bridge after it was hit by Ukrainian drones. Photograph: Ukrainska Pravda

Photos suggest that a 227-metre road arch span has fallen into the Kerch strait, which connects the Sea of Azov with the Black Sea. The adjacent railway arch span has also sustained damage.

Ukrainian commentators say the massive steel arch cannot easily be replaced. The closure of the bridge means that traffic from Crimea can travel out of the peninsula only via a land corridor through occupied southern Ukraine, vulnerable to Ukrainian drones.

But Russian officials, quoted by Reuters, insisted that damage was only minor, and would be quick to sort out.

“We have already gathered together the resources, designers, and builders ‌for repair work. We are sorting out logistics issues with the regional leadership,” Russian deputy prime minister Marat Khusnullin wrote on Telegram.

The bridge has in the past been a key supply route for Russian forces in Ukraine ​and is widely viewed as one of Russian president Vladimir Putin’s signature infrastructure projects.

The overnight strike followed ‌an intensification of ​Russian attacks in the ​past few months on bridges in Ukraine, including in the capital Kyiv.

Elsewhere, EU defence ministers are meeting in Brussels this morning for their latest discussions on Ukraine, followed by the Ukraine defence contact group.

Meanwhile, Germany’s chancellor Friedrich Merz is hosting Finland’s influential Alexander Stubb, two days before a major meeting of the coalition of the willing in Berlin.

They will no doubt offer their reaction to today’s events in Ukraine. I will bring you all the latest.

It’s Tuesday, 17 November 2026, it’s Jakub Krupa here, and this is Europe Live.

Swedish parliament speaker wants to see new government or snap poll called by Christmas

Miranda Bryant

Nordic correspondent

The Speaker of the Riksdag, Andreas Norlén, has said granted Ulf Kristersson’s request for more time for coalition talks (15:50) but that he wants to have clarity for the Swedish public by Christmas.

He said he would give parties one more week before he calls in the party leaders to decide what will happen next, but that he is determined to get to the end of this process soon so that by Christmas, Sweden will either have a new government or can prepare to go back to the polls with a second election.

He said:

“My opinion is that this formation of government needs to come to an end in good time before Christmas. I want that when the Swedish people celebrate Christmas they will know that Sweden either has a new government or are preparing themselves for another election.”

Swedish minister says he has 'not seen any serious intent' from Russia to negotiate Ukraine peace

Sticking to the Swedish theme for a moment, Sweden’s defence minister Pål Jonson is the latest leader to stop by the journalists upon his arrival at today’s Ukraine Defence Contact Group in Brussels.

Swedish defence minister Pål Jonson attends a meting in Brussels. Photograph: Olivier Hoslet/EPA

In his comments, he said the meeting was “timely” as “the situation in Ukraine is very challenging due to the fact that Russia scaled up its production, both of ballistic missiles and also drones and glide bombs.”

Sweden will spend €4bn on helping Ukraine in 2027, Jonson said, noting cross-party backing across the parliament.

The minister also said that Sweden signed a letter of intent with Ukraine and the UK on the upcoming delivery of Gripen fighter jets for Ukraine.

Responding to questions from reporters, Jonson also said that the US undersecretary of defence Colby did not say “specific” information on the ongoing review of the US posture in Europe.

“We are expecting it to come by the end of the year,” he said, adding that “the message is clear that Europe is going to shouldering the lion’s share of conventional deterrence by 2028.”

Asked about the peace negotiations with Russia, the Swedish minister said he “has not seen any serious intent from the Russian side” on ending the war in Ukraine, stressing the need to keep supporting Kyiv in the meantime.

Jonson pointedly disagreed with Finland’s president, Alexander Stubb, who has repeatedly called for direct talks with Russia’s Vladimir Putin.

Asked if he sees any merit in that approach, he said:“Not quite, because we do not see any serious commitment from the Russian side to making any kind of compromise.”

“I know there’s all kinds of ideas drifting around that we should also have a discussion about the European security architecture. We do not welcome that.”

Sweden's Kristersson asks parliament speaker for more time to form new government

Miranda Bryant

Nordic correspondent

In other news, after 65 days without a government, Moderates leader Ulf Kristersson has asked for more time before parliament votes on whether they want him as prime minister.

Swedish prime minister Ulf Kristersson Photograph: János Kummer/Getty Images

Two weeks after he was given the task to attempt to form a “middle” coalition government by the Speaker, he said on Tuesday afternoon that he had requested a few more days.

The Speaker will hold a press conference later this afternoon when he is expected to respond to the former prime minister’s request.

His announcement comes after the Centre party leader, Elisabeth Thand Ringqvist, asked Kristersson and the Social Democrats leader Magdalena Andersson to present a “total package” for her party to choose between after talks between the left-leaning parties failed to produce a result.

She has already said yes to Andersson’s proposal of a coalition between the Social Democrats, Green party and the Centre party with cooperation with the Left party. But the Left party has since continued to demand ministerial posts, which the Centre party has refused to accept.

Kristersson said:

“A majority government that cannot agree does not work, so nothing would get done. A few more days are needed before a vote takes place, so that the Centre party can have a complete basis for decision making which is needed to take a position.”

If the vote on Kristersson does not lead to a government, parties have just one more attempt before another election will be automatically called.

Kristersson said he does not want another election, but is “totally prepared” for one and already has a team of people preparing in case.

North Macedonia says it foiled Iran-linked attack in Greece

North Macedonia’s security agency said it had foiled a “terrorist” assassination plot targeting a person in neighbouring Greece and dismantled an Iranian-linked network, AFP reported.

The National Security Agency (ANB) said it had busted “a wide network of proxy actors” linked to Iranian Edris Jahangiri Mavaneh, who was a “collaborator of the Iranian security and intelligence services”.

The network, “under the direct instructions of the Iranian national, undertook preparatory logistical activities to carry out a terrorist act/assassination against a person in Greece”, the agency said.

The operation was led in cooperation with the Greek national intelligence agency, it added.

Four suspects have been remanded in custody and another placed under house arrest, interior ministry official Aleksandar Janev told local media.

Serbia's new deputies take seats in parliament after landmark Student List win

Guy de Launey

in Belgrade

The 140 successful candidates from Serbia’s Student List have just been sworn in as MPs at the National Assembly in Belgrade. They took the oath en masse, alongside members of the long-governing Serbian Progressive Party (SNS), which now finds itself in opposition for the first time in 14 years.

Student List deputies take seats in Serbian parliament after landmark election win

It follows the spectacular triumph of the Student List in last month’s parliamentary elections. It comfortably defeated the SNS and its populist leader Aleksandar Vučić with a slate of candidates drawn from academia, athletics and economics, among other disciplines. None of the new MPs have any political experience.

SNS deputy Aleksandar Vucic attends the inaugural session of the National Assembly. Photograph: Marko Đoković/EPA

Before the parliamentary session, Student List supporters gathered outside the National Assembly to cheer their representatives. Some of them performed the traditional kolo circle dance in celebration.

Meanwhile, there were jeers and whistles for arrivals from Vučić’s party. A woman in the crowd poured a bottle of drinking yoghurt over the head of SNS candidate Lidija Vukićević, who said she felt “very bad” about the “primitivism and vulgarity” of Student List supporters.

Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) deputy and actor Lidija Vukićević stands in front of the parliament after protesters poured yoghurt on her head during the constitutive session of the newly elected National Assembly in Belgrade, Serbia. Photograph: Reuters

Now the MPs have been sworn in, the next task is to elect a parliamentary speaker. Whoever it is become the acting president of Serbia – because Vučić resigned as head of state to make his unsuccessful attempt to become prime minister.

Biden's memoir uncovers his views on Zelenskyy, Ukraine, Macron, Brexit

Jakub Krupa

Separately, former US president Joe Biden’s memoir, Promise Me, America, got published today, and it features some interesting lines on European affairs.

Just skimming the book for now, there is a bit of interesting background on the US response to the Russian invasion of Ukraine, and his secret visit to Kyiv in February 2023, as well as some of Biden’s unfiltered views on Ukraine’s Volodymyr Zelenskyy.

“But I must admit that it could be difficult to work with Zelensky[y]. As brave as he and the Ukrainians were, they tested our patience on many occasions, demanding more armaments and money. …

Zelensky[y] could be moody and stubborn in our private conversations, and not as grateful as I would have expected. That could be frustrating, and I let him know that in no uncertain terms. Still, I refused to cut and run on the Ukrainians, who possessed a tremendous fighting spirit. And so did Zelensky[y].”

US president Joe Biden and his Ukrainian counterpart Volodymyr Zelenskyy visit St. Michael's Golden-Domed Cathedral during an unannounced visit in February 2023 in Kyiv. Photograph: Ukrainian Orthodox Church/REX/Shutterstock

In longer paragraphs on Ukraine, Biden writes he was “not blind to Ukraine’s faults,”including “too much corruption” in the country’s judicial system, “where the rich and powerful were often free to buy their way out of accountability.” “Ukraine would have to deal with these challenges if they wanted genuine integration with Europe and the West.”

“But I had also seen the inner strength of the Ukrainian people.They deserved a fighting chance. If they could create a real democracy, with checks and balances, and fair elections, they could be a bulwark in the region, and an inspiration to peoples everywhere who were trying to claw their way out of a history of repression.”

He also reveals that Donald Trump’s transition team after the 2024 elections wanted to get a permission to immediately start negotiations with Russia’s Vladimir Putin.

“During the transition, Donald Trump’s team had asked me to sign a letter empowering them to negotiate directly with Putin, even before he took office. But I refused, unwilling to give them carte blanche for what I expected would be a kind of surrender, at Ukraine’s expense.”

Elsewhere, Biden also writes about his relationships with other leaders, including first foreign trip as president, to attend a G7 summit in Cornwall in 2021, noting his “bittersweet” feelings about “saying goodbye to an old friend, Angela Merkel,” while remarking how “impressed” he was by France’s Emmanuel Macron.

“[He] enthusiastically welcomed me into the group, but he asked hard questions about the rise of populism in the United States and how it might affect our long-term foreign policy.”

“When I said, ‘America’s back,’ he responded, ‘For how long?’”

Biden and Macron talk during the G7 summit in Cornwall in 2021 Photograph: Leon Neal/AP

Writing about Brexit, Biden – who is of Irish heritage on his mother’s side – expresses his frustration that the withdrawal from the EU was “threatening to undermine the Good Friday agreement of 1998, which had done so much good in bringing peace to Northern Ireland.”

“I also disapproved of Brexit for its ultranationalist claims, and for making many promises that could not possibly be kept. I believed that the populist leaders and opportunists who backed Brexit had sold the British people a false bill of goods.”
