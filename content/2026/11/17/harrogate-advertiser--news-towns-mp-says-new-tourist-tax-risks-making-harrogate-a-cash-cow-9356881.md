Harrogate MP Tom Gordon says Harrogate and Knaresborough’s new tourist tax money "must stay local".

The Lib Dem MP says local businesses back a visitor levy on their terms but not one that, he claims, would fund a “distant mayor's pet projects” on top of a new proposed bus tax.

And, he argues, a visitor levy without this guarantee would risk using Harrogate and Knaresborough as “cash cows”.

The Harrogate and Knaresborough MP raised the matter in Parliament during last night's Second Reading debate on the Government's Visitor Levy Bill.

The new powers would let the Mayor of York and North Yorkshire, Labour's David Skaith, charge a percentage on every overnight stay.

While council leaders could object, final say rests with the Mayor alone.

"Harrogate and Knaresborough businesses tell me they support a visitor levy in principle,” said Mr Gordon.

“What they can't accept is a far-off mayor in York funnelling the money away.

"If ministers want this levy to work, every penny raised in Harrogate and Knaresborough must be ring-fenced for Harrogate and Knaresborough.

“That means dualling the Knaresborough to York line, fixing our pothole-riddled roads, and backing the town and parish councils that run festivals and events and look after our toilets and flower beds.”

This week has seen Mr Skaith launched a consultation on adding a new transport charge to council tax to fund buses.

It could raise up to £2 million a year, despite his pre-election pledge that he had no plans for extra mayoral taxes.

But Harrogate and Knaresborough’s MP believes the whole scheme risks turning his constituency into a ”cash cow” for the county.

“Harrogate cannot be used as a cash cow to pump money into other areas and a mayor's pet projects,” said Mr Gordon.

"Now he wants a bus tax on top.

“If buses are being paid for by a transport levy, where will the visitor levy really go?

“It's yet another tax on working people and the visitors who keep our local economies alive and that simply won't do.

"Local leaders can object, but the Mayor has the final word.

“That isn't devolution. Quite frankly, it's a con."

Mr Gordon also criticised the Government for publishing no impact assessment for the Bill, even though any mayor wanting a levy would have to produce one first.

He raised the Bill's offences, which carry up to 14 years in prison for fraudulent evasion, and called for the justice impact test to be published so the effect on courts and prisons is known.
