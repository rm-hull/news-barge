For Elif Eralp, it is her first major appearance in the plenary chamber as leader of the largest parliamentary group in the Berlin House of Representatives. She knows she cannot simply deliver an ordinary speech. After all, her Left Party parliamentary group, together with the Greens and SPD, has requested a debate on November 9 – perhaps the most historically significant date in German history – and the culture of remembrance. Negotiators from the Greens and SPD will spend at least another two months discussing the formation of a joint government with Eralp’s party colleagues.

It is the first joint initiative by the prospective red-green-red coalition. And Eralp delivers her first speech in the style of a Governing Mayor. With the formation of a Left-led coalition still uncertain, her rival Stefan Evers also takes the opportunity to present himself as a potential mayor.

Of the three key years associated with November 9, the daughter of Turkish political refugees begins with the November pogroms of 1938. “What followed was the greatest crime against humanity, the Holocaust,” Eralp says. But she quickly connects it to the present and directly addresses the debate around antisemitism, of which many accuse sections of the Left Party.

She deplores the many antisemitic attacks that occur “on many days of the year”, as Eralp puts it. “Today, Jewish people are afraid again,” the Left Party politician says.

### Elif Eralp: “My Pledge to Protect Jewish People Stands”

Eralp says there are acts of hostility, attacks and insults “from different sides”. Antisemitism exists across all social classes and political circles, and it must be confronted “regardless of where it comes from”.

According to Eralp, the *Mitte Study* finds that antisemitism is concentrated “clearly on the right”.

“I take it seriously when Jewish people express concerns, including about our party,” Eralp says.

“My pledge to protect Jewish people stands,” she declares, as though she were already governing from Berlin’s Red City Hall.

She pledges to promote Jewish life and Jewish culture and make them even more visible. She also wants to provide stronger support for the many people working against antisemitism and racism.

The AfD heckles her. The CDU does not applaud.

When Eralp criticises the CDU, she drops her stateswoman-like demeanour. CDU members of the Saxony-Anhalt state parliament had voted for a “fascist president of the state parliament”, she says. In the Bundestag, the CDU/CSU had voted alongside the AfD to pass an anti-immigration motion.

She calls on the CDU to engage in “critical self-reflection”.

She also mentions the fall of the Berlin Wall on November 9, 1989: “The fact that I am speaking here for the Left Party would be unthinkable if my predecessors had not broken with Stalinism.”

Eralp says this is why commitment to human rights and to freedom of assembly and expression is so important.

She also addresses the founding of Germany’s first republic on November 9, 1918.

“The history of the Weimar Republic teaches us that democratic forces must never become irreconcilably opposed to one another,” Eralp says.

“I hope that together we can overcome the divisions in our society.”

Eralp emphasises her determination: “Many Berliners place their hopes for a better and fairer Berlin in me and our party. That is precisely why I am carrying on.”

### Stefan Evers: “What We Have Been Witnessing for Weeks Leaves Me Stunned”

After Eralp, Stefan Evers takes the podium. The Senator for Finance and Culture speaks in his capacity as leader of the CDU parliamentary group.

He begins with 1989. The sight of Germany’s black, red and gold flag on the Berlin Wall still gives him “goosebumps”, says the Christian Democrat, who continues to harbour hopes of becoming Governing Mayor after all.

He then connects this memory to a current controversy. According to Evers, the flag of German unity had been torn down from the Reichstag and a Palestinian flag raised in its place.

“An image of shame, an image of contempt,” Evers says.

The Left Party does not applaud.

Evers also turns his criticism towards the AfD. By describing remembrance of the Holocaust as a “cult of guilt”, the party “mocks the victims and the foundations of our free democratic order”, the CDU politician says, earning applause from the Left Party as well.

He then targets the Left, lamenting that “people even within parties represented in this chamber have difficulty” describing Hamas’s massacre of Israelis “as what it really is: hate-filled terror”.

“What we have been witnessing for weeks leaves me stunned,” Evers says, slipping into the role of opposition leader.

Evers says prospective coalition partners are spending hours negotiating how to deal with antisemitism and organised crime.

“There is only one possible position on that,” Evers calls out.

“Consequences are announced, decisions are postponed. A personnel decision is supposed to solve, at some point, a problem that extends far beyond one individual,” the CDU leader says, referring to the debate surrounding Left Party parliamentarian Vanessa Emde, who is absent from the plenary chamber that day.

“Antisemitism will only disappear if there are consequences.”

Israel’s right to exist must not be called into question, Evers says. Protecting Israel is an “obligation arising from November 9”.

With Eralp’s earlier speech in mind, Evers makes his point clear:

“You don’t demonstrate conviction when it wins you applause, but when standing by your principles has consequences.”
