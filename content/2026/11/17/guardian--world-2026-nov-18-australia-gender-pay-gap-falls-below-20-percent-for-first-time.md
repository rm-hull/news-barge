Australian women earn 80c for every $1 men earn, on average, after Australia’s gender pay gap fell below 20% for the first time in 2025-26.

The workplace gender equality agency’s (WGEA) report on Wednesday showed men still dominated top-paid positions but businesses’ decisions have narrowed the gap.

WGEA chief Christine Castley said most Australian employers were ignoring backlash to diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) and improving their hiring and promotion to ensure women reached senior positions.

“Employers are actually thinking in a very deliberate way about what’s going on with workplace gender equality and then taking action,” Castley told Guardian Australia.

The overall WGEA pay gap was 19.7% for the year, with the 2025-26 survey including federal government public sector employers for the first time.

The figure has improved significantly in the last decade, from 26.3% in 2015-16. Coverage has also increased, now covering more than 6m employees across 9,096 employers.

The private sector’s pay gap fell from 21.1% last year to 20.3% this year, as women accounted for a growing share of full-time management and leadership roles.

Bigger pay rises in female-dominated industries, including retail, aged care and childcare, also helped narrow the gap.

However, women-dominated industries and occupations were still typically paid less than those dominated by men. The average woman still earned $27,467 less per year than the average man, WGEA found.

The survey accounted not only for wages but all earnings, including penalty rates, superannuation, bonuses and overtime. Average total remuneration in the survey was $125,568.

The overall distribution of pay did not improve from 2024-25. Women make up about half of Australia’s workforce but men remained overrepresented in the highest-paid positions.

Every level of management was dominated by men, WGEA found. The disparity was greatest at the chief executive level, where three in four CEOs were men and the gender pay gap widened to 27.6%.

Women accounted for just 36% of employees in the top pay quartile in the private sector. In the commonwealth public sector, women’s representation in the top quartile was 46% and the gender pay gap was just 7.8%.

Promotion rates are similar between genders but women are less represented at higher levels, likely because they resign or take time off for care and domestic responsibilities, previous WGEA work has found.

Wednesday’s report found just 7% of manager roles are part-time, but 29% of women work part-time compared with 12% of men. WGEA called for workplaces to redesign roles to allow more part-time work.

In the financial services sector, which has the worst pay gap at 26.7%, men would be more likely to be promoted or get bonuses as they took on more visible work, Castley said.

“[It’s] the person who can attend the client functions [and] can be available 24/7, the person who’s most visible to the leadership of the organisation, versus, perhaps, the person [doing] the work that’s behind the scenes,” Castley said.

Superannuation provider Rest was an outlier in the finance industry. Its pay gap has fallen to 9.4%, from 20.1% in 2022.

Rest’s chief people officer, Amy Murrell, said the improvements had been helped by extending paid parental leave to all permanent staff, regardless of gender, time of arrival or primary or secondary carer classification.

“It’s not going to happen naturally,” Murrell said. “I think you have to prioritise it for it to make sense.”

The firm had also increased representation after setting targets for at least 40% of employees to be women and 40% men at every level of its business, from its board to its investment team.

Castley said some Australian business leaders had taken on an “anti-DEI” narrative, amid global backlash to diversity, equity and inclusion measures, but most businesses were “getting on with the job”.

WGEA asked businesses to set new targets for the first time in 2026. The most popular goals included improving gender balance at junior and managerial levels and consulting employees on gender equality efforts.

“These are the real issues that need to be tackled … and it’s actually very reassuring that those are the targets that employers have picked because it demonstrates, I think, a very real commitment to actually taking action,” Castley said.
