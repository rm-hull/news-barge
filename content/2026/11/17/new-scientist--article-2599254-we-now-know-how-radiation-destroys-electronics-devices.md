Researchers are gaining a new understanding of exactly how radiation destroys electronics devices. The work is a step towards building more resilient electronics for use in nuclear fusion experiments or in space.

Cristyan Quiñones at North Carolina State University stayed up for nearly three days so he could watch a beam of heavy ions destroy an array of electronics devices. It was a high-stakes experiment: few labs around the world operate heavy ion beams and securing access to run experiments is difficult. But it is only with experiments like this that researchers can systematically study the dangers that electronics devices would encounter on board a satellite or near a nuclear reactor.

In those challenging high-radiation environments, electronics devices can dramatically and rapidly fail during what is called “single-event burnout” (SEB). Thanks to their experiments, Quiñones and his colleagues have now zeroed in on exactly how SEB happens.

The researchers focused on electronics components called diodes that they built from aluminium nitride, a semiconductor made from aluminium and nitrogen. Similar diodes are used to help power electronics devices, and aluminium nitride has been put forward as a promising foundation for the next generation of such power components.

Researchers have also theorised that aluminium nitride is resilient to radiation exposure, which would make it a good choice for use in high-radiation environments such as space. But this idea has not been thoroughly investigated in experiments, says team member Ramón Collazo, also at North Carolina State University.

![optical microscope images of an irradiated diode before and after an SEB event](https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/SEI_320972327.jpg?w=840)

In the new experiment, which also involved researchers from NASA, energetic ions of silver, gold or the rare earth metal praseodymium hit and entered each aluminium nitride diode, and the researchers tracked how much electric voltage the diode could then sustain before SEB destroyed it.

Through data analysis and computer simulations, they found that each ion caused a disturbance within the diode, which, combined with the voltage, created a localised electric field. At some threshold voltage, that field became strong enough for nearby electrons to flock towards it, and this avalanche of electrons onto the small area led to a miniature explosion.

Microscope images from the experiment clearly showed tiny, melted craters in the aluminium nitride diode where SEB happened in this way. Additionally, comparing aluminium nitride to similar semiconductors, the team determined that it could withstand higher electrical fields before exploding, but relatively low voltages. Such a systematic and careful comparison had not been done before, but will inform how the material is used going forward.

Understanding SEB is important because it is among the biggest challenges for making electronics reliable in space, says Ronald Schrimpf at Vanderbilt University in Tennessee, who wasn’t involved with the experiment. He says that power systems are often the most vulnerable part of an overall spacecraft, with phenomena like SEB occurring even when there are physical shields to protect electronics, so studying proposed power device materials that are potentially more resilient, like aluminium nitride, is timely. “If your power supply goes down, nothing works, right?” says Schrimpf.

While radiation-resilient electronics are important in nuclear engineering and a variety of other settings, including the technology used in radiation therapy for cancers, they have recently gained more attention because of proposals to put data centres into space. But Schrimpf says that making electronics ready for space is a complex scientific problem that also involves logistical challenges because the facilities for testing electronics materials, such as the ion beam in the new experiment, are scarce. For Collazo, uncovering physical processes such as the electron avalanche in aluminium nitride is key for innovation in this area.

“Cristyan is telling us the physics of what is happening. You can now design the device around it because you know what is actually causing the problem,” he says. The researchers are now working on developing design principles based on what they have learned about SEB so far, says Collazo.
