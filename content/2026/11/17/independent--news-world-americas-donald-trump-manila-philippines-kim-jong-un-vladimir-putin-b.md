President Donald Trumparrived in the Philippines on Tuesday, where he was scheduled to meet regional partners, talk trade, and seek a break from a series of setbacks at home, ranging from a major disruption to the U.S. aviation industry to a scandal that has shaken the global AI industry.

Manila, the Philippine capital, is Trump’s first stop on a three-day trip that will later take him to China for historic three-way nuclear talks with Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin, and, according to Trump, potentially for a meeting with North Korea’s leader Kim Jong Un.

Trump administration details its $50 billion request for aviation system improvements

The Trump administration has formally updated its request to lawmakers for $50.1 billion to modernize the nation’s air traffic control system and to make other improvements at airports.

A letter to key lawmakers dated Tuesday says that less than 24 hours after the administration’s request of $30.1 billion, “the urgent need for this investment became undeniable.” That’s when outdated equipment disrupted aircraft communications and radar feeds, forcing the Federal Aviation Administration to initiate a nationwide ground stop.

The request to lawmakers calls for $10.1 billion for updating the nation’s air traffic control system. Another $10 billion would go to boosting airport infrastructure.

Meanwhile, $10 billion would go to replace or refurbish terminals at high-impact hubs. Another $10 billion would go to construct five new traffic control centers and $10 billion would go to improving or replacing up to 140 FAA-owned air traffic control towers.

Trump’s pick to lead the Navy focuses on shipbuilding in his opening statements

Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao told senators at his confirmation hearing Tuesday that he’ll focus on revitalizing shipbuilding in an effort to expand the nation’s fleet of warships.

But Cao avoided potentially thorny topics that are likely to come up before the Senate Armed Services Committee. Those include the Iran war, the recent firings of Navy admirals and the waiver Cao will need from Congress to become Navy secretary because he left active duty less than seven years ago.

Cao also spoke of his biography, which includes fleeing Vietnam with his family right before the fall of Saigon in the 1970s. He also focused on his 25 years of service in the Navy.

“I spent my entire adult life serving in the United States Navy special operations community with multiple combat deployments to Iraq, Afghanistan and Somalia,” Cao said.

Bipartisan concerns arise over forces being ‘stretched thin’ during Navy secretary hearing

Despite deep divisions over the Iran war, the Republican and Democratic leaders of the Senate Armed Services Committee voiced a unified concern over the impact the ongoing military operation and internal issues at the Department of Defense have had on the U.S. Navy.

“Our sailors and Marines are our greatest asset, but they cannot sustain an increasingly demanding operational tempo without adequate support,” GOP Sen. Roger Wicker, the committee chair, said during his opening statement.

His Democratic counterpart, Sen. Jack Reed, echoed his worry, saying that “disruption” from high-ranking officials, including Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, “sends the wrong signal to our sailors and Marines.”

Confirmation hearing for Trump’s nominee to lead the US Navy gets underway

Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao, a Trump loyalist and combat veteran, appeared Tuesday before the Senate Armed Services Committee as he seeks the permanent top civilian job during a particularly volatile time in Washington. It’s the first step in a nomination process that could turn into a fierce debate over the Iran war, military transparency and the well-being of American sailors.

Cao also faces the extra hurdle of needing a waiver from Congress, because he left active-duty service less than seven years ago.

Trump could meet Imelda Marcos during summit in Philippines

Officials are arranging a possible brief reunion between Trump and an old acquaintance — Imelda Marcos — if she’s well enough and gets her doctors’ nod.

Now 97 and using a wheelchair, the former jet-set Philippine first lady and mother of the incumbent president partied in New York decades ago in her heyday as the controversial wife of a strongman leader, who was to be ousted in a pro-democracy 1986 “People Power” public uprising.

But the friendship with Trump endured, with Trump often asking President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. and other Philippine officials: “How’s Imelda?” Philippine Ambassador to Washington Jose Manuel Romualdez told The Associated Press.

“Personal connections are very important to him,” Romualdez said.

When the Philippine leader met Trump in the White House last year, he carried a note from his mother that thanked the U.S. president for his strong backing of the Philippines. “President Trump was very happy and told Marcos, ‘Please say hello to your mother,’” according to Romualdez.

US and Thailand sign joint statement affirming terms of forthcoming trade deal

At a brief signing ceremony in Manila, Trump said Thailand has agreed to eliminate all tariffs on U.S. exports and remove “substantial” nontariff barriers on American automobiles, medical devices, pharmaceuticals, ethanol and poultry.

Trump said Thailand will also be buying an estimated $2.6 billion in additional U.S. agricultural products and “that makes our farmers very happy.”

Thai Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul said there’s still “a little more to do” before the agreement itself is finalized and that it should be “ready for us to sign real soon.”

Trump still talking about possible meetings with Russia, North Korea leaders

Trump says North Korea’s leader would like to meet with him in China later this week. And he’s open to sitting down with Russian President Vladimir Putin, too.

But so far, it appears neither meeting has been arranged.

Trump landed in the Philippines on Tuesday for talks with Asia-Pacific leaders. On Thursday, he’s due in Shenzhen, China, for more talks, including a historic three-way confab with Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping.

“We’ll see what happens,” Trump said in Manila when reporters asked about North Korean leader Kim Jong Un.

As for Putin, Trump said he’ll take that meeting “if it’s available.”

Trump and Xi are scheduled to meet separately.
