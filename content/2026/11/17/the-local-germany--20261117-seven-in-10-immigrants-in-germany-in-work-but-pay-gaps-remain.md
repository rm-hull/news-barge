Seven in 10 immigrants in Germany aged 15 to 64 now have a job, new statistics show. This represents a 13-point jump from 57 percent in 2005.

The figures come from the Federal Statistical Office (Destatis) and feature in Germany’s latest integration report, a biennial report by the German Centre for Integration and Migration Research.

The same figures show that people without an immigrant background have moved up too, from 68 to 81 percent over the same time period.

Of the 52.7 million people in Germany aged 15 to 64, 12.8 million (24 percent) are immigrants. Another 3.2 million are the children of two immigrant parents, and 2.5 million have one immigrant parent.

The steepest climb is among the children of immigrants. The employment rate among people with two immigrant parents went from 47 to 70 percent. Among people with one immigrant parent it went from 48 to 72 percent.

One major reason for this is simply the passage of time. The average descendant of immigrants in Germany was 12.8 years old in 2005 and is 22.2 now, which means that a far greater number have reached working age.

**A bigger gap for women**

The gap is wider among women than men. Some 76 percent of immigrant men are in work, against 62 percent of immigrant women.

Compared with people without an immigrant background, immigrant men are 7 points behind (76 against 83 percent). For women the gap is 17 points (62 against 79 percent).

**The pay gap**

Getting a job isn't the same as getting equal pay. The official figures compare people in full-time jobs only, so the gap isn't down to immigrants working fewer hours. The pay figure also includes bonuses, holiday pay and other extras, before tax and social security contributions are deducted.

On that basis, immigrants aged 17 to 64 earned an average of €3,494 gross. That is 16 percent less than the €4,165 for people without an immigrant background.

Their children, meaning people with two immigrant parents, earned €3,676, which is 12 percent less. People with one immigrant parent earned €3,997, 4 percent less.

The report says part of the difference is because immigrants are more often in lower-skilled jobs. But the figures don't reveal how much of the gap that explains. They also don't demonstrate that anyone is being paid less for identical work.

**Job security**

Everyone in Germany worries less about their job than they did 20 years ago. Among immigrants, the share with no job worries rose from 28 to 55 percent, but among people without an immigrant background it went from 38 to 71 percent.

Interestingly, people with one immigrant parent are the least worried, at 74 percent.
