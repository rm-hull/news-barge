## Europe urges households to be self-sufficient for at least the first three days of a crisis. Citizens should have enough food, water, and medicine, keep evacuation bags ready, and have emergency plans to face natural and human-induced disasters.

EU Commissioner for Preparedness, Crisis Management and Equality Hadja Lahbib presented the EU's new emergency preparedness guidelines on November 3.

The guidelines aim to make crisis preparedness part of everyday life, ensuring households can meet their basic needs for at least the first 72 hours when emergency services are stretched.

They cover everything from keeping emergency supplies and cooperating to making decisions under stress, teaching preparedness in early school education, and communicating risks.

"These guidelines have been developed with EU Member States and Union Civil Protection Mechanism (UCPM) Participating States. This has been a collaborative process, including workshops and consultations", Lahbib explained.

The move comes after a wave of cyberattacks, drone attacks, sabotage of critical infrastructure, and natural disasters, which can cause disruptions to communications, transport, and energy networks, leaving entire communities paralysed.

### Why now?

Threats take many forms, and some are close to home. Though the European Commission considers full-scale war unlikely, the EU's proximity to Ukraine poses a vulnerability. The bloc faces Russia's ongoing aggression in Ukraine, instability in the Middle East, and climate-driven floods and wildfires, alongside a surge in sabotage, drone incursions and cyberattacks.

The Commission adopted its Preparedness Union Strategy in March 2025 to shift Europe from reactive crisis management to proactive defence. But the guidelines gained importance in late 2026 following a string of hybrid incidents across Europe.

In late October, hackers remotely compromised internet-connected fridges across dozens of supermarkets in seven EU countries: Belgium, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Sweden, Finland, Estonia and Ireland. They switched cooling units to "defrost" mode, raising temperatures and ruining tonnes of food. A similar attack earlier in October hit roughly 700 supermarkets in France. French authorities attributed it to hackers from Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps.

Beginning on November 3, a week-long wave of anonymous bomb threats targeted at least 20 European airports, over 35 commercial flights and public broadcasters. Although all were hoaxes, they forced emergency landings, fighter jet escorts and terminal evacuations in Belgium, France, Italy, Ireland, Portugal and Denmark. Authorities in Ireland and France investigated possible foreign interference, concluding that the aim was to exhaust emergency resources and sow public panic.

Throughout 2026, EU officials also reported suspected sabotage and drone incursions near critical energy and transport networks, including the discovery of an explosive-laden drone at Germany's Leipzig/Halle Airport in August 2026.

### How you should prepare

The new rules focus on ensuring households can meet their essential needs for at least the first three days, helping them manage until emergency aid is deployed. This includes having enough food, water, and cash, as well as practical skills such as cooking without electricity and first aid. Households should have emergency plans and evacuation bags containing important documents and medicines, ready to grab if evacuation becomes necessary.

The goal isn’t to create a single emergency kit for all of Europe, but to help national governments provide clear guidance on how to best prepare for emergencies.

"There is no one-size-fits-all emergency kit. Everyone should tailor their emergency kit to their individual needs, environment and geographical location. Everyone should use common sense and sound judgement when adapting what to include", Lahbib said.

The rules also encourage national authorities to use surveys to regularly assess whether households understand risks, have taken practical steps, and can meet essential needs.

Age-appropriate safety education should be embedded in pre-primary and primary schooling, helping children develop the knowledge and skills to recognise risks, follow warnings, call 112 and provide basic first aid. Preparedness can be taught through everyday teaching, play and routines, without imposing extra burdens on schools or causing fear or anxiety.

Preparedness must be inclusive and accessible so everyone can understand risks, access information, and act safely. Evacuations, transport, and shelter must account for different accessibility needs and involve people with disabilities in planning. First-aid responders should receive training to develop the knowledge and skills to communicate effectively, recognise specific support needs, and provide accessible assistance and protection.

Public authorities should share potential risks and threats in ways people can understand, trust, and act on, providing citizens with timely, reliable, and accessible information before, during, and after emergencies. This can include using appropriate communication channels tailored to local contexts or socio-demographic groups, addressing disinformation, and linking information to clear actions.

### Why 72 hours?

The first three days after a crisis or disaster are crucial.

“Seventy-two hours is roughly how long authorities need to assess the damage, mobilise, and reach people who can’t help themselves […]. After day three, people should expect organised support to become available”, explained Ieva Birka, lead researcher at the University of Latvia Baltic Individual Crisis Preparedness Barometer.

This timeframe is drawn from a report titled “Better Together” (2024) by Sauli Niinistö, former President of the Republic of Finland, which calls on communities to be self-sufficient during various types of emergencies.

The first 72 hours are when emergency services are under the greatest pressure, searching for survivors in collapsed buildings and restoring communications and energy networks. Self-sufficiency can ease the burden on emergency services, allowing them to “prioritise casualties and those most at risk”, Birka explained, but it “can’t replace professional help. No household preparedness measures can replace rescue, firefighting or trauma care”.

But this timeframe should be used as a rule of thumb, as pressure varies by crisis type. “In a flood or storm, demand peaks early with rescue and evacuation, then shifts to recovery. A cyberattack on critical infrastructure works differently. Pressure can build slowly and last a long time”, Birka told Euronews.

The guidelines recommend being self-sufficient for longer. “72 hours is a minimum, not a ceiling. In a long blackout, a hybrid campaign or a war, day four can look like day one”, Birka told Euronews.

Armed attacks are the crisis most likely to require long-term self-sufficiency because “infrastructure is targeted on purpose, the disruption lasts, and access for responders may be blocked”, she explained. Cyberattacks also call for long-term preparedness, as “failures cascade from one system to the next, and nobody can say when services will be restored. Natural disasters in Europe are usually local, so help can come from unaffected areas nearby.”

### From recommendation to implementation

EU preparedness guidelines are non-binding, and implementation largely depends on national governments, creating differences across the bloc.

"The EU cannot guarantee that citizens are ‘equally prepared’. That is the responsibility of each Member State," Lahbib told Euronews.

She added that the guidelines could help bring countries closer together through practical advice, good practices and checklists to identify where preparedness needs strengthening.

Finland, Sweden, Denmark, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania already have mature systems, such as Finland's 72 tuntia (72 hours) programme, which helped inspire the EU's. Sweden distributes crisis booklets to every household, while Estonia advises citizens to prepare for up to a week without outside help.

Germany, Poland, the Czech Republic and Romania were also pushed into action by their proximity to the war in Ukraine. Germany advises citizens to stock up on 10 days' worth of supplies, while the other three countries have introduced 72-hour preparedness programmes.

Lahbib said the EU developed its guidelines in consultation with member states, allowing governments to share experiences and learn from one another.

"Member States have been involved throughout. They have made valuable contributions," she said, highlighting the sharing of good practices.

However, countries like Belgium, France and other Western and Southern nations lag behind. According to the Centre for European Policy Studies (CEPS), public readiness is lower in these regions.

Preparedness also raises affordability concerns. Not every household can afford emergency supplies, potentially leaving low-income communities more exposed during crises.

"Being prepared does not have to be expensive. Simple steps can make a real difference," Lahbib told Euronews, citing family emergency plans and essentials such as food, water, medicines and a torch.

This is a European problem, as the bloc shares interconnected power grids, transport networks and supply chains. Uneven preparedness creates cross-border vulnerabilities, potentially straining the EU Civil Protection Mechanism if blackouts, cyberattacks or extreme weather hit multiple countries simultaneously.

Emergency services could become overwhelmed and draw on shared European reserves like rescEU. Panic buying in unprepared countries could also spread across the single market, triggering shortages in neighbouring regions.

Unprepared countries could also become weak links in the EU's wider strategy, allowing adversaries to exploit vulnerabilities and maximise disruption.

But preparedness is not just about governments and emergency services. It also depends on citizens supporting one another during crises.

"Preparedness is not just about what you have at home. Practical skills, previous experience, strong social networks and support within communities can make a real difference," Lahbib told Euronews.

"This is not about replacing the authorities’ role," she added. "It is about building a culture of community and shared responsibility: we are all in this together, and we all have a role to play in helping ourselves and each other to the best of our capacities when a crisis hits."
