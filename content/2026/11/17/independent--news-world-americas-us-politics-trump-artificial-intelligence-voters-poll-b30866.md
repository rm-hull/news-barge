Few Americans approve of President Donald Trump’s approach to artificial intelligence, according to new polling, as concerns about the technology cut across party lines.

Just 26 percent of registered voters approved of Trump’s handling of AI in the latest Reuters-Ipsos survey, conducted earlier this month, while 56 percent disapproved. Only 16 percent said the administration has “AI risks about right.”

“The American people, simply put, don’t believe he has the right priorities, that the Trump administration has the right priorities,” CNN chief data analyst Harry Enten said Tuesday while reviewing the findings.

Enten also pointed to a recent CNN survey showing that Americans in both parties are more likely to say AI will hurt society than help it.

The poll comes as Americans' fears about AI grow. While officials haven’t connected a recent string of tech troubles - such as Apple, flight traffic and banking systems going offline - to AI, that hasn’t stopped some from speculating they are connected. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said Tuesday that if the recent airline ground stop was connected to an AI attack, he would let the public know.

![Few Americans approve of President Donald Trump’s handling of artificial intelligence, according to new polling](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/19/2305048871..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

In the recent poll, 66 percent of Democrats said AI will hurt society, compared with 17 percent who said it will help. Republicans were less pessimistic, but still expected harm more than benefits: 47 percent said it will hurt society, while 33 percent said it will help.

“Trump really isn’t listening to his own base on this issue,” Enten said. “He is dismissing the concerns of his own base. That might have been why there was depressed Republican turnout in this midterm election because we’ve seen it on issue after issue.”

Trump has largely favored a light-touch approach to AI regulation as the technology advances rapidly and the U.S. competes with China for dominance in the field.

“The only control or ‘guardrails’ that AI needs is a STRONG AND SMART (High IQ!) PRESIDENT,” Trump wrote on Truth Social in September. “And the U.S.A. has that, in spades!”

The same month, he rebranded AI as “super intelligence” and reached an agreement with tech leaders to adopt voluntary safeguards and police themselves — a commitment he described as “morally binding.”

“I think I'm seeing tremendous self-policing, and they understand that they have to self-police,” the president told reporters, while standing alongside SpaceX CEO Elon Musk, Facebook CEO Mark Zuckerberg and other tech titans. “This is going to be all for the good, and we're going to have it be nice and safe.”

The voluntary agreement came amid warnings from some of the industry’s own leaders about AI’s potential harms.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei warned last May that the technology could eliminate half of all entry-level white-collar jobs within one to five years. In September, current and former employees of Anthropic said they believe AI could potentially kill all humans within the next 10 years.

Earlier this month, a scandal involving one of AI’s most prominent leaders also spilled into the White House, when Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang resigned as CEO amid scrutiny of his affair with a woman alleged to be a Chinese spy. The woman allegedly helped facilitate shipments of Nvidia chips — used to train and run AI models — to Chinese firms.

The president was reportedly warned earlier this year about Huang’s ties to the suspected spy. In recent days, he has called the Nvidia co-founder “honorable” and said he believes Huang was unaware of the connection.

On Monday, the Trump administration issued new rules to restrict remote access to advanced U.S. computing chips and servers
