# How mayors like me can deliver Alan Milburn's vital youth employment plans: Tracy Brabin

## Across the country, almost a million young people who are neither earning nor learning – as well as their parents and grandparents – lie awake at night worried about their futures.

“Who is there to help me get a job, or back into education, or onto a training course?” Too often, the answer is “no-one”. After walking through the school gates for the final time, most 16 year-olds head to sixth form, college or an apprenticeship. The law states they must all stay in education or training until they’re 18. Yet some drift out of sight and, too often, nobody notices until they’re 21 or 24 – by which time the gap in their CV has become a chasm.

A total of one in eight 16 to 24 year-olds are not in education, employment or training (NEET). Six in ten have never had a job and, the longer it takes, the less likely it becomes. 84 per cent say they want to be in work or education – and I’ve seen enough Yorkshire grit in our young people to know that’s true. The problem is a system that nobody owns.

I’ve spent the past year on Alan Milburn’s expert panel for his review into NEETs. His interim report described what I hear from council leaders, colleges and employers – a system where “responsibility is dispersed and accountability is absent”. Part of the answer must be to put someone in charge, as close as possible to the young people who need help. Here in West Yorkshire, we’ve already started that work.

![The Mayor of West Yorkshire Tracy Brabin](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOjM4ZDhlOGVmLWMzNmEtNDMyNC05YWExLWRjODE5NzUzYmZkOTpkNWJiMGNiYi0zYjVlLTRiYmUtYWE2NS1jZTZhOTRkZTM3NTc=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)


When the adult skills budget came from Whitehall to West Yorkshire in 2021, we spent it on the people the national system had long failed to see.

When people on low incomes were being excluded, we extended free training to those earning below the real living wage.

When our bus network was short of drivers, we funded the training, and 92 per cent of learners went into work.

And when West Yorkshire Police flagged a gap in support for women who had committed low-level offences, we built a course taught by and for women, with childcare and travel covered. Six months on, 91 per cent of those we tracked had not reoffended.

No single Whitehall department could have designed these courses, because the effort cuts across skills, work, transport and policing. It takes a mayor, with powers spanning all four, to join the dots.

The same goes for health. Milburn found that 44 per cent of young people who are NEET have a health condition that limits them. So through Healthy Working Life – our £37 million partnership with the NHS – we’re putting employment support where people already go, from their GP and dentist to their hospital and community hub. The programme will help 2,000 young people with health conditions get closer to earning or learning.

Councils matter too. Bradford is the youngest city in the country, and last month the Children’s Commissioner named it the third hardest place in England to grow up after Hounslow and Middlesborough. Yet its share of 16 and 17 year-olds who are NEET has fallen from 6.8 per cent in 2023 to 4 per cent, well below the national average. It did this by tracking pupils at risk of dropping out while they were still at school, making sure a college place was waiting for them.

Where you grow up shouldn’t decide where you end up. As a council estate kid on free school meals in Batley, I learnt how much the basics matter – food in your belly, a roof over your head, a library full of free books. Without them, opportunity – unlike talent – gathers in some places and not others.

This isn’t just about a fairer society but a wealthier one, too. Milburn’s interim report put the cost of all this lost talent at £125 billion a year. A young person shut out of work and learning can lose up to £300,000 in lifetime earnings – money that should be in their pockets, spent on our high streets, and paid back in tax.

That’s why West Yorkshire has just been awarded the national Regional Growth Award by the Learning and Work Institute. Three in four of our adult learners are unemployed, and more than four in ten come from our most deprived communities.

Because we cannot grow our economy without unlocking the potential of everyone in it. Employers tell me they can’t find the talent they need, and behind every unfilled vacancy is a business that cannot grow.

Take construction – we cannot build our tram, retrofit homes or deliver the housing we need without bricklayers, joiners and electricians. So we’ve embarked on a multimillion-pound programme of capital investment just for construction, with the Chancellor injecting £15 million into the Leeds College of Building so it can take on an extra 2,000 learners a year.

This year, I made a promise to every 14 to 24-year-old in West Yorkshire – work experience before they turn 18, twice as many apprenticeships, skills for life not just exams, and support for those whose health holds them back. Our goal is to halve our share of 16 and 17 year-olds who are NEET by 2035.

But a promise is only as strong as the powers behind it. Here’s what mayors like me need to tackle youth unemployment across the country.

First, devolve 16-19 education. The prime minister – who led the devolution revolution – has promised mayors control of 16-19 funding, and says Britain will “value the hard hat every bit as much as the graduation cap”. This is the philosophy of mayors up and down the country, and we need the ability to shape education around the jobs our local economies are actually creating.

Second, let us share data. A young person’s college, council, jobcentre and GP can each glimpse a young person’s life, but too often there’s nobody who can see the full picture, meaning young people don’t get the joined-up support they need.

Third, devolve responsibilities for employment, with the funding and convening power we need to bring employers and educators together around one local growth plan.

Young people are ready to graft. Next summer, when another year group walks out of the school gates, I want every one of them to have somewhere to go – and someone who’ll notice if they don’t.

We will not write this generation off. Give mayors responsibility for their futures, and we’ll make sure every young person gets a fair chance to flourish.

Because now is the time to turbocharge this generation to be the very best it can be – something that should matter to every one of us if we want to see a brighter Britain.
