Rwanda has found itself at the centre of plans to deport rejected asylum seekers once again, as five EU countries unveil plans to create a so-called “return hub”.

In 2022, the East African country struck a deal to take migrants who attempted to claim asylum in the UK as part of a five-year arrangement unveiled by the Conservatives.

However, in 2023 the Supreme Court ruled that the policy was unlawful because Rwanda was not a safe third country for migrants sent there, and it was scrapped by Sir Keir Starmer.

It cost Britain £700 million despite only four volunteers being sent to Kigali, then-home secretary Yvette Cooper said, as she branded the policy the “most shocking waste of taxpayer money I have ever seen”.

After Britain scrapped the deal, which included payments to Rwanda to help cover costs, Rwanda brought a case at the Permanent Court of Arbitration alleging breaches of the agreement. A panel of international arbitrators in June rejected a multimillion-dollar claim by Rwanda against the UK.

![Several EU countries may start deporting asylum seekers to Rwanda](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/14/2304952831..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Now, five European countries are planning to create a similar scheme as part of efforts to deter more people from entering Europe.

Austria, Denmark, Germany, Greece and the Netherlands are in talks with the United Nations refugee agency about ensuring the proposal complies with international human rights standards.

No agreement has been signed, but a statement issued at the ‌start of ​a press ‌conference by ⁠Austrian Chancellor Christian Stocker ⁠and his Danish counterpart ‌Mette ​Frederiksen said negotiations should be quick so the first deportation can take place in 2027.

The EU plan differs from Britain's abandoned Rwanda deportation scheme in one key respect: it would apply only to migrants who have exhausted all legal avenues to remain in the European Union, rather than to those whose claims are still being processed.

![EU countries could use facilities earmarked for the UK's failed Rwanda deportation scheme](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/08/01KT188MPB8VRAYXYXNXYR14C5.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Number 10 has not ruled out joining the scheme, with a spokesperson for Prime Minister Andy Burnham saying the country will watch the scheme “with interest”.

Rwanda’s interior minister, Vincent Biruta, said in a statement on Monday that the new proposed partnership with Europe “will ultimately save lives by helping to stop criminal human smugglers who profit from migrants making desperate, dangerous journeys.”

He described Rwanda as “a welcoming country for refugees and migrants” who may have nowhere else to go.

The European nations, meanwhile, said Rwanda “has established itself as a reliable and responsible partner in addressing the global challenges of migration and forced displacement.”

## Why does Rwanda keep emerging as a destination for deportees?

Rwanda’s government has a reputation for efficiency and little tolerance for petty corruption in its bureaucracy.

It tells investors, for example, that they can set up a company in a single day, projecting an image of efficiency considered rare in the region.

Rwanda ranks 41st globally and first in East Africa on Transparency International’s most recent Corruption Perceptions Index. Corruption persists, but public officials face severe consequences if caught obstructing entrepreneurs or undermining government policy.

That reputation helps reassure potential partners that Rwandan authorities will follow through on their commitments.

Under President Paul Kagame, whose rebel group stopped the genocide and whose party has ruled since 1994, Rwanda has sought to bridge ethnic divisions through legislation and other measures. Critics accuse Kagame of crushing dissent, while supporters credit him with presiding over relative peace and stability.

The government has imposed tough penalties for genocide and outlawed the ideology behind it, while Kagame has fostered a culture of obedience among the country’s roughly 14 million people. Rwandan identity cards no longer identify people by ethnicity, and lessons about the genocide are part of the school curriculum.

This sense of order, however constraining some Rwandans find it, also appeals to some people elsewhere in Africa. In neighbouring Uganda, where Kagame grew up as a refugee, some say a leader like him is needed to tackle corruption and build a capital as clean and orderly as Kigali.

Rwanda’s “top leadership can make a quick decision” without having to secure support from other groups, including potential critics in civil society, said Dismas Nkunda, a regional analyst based in Uganda.

![The EU plan has already garnered backlash from human rights activists](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/14/2304924178..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Rwandan authorities have pursued opportunities to raise the country’s international profile, including sponsorship deals with European soccer clubs such as Paris Saint-Germain, Atletico Madrid and Aston Villa.

Formula One announced last month that it would return to Africa with a Grand Prix in Rwanda early in the next decade. Kagame championed the campaign, describing sport as an “equaliser” at the event in Kigali, announcing F1’s first return to Africa since 1993.

Despite these ambitions, Rwanda remains poor, with annual GDP per capita of just over $1,100 (approximately £834), according to the World Bank. A migration deal with European nations could bring much-needed revenue.

Rwanda has also faced diplomatic setbacks in recent months, notably sanctions over its alleged role in violence in neighbouring Congo. The Congolese government accuses Rwanda of arming M23 rebels and sending troops to support the militants. Rwanda has denied supporting the M23.

Last month, the US imposed sanctions on Rwanda’s defence ministry over the country’s involvement in mineral-rich eastern Congo, where fighting continues despite peace efforts by the Trump administration.

“Rwanda has the political will and wants these deals for its own reasons, including diplomatic and economic benefits from these partnerships,” said Gonzaga Muganwa, a political analyst in Kigali.

Stephanie Wolters, a senior research fellow with the South African Institute of International Affairs, said Rwanda makes itself attractive “by opening its doors to arrangements that suit European countries who are trying to offload a complicated and difficult issue.”
