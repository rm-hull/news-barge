## Good morning. It’s Tuesday and my name is Angela Skujins. On today’s agenda: Defence ministers descend on Brussels to discuss hybrid threats and the China challenge, while the migration debate enters its second day from ice-cold Vienna.

**Just in:** As Europe weighs its role in potential Russia-Ukraine peace talks, the Kremlin launched strikes hitting two major bridges in Kyiv overnight. The attack damaged critical infrastructure and disrupted heating and water in parts of the capital, the city's mayor said.

“The 22nd package of sanctions, where is it? We are late.” That was the message from Lithuania’s Foreign Minister **Kęstutis Budrys**, who spoke to me on the sidelines of Monday’s Foreign Affairs Council meeting in Brussels, ahead of today’s gathering of EU defence ministers. He was pressed on how to respond to Russian aggression in Ukraine and Moscow's menace manifesting itself through hybrid warfare, impacting Europe.

On Monday, foreign ministers were tasked with putting flesh on the bones — or adding “substance”, as EU foreign policy chief **Kaja Kallas** put it — to discussions about how Europe should be represented in any potential Russia-Ukraine ceasefire negotiations.**Ceasefire first:** From a Baltic perspective, Europe needs to take a tougher line with Russia. That starts with not falling for traps laid by Russian President Vladimir Putin and insisting that a ceasefire must come before discussions about what comes next.

This means proposals such as the four-pillar pitch outlined by Austria on Monday could prove harmful to Ukraine’s future. Budrys said Europe’s first and foremost responsibility is to look after Ukraine: accelerating its EU accession negotiations, discussing its NATO membership and strengthening its security through collective defence.

“So let's first decide on Ukraine: How will they be secure? We know how to strengthen ourselves. We should ask ourselves how Ukraine will fit into the Euro-Atlantic family,” he said, adding: “I think it is harmful to be fast with the ideas and propose something new.”

**Actions have consequences:** Pressure is also mounting for the EU to step up its sanctions regime against Russia, with work on the 22nd package looming. Defence ministers will also discuss how to respond to China’s role in Russia’s war effort, including its provision of dual-use technology found in Russian weapons, such as semiconductors used in lethal drones, and allegations that it has helped train Russian soldiers.

Europe also has skin in the game. Countries across the bloc, particularly those along its eastern flank, are experiencing the spillover effects of Russia’s invasion and its expanding hybrid campaign. Romania’s Foreign Minister **Dan Neculăescu** struck a defiant tone ahead of Monday’s Brussels meeting, calling for closer NATO-EU coordination following an “unacceptable” escalation of incidents off Romania’s Black Sea coast.

“We have had four alerts in 24 hours, and we have had three ships hit over the weekend. This is unacceptable,” he said.

The warning was echoed by Estonia’s Foreign Minister **Margus Tsahkna**, who said Russian intelligence services had once again organised an attack against an Estonian defence company — an attempted arson attack that was thwarted. “Russia is attacking Europe,” he said. Estonia has announced plans to close all road border crossings with Russia from 1 December in response.** Back to Ukraine:**Latvia’s Foreign Minister** Baiba Braže** told me that, whatever happens in the peace negotiations, strong sanctions remain essential. Europe must also ensure that: “Russia doesn't read our readiness to be at the table as a weakness, to give up something.”

European allies will also hear directly from Kyiv, with Ukraine’s Defence Minister **Yevhenii Khmara** expected to brief ministers on the battlefield situation and Ukraine’s military needs. One issue to watch: Starlink satellite interruptions.**‘Super into NATO’:** Another moment to watch comes over lunch, when EU defence ministers are due to meet US Under Secretary of War for Policy**Elbridge Colby**. The agenda lists transatlantic security and defence as the focus of their discussions.

One EU official described Colby as “super into NATO”, while another suggested there was little need for a crystal ball to predict that the Pentagon’s force posture review would feature in the conversation. The bigger question is what those discussions will reveal about the future shape of the transatlantic alliance.

Keeping the lights on: the EU has promised to ramp up humanitarian aid to help Ukraine make it through the harshest winter yet of the war. The new pledges include power generators, buses and floating bridges to prevent Russia from breaking the country in two parts along the Dnipro river.

“Russia is turning the winter into a weapon of war to plunge Ukrainians into the darkness and freeze them into submission but this is not going to happen because we’re there,” **Hadja Lahbib,** the European Commissioner for crisis management, told**Jorge Liboreiro** on Monday. “We have no choice, this is our security that’s at stake.”

Asked if the suffering inside Ukraine could trigger a new migratory wave to Europe, Lahbib said she’s “preparing for even the worst-case scenario” but expressed confidence that Ukraine will make it through the gruelling winter.

**The big five:** Denmark, Austria, Germany, the Netherlands and Greece have announced a partnership with Rwanda to establish the first-ever return hub outside the EU. The five countries aim to begin deporting rejected asylum seekers to the African country in 2027, although the final details of the deal are still being negotiated. The plan has also raised concerns over human rights and potential legal challenges.

The first facility is expected to accommodate around 100 people at a time. Those sent there will be free to move around Rwanda and are expected to integrate into local society and the labour market, according to people familiar with the negotiations.

**Going home?:** After a period at the facility, the length of which has yet to be determined, migrants will be expected to rebuild their lives in Rwanda or voluntarily return to their countries of origin or another country where they have the right to reside.

What Rwanda will receive in return remains unclear. Rwandan Interior Minister **Vincent Biruta** said his country's motivation for participating was a willingness to find solutions to migration management.

He is set to deliver a speech today at the Vienna Migration Conference, organised by the International Centre for Migration Policy Development (ICMPD), on his country's approach to migration and may provide further details about the partnership.

## After peace talks snub, Kallas faces reproach and uncertain future

Kaja Kallas is standing her ground after being dismissed by EU leaders as special envoy for talks with Russia. Asked whether she regretted making her pitch so forcefully last week in Dublin, Kallas remained defiant.

“I stand by my words and I’m ready to do this job,” Kallas said after chairing a meeting of foreign affairs ministers. She then admitted: “You know, it’s also some kind of relief that we don’t talk about who anymore, but we can focus really on the substance.”

In a tongue-in-cheek (yet scathing) remark, Kallas said that if leaders want to preach about the rule of law around the world, they should start by upholding the EU treaties, which leave foreign policy in the hands of the High Representative. The inference is that this means her.

The latest snub adds to an ever-growing list of missteps and setbacks that have plagued Kallas’ tenure. She’s now facing a difficult reality in which she, the High Representative, is excluded from the most consequential diplomatic process the continent has lived through since the end of the Cold War.

It’s a contradiction so glaring and profound that it challenges the very essence of her job, **Jorge** and**Maïa De la Baume** write in this must-read analysis.
