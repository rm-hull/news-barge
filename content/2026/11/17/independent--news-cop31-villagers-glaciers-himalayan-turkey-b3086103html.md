Villagers whose families prayed for generations at a local temple now gather around the mound of dirt where it once stood, unsure whether it will ever be rebuilt. Farmers who once grew rice to feed their families and earn a living now sit in makeshift shelters, wondering if they’ll ever own land again.

In Nuwakot and surrounding areas of Nepal, where deadly floods in August washed away entire villages and left thousands dead or missing, life has ground to a halt.

Along the Trishuli River, power lines, solar panels, cars and homes remain partially buried in the mud, while broken bridges leave communities cut off from neighboring towns. Survivors sleep in makeshift metal shelters, relying on donations for drinking water and food as they wait to see how, where and when they can get back on their feet.

“God knows how long we will have to stay here," 45-year-old Lakchin Bahadhur Tamang, a hydropower plant worker, told The Associated Press on a recent morning. “I fear that once these aid organizations and helping hands leave, we will be left helpless, without even enough drinking water.”

Meanwhile, thousands of miles away at the U.N. climate summit in Turkey, known as COP31, leaders from Nepal and other mountain countries are warning the world there is no time to wait, as they sound the alarm about future disasters from global temperature rise, including the collapse of Himalayan glaciers.

They and other climate activists are calling for discussions on the heightened peril that people face from a warming planet, caused mainly by the burning of gas, coal and oil.

They are focusing in particular on a fund established three years ago to compensate developing nations hit hard by climate-fueled disasters. The fund doesn't have nearly as much money as needed and is mired in bureaucracy. Experts say more funding and faster disbursement are essential to prepare for more efficient recovery as climate disasters become more frequent and severe.

Nepal pushes for Himalayan climate resilience at COP31

Nepal and other mountain countries are pressing negotiators to make the dangers facing their communities a lasting priority.

While Nepal and Bhutan sought a dedicated negotiating agenda item on warming in mountain regions at the start of the summit, their proposal did not make the cut.

Nepal’s foreign minister, Shisir Khanal, said the country wants a dedicated program on mountains and climate change so mountain risks are reflected in international climate action, including funding decisions.

“After the disaster, we received an outpouring of sympathy. This is the time to put sympathy into the system,” Khanal said when speaking at the climate talks last week.

The stakes extend far beyond mountain villages. Glaciers and snow in the Hindu Kush Himalaya feed rivers that nearly 2 billion people depend on for drinking water, farming and electricity, according to the Kathmandu-based International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development, or ICIMOD.

Warming can destabilize mountain slopes as glaciers retreat and frozen ground thaws. Floods carrying rock, ice and debris can cross borders within minutes, leaving little time to warn people downstream, especially as early warning systems face funding cuts.

Temperature rise won't stop any time soon

Over the next years, global average temperatures are set to surpass 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 degrees Fahrenheit) of warming compared to the early 1800s, a threshold that delegates set during the 2015 Paris Agreement.

Amid that stark reality, many discussions focus on how and when the world can lower the global average temperature rise and go back below that threshold, which would require massive reductions in greenhouse gas emissions that the world has not been able to achieve.

Pam Pearson, director of the International Cryosphere Climate Initiative, warned against treating the temperature rise as something that can be easily reversed. Snow and ice can disappear rapidly, while recovery can take centuries or millennia, she said.

“There is no negotiating with the melting point of ice,” Pearson said.

Monitoring unstable slopes and dangerous glacial lakes, sharing information across borders in real time, coordinating early warning systems and finding global funds are imperative, said climate experts at COP31.

“Every time there’s a disaster in the Himalayas, we tend to call it a wake-up call,” said Pema Gyamtsho, director general of ICIMOD. “The question before us is: What will it take for us to wake up and take action?”

COP31 is a critical moment for the loss and damage fund

The U.N. Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage was established in 2022 to provide financial support to vulnerable developing nations suffering impacts from climate change. Its creation came after years of debates about how much developed nations, including the United States, historically the No. 1 polluter, should pay to help poorer nations adapt to climate change, which they had little role in causing.

At COP31, one of the most constant refrains of protesters has been “climate justice now,” calling for help for Nepal.

But the fund has so far fallen far short of expectations. For Nepal, it pledged about $20 million, only a fraction of the estimated $2.5 billion in losses.

Khanal, Nepal's foreign minister, has said rebuilding after the disaster would cost about one-tenth of Nepal’s economy.

Manjeet Dhakal, a member of Nepal’s delegation and lead adviser to least developed countries at COP, welcomed the fund’s initial response but said that “the scale of the resources needs to increase.”

Overall, experts have calculated that the amount currently pledged through the fund falls vastly short of the world’s future needs. Countries have pledged more than $820 million to the fund, while it faces 176 requests for support from developing nations totaling about $2.8 billion. The fund’s board members will meet in December to consider more support requests.

The Stockholm Environment Institute estimated that by 2030 the loss and damage finance needed in lower-income countries would be between $400 billion and $1.6 trillion per year. The loss and damage fund has been slow to turn promises into payouts. Money earmarked for Nepal remains subject to a formal funding proposal and final board approval, with no disbursement date announced.

For survivors in Nepal, the gap between what has been offered and what is needed is huge.

Standing between destroyed buildings, 41-year-old Dhurba Bhandari, a small store owner, noted that his country had contributed very little to global carbon emissions yet had been devastated.

“The international community must take responsibility and help people who have been displaced, as well as support reconstruction," he said.
