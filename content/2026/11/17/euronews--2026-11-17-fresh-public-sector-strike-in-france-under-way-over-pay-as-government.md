## Tuesday's demonstrations follow talks with Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu, which left unions dissatisfied, as they continue to push for across the board pay rises.

Another major day of protests is under way in France, as part of a nationwide strike led by public sector workers calling for pay increases, despite government efforts to ease tensions as demonstrations roil the country.

This come as France races to adopt a 2027 budget against a backdrop of strained finances, eight weeks after social unrest erupted in mid-September, amid student anger at school conditions.

The movement has since broadened in scope, taking aim at the cost of living crisis and rising fuel prices. Tuesday's demonstrations follow talks with Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu, which left unions dissatisfied, as they continue to push for across the board pay rises.

In an attempt to meet union demands, the government has announced eight additional index points for the lowest-paid public sector workers. It has also brought forward an increase in the national minimum wage from 1 December, in reaction to inflation.

Despite this, in its draft budget, the government has ruled out any general pay rise for the fourth consecutive year, citing the country's finances, amid a mounting public deficit and national debt.

This is unacceptable for the unions, which put the loss of purchasing power for public servants at 16 percent since 2017, leading to increasing numbers of public employees ending up on or close to the minimum wage against a backdrop of inflation.

Demonstrations have seen a decline in turn out in recent weeks, according to figures from France's Interior Ministry.

Protests have been largely peaceful, however there have been instances of fringe violence, including attacks on journalists and police, public buildings, as well as the use of firecrackers.

Meanwhile, police forces have been accused of using disproportionate measures to clamp down on demonstrations, prompting a number of investigations into law alleged police misconduct.
