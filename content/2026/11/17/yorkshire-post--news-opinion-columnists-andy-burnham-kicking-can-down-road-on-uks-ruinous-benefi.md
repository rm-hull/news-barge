# Andy Burnham kicking can down road on UK's ruinous benefits bill: Andrew Vine

## Today ought to be the moment when we finally start getting to grips with one of the most serious challenges facing the country – the ruinous benefits bill that is draining the nation’s finances.

Publication of the year-long review by the former Labour health secretary Alan Milburn into how the benefits system should be reformed gives every appearance that the government is going to take action.

But is that really going to happen? Don’t count on it. What we’re likely to see instead is another example of this government’s talent for kicking problems down the road to be sorted out at some future and unspecified date.

This tendency to put off the really difficult decisions for another day is becoming arguably the defining characteristic of Andy Burnham’s premiership, which generates a lot of hot air about transformative policies but doesn’t actually deliver anything much of substance.

We seem to have entered an era of performative politics in which a Prime Minister with all the advantages of a huge Parliamentary majority, personal popularity and a knack for connecting with the public talks a really good game, and that’s it. Nothing actually happens.

The government’s focus doesn’t seem to be on what can be done to improve the country’s fortunes in the almost three years it has left before the next likely general election, but on planning for some distant future.

All its talk is of 10-year plans, which assumes that Labour and its leader are destined for another decade in power, while in the here and now the difficulties facing the country only grow greater.

The cost of benefits looms large among them. The welfare bill is set to reach £100bn a year by the end of the decade, and a great deal of that cost is attributable to the million young people aged between 18 and 24 who are not in employment, education or training.

These so-called Neets are at the heart of the report by Mr Milburn, who has reached the inevitable conclusion that the benefits bill must be cut, and put forward sensible and well-argued ways of doing it.

We’ll see the full details later today, but in interviews Mr Milburn has stressed how the system has become skewed towards effectively paying young people to do nothing instead of diverting the money towards getting them into jobs, to the extent that for every £25 paid in benefits, only £1 is being spent on supporting people into work.

Mr Milburn has not denied suggestions that reforming the system may cost £6bn, but it will save money in the long run, as well as giving a million young people much better prospects for their lives than existing on benefits.

Even if opposition parties don’t support the proposals, Mr Burnham has the clout to make them stick, which means an immediate start could be made on shaking up the system.

Except it won’t be. For proof of that we only have to look back to last month, when in the aftermath of his budget, the Chancellor, John Healey, was asked if the benefits bill would be cut before the end of this Parliament.

He said it would, but was immediately contradicted by the Prime Minister’s office, presumably scared of causing ructions on the back benches which rebelled against cuts attempted by Sir Keir Starmer.

This gives us the clearest indication that benefits reform will be kicked down the road, probably into the next Parliament,

So much else on this government’s watch is going the same way

Take the crisis in social care, an issue as urgent as the ballooning benefits bill, and one which is costing both the country and families dear. As Mr Milburn was outlining his proposed reforms, the second report by Baroness Louise Casey into the dreadful and unsustainable state of care was being released.

What is the government doing about it? Again, nothing until after 2030, despite the Prime Minister proclaiming that he intended to tackle the crisis and his ambition to create a national care service, which sounds great but is no more than an idea that might become reality at some point in the future.

Then there is defence. In war-torn Ukraine and in a Downing Street lined with guardsmen in full dress uniform, Mr Burnham solemnly pledged to increase defence spending in the face of multiple threats to Britain.

When? The issue was swerved in the budget and kicked down the road into a spending review next year, which will most probably push it to 2030 or beyond.

All of which means we’ve got an awful lot to look forward to in about three years’ time. That’s when all the problems are going to be sorted out, apparently.

But it leaves an awkward question for the government as the close of 2026 approaches – what are you doing about any of them now?
