Australia, which is leading the negotiations at the Cop31 UN climate summit, is on course to keep exporting coal and gas on a large scale for decades, a new analysis has found.

The report, released on Tuesday by Climate Analytics at the summit in Antalya, Turkey found that emissions from Australia's fossil fuel exports will fall only marginally by 2040, to about 900 million tonnes of carbon dioxide a year, roughly twice the country's reported domestic emissions in 2025.

Combined with its domestic emissions, which account for slightly more than 1 per cent of the global total, the exports gave Australia a carbon footprint of 4.3 per cent of global fossil fuel CO2 emissions in 2025, a share that has barely changed since the institute's 2024 analysis.

Australia is the world's second-largest coal exporter, having shipped 352 million tonnes of black coal in 2025, or 25 per cent of global exports, and the third-largest exporter of liquefied natural gas (LNG), which is gas cooled into a liquid for shipping, at 79 million tonnes.

Government projections cited in the report show coal exports staying at about 350 million tonnes a year until 2030, before falling to about 300 million tonnes by 2035 and 280 million by 2040, while LNG exports remain close to 80 million tonnes a year until 2035.

The report says the Australian government has approved at least 37 new or expanded coal, oil and gas projects since the prime minister, Anthony Albanese, took office in 2022. It says some coal mine permits run as late as 2088, nearly 40 years after Australia's pledged net zero date of 2050, and that the government has approved an extension of operations at Woodside's North West Shelf LNG facility until 2070.

It also names two gas developments that together would produce several billion tonnes of emissions, the Browse project at Scott Reef off Western Australia, which awaits final federal environmental approval, and the Beetaloo Basin in the Northern Territory, which has begun commercial production with federal and territory backing.

Under the pathways the report sets out for limiting warming to 1.5C, Australian coal production would need to fall 32 to 50 per cent below 2025 levels by 2030 and 72 to 86 per cent by 2035, approaching a full phase-out by 2040.

Current policies would produce cumulative emissions from exports of 17.9 billion tonnes of CO2 between 2025 and 2040, between 62 per cent and 116 per cent more than under those pathways.

A Paris-aligned transition would avoid as much as 9.6 billion tonnes of emissions over 15 years, the report finds, sparing 4 million to 5.7 million people exposure to unprecedented heat and saving 126 million to 176 million coral colonies on the Great Barrier Reef from mass bleaching.

"There is a deep contradiction in a government position that champions climate ambition abroad but plans for continued fossil fuel exports, with approvals for coal mines and gas fields that lock in production for decades," said Bill Hare, chief executive of Climate Analytics.

Thomas Houlie, the report's lead author and a senior analyst at the institute, said Australia has little intention of starting its own transition away from fossil fuels.

"So the question is what does Australia get out of its fossil fuel exports? The industry is a relatively small employer, Australians just get more climate change, and the coal and gas we sell abroad erodes the government's credibility here in Antalya," Mr Houlie said.

"The first step is to take its foot off the gas. Let's see its roadmap,” he added.

The report calls for a national roadmap to phase out fossil fuel production, a halt to approvals of new projects and extensions, and a whole-of-economy plan built on renewables and electrification.

Cop31 is the first climate summit shared by two countries, with Turkey as host and Australia presiding over the negotiations, an arrangement agreed after a standoff of more than a year over who would host.

Mr Albanese defended the summit's focus on electrification at a press conference with Pacific leaders this week, calling a proposed global target of 35 per cent electrification by 2035 a significant advance and saying tackling the climate crisis requires several approaches.

Chris Bowen, Australia's climate change and energy minister, said electricity accounts for about 23 per cent of global final energy consumption and that its share is rising by about 0.3 percentage points a year, a pace that would need to more than quadruple to meet the target.

The pledge has been criticised because it does not require extra electricity to come from clean sources or commit countries to phasing out fossil fuel production.
