Julian\* enters the room with a smile on his face. “I got fired,” he says in greeting, casually settling into one of the two armchairs. Trainers, baggy trousers, a long jumper, glasses and tousled hair. “I wasn’t expecting it, but it’s not exactly a punch in the face either,” he says with a grin. All the employees with mini-jobs have been let go, he says. The 17-year-old works alongside school. His Abitur graduation ceremony is coming up soon. Julian is one of the top students in his year – and he has a problem with violence.

This Wednesday, he is sitting at Selam’s premises in Spandau. The non-profit organisation works with violent young people across Berlin. Sitting opposite Julian is Benjamin Stoeckel, an anti-violence trainer. The two have been meeting at least once a week since January 2026. Willy the dog is always present, making himself comfortable beside Julian. “He changes the atmosphere in the room and goes over to people when they are sad,” Stoeckel says of his “therapy assistant”. He takes Willy into classrooms, where he works with children and teenagers who behave aggressively and become physically violent – sometimes even first-graders. Stoeckel also works one-to-one with young people such as Julian.

### Julian Choked His Mother’s Boyfriend

“He is one of the few who contacted the Youth Welfare Office themselves,” Stoeckel says. Usually, concerned parents, teachers or the Youth Welfare Office get in touch. The extent and nature of the violence vary from case to case. “Julian has recognised that he has a problem,” Stoeckel explains.

Recently, Julian called his teacher a “whore”. He defends himself by saying he had only intended to whisper it to the classmate sitting next to him. “You know that’s not true,” Stoeckel intervenes. “You said it because you thought she wouldn’t hear you, and now you’re trying to use that to justify it.” Julian smiles again.

But his violence has not been limited to words. Julian admits freely that he squeezed his mother’s boyfriend’s throat during an argument. “That was funny.” He also lashed out at his best friend for arriving late at the cinema.

His behaviour has consequences: friends are distancing themselves from him. He also has repeated conflicts with his girlfriend.

### Youth Violence: Perpetrators Are Getting Younger, Attacks More Brutal

Recent incidents in Brandenburg have brought youth violence back into the public spotlight. In Potsdam, teenagers beat one of their peers unconscious. In Frankfurt (Oder), a violent attack was filmed and uploaded to social media.

“Everyone is jumping on it now because of the brutality,” says Mesut Göre critically. He founded Selam-Berlin in 2014. He can confirm that violent offences are becoming more brutal and that the perpetrators are getting younger.

During his time as an anti-violence trainer, he has encountered several cases in which young people were seriously injured – or seriously injured others. Göre recalls, for example, a young autistic person who attacked a Youth Welfare Office employee and had to be restrained until the police arrived. “Only an injection calmed him down.”

What bothers Göre about the latest debate is that “people are now rushing to find simple explanations for these acts”.

In the Brandenburg cases, the causes of the violence were largely reduced to the perpetrators’ migrant backgrounds, and there were calls for deportations.

“But it’s not that simple,” Göre says. “Deportation doesn’t solve the problem. What do we do with German offenders?”

Selam’s clients come from a wide variety of ethnic backgrounds. But that is not the decisive factor, Göre says.

### It Often Takes Months Before the Violent Act Is Discussed

Instead, he says, factors such as living circumstances – violent young people often come from disadvantaged backgrounds with limited access to education – their social environment and drug use play a role.

Most important, however, is examining each case individually. To do this, one must first establish trust. The violent act itself is not initially the main focus.

Anti-violence trainer Benjamin Stoeckel recalls that it took months before he and one 17-year-old discussed the day the teenager beat up his mother and threatened her with a bread knife. “Before that, we talked about all sorts of things.”

At the beginning of a conversation, Stoeckel shares a great deal about himself. He used to be a street brawler who beat up Nazis.

“When I broke my collarbone doing that, I told my parents I had fallen off my bicycle,” Stoeckel says.

“The point is to show the young people that I wasn’t any better than they are.”

From his own experience, he knows how difficult it is to work through trauma.

But what is Julian’s trauma? After more than ten months, they are slowly beginning to address it.

“He was sexually abused by his great-uncle when he was a teenager,” Stoeckel explains with Julian’s consent. “His little brother came into the room and saved him.”

### “You Have to Believe You’re Worth Something”

The topics this Wednesday are Julian’s job, his mother, to whom he has lent money, his relationship with his girlfriend and his upcoming Abitur graduation ceremony.

Two months ago, Julian announced that he wanted to deliver a speech on behalf of his graduating class. Now he is considering calling in sick.

“Are you afraid you won’t get the recognition you need from your classmates? You’re scared, be honest. Do you think you’re not good enough?” Stoeckel challenges him.

“Could be,” Julian admits.

Once again, he puts on his smile.

Suddenly, Stoeckel takes out a €20 note and folds it. Each crease represents a problem in Julian’s life: his sometimes difficult relationship with his mother, his absent father, the sexual abuse.

Stoeckel throws the note onto the floor and steps on it. Then he picks up the banknote, hands it to Julian and asks him to unfold it. “How much is this piece of paper worth?” he asks.

Julian replies: “Still €20. But it has a lot of creases.”

“You have to believe that you are worth something,” he tells the 16-year-old emphatically.

“You only say that because you’re paid to,” Julian replies.

Stoeckel strongly disagrees. “That’s what you tell yourself. You’re a great, cool, intelligent guy. Willy senses that too, and he always comes straight over to sit with you.”

The grin has disappeared from Julian’s face. He leans forward, looking at Stoeckel, his head resting in his hands. He is silent.

Stoeckel says: “Self-worth comes first, then self-confidence.”

After almost two hours, the session is over.

“That was a lot today,” Stoeckel sums up. “It took us a long time to reach the point where he became emotional.”

*Julian wishes to remain anonymous, so his name has been changed. The editorial team knows his real name.*
