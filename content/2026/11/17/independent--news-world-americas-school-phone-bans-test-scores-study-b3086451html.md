The case for banning cellphones in schools sounds straightforward: mobile devices create non-stop distractions. Removing them should allow students to focus on lessons, interact with peers, and improve learning.

Yet fresh research demonstrates that the reality is considerably more complex. Even if schools require phones to be stored in pouches or backpacks, keeping digital disruptions out remains challenging.

Data from the Phone Free Schools State Report Card, which monitors state-level legislation, reveals that over 40 U.S. states have enacted laws restricting mobile devices in classrooms in recent years. Regulations vary significantly, frequently granting individual schools the authority to design their own guidelines and creating inconsistent enforcement across the nation.

A major study released on Tuesday indicates that strict mobile phone bans — specifically those forcing pupils to keep devices secured in pouches throughout the entire school day — yield minor academic benefits. Researchers noted, however, that these incremental gains could accumulate if such bans remain in place long-term.

## Mobile bans offer measurable, if limited, benefits

Conducted by Harvard’s Center for Education Policy Research, the study observed "modest" improvements in academic progress among schools with stringent device rules compared to similar institutions lacking such policies. By examining standardized test results from 3.3 million students across 4,600 nationwide schools, the research calculated that these educational advances equaled roughly one to two additional weeks of instruction during an academic year.

![](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/14/45/AP26320837984025.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Academic performance has declined internationally since 2013, coinciding with the widespread adoption of smartphones featuring social media platforms. Harvard professor Thomas Kane, a co-author of the study, explained that researchers wanted to examine whether prohibiting devices in classrooms could help reverse this downward trend in student achievement.

"Our answer is yes, phone bans do have a positive effect on growth. But it’s not a panacea," Kane said.

He observed that learning declines occurred gradually across a decade, making it logical that recovery would similarly take time. The analysis examined standardized reading and mathematics test results across grades four to 10 over five recent school years.

"While schools should not expect large boosts in achievement immediately, storing students' phones seems to be a cost-effective way to add 1-2 weeks of instruction each year, which could add up over time, just as the initial declines did," the study says.

This progress represents roughly half of the annual score drop recorded since smartphones became widespread, according to the research.

## Some scholars describe academic improvements as disappointing overall

Furthermore, the link between classroom device bans and achievement remains unclear. An earlier study released in May determined that strict classroom mobile prohibitions had "close to zero" impact on student test performance after analyzing data from tens of thousands of schools nationwide.

That study, published by the National Bureau of Economic Research, evaluated distinct standardized examination scores alongside teacher surveys, GPS device metrics, and information from Yondr — a major manufacturer of secure phone pouches — across 4,600 educational institutions utilizing the locking pouch technology.

![A student views her cell phone near a cell phone locker at Ronald McNair Sr. High School](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/14/47/AP26320837963316.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

While the analysis detected "modest positive effects" in high school examination results over a three-year period, particularly in mathematics, the researchers — affiliated with Stanford, Duke, the University of Michigan, and the University of Pennsylvania — expressed caution regarding broader conclusions about academic benefits.

Stanford education economist Thomas Dee, who co-authored the NBER paper, noted both reports point toward similar findings. "The early results from school phone bans are largely disappointing and raise unresolved questions," Dee said.

Acknowledging the Harvard study's suggestion that benefits might compound over time, Dee remarked: "I hope they're right. However, I’m not comfortable making that extrapolation from estimates that are small, sensitive and based on just a few years of phone bans."

Dee suggested that ongoing distractions from alternative electronic devices during class might explain the limited impact. Alternatively, he noted, "it may be that we see these disappointing results because out-of-school phone use is harming child development."

## Mobile devices represent just one digital distraction

Enforcement remains a key issue regardless of state mandates or campus rules, according to Jean Twenge, a psychologist at San Diego State University and author of *10 Rules for Raising Kids in a High-Tech World*.

Educators and students alike acknowledge that many restrictions exist mostly on paper. Students frequently bypass rules by placing secondary devices or phone-shaped stones into secure pouches. In districts where students are simply asked to keep devices inside their school bags, compliance with the policy remains highly variable across classrooms.

"It happens pretty often that there’s a policy of no phones during the school day, bell-to-bell, and you go into the school and phones are everywhere," Twenge said.

Even if phones are put away, Twenge asked: "Do they have laptops at school? Are they just doing social media on laptops instead?"

Off-campus screen time also impacts academic performance, Twenge noted, asking: "If they have that phone available when they’re studying, how much are they really taking in?"

Kane acknowledged that off-campus mobile usage remains "the big question." Nevertheless, investigators have been unable to rigorously evaluate concerns shared by educators and parents that late-night activity on video platforms like YouTube negatively affects student performance during the school day.

"I find it astonishing that social media companies know how much time kids spend on their platforms, but that data has never been linked to student outcomes," Kane said. "We can’t provide answers until the social media companies share data."
