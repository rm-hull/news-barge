Thousands of people poured into the streets of Haiti’s capital on Tuesday to praise El Salvador’s president, Nayib Bukele, and his troops as they fight gangs in the troubled Caribbean country.

Many in the crowd raised their fists and some clutched Haitian flags and signs in favor of Bukele.

“Give them the drones and the dogs!” some Haitians shouted in reference to the explosive drones that have targeted areas controlled by powerful gangs and the newly unveiled robot dogs making their rounds in Port-au-Prince.

Last month, Bukele deployed some 2,500 troops to Haiti after four of their own came under attack on Oct. 18 and remain missing. Fighting between gangs and foreign forces backed by Haitian authorities has only increased since then as Haiti remains mired in violence, with more than 20,000 people killed in the past five years.

While chants of “Bukele!” are spreading across Haiti, experts warn there’s no long-term plan to keep the country safe once the Central American troops pull out.

In a report published Tuesday based on more than 100 interviews with officials in Haiti and abroad that reveal government failures and missteps, International Crisis Group urged Haitian officials to develop a plan to prevent their country from imploding once international forces leave.

“The new foreign mission promises temporary relief from the bane of gangs, but its goal is to weaken, not dismantle them," the report said. “Haiti should start preparing now for the day when its own forces are once again summoned to take sole charge of the nation’s security.”

Forces currently fighting gangs include Haiti’s police and soldiers; a U.N.-backed gang suppression force; self-appointed vigilantes; and Vectus Global, a private security firm founded by former U.S. Navy SEAL and Blackwater founder Erik Prince. Fighting gangs on their own are some 2,500 Salvadoran troops deployed when four of their own went missing after they were attacked on Oct. 18 deep in gang-controlled territory.

International Crisis Group warned that authorities “lack a strategy to identify the most pressing threats, coordinate state efforts, and bring a lasting reduction in violence.”

Echoing that sentiment was a recent cartoon in Le Nouvelliste newspaper that depicted Haitians cheering on Bukele as they stood near a table with a piece of paper on which someone had written “PLAN ANTI GANG” with crayons of various colors.

Haiti's police force faces mounting troubles

Tuesday’s report noted that plans to establish a National Security Council tasked with creating a long-term strategy to make Haiti safer two years ago never materialized.

Citing interviews with former senior government officials, the report stated that some people were concerned the council would oversee intelligence gathering, which has previously been used by Haitian leaders to target political opponents. Sources also told International Crisis Group that top officials did not want to see intelligence funds redirected because they feared losing that money.

International Crisis Group also said a foreign adviser told it that a lack of national strategy means that various police units “often follow different operational guidelines depending on their foreign donors.”

As of last year, Haiti’s National Police had no more than 8,000 active officers for a country of nearly 12 million people, the report said, citing “sources with first-hand knowledge.” Many of those officers occupy command and administrative positions and are not on the street, “while others are not trained for combat.”

The report said the government shortened training from seven months to less than four to quickly increase the number of police officers.

It also warned that police promotions rarely follow criteria like seniority or merit, with some rank-and-file officers opting to work with gangs or turning to private security or arms trafficking for additional income. Lower-ranking officers in Haiti are paid about $300 a month before mandatory deductions.

International Crisis Group called for improving salaries, benefits and facilities for police officers, as well as offering them specialized training and a “clear promotion system based on performance rather than politics.”

Explosive drones considered a necessary evil

The number of victims is expected to grow as foreign forces increasingly rely on helicopters and explosive drones – two things that Haitian security forces lack – to target gangs.

In the first quarter of 2026, the number of civilians killed or injured by security forces as a fraction of all casualties rose 24%, the report found, and that number is expected to increase, but Bukele is finding fans in Haiti.

“In a security environment as troubled as Haiti’s, it is understandable that many have rallied around the use of explosive drones, particularly as initially the technology had a visible effect on gang leaders’ confidence and their willingness to attack state forces,” the report said.

Among the Haitians celebrating is Cliford Vital, who lives in Port-au-Prince, where gangs control an estimated 70% of the city. He noted that Bukele swiftly deployed more than 2,500 soldiers and helicopters to Haiti while U.N.-backed missions have lagged in his view.

“We’ve seen a wind of change in the country,” he said. “The fear that had persisted for so long has now shifted sides.”

But International Crisis Group said the use of such weapons “has brought diminishing returns…producing no meaningful changes in territorial control” or a change in the balance of power on the ground.

“That said, an immediate outright ban on the use of drones is unrealistic,” the report stated.

This week, Haitian Justice Minister Patrick Pélissier flew to El Salvador for an international conference and is expected to meet behind closed doors with Salvadoran officials.

Salvadoran Vice President Félix Ulloa said Monday that the bilateral agreement with Haiti would not be made public.

“There is a hidden agenda to criticize our operation,” he said when asked whether the deal guarantees respect for human rights and the rule of law.

Ulloa and Salvadoran Security Minister Gustavo Villatoro said that the Salvadoran soldiers would return home as soon as the four missing aviation technicians are found.

“We must begin preparing Haiti for a genuine rule of law, so that its institutions function and its legal frameworks work effectively in the future,” Villatoro said.
