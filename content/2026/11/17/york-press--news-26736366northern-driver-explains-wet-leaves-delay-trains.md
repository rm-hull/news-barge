An experienced train driver has revealed the hidden dangers that can happen along the tracks during autumn.

Stuart Ashton, an instructor with Northern, has spoken out as part of a campaign called 'It's not just a leaf' to raise awareness about the serious impact of wet leaves on train services.

With 26 years of experience behind the controls, wet leaves are known as the black ice of the railways.

Mr Ashton said: "The wheels can slide on the compressed leaves when you apply the brakes.

"You can feel it when you're driving.

"When it’s really slippery, we have to drive at slower speeds.

"We don’t want to delay the train, but sometimes we need to do it to make sure everyone gets there safely."

He explained that drivers are taught to “shut the power off earlier, double the braking distance, and then brake earlier and lighter” when approaching stations or junctions during autumn.

Wet leaves are compressed under passing trains, forming a thin, slippery coating that can cause wheels to slide and affect stopping distances.

Mr Ashton said: "You can also struggle when you’re pulling away from a station, especially on some of the older trains.

"The wheels can start spinning without getting any traction when you're accelerating, so we hit a button which blasts sand onto the rail to help them grip."

He added that the rails themselves offer a visual clue.

He said: "When the weather is nice, they look silver and shiny.

"But if they look black and oily during autumn, you know a lot of wet leaves have been compressed onto them.

"That’s why you have to be switched on and ready to take full control of that train as soon as you get out of bed in the morning."

Some routes are more challenging than others.

He said: "Some of the routes are more difficult, because there are a lot of trees and vegetation.

"But knowing the routes inside out makes a massive difference, because you know exactly where the signals and stations are, the gradient of the approach to each station, and where the stopping distances are so you can double them."

Wet leaf mulch can also interfere with electrical signalling systems, leading to longer waits at red signals to maintain safe train separation.

To combat the problem, Network Rail operates specialist ‘leaf-busting’ trains across northern routes six days a week.

These trains clear leaf mulch and apply a gel containing a mix of sand and steel grains to help train wheels grip the rails.

Mr Ashton said the intervention has made a noticeable difference, saying the trains are "fantastic" and have made driving in autumn conditions less challenging in recent years.

Northern, TransPennine Express, and Network Rail work together each autumn to manage the risks posed by slippery rails.

Northern takes every step possible to minimise disruption. This includes route mapping, driver updates, and regular weather forecasts. It has also fitted hundreds of its trains with the latest technology, including Wheel Slide Prevention technology, Water-Trak equipment, and double variable rate sanders to help improve traction.

Northern is the second largest train operator in the UK, with 2,500 services a day to more than 500 stations across the North of England.
