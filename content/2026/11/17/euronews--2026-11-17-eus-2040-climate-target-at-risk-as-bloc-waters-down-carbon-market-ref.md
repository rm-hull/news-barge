## A leaked compromise proposal seeks to shield European industry from rising carbon costs, but environmental groups warn that concessions on free emissions allowances and international carbon credits could undermine the bloc's 2040 climate ambitions.

The European Union's 2040 climate target risks being undermined by reforms to the bloc's carbon market, the Emissions Trading System (ETS), according to a leaked document seen by Euronews.

The Irish Presidency of the EU Council has softened a proposal, to be discussed by EU ambassadors on Wednesday, amid growing political pressure on the ETS as national governments struggle to reconcile emissions targets with industrial competitiveness and high energy costs.

The compromise text seeks to secure political support for the bloc's carbon market beyond 2030. However, climate advocates warn that concessions to protect European industry could weaken the system's contribution to the 2040 target, leaving other sectors of the economy to make up the shortfall.

The Council text retains the European Commission's July proposal to slow the decline in carbon allowances, extend free permits for industry and introduce international carbon credits into the EU carbon market, allowing emissions at home to be offset through projects abroad.

But negotiations are further reshaping the plan, especially around how much flexibility governments and industry will have to avoid domestic emissions cuts.

## Free allowances and international credits

Under the Irish Presidency's proposal, industrial companies would retain 80% of their free allowances if they submit an investment plan, even if they subsequently fail to carry out the proposed decarbonisation investments. The remaining 20% would be conditional on starting construction of eligible projects.

Free allowances are permits that companies receive without having to buy them at auction. They are designed to protect EU producers against competition from countries with less stringent climate rules, such as China and the US.

The proposal marks a significant political concession to industries concerned about losing free allowances, raising questions about whether the ETS will provide sufficient incentives to cut emissions.

The compromise would shield industry from carbon costs for longer, while leaving governments to ensure that this protection translates into investment in European production and clean technologies.

On international carbon credits, the Council has retained the Commission's proposal to set aside allowances equivalent to 260 million tonnes of CO2 to finance their purchase. It also leaves open the possibility of a pilot phase between 2031 and 2035, allowing the EU to test the system before implementing it in full.

The Council has also maintained stringent quality and integrity requirements for international credits, making the ETS emissions reduction trajectory increasingly dependent on their availability.

However, if industries can't buy them, it could jeopardise the 2040 climate target, since it requires at least an 85% domestic net emissions reduction, with international credits accounting for the remaining 5%.

The Irish Presidency is seeking to build consensus among the 27 EU countries before negotiations turn to more politically sensitive questions of climate ambition and burden-sharing between member states.

It hopes to secure an agreement at the Environment Council on December 11. The European Parliament is expected to vote during its December 14-17 plenary session in Strasbourg.

## Fragmentation at national level

The Irish Presidency faces an uphill battle given the depth of divisions among member states and the complexity of the negotiations.

EU countries are already divided over how quickly the supply of carbon permits should shrink, whether companies should be allowed to use international carbon credits to meet their obligations and how far industrial sectors should be shielded from rising carbon costs, according to a second document seen by Euronews.

Taken together, the demands risk diluting the ETS when it is expected to drive a large share of the bloc’s next phase of emissions cuts.

In general, governments want to preserve the ETS as a source of revenue for decarbonisation, but concerns are mounting about the costs it imposes on energy-intensive industries competing with manufacturers from China or the United States.

EU capitals are split over the total number of emissions permits available to companies — some argue they should decline to increase carbon pricing and encourage divestments in fossil fuels.

Others fear the impact of withdrawing free allowances on their struggling industries.

A slower reduction would ease the pressure on power plants and factories to cut emissions, potentially tempering carbon prices and giving companies more time to adapt. But it would also mean fewer emissions cuts in the sectors covered by the ETS, making the 2040 target harder to reach.

## Environmental groups sound the alarm

A recent analysis by the Öko-Institut, commissioned by environmental campaign group WWF Germany, warned that the Commission's proposed ETS reform was "incompatible" with the EU's target of cutting net emissions by 90% by 2040.

“The Commission would rather gamble on climate protection than safeguard the future of European industry," said Lisa Okken, industrial policy expert at WWF Germany.

"Its proposed reform lacks the carbon price signal needed to incentivise the urgent modernisation of European industry. This gap is being filled with complicated accounting tricks and excessive flexibility.”

Another key concern cited by the environmentalists is the proposal to allow additional emissions beyond the existing emissions cap in exchange for future permanent carbon removals through expensive technologies designed to capture CO2 from the air.

"It remains unclear whether these removals can be delivered at the scale required, as the necessary capacity simply does not exist today," reads the study.
