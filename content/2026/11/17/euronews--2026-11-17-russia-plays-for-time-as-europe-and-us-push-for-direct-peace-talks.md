## The next phase of the peace talks remains uncertain as Russia continues to play down the possibility of face-to-face talks with Europe, despite mounting pressure from Washington to make it happen.

Europe has finally settled on a joint team to speak on the continent's behalf in the negotiations to end Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

Yet, despite this diplomatic breakthrough, uncertainty remains over what comes next.

Russia has again cast doubt on the possibility of holding face-to-face talks with the European delegation, which now comprises France, Germany, the United Kingdom, Italy, Poland, the EU institutions (Council and Commission) and NATO.

European, US and Ukrainian officials all agree that the only way to move the process forward is to start a multilateral format with the Russians.

But Moscow seems to be in no hurry.

"The date has not yet been agreed and the expansion of the format – some people are talking about it – but it has not been agreed with us," Yury Ushakov, a close aide to Russian President Vladimir Putin, said on Monday.

Adding to the confusion, Ushakov suggested that "it may be necessary to hold another round, or perhaps more than one round, of trilateral talks" with the US and Ukraine before sitting down with the Europeans.

After speaking with Ushakov, US special envoy Jared Kushner sounded cautiously optimistic, saying preparations were underway for a multilateral meeting that should take place "in the near future".

"We discussed how to facilitate direct engagement between Europe and Russia, which has been largely absent over the past four years," Kushner said on social media.

"It is important that both sides hear each other's concerns and proposals directly as we work toward a more stable and secure future for Europe."

So far, the peace talks have proceeded in two parallel tracks: Ukraine, Russia and the US, on the one hand, and Ukraine, Europe and the US, on the other.

In practice, this has left Europe in the room next door, trying to catch up with what is discussed in the main room, as it happened in Abu Dhabi.

The US side has concluded the two-track configuration has reached its limit and the time has come for Europe and Russia to sit at the same table, at the same time.

Europe, long opposed to this direct engagement, is now on board.

"We are absolutely ready to engage at the multilateral level," Paula Pinho, the Commission's chief spokesperson, said.

Privately, European officials and diplomats say that expectations should remain low because, when it comes to Russia, nothing can be guaranteed, particularly given the volatility of the negotiations, which have gone through multiple ups and downs – and a protracted pause – in the past year alone.

Moscow'scontinued delays reinforce the widespread belief that Putin is playing lip service to the White House and has no intention of treating diplomacy seriously. The brutal attacks against Ukraine ahead of the winter bear witness to that.

The fact that Moscow talks about **Europe's security architecture** but refuses to talk with Europeans is cited as further reason to be wary.

The topic is **seen as a ploy** to divert attention from Ukraine, regurgitate old grievances and reassert Russia's long-lost sphere of influence. According to the New York Times, the Russian demands presented to the US were that NATO stop expanding, withdraw its troops to 1997 borders and halt military support for Ukraine.

"It's absurd. Russia misbehaves, and then they want to be rewarded for it," a senior diplomat snapped. "I don't see how it could fly at all."

Despite the daunting odds, Europeans are keen to show their readiness and availability, while preparing **new sanctions on Russia** and support for Ukraine.

Senior officials from the joint European team will meet with their US and Ukrainian counterparts on Thursday to work out the next steps in the process. The meeting will take place on the sidelines of the Coalition of the Willing in Berlin.

Brussels, meanwhile, has begun drafting a common mandate in case the multilateral format happens. The mandate is meant to compile the principles that Ukraine's allies have followed since the start of the war.

Austria, one of the first member states to publicly call for direct talks with Russia, has circulated a two-page document, seen by Euronews, to structure the negotiations built around four key pillars: political, security, humanitarian and economic.

The paper calls on the EU to maximise its "sanction leverage", prepare a special mission to monitor an eventual ceasefire and develop a strategy for the use of the immobilised Russian assets. It also floats a "division of labour" among the EU, NATO, the United Nations, the OSCE and the Coalition of the Willing to safeguard a future peace.

"Europe finally has a negotiating team that is expected to join the next round of talks. Now the focus must shift from the 'who' to the 'what'. This team urgently needs a mandate on substance," Austrian Foreign Minister Beate Meinl-Reisinger said.

"What is at stake is nothing less than Europe's own security."
