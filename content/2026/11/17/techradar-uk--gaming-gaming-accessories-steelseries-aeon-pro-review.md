## SteelSeries Aeon Pro: two-minute review

The SteelSeries Aeon Pro is a pro-level wireless controller compatible with PC and Xbox Series X / S. Not only does it offer many of the premium features you’d expect from a pad at its price point, but it also offers several huge innovations that separate it from other options in its class.

Let’s start with the most attention-grabbing features. First off, the Aeon Pro offers dual-platform connectivity. That doesn’t mean it has a dongle you can swap between devices or use Bluetooth for your second system — its dock has dual outputs, meaning that you can flip its 2.4GHz Wi-Fi connectivity between PC and Xbox using the pad’s built-in OLED screen. As someone who regularly games on both, I loved how easy this made it to switch between them on the fly.

But honestly, I’d say my favorite feature is SteelSeries’ Infinite Power System. Let’s set aside that slightly grandiose name: essentially, this means the Aeon Pro not only charges on its dock when not in use, but it also comes with hot-swappable rechargeable batteries. Every time I ran out of juice — which didn’t happen often, thanks to the pad’s 12-hour battery life — I could just swap in a fresh battery without having to resort to a cable, giving the Aeon Pro the edge over many of the best PC controllers.

Elsewhere, the Aeon Pro’s design is similarly high-tier. Front and center, it features a built-in OLED screen, which allows you to switch between platforms and profiles straight from your controller. It also felt suitably ergonomic in my hands — every button sat right under my fingertips, even the rear paddles, while the textured grips made it easy to hang onto even during frantic pitched battles in FPS games.

In use, the Aeon Pro is super responsive, thanks to its 1,000Hz polling rate. It also rocks dual-mode triggers, meaning you can switch between analog and micro-switch modes at the flip of a toggle. Meanwhile, both the triggers and thumbsticks are Hall effect — as a result, they’re rapid and drift-free, although they don’t offer quite the same sensitivity and energy efficiency as TMR alternatives.

![A closeup of the SteelSeries Aeon Pro&#039;s right thumbstick, also showing the face buttons, Xbox button, Menu, Share and Screen buttons, D-pad and left thumbstick.](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/bJoNBjEw2cv5tgZgFHDcRT-1200-80.jpg)

SteelSeries’ GG software also allows you to create potentially infinite custom profiles for different games, while you can load up to eight at a time onto the controller itself. On PC, you can also create profiles for each game in your library that then auto-switch when you fire the game up — I love the fact that I could have my thumbsticks automatically invert for games like *Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4*, particularly as these profiles are also compatible with SteelSeries’ Arctis Nova Pro Omni gaming headset as well.

There are a couple of improvements I’d like to see to the SteelSeries GG app, though. First of all, unlike the software offered by rivals like Razer or ROG, GG doesn’t currently allow you any control over trigger response curves, and the options for the thumbsticks are pretty limited. Additionally, remapping only allows you to assign existing buttons from the controller to its back paddles — so if you were hoping to be able to tweak system controls or assign keyboard shortcuts there on PC, you’re out of luck.

## SteelSeries Aeon Pro review: design

When you’re first setting up the SteelSeries Aeon Pro, you’ll likely notice that its dock has two USB-C outputs. That’s because the Aeon Pro is the first controller of its kind to offer dual-platform wireless connectivity. When I plugged it in to both my PC and Xbox Series X, I was able to switch between 2.4GHz connectivity to either system straight from the pad itself — this feels so much more fluid and intuitive than having to faff around with moving dongles or switch to Bluetooth pairing to use a different device.

But for me, this isn’t the Aeon Pro’s most transformative feature. For that, we have to look under the hood: the SteelSeries’ Infinite Power System.

Okay, don’t get carried away with visions of free-energy conspiracy theories — the Aeon Pro’s battery is definitely finite. But — as is the case with the recent SteelSeries Sensei and Rival Pro mice, as well as the Arctis Nova Pro Omni — it is replaceable and can be hot-swapped out for a full one, with the empty battery clipping into a charging port on the controller’s base. As a result, you should always have a full battery waiting in the wings to take over even when your current one is tapped, which is a really smart system. And the batteries are interchangeable with the Arctis Nova Pro Omni, meaning if you’re using both, you’ll always have a steady cycle of juiced-up power packs waiting in the wings.

![A man&#039;s hand replaces one of the SteelSeries Aeon Pro&#039;s hot-swappable thumbsticks.](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/yt8dYfCGScAGEE6ZzW8LeT-1200-80.jpg)

When you’ve popped in a charged battery and powered on the controller, the thing that will most grab your attention is that OLED smart display. While this is hardly the first pro controller we’ve seen with a screen, I would say this is one of the few I’ve seen that actually has a decent range of use cases. Not only does it allow you to flip between different game profiles, but you can also use it to switch sources between Xbox, PC, and Bluetooth, check battery levels, and even remap buttons on the fly. This definitely elevates it from gimmick to something I actually interacted with a lot.

Elsewhere, the design looks smart and suitably pro, and the Aeon Pro feels nicely ergonomic. The gentle slant of its grips sat nicely in my hands, while their finely textured surface gave me a good amount of traction without getting too sweaty — something I can’t say of other controllers I’ve tested. Elsewhere, its body is fashioned from pleasantly tactile matte plastic and its build quality all feels appropriately premium, while still managing to weigh a light and floaty 10.9oz / 310g.

For the most part, the Aeon Pro adopts the standard Xbox controller format, with asymmetrical thumbsticks. However, as a result of that OLED screen at the top of the pad, the start and menu buttons have been relocated to the bottom. I’ll admit this might be making virtue out of necessity, but this placement does actually feel preferable to me — rather than having to adjust your grip to reach past the left thumbstick or face buttons, you can just move your thumbs laterally and easily access in-game menus without stretching.

![A closeup of the SteelSeries Aeon Pro&#039;s Menu, Share and Screen buttons, that also shows D-pad and right thumbstick.](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/SuS9tZHXubbNTGEhARYVBT-1200-80.jpg)

Speaking of the thumbsticks, the Aeon Pro has moved away from drift-prone potentiometer-based sensors for its sticks, like most of its contemporaries. Personally, I’d prefer TMR-based thumbsticks and triggers for their marginally higher sensitivity, but the Hall-effect system used here is going to be plenty sufficient for all but professional esports players. And while TMR does provide a slight buff to energy efficiency, the Aeon Pro’s battery system should make this a bit of a non-issue.

SteelSeries has offered another neat trick for this controller’s thumbsticks as well. Included with the Aeon Pro are two alternate sticks — these are magnetic and hot-swappable, meaning that I could quickly pop them off and replace them mid-game in a matter of moments. Another neat design decision here is the fact that the controller’s base has two magnetic docks for the sticks you aren’t using, which is just a thoughtful detail.

I’ve raved before about how much I love dual-mode triggers, so I’m definitely pleased to see them included here on the Aeon Pro. Enabling you to switch between full-range analog and micro-switch triggers at the flip of a toggle, these give a controller huge versatility, whether you want sensitive control of an accelerator pedal in *Forza Horizon 6*or want clicky, near-instant parries in* Clair Obscur: Expedition 33*.

All the Aeon Pro’s other buttons are similarly satisfying. Its face buttons also use micro-switches, feeling super accurate while giving immediate feedback. And its mappable rear paddles are perfectly positioned to sit under your fingertips, while feeling nice and snappy when you activate them. Everything feels easy to reach and quick to activate, which was fantastic when I needed to leap out of harm’s way or pull out my chainsaw to grind through imps in *Doom*.

![A closeup of the SteelSeries Aeon Pro&#039;s right trigger and shoulder button.](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/ZtzUZmMCmXrHKsVBY5sYqT-1200-80.jpg)

## SteelSeries Aeon Pro review: performance

All the innovative features in the world don't matter if a controller can't match them with powerful performance. Fortunately, the SteelSeries Aeon Pro doesn't struggle here in the least, offering snappy and fluid control over your games.

Thanks to its 1,000Hz polling rate, the Aeon Pro has the reaction times of a hummingbird that’s just had its third energy drink of the day. Whether I was popping off headshots in FPSes or requiring precise steering to avoid flying off a hairpin bend in racing games, its thumbsticks always felt super responsive, and its micro-switch face buttons never betrayed a hint of lag.

Dual-mode triggers are, once again, a totally worthwhile investment. When toggled on to their micro-switches, they aced my go-to test: helping me perform snappy parries and counters in *Clair Obscur: Expedition 33.*With their assistance, I comfortably shrugged off even the remorseless combos of Aberrations. In their analog mode, the triggers feel fast and responsive — almost too fast, in fact, as occasionally while racing in* Forza Horizon 6* I found myself wishing for a tiny bit more resistance to give me more granular control over my accelerator pedal.

On the whole, SteelSeries GG software offers some fantastic features, although I did find it was lacking in a few options I’d come to take for granted from other brands’ apps.

![A closeup of the SteelSeries Aeon Pro&#039;s right-hand rear paddles.](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/D3tiLfX56rxYLmyBeFeujT-1200-80.jpg)

Not only does SteelSeries GG allow you to create, in theory, an infinite number of profiles, but you can load up to eight at a time on the Aeon Pro and flip between them using its OLED screen. That gives it far more flexibility than something like the Asus ROG Raikiri II, which requires you to swap back to its PC-only software just to change profiles.

But even more innovative is its automatic profiles for your favorite PC games. For example, I was able to set my sticks to be directional rather than circular for the 2D platformer *Hollow Knight: Silksong*— then when I fired up the game, it automatically switched to the matching profile. Even better, this functionality also extends to the SteelSeries Arctis Nova Pro Omni gaming headset, meaning I could set it to invert my thumbsticks and crank up the headset’s spatial audio every time I launched* Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4* without pressing a button. That honestly feels a little like magic.

There’s a decent range of settings you can tweak in SteelSeries GG. These include remapping buttons and the back paddles, setting deadzones for the triggers and thumbsticks, adjusting the response of, or inverting, the sticks, altering the vibration of the grips and triggers, and tweaking the settings of that OLED screen.

![A man&#039;s hands replacing the hot-swappable battery in the SteelSeries Aeon Pro controller.](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/xcQLLT8CCEV7J7HL3X9JqT-1200-80.jpg)

However, there are a few options I found myself missing here. First off, while you can change the response of the thumbsticks, there are only three options — default, dynamic, and exponential — while you can’t change the response of the triggers at all. That's a real shame, as I find the fully customizable curves that some apps offer are what really unlock the value of these tailor-made profiles; I definitely found myself missing S-shaped curves for controlling my pedal in *Forza Horizon 6*, for example.

Secondly, while you can remap the rear paddles, you only seem to be able to set them up as shortcuts for existing buttons. There’s seemingly no way to set them to macros, control system-level functionality, or map them to in-game functions. I’d love to be able to use them to control my media player or bring up the quest menu in *Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion Remastered,* but the range of options is limited enough that it slightly hampers the paddles’ utility for me.

Fortunately, these quibbles aren’t so severe that they detract from the Aeon Pro’s biggest draw for me: that battery system. It really can’t be overstated how fantastic it feels to never need to plug your pad in to charge. Not only did the full charge it got from its dock give me comfortably 12 hours of play per battery, but knowing I always had a full battery waiting in the wings meant I never had to experience range anxiety when I settled in for a marathon gaming sesh. Knowing I could play all weekend without ever needing to resort to an umbilical USB cable is a really freeing experience, and I’m here for it.

## Should I buy the SteelSeries Aeon Pro?

## Buy it if...

**You game on both PC and Xbox** Thanks to the Aeon Pro’s dual-platform system, you can switch between playing on your console and PC with the tap of a button — and without having to sacrifice 2.4GHz connectivity for either.**You never want to run out of battery again**  
Not only does the Aeon Pro charge when it’s on its base, but so does a backup hot-swappable battery, meaning you’ll never get caught short without charge during an epic GTA 6 session.

## Don't buy it if...

**You aren’t feeling flush** Look, this almost goes without saying. But at $259.99 / £229.99 / AU$529, the Aeon Pro is an unabashedly premium controller. So if you’re looking for bargain prices, look elsewhere.**You want full customizability**  
While it’s crammed with great features like auto-detecting game profiles on PC, SteelSeries GG software has a few limitations like a lack of trigger response curves and an ability to map system functions to rear paddles.

I also tested it alongside the SteelSeries Arctis Nova Pro Omni to see how you can use profiles to switch up the settings of both simultaneously.

When it comes to my own personal experience, I’ve been testing and writing about gadgets for over ten years. During that time, I’ve reviewed a range of controllers, headsets, and portable consoles. On top of that, I’ve been gaming for at least 35 years — having cut my teeth on early NES games, I’m now most likely to be gaming on my PC, Xbox Series X, Steam Deck, or Nintendo Switch 2.
