## 'I couldn’t even buy a Christmas bauble, then something struck me'

Why I couldn't be bothered to buy a Christmas bauble, says Fiona McCulloch, of Citizens Advice Bureau, York. Image: Pixabay

This year, for the first time ever, I can’t find my Christmas spirit or sparkle. Standing in Imaginarium, one of the most Christmassy little corners of York, it suddenly hit me. I COULDN’T BE BOTHERED TO BUY A CHRISTMAS BAUBLE.

This was an awful revelation. I usually love Christmas. Everything about it: putting the decorations up, opening the advent calendars, the excitement of choosing presents and, of course, the food on Christmas Day. I am normally firmly in the camp of believing that it is never too early to start celebrating.

So, what has changed this year?

![Fiona McCulloch, chief executive, Citizens Advice York.](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21721741.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Fiona McCulloch, chief officer, Citizens Advice York.

Obviously, the global situation is far from stable. There are three major wars raging, and the news seems to get worse with each passing week. The consequences are devastating, bringing unimaginable hardship to millions of people and leaving the rest of us with a general feeling of powerlessness. We watch, we worry, we despair, but what can we actually do?

Closer to home, we are being urged to fill our store cupboards with essentials to see us through three days of disruption.

Even this seemingly sensible bit of preparation appears to have become another opportunity to stir division. I saw a headline this week asking, "How to tell if your food stockpile makes you middle class". Words fail me… Surely the ability to prepare for an emergency shouldn't become another measure by which we judge one another?

Then there has been the news coverage of two rich and powerful men, with allegations and revelations of the abuse of women over many years.

Whilst exposing wrongdoing and securing accountability should be good news for women, it is difficult not to feel depressingly familiar frustration. Will anything ever really change? There have been so many investigations, so many headlines promising that "this will change everything". Yet how often does it feel as though we are still having exactly the same conversations?

When you strip much of this back, so much of what is happening comes down to how human beings treat one another. At Citizens Advice York, we see this up close and personal every single day.

Most people come to us because something awful, unexpected or unavoidable has happened in their lives. A relationship has broken down, a job has been lost, a benefit payment hasn't arrived, a landlord is threatening eviction or a bill has become impossible to pay. Sometimes life simply deals people a terrible hand.

But others come to us because someone has been unkind, unfair or downright cruel to them. And cruelty doesn't always make the headlines. It can be a threatening letter, an employer taking advantage, a landlord refusing to listen, or an organisation making someone feel small and powerless.

Indeed, in a widely reported court case this week, a defence barrister stated that "it is not unlawful to be unkind to someone". No, perhaps it isn't. But that doesn't mean it is harmless. Unkindness can have devastating and lifelong consequences for the person on the receiving end.

And this is where I think there is reason to be hopeful.

Every day, across York, Citizens Advice York sees the very best of people. Our staff and volunteers give their time, knowledge and compassion to help people navigate some of the most difficult moments of their lives. They listen without judgement, explain complicated systems, challenge unfair decisions, help people access the money they are entitled to and provide practical support when everything feels overwhelming.

Sometimes that support means helping someone keep a roof over their head. Sometimes it means making sure a family can afford to eat, helping someone escape an impossible debt situation or ensuring that a person who has been treated unfairly finally has someone in their corner.

And it isn't just about solving an immediate problem. It is about restoring dignity, confidence and a sense of control. It is about making sure people know they are not alone and that someone cares enough to help.

What strikes me is that none of this requires us to change the world overnight. It doesn't require enormous wealth, power or influence. It requires people who are prepared to give a little of themselves to make life better for someone else.

York has countless examples of this. Volunteers giving their time, neighbours looking out for one another, community groups supporting those in need, people donating food, money and essential items, and individuals quietly doing good things without expecting recognition.

Perhaps that is what I have been missing. We are surrounded by so much bad news that it is easy to forget the extraordinary amount of good that happens every day, often quietly and without a headline.

So, is all of this the cause of my lost Christmas spirit? Probably some of it. Perhaps I have been looking too hard at the big things I cannot change and not hard enough at the little things that I can.

I still can't promise that the bauble-buying enthusiasm has returned. But perhaps Christmas isn't really about the decorations, the presents or even the chocolate (although I am certainly not giving that up).

Perhaps it is about remembering to be kind, to notice other people, to offer help where we can and to recognise that even the smallest gesture can make an enormous difference to someone else's day.

Perhaps it just takes many little acts of kindness to restore Christmas spirit, maybe buy someone a bauble!
