A 95-year-old widow has been left facing a £1million-plus court bill after a 10-year neighbours' battle in a historic Kent village over a garden fence.

Millionaire businessman Neil Morgan, 65, slammed a judge as "not fit to judge vegetables at a village fete" during a decade-long court fight with nonagenarian neighbour Mary Egan.

Mr Morgan and wife Fiona, 61, went to war with elderly Mrs Egan in 2016 after a major disagreement over where the boundary between their multi-million pound country homes lay.

Ten years of "hostile litigation" later, and with Mrs Egan having spent over £1m on lawyers after numerous court hearings, the row ended with both sides claiming victory.

But after declaring the result a "score draw," a county court judge ordered each side to pay their own legal costs, leaving Mrs Egan having to foot her own £1m-plus lawyers' bill.

Last week, the widow appealed, claiming she was the true winner and that the "deplorable" conduct of her neighbour Mr Morgan - who represented himself and so spent far less - justified her seven-figure bill being paid by the couple next door instead.

![Fiona and Neil Morgan outside High Court after hearing in dispute with neighbour Mary Egan](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/8/55/NeilMorganFionaMorgan.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

As well as branding a judge "not fit to judge vegetables," Mr Morgan had called a lawyer a "lying hypocritical devious arse" and even termed his nonagenarian neighbour "malevolent" and "all-knowing self-appointed royalty" in case papers, she complained.

However, ruling on the appeal yesterday, the High Court said that despite Mr Morgan's "deplorable and unacceptable" language "towards opposing lawyers and their staff...judges and court staff," he and his wife do not have to pay their neighbour's costs.

Giving judgment on the appeal, Mr Justice Tappin said the row over a 60m-long sliver of grass, tree and scrub - varying between a few centimetres and around a metre wide - had resulted in "expenditure of time, cost and energy wholly out of proportion to the financial value of the area of land in dispute."

The two sets of neighbours live in adjacent multi-million pound properties in the affluent village of Sissinghurst, near Tunbridge Wells, in Kent.

The Morgans, who are directors of numerous businesses, own Sissinghurst Court, a 17th century timber-framed house, where even its landscaped gardens are Grade-II Listed, with a field attached to Mrs Egan's house bordering their land.

![Image shoes layout of dispute between Mary Egan and neighbours Neil and Fiona Morgan](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/8/44/MorganEganAerialAnnotated.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

For their neighbour, barrister Daniel Petrides told the judge that the genesis of the long-running dispute was the Morgans deciding to erect a new fence between their garden and Mrs Egan's land to the east.

"In September 2015, without prior consultation with or warning to Mrs Egan, they cut through the existing wooden fence along the boundary and erected a series of posts on Mrs Egan's land," he said.

"Mrs Egan's then solicitors sent a number of letters to the Morgans in October 2015 stating that they had trespassed on Mrs Egan's land, and asking them to remove the posts.

"At some point in late October/early November 2015, Mr and Mrs Morgan re-entered Mrs Egan's land, removed the posts, bulldozed the existing fence and erected another fence with wooden posts.

"On November 2, 2015, her solicitors sent a further letter stating that 'once again you have committed a flagrant trespass onto our client's land', but allowing the second fence to remain on certain terms, failing which Mrs Egan would remove the trespassory structure.

"The Morgans responded, asserting that the second fence was on their side of the boundary. At some point thereafter, Mrs Egan cut down the second fence.

"The Morgans then instructed contractors to, in late 2015, erect a temporary fence even further onto Mrs Egan's land and erect a further permanent fence with concrete posts in January 2016, the third fence."

The dispute spilled into litigation after Mr and Mrs Morgan sued their neighbour for destroying the second fence, claiming damages for materials and work.

However, Mrs Egan counterclaimed, accusing the couple of trespassing on her land by erecting the two fences and asking for a declaration that the boundary between the properties ran down the middle of a line of hedges and trees.

![The land dispute between Mary Egan and neighbours Neil and Fiona Morga](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/8/05/MorganEganAerialClean.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Following a trial at Maidstone County Court in 2021, Recorder John Dagnall dismissed the Morgans' claim about the damage and found that they had indeed trespassed by erecting the fences, awarding Mrs Egan £2,600 damages.

However, he rejected her bid to have the remaining third fence ripped out, instead allowing it to remain in place, declaring that to be the new boundary and awarding Mrs Egan £3,250 in damages in lieu of the injunction she had sought to have it removed.

At a subsequent hearing in 2024, he went on to order each side to pay their own costs of the dispute, describing the result as effectively a "score draw."

Whereas the Morgans had run up a six-figure bill early in the dispute, they had mostly represented themselves without lawyers afterwards - but Mrs Egan had used lawyers throughout, leaving her with a £1m-plus bill for the row.

Appealing last week, her barrister argued that it was wrong to leave her to foot her bill when she had been the real winner in the case, and amid "disgraceful conduct" from next door.

"That conduct was egregious and so inflated the cost of the proceedings that the only reasonable conclusion open to the judge would have been to make a costs order in Mrs Egan's favour," said Mr Petrides.

"The level of personal invective with which the claimants, and particularly Mr Morgan, have seen fit to bombard Mrs Egan's advisors, judges and other professionals is simply staggering."

He said much of the brunt was borne by junior and trainee solicitors, with one branded a "lying hypocritical devious arse of spectacular proportions" in an email and another a "conceited, sanctimonious, conniving hypocrite."

Another was accused of "sheer stupidity" and given the name "Mr I-qualified-last-week," while even judges did not miss out on the abuse, said the barrister.

In court during one earlier hearing in the case, Deputy District Judge Swales was called "an offensive, provocative and prejudiced man," following which a letter was sent to the court describing the judge as "moronic and genuinely not fit to judge vegetables at a village fete," said the barrister.

"Mrs Egan herself - a 95-year-old widow - has been described in correspondence as 'patronising', 'arrogant', 'malevolent' and 'frankly poisonous'," he continued.

"Some similarly colourful language - 'all-knowing self-appointed royalty' - about her even found its way into the particulars of claim before they were amended."

Mr Petrides said Recorder Dagnall had not treated the conduct properly when deciding who paid the costs of the case, having stated in court that "lawyers should be appropriately thick-skinned."

"It is not acceptable to treat junior lawyers as punchbags and the court should be astute to discourage such conduct in the course of litigation via the use of its costs powers," he said.

"The only reasonable response to very extreme abuse of the type described was a costs order against the claimants, on the indemnity basis."

Disputing that the case had been a draw, he said the Morgans' claim that Mrs Egan had unlawfully removed the second fence had been rejected, while she had won a ruling that her neighbours had committed trespasses and been awarded damages.

"In those circumstances, the judge's conclusion that the litigation was a 'score draw' is baffling," he said.

"The only correct analysis can be that a party which has defeated a claim against it and succeeded on its counterclaim is the 'successful party'. It is not a 'score draw', it is a 4-0 win.

"Mrs Egan, who is the defendant in these proceedings, did not choose to be sued. Having been sued, her costs of successfully vindicating her rights have been considerable: in excess of £1m over more than a decade of litigation.

"This has been in no small part due to the extraordinary and disproportionate approach of the claimants. For her to be denied any meaningful recovery in respect of those costs was profoundly unjust and wrong."

Again representing the couple, Mr Morgan told the court that, in relation to the conduct argument, they had been "provoked into reacting" and the limited occasions when he bit back were now been weaponised against them.

"Most of the time we bit our lip," he said.

"The few occasions when I responded pithily are now being used."

He also insisted that he and his wife were the true winners of case and that the "score draw" description was a "most generous outcome" for his neighbour.

The case had been almost entirely about the location of the boundary, with Mrs Egan insisting in her primary claim "from word go" that it ran down the middle of the trees and hedge separating the gardens - and she had not succeeded on that point.

Describing the case as a "battle" for the trees, he said the location of the boundary was 95 per cent of the case and "critical because if the exact position was in the trees then we lost our trees."

"She didn't litigate for £5,000 worth of damages - not for 10 years. She didn't get the rewards she was looking for. A small amount of damages doesn't bear a conversion into a victory. This wasn't about money. It never was.

"We put a fence up where there had always been a fence. We put it up because the old one had fallen down and our dogs and cats were getting out.

"We put the fence up where we believed the boundary had always been."

Dismissing Mrs Egan's appeal against the costs decision, Mr Justice Tappin said: "While I entirely agree with the appellant that the use of abusive language towards opposing lawyers and their staff, or towards judges and court staff, is deplorable and unacceptable, I do not agree that the way in which the judge dealt with these matters means that his decision was wrong in the relevant sense.

"He was best placed to assess the way in which Mr Morgan had conducted himself and I can see no basis for interfering with his view that his behaviour towards opposing lawyers and their staff and judges did not merit being given particular weight - or in the case of court staff, any weight - on the question of who was to bear the overall costs."

He added that Recorder Dagnall was not wrong to take account of the fact that Mrs Egan had failed in her case that the boundary was in the middle of the hedge.

"That had been the appellant’s case in pre-action correspondence, it was the core of her pleaded case and she sought a declaration to that effect," he said.

Bemoaning the amount of time, effort and money spent on the dispute, he said: "Recorder Dagnall noted that boundary disputes are often said to result in an expenditure of time, cost and energy wholly out of proportion to the financial value of the area of land in dispute.

"That is certainly true of this dispute. The judge noted that the dispute was about approximately 45 square metres of land over a boundary of approximately 60 metres in length, being either grass, scrub or tree, and of extremely limited, if any, usable or other value.

"However, the parties have been in litigation about that strip of land since 2016, and the appellant claims to have spent over £1million in legal costs, while the respondents have spent a six figure sum, even though for much of the litigation they have been representing themselves."

The decision means Mrs Egan is liable to pay her own seven-figure costs of the row.
