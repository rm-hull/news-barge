Love it or hate it, technology touches every aspect of our lives. Whether it’s checking your email when coming into work, catching a flight during the busy holiday season or checking an app to see how much money is in your bank account.

With these digital tools, you can manage your life with the click of a button. But on the flip side, your day can be thrown into chaos with a simple copper wire.

In the past week, millions of global Microsoft users lost access to its online office services and thousands of travelers were left stranded after a technical failure disrupted the country’s air traffic control system — and now customers of major banks are having trouble getting into their online accounts.

Danielle Lemons, vice president of operations at Nashville-based IT firm DirecTech Connect, told *The Independent* that people should be “concerned enough to plan, not to panic.”

“The real lesson is how many separate services quietly depend on the same few providers. When one of them goes down, email, file sharing, sign-ins and even phone systems can fail together, because they were all built on the same foundation,” Lemons said.

![In the past week, millions of global Microsoft users lost access to its online office services and thousands of travelers were left stranded after a technical failure disrupted the country’s air traffic control system — and now customers of major banks are having trouble getting into their online accounts](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/23/05/GettyImages-2304840726.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Gabrielle Hempel, security operations strategist at Colorado-based cybersecurity firm Exabeam, told *The Independent* that recent outages are “an important reminder that our understanding of technology risk needs to evolve alongside the systems we are building.

“We’ve become very good at discussing cybersecurity in terms of attackers, vulnerabilities, and data breaches, but that is only part of the risk landscape. Bad changes, failed dependencies, and aging infrastructure can cause disruption on the same scale,” Hempel said.

Fran Villalba Segarra, founder and CEO of INTERNXT, a cybersecurity company based in Spain, warned of society’s increasing reliance on technology in a statement to *The Independent*.

”The real cause for concern isn't a single master threat—it is the underlying reality that our digital economy has become so hyper-connected that isolated software bugs, database corruptions, or routing errors now trigger immediate, high-visibility disruption across millions of lives.

“We are living through an era of heightened digital fragility where the frequency of these outages simply mirrors how heavily modern society relies on complex, constantly updating codebases,” the expert said.

![Cybersecurity expert Gabrielle Hempel warned that the recent outages are ‘an important reminder that our understanding of technology risk needs to evolve alongside the systems we are building’](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/23/18/GettyImages-2304485779.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The Microsoft outage began in the middle of a workday on Thursday as millions were checking their emails through Outlook and conducting virtual meetings through Teams.

While some were making light of the situation by posting social media videos of themselves overjoyed that they could take a break from work, it showed just how reliant companies, governments and other public services are on the technology.

Microsoft apologized for the disruption and reassured users it was not related to cyber or AI agents.

“The incident was introduced by an issue during an earlier network capacity upgrade that surfaced following a recent network change. We have addressed the underlying issue and continue to restore service to remaining customers and regions,” the company wrote on X Friday.

Microsoft’s services were completely restored on Saturday.

Sharon Martin, the Director of Internal Innovation at Maryland-based cybersecurity firm Huntress, told *The Independent*, “When mass organisations rely on the same few providers, one fault becomes more than being one company's problem.

“Microsoft went down for one day last week, and businesses worldwide had to find workarounds just to get their jobs done,” Martin continued. “It's like a city with one bridge. You don't really see it as a huge problem until there is roadwork, and then no one can get out. It's not the council's fault; it's just that there isn't really an easy way around.”

Airlines, which were already affected by the Microsoft outage, were then told by the Federal Aviation Administration to ground all their flights after an issue arose from a contractor replacing old copper wire with fiber optic cable at a radio facility in El Dorado, Arkansas.

Nick Reese, a professor at New York University’s Center for Global Affairs who previously served as the director of emerging technology policy at the Homeland Security Department, told *The Independent*, “The FAA grounding all flights in the US is an extremely rare measure taken last week due to a system outage. While many were quick to think cyberattack or AI-enabled event, the real lesson here is about resilience.

“Our flight system is one example of many fragile systems on which we depend for our critical services. Old infrastructure that should have been replaced years ago is making our system more fragile every day. Fragility opens us to a host of potential problems from inherent system weakness to potential attacks in the future,” Reese said.

The expert warned that the outage “demonstrated to hostile actors how easy it would be to create a massive effect within the US that affects people worldwide,” adding, “This event should be a wake-up call to build resilience and replace aging systems, even if it will cost a lot of money.”

![Airlines, which were already affected by the Microsoft outage, were then told by the Federal Aviation Administration to ground all their flights on Friday after an issue arose from a contractor replacing old copper wire with fiber optic cable at a radio facility in El Dorado, Arkansas](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/23/35/GettyImages-2305027509.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The nationwide ground stop lasted for more than four hours on Friday.

More than 7,000 flights within, into, or out of the country were delayed, and more than 9,000 were canceled that day, according to flight tracking site FlightAware.

"I saw a couple of people in tears," Chad Brightbill, who was traveling through the Patrick Leahy Burlington International Airport in Vermont, told local outlet WPTZ.

Another passenger at Albuquerque International Sunport, Maritza Brummer, told KOAT, "I have to get home to my family, so now I'm a little concerned. Right. I'm thinking, what's going on?”

Unsurprisingly, President Donald Trump shifted the blame onto his Democratic rival, former President Joe Biden.

“The Biden Administration was an FAA disaster. Everything they touched turned bad. Big money spent unwisely, making an old and broken system worse than when they began,” he wrote on Truth Social Sunday.

![Thousands of customers at major banks, including JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Citigroup, Capital One and Wells Fargo and the peer-to-peer payment platform Zelle reported difficulty accessing online accounts on Monday](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/16/23/27/GettyImages-2271202498.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Following a tumultuous end of last week, a new outage sparked concerns.

Thousands of customers at major banks, including JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Citigroup, Capital One and Wells Fargo and the peer-to-peer payment platform Zelle reported difficulty accessing online accounts on Monday.

The issue stemmed from a technical issue at Chase that seemed to impact other financial services that the bank interacts with.

“JPMorganChase has experienced a temporary issue related to a software bug in on-premise hardware systems – there is no evidence this is cyber-related. It is taking longer than usual to service some customers,” Chase wrote on X Monday.

“Where we were party to a transaction involving other banks, those banks may have been impacted, but this is not a systemic issue. Customers still have access to their accounts and funds. We’re working diligently to resolve this by the end of the day today.”
