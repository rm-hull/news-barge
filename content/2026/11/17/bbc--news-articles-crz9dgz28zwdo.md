Footage shows Ukrainian missile approach Crimea bridge

**Stretching across the Kerch Strait in the Black Sea, the Crimean Bridge was always a symbol: for Russia, a prestige project cementing its claim to sovereign Ukrainian territory, and for Ukraine, a hated piece of infrastructure emblematic of Moscow's occupation of the Crimean Peninsula.**

A key section of the double-arched, 12-mile-long bridge now lies on the seabed, brought down by an arsenal of Ukrainian long-range weaponry in a seven-stage operation culminating in a dramatic collapse.

Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov has said that the bridge "will be rebuilt, and it will be even better than before".

But eight years after its unveiling, the bridge has now become material proof of Kyiv's ability to pierce through Moscow's military defences to hit even its most treasured and protected structures.

## How did the seven-stage operation play out?

The attack on the strategic Crimean Bridge was carried out gradually to cut across several layers of defence, Kyiv confirmed on Tuesday.

For several weeks Ukraine's Security Service and its General Staff phased out air defence systems, radars, electronic warfare assets, and other designated targets, President Volodymyr Zelensky said.

A significant strike had already been reported on 16 November, when drones hit the bridge at night. Traffic was paused briefly then reinstated, and although the arch over the road section was visibly damaged, the structure remained standing.

But that hit turned out to have been only the penultimate stage of a complex attack.

In the early hours of 17 November, the final strikes dealt a fatal blow to the weakened arch structure.

One verified video - filmed from a camera on board a Ukrainian-made Flamingo missile - shows the weapon approaching the main span of the bridge and heading towards the steel arches, with the footage freezing just before impact.

The Flamingo had potentially been modified to be controlled remotely, which improved its accuracy enough to strike the bridge supports, munitions expert Justin Bronk from the Royal United Services Institute said.

The missile's "very heavy warhead" made it "more suitable" for attacking the bridge's reinforced concrete spans and supports, Bronk added.

Zelensky later confirmed that several types of missiles and drones had been employed in the attack, which led the massive road arch - weighing thousands of tonnes and measuring about 227m in length - to collapse into the strait.

Daylight images taken from the shores of Crimea to the north show a section about 200m (660ft) long of the tarmac was too dragged below into the sea.

This is clearly critical damage to the crossing. Far from just being a decorative feature of the bridge, the arch played a structural role, transferring loads and supporting the span and helping to ensure the stability of the entire structure.

The parallel arch supporting the railway section has remained standing, although it may also have been damaged. All traffic on the bridge was initially suspended, though trains were later allowed to resume running.

But the damage to the bridge's main span is "catastrophic", Colin Capriani, a structural engineer from Monash University in Melbourne, Australia, told BBC Verify. It was "very hard to envisage" how the bridge could be rebuilt given it is clearly well within range of Ukrainian weaponry, Capriani added.

Major attacks in 2022 and 2023 had already caused serious damage to the road spans of the bridge, but the structure survived.

The difference this time is "the most critical and vulnerable part of the bridge has been hit", said David MacKenzie of engineering consultancy COWI, adding that "although the wreckage of the bridge can be cleared fairly quickly, reinstating full road traffic will take over a year".

## What effect could the collapse of the bridge have on Crimea?

Moscow uses Crimea as a military base, with airbases, troop concentrations, training grounds and artillery stockpiles. From there it also carries out strikes on the rest of the country and resupplies its troops in southern Ukraine.

The attack comes after a summer of frequent strikes on Crimea, dictated by Ukraine's stated goal of cutting the peninsula off from Russia. At the time, Kyiv vowed to turn the Crimean Peninsula "into an island".

Now - although it is too early to speak of a complete blockade of Crimea – the risk of a serious transport and logistics crisis has increased significantly.

There is still a land corridor running through the occupied parts of the Zaporizhzhia and Kherson regions. However, Ukraine has been systematically striking transport routes across northern Crimea, as well as bridges and other crossings in the area around Chongar, which has already caused problems with the delivery of fuel and other supplies, including military cargo.

Moscow can also still utilise the ferry crossing across the Kerch Strait. It could partly compensate for the loss of the bridge, but its capacity is very limited - and anything on the Black Sea has become vulnerable to attack. Resupplying Crimea by sea is risky, as Ukrainian naval and aerial drones operate extensively in the area.

In addition, debris from the Crimean Bridge - particularly its enormous arch - may have blocked the shipping channel. This would make it harder for larger vessels to pass through and create further problems for ports in the Sea of Azov, including the occupied cities of Mariupol and Berdyansk.

Away from the operational value of the damage to the bridge, the symbolic significance remains.

In 2018, Putin triumphantly drove over the bridge representing the unification of Russia and Crimea in a Russian-made truck. That section of the road now lays on the seabed.

Nearly five years since he launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine, Putin continues to wreak destruction on Ukraine's civilian population, but his most prized symbols of power are increasingly threatened by Kyiv's reach.
