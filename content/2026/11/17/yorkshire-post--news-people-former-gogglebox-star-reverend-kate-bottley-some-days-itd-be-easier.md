# Former Gogglebox star Reverend Kate Bottley: 'Some days it'd be easier not to believe a word of it, but most of the time faith makes me want to put one foot in front of the other'

## Yorkshire’s Reverend Kate Bottley, who rose to fame on Gogglebox, speaks to Laura Reid about people seeing faith as a pick and choose buffet, as she releases her second book.

![](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOmY4YTUwZjA2LWUyYTEtNDY1My04NGUwLTdhYWU0OWQzZmRmMDpkODRiZGI4Mi00MDY2LTQzYTItYmVmMC1hMjNkZjUxOTdmNDM=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=990&quality=70&enable=upscale)


By her own admission, the Reverend Kate Bottley wasn’t too keen when she was approached to do a second book. Writing her first, Have A Little Faith, published three years ago was a real labour of love.

“It gave me a new found respect for people who do writing for a living because it's really hard,” says the Sheffielder, who rose to fame on Channel 4’s Gogglebox in 2014 and now enjoys a unique career as vicar-broadcaster and writer. “It's one thing to walk out onto a quiz show or to set up myself for Gogglebox or even stand up in a pulpit. You do the thing and it's over and you don't have to think about it. But a book, it's proper work. It's proper graft.”

Her latest, just released, is The Faith Almanac: A Month-by-Month Guide to Christian Traditions – and it was actually a joy to write. “It was really exciting because an almanac is a little bit like my brain spilling out on a page. So it's got recipes in it. It's got drawings in it. It's got little stories. It's got poems. It's got all sorts of things. And because it's an almanac, it goes month by month and you can dip in and dip out. So, it's perfect for someone with a short attention span like me who likes a quick dopamine hit rather than an extended one.”

![Reverend Kate Bottley. Photo: John Mannell](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOjliMjUxYmY5LTk0YTAtNDMxYS04MjczLWUzNGM5OWNiOGE2ZTozMmQzMzRjMS01ZjhjLTQxNzEtODExMC00MTY1OWYyMjBlY2M=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)


The book looks at practices and key dates throughout the Christian calendar, from chalking the door post on Twelfth Night (even the dog gets involved in 51-year-old Reverend Kate’s home) to recipes for King Cake on January 6. Starting with Advent and Christmas, it explores the main religious ceremonies and holy days of each month, as well as age-old Christian advice for navigating milestones like births, marriages and funerals.

Reverend Kate hopes to make Christianity feel less remote and intimidating, and more accessible to people curious about exploring a spiritual dimension to modern life. “What we're seeing increasingly as the gap between church and culture gets wider is, particularly in my daughter's generation that's coming through (she's 25), people are seeing faith much more like a buffet rather than a set meal.

“Years ago when you checked into hospitals or that sort of thing you ticked the box that said Church of England and that was it. And now people are a bit more like well I like a bit of this and I like a bit of that and I want to light a candle if I go into church but I also think Diwali is great. Especially if we live in big some of our big wonderful multicultural cities that we've got in Yorkshire, we dip in and we dip out of all sorts of festivals and I think that's wonderful.”

“There was a time when people said they either were religious or weren’t religious,” she continues. “We’re now seeing in faith and belief that people feel much more free to go, well, actually I’ll have a little bit of that. It's like Countdown. Have two from the top and one from the bottom. Some people think that's dreadful, the picking and choosing. I see that as a really exciting opportunity (for Christianity) to continue to have a voice.”

In 2025, sales of bibles were at their highest since the 1990s according to book data firm NielsenIQ BookData. Against a backdrop of political divisions and the increasing cost of living, perhaps people are turning to faith in difficult times? “When things feel so unstable on the terra firmer, we do look to the mystical,” muses Reverend Kate, these days based in Retford. “If you are looking to faith to get certain answers, it's not going to give you that. But what it will give you is the things that we can't measure like hope.”

“During the pandemic, we saw a rise in people being interested in faith and the stuff that is not tangible, that we can't prove. And we've seen that before through history that there are peaks in faith and religious belief during times of crisis because people turn to stuff that they feel has been there for centuries in one way or another.

“We do this all the time, don't we? What do we do when granny dies? We hope she's in a better place. We might never have ticked that box on the census form that says Christian or Jewish or Muslim. We might not dream of ticking a box that says I believe in this, but I defy anybody when somebody they love dies to not think even just a little bit of hope. As human beings that's what we need. We need hope. We need love. We need joy. We need someone to sit with us in the darkness when we're feeling despair.”

Reverend Kate turned to Christianity in her teenage years after being invited to church by a schoolfriend, and then taking a fancy to the vicar’s son. They’ve been married for 28 years now and she often jokes how she went “for a snog and came away with a dog collar”. The point she makes though is that she wasn’t raised in a religious family – and so she’s open and permissive when it comes to people exploring faith.

“I think sometimes people find the doors locked or they're too scared to try it because they think they've got to believe all of it,” she says, adding: “That's what the book's about, saying ‘look, if you want this, it's here for you. If it's helpful, if it's going to enhance your life’…It might challenge you at times because my gaffer said some things that were really challenging about loving your neighbour and not judging people...But the stuff of faith doesn't just belong to the religious. You can have some of this if you want it.”

It’s the message she tries to spread through her work these days too, as a voluntary vicar in her local parish, and a media personality who presents Good Morning Sunday on BBC Radio 2 and is a regular on BBC’s Songs of Praise. After a career as a Church of England priest, and a couple of years on Gogglebox, the broadcasting work started to pick up “and it became obvious that I wasn't going to be able to keep my parish full-time,” she explains. “But I don't see it as any different. I'm a public Christian who has got a platform to speak on. It's just an unusual pulpit, that's all.

“Do I think anybody's going to fall on the knees in front of The Wheel on a Saturday night when I'm on it and go, ‘Oh, I've seen the lights. Hallelujah. Praise Jesus.’ No, of course not. But they might think, ‘Oh, she's religious and she seems alright’. It's just about normalising faith.

“And we can't do that unless people like me and Richard Coles and other people of faith are in the public eye because people aren't necessarily coming through the church door anymore to meet their vicar. So sometimes we have to be public and be present...We've done such a rubbish PR job on religion that anything that we can do to make people feel warm about faith and to know that it's always there for them (has to be a positive).”

As for her own experience of faith, it’s both the best and most frustrating thing in her life, Reverend Kate says. “Some days it'd be easier not to believe a word of it, wouldn't it? People think those of us that are professionally religious, we believe every single word of it every single day.

"We don't - on days when I have to do a funeral for somebody who's took their own life or a funeral for a little one or even a funeral for a 51-year-old woman with two kids like me, that's really tricky. Or when I had to stand at the bedside of my mum who was dying, it's hard to believe in God. It's hard to hold a faith.

“But most of the time it makes me want to put one foot in front of the other. It makes me feel loved. It makes me feel safe. And it makes me want to love other people. And if I'm wrong about this whole thing, and when I die there isn't anything, it's still not a bad way to live your life, loving other people as much as you possibly can.

“And I get it wrong all the time. I'm an absolute rat bag. I try not to judge others, and I do, and I try not to say rude words, and I do, and I try not to lie, and I do, because I'm human and fallible like everybody else. But knowing that I'm loved by the very creator of love in the universe makes me a better person. I think it gives me hope.”

- The Faith Almanac: A Month-by-Month Guide to Christian Traditions, by Reverend Kate Bottley, published by Bloomsbury, is out now.
