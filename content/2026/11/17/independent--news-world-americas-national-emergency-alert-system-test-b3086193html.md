Americans have been urged not to panic when the nationwide Emergency Alert System test takes place on Tuesday.

​Authorities say that the minute-long alarm will interrupt a number of services with a warning message and a familiar high-pitched tone at around 2.20 p.m. Eastern Time on November 17.

​The test has become the subject of rampant conspiracy theories on social media, with some claiming that the federally mandated alarm signals an impending crisis. Some have pointed to how governments in the United Kingdom, France, and Canada have urged residents to have supplies prepared in the event they are left without electricity or water.

FEMA uploaded a statement to social media urging people not to be concerned.

![The Federal Emergency Management Agency has warned Americans not to panic when an emergency alert goes out on November 17. This image shows a test from 2011](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/11/131975116..?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&auto=webp)

​“We’ve heard a lot of rumors about tomorrow’s EAS test. Let’s clear things up,” the statement reads. “It lasts 1 minute, and no action is required. No blackouts, no secret emergencies.

​“Just our legally mandated, routine test on TV and radio at approximately 2:20 PM ET.”

FEMA administrator Cameron Hamilton explained why the alarm is so important.

​“During an emergency, every second is crucial,” Hamilton said. “FEMA’s alert and warning systems ensure that no second is wasted getting you the critical information needed to keep you safe.

​“Operational tests, like the one to be conducted tomorrow, allow us to identify and fix potential issues before an actual emergency occurs.”

​Normal programming on radio, TV, cable systems, satellite radio, and wireline video providers will be interrupted nationwide when the alert goes out. The message will make clear that it is a test message and that no action is required by the public.

​Cell phones will not be affected by the test, unlike the most recent test of the system in October 2023, which included Wireless Emergency Alerts. A federal law passed in 2015 mandates that the alarm is tested every three years.

![The warning will go out on TV screens across the country, as well as on radio broadcasts. This message shows the 2011 test](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/17/08/131972220..?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&auto=webp)

​The signal used for the test is intentionally loud and attention-grabbing, with two tones at 853 Hz and 960 Hz played simultaneously.

​The warning can be sent out in both English and Spanish, according to the Federal Communications Commission.

​The alarm is part of the wider Integrated Public Alert and Warning System, which FEMA says is designed to warn people about sudden developments, such as a wildfire, flash flood or missing person.

​The test comes just days after a “technical issue” at Microsoft on Thursday caused a global outage that disrupted services including Outlook, Copilot and Teams. Then, on Friday, a rare nationwide ground stop on flights was issued because of what the FAA has described as a “technical problem”.

​On Monday, customers at several major banks had trouble accessing services due to what JPMorgan Chase described as a “software bug.”

​All three organizations - the FAA, Microsoft, and JPMorgan Chase - have stressed that the outages were not caused by cyberattacks.
