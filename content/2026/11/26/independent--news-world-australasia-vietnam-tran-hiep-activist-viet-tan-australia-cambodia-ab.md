Chloe Dang went to Bangkok’s Suvarnabhumi airport at midnight, desperate for information about her missing husband.

Tran Hiep was supposed to land in Thailand on a flight from Cambodia on 19 September, and Dang had planned to meet him at the airport. He never arrived.

The previous morning, they had spoken about birthday celebrations for his family. His mother and niece had birthdays coming up, and the couple were discussing where to celebrate.

It was an entirely ordinary conversation, Dang tells *The Independent*. Nothing suggested anything was wrong.

A few hours later, she messaged him casually about something at their home. He did not respond. At first, she assumed he was busy with work or would reply after returning to his hotel.

By evening, she had still heard nothing.

“I thought maybe he got back to the hotel really late or something like that,” she says. “So, that was the last contact with him on 18 September.”

When Hiep didn’t arrive the following day at the airport where the Australian couple were supposed to meet, Dang reported him missing to local police, who directed her to the tourist police and immigration authorities.

She soon learned from the airline that Hiep had never boarded the flight. “My mental health just broke down,” she tells *The Independent*. But she still had a glimmer of hope that he would show up.

![A vigil for Tran Hiep and fellow activists](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/10/16/20261118_183952.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Hiep is a member of Viet Tan, an international pro-democracy organisation designated a “terrorist” group by Vietnam in 2016. Viet Tan was engaged in armed resistance against Vietnam’s communist government in the 1980s. It re-emerged in 2004 as a political party advocating non-violent change.

However, according to Human Rights Watch, Vietnamese authorities have continued to arrest and imprison its members.

Hiep, 51, has been involved in activism for more than 20 years.

For two days after Hiep disappeared, Dang did not tell his family. Eventually, she told his mother and sister. For the first six days, only the three women knew about his disappearance. They contacted his colleagues, the people he had met on the ground in Cambodia.

Nothing emerged.

![Tran Hiep and Chloe Dang love to spend time outdoors](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/10/13/ChloeHiep-1.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Nearly 10 days later, Vietnamese authorities acknowledged that Hiep, Norwegian activist Nguyen Duc Thuan, and a Vietnamese national named Thai Cong Truong Son had been detained while attempting to cross into the country “illegally”.

Dang disputes the allegation. Hiep, an Australian citizen, had previously travelled to Vietnam on a visa, she said, while his Norwegian companion did not need one for trips of up to 45 days.

“Why would he enter Vietnam illegally when he could go legally?” she asked, describing the allegation as a pretext for their detention.

On 7 October, Cambodia’s interior ministry said Hiep and Thuan had entered the country, but there was no record of their departure. It “categorically” denied handing the two men over to Vietnamese authorities.

Elaine Pearson, Asia director at Human Rights Watch, described the disappearance of the activists as “apparent abduction”.

“The apparent abduction and detention of the foreign nationals raises many questions about the roles of both the Vietnamese and Cambodian governments,” she said in a statement then. “The Norwegian and Australian governments should press Vietnamese authorities to grant them immediate access to their nationals and to drop baseless charges against them.”

![A vigil for Tran Hiep and fellow activists](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/10/04/20261114_194028.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Hiep has since received two visits from Australian consular officials, on 15 October and 20 November, at a pretrial detention centre in Ho Chi Minh City.

He sent a message through the officials who visited him last month saying he was traumatised and struggling mentally. He denied crossing the border voluntarily or intending to enter Vietnam at all.

After the second visit, Dang received a more reassuring update about her husband’s physical condition. He was doing well and managing to look after himself, she said, although he remained traumatised.

Activists allege that Vietnam is intensifying its crackdown on Viet Tan and fellow dissident groups, restoring to arrests, trials in absentia, abductions, and pressure on foreign nations.

In 2019, Vietnamese authorities arrested Australian Viet Tan member Chau Van Kham and his local associates Nguyen Van Vien and Tran Van Quyen, and sentenced them to 12, 11 and 10 years in jail, respectively, on terrorism charges linked to their involvement with the group. Kham was released in July 2023, but Vien and Quyen remain imprisoned.

In 2018, they had arrested Le Quoc Binh in Binh Dinh province and accused him of working with Viet Tan and smuggling weapons from Cambodia to “carry out terrorist activities” in Vietnam.

Viet Tan rejected the allegations at the time, calling them “a ridiculous fabrication”.

![Tran Hiep and Chloe Dang](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/10/32/ChloeHiep-3.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Vietnamese security agencies have been accused of abducting dissidents in Germany and Thailand, including Trinh Xuan Thanh, Truong Duy Nhat, and Duong Van Thai.

In the past year, courts have tried several overseas nationals in absentia, convicting and sentencing them to lengthy prison terms.

Vietnam has also been accused of pressuring foreign nations like Thailand and Cambodia to return its citizens who have fled out of fear of arbitrary arrest, torture, or imprisonment.

“Being a human rights activist or helping people is not a crime,” Dang says. “You shouldn’t be punished for being a human rights activist or helping people.”

Vietnam currently holds more than 160 political prisoners, activists say, allegedly for exercising their right to free expression or advocating human rights and democracy. Dozens are in detention awaiting trial.

In the first nine months of 2026, Vietnamese courts sentenced 31 activists to lengthy prison terms, including bloggers Hoang Thi Hong Thai and Huynh Ngoc Tuan. In the same period, police allegedly arrested 46 people for criticising the government.

For a minute, when Dang forgets the serious allegations facing her husband and the torment of the last few months, she recalls a man who can be “cheeky” and “funny”.

His friends in Australia know him as someone who cracks jokes and makes everyone laugh, she says.

He loves music and plays the drums, guitar and keyboard. At gatherings, he often takes charge of the instruments while everyone else sings.

Hiep moved to Australia when he was 17. While still a student, he co-founded a group campaigning against human trafficking.

“He’s a normal man. He’s a good husband and also a good son to his family,” Dang tells *The Independent*.

Dang is now travelling across Europe to draw attention to his detention and seek international support for his release. She says the alleged abduction of her husband and the Norwegian activist is concerning.

“This time we are talking about Australians and Norwegians,” she says. “Next time it could be British, French, or anyone.”

![Tran Hiep and Chloe Dang](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/10/49/ChloeHiep-4.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

She acknowledges the Australian government’s efforts to secure her husband’s release, not least the decision by prime minister Anthony Albanese to raise the case at the recent Asean summit in Manila.

“But it has been two months already in this case,” she says. “My husband got abducted, so I still want our government to do more to bring my husband back home safely soon.”

She calls for action to tackle transnational repression. “We have to do something to stop it because it’s not just my husband. There have been many other cases recently, involving a lot of other people.”

*The Independent* contacted Vietnam’s embassy in Canberra for comment, but didn’t get an immediate response.

In a statement earlier, the embassy rejected the allegation of abduction as “unfounded and misleading”, and maintained that the men were arrested as per the law.

Viet Tan said the “abduction” of the activists in Cambodia and “their subsequent imprisonment in Vietnam constitute a grave violation of international law”. “Democracies, most notably Australia and Norway, must not tolerate this type of transnational repression carried out by the Vietnamese authorities,” the group said in a statement.
