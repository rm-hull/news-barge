The government has no plans to introduce a bank holiday to mark 1,100 years since England's unification, a minister has confirmed, despite calls to commemorate the date.

A gathering organised by King Athelstan on 12 July, 927 at "Eamotum" in Cumbria is widely regarded as the moment the English people were united under a single monarch.

King Athelstan, already king of Mercia and Wessex, brought additional realms – including Northumbria – under his authority, after which he assumed the title Rex Anglorum, King of the English.

Reform UK MP Andrew Rosindell has proposed a one-off public holiday on 12 July, 2027 to celebrate the 1,100th anniversary of the nation's creation.

He said: "It’s no overstatement to say that this is an occasion that needs to be celebrated and marked.

"In other nations, there would be postage stamps, there’d be coins, there’d be bank holidays and other kinds of celebrations, but it appears that nothing is happening in England.

"Will the minister please treat this unique occasion with the dignity it deserves to ensure that all the people of England can celebrate their proud English heritage?"

![A meeting arranged by King Athelstan on July 12, 927 at "Eamotum" in Cumbria is widely considered to represent the unification of the English people under one ruler](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/13/51244823..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Responding, business secretary Jonathan Reynolds acknowledged the importance of the date, but said he could not promise a bank holiday.

He said: “Being responsible for the designation of bank holidays is not as much fun a power as it sounds, because Members of Parliament do tend to ask for quite a lot.

“I’ve got to tell him there are no plans to establish one on this occasion, but I’m grateful for the opportunity to mark this milestone in the House.

“I can’t promise a bank holiday, but I think this is an important occasion, an important time to recognise the achievements of Athelstan.

“I think we might consider his work on strengthening English government in law and regulating our coinage relevant to this department.

“I think he took quite significant measures against theft and corruption.

“But I’d also say, when you look at the history, he had a significant set of achievements in forging stronger links with leaders in continental Europe and I’m pleased that side of the House is recognising the importance of that.”

The call from the Reform benches comes after a petition was started on the government’s official petition website by Derek Hilling that attracted 454 signatures.

![Business secretary Jonathan Reynolds acknowledged the importance of the date, but said he could not promise a bank holiday](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/13/2306626683..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The petition called for Parliament to “help most people in England celebrate the occasion” and for the government to “financially support those charities who are organising celebrations for the occasion”.

Mr Hilling wrote: “For the first time all the people of England lived under one ruler and one law code.

“The 12th July is to England as 4th July is to America. England’s creation in 927 should be officially recognised.”

While the government has stopped short of offering a bank holiday to celebrate the milestone, Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy promised earlier this month that ministers will organise a “fitting commemoration”.

She said ceremonials minister Stephanie Peacock would work with the Conservative Party after it called for “a moment of national joy”.

Shadow culture secretary Rebecca Paul said there should be “coins, stamps and special lessons in schools, Athelstan on Trafalgar Square’s fourth plinth and maybe even a visit by His Majesty to this Parliament” to mark the occasion.

Campaigners including historian Tom Holland have called for the anniversary to be properly celebrated.

Liberal Democrat leader Sir Ed Davey has previously hailed King Athelstan as a historical hero of his, and last year suggested the milestone should be marked with a bank holiday.
