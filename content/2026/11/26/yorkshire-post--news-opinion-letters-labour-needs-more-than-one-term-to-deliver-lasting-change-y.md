# Labour needs more than one term to deliver lasting change: Yorkshire Post Letters

## David Ingham, Ripon.

At this year's Labour Party Conference, Norway's Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre made an important point about the need for Labour to have more than one term in government if it is to deliver lasting change in Britain.

His message deserves serious consideration. Britain is not starting from a clean slate. After years of Conservative austerity, underinvestment and short-term political decisions, the country has to be rebuilt as well as reformed.

Our public services are under enormous pressure. Councils have lost much of their capacity to provide preventative services, our NHS faces huge backlogs, and communities have seen the gradual disappearance of youth services, libraries and other essential facilities.

Repairing this damage will take more than a few spending announcements or a change of minister. It requires sustained investment, long-term planning and the determination to see policies through. Kemi Badenoch's response to the suggestion that Britain needs a longer-term approach illustrates the problem with our increasingly short-term political debate. Her insistence that we cannot wait ten years and need results now is understandable as a demand for urgency, but it misses the point.

Nobody is suggesting that people should wait ten years for better jobs, improved public services or a decent standard of living. The question is whether we are prepared to make the sustained commitments needed to achieve those things.

Here in Yorkshire, we have a practical example. The York and North Yorkshire Combined Authority has developed a ten-year Growth Plan, aiming to create better jobs, improve skills, deliver more affordable homes and transform transport connections.

These ambitions cannot be delivered by political soundbites or quick fixes. They need continuity, investment and effective cooperation between national government, the mayor, councils, businesses and communities.

The same principle applies nationally. Rebuilding the NHS, improving social care, giving young people genuine opportunities and restoring confidence in local government are long-term tasks. Cutting investment to achieve immediate savings may look attractive on a balance sheet, but it can create much greater costs for society in the years ahead.

Of course, Labour must demonstrate progress during this Parliament. People are entitled to see improvements in their lives, and ministers must be held accountable for delivering them. But we should not confuse the need for urgent action with the expectation that deep-rooted problems can be solved overnight.

Good ministers need time to turn policies into practical improvements, to learn from experience and to make sure that changes become embedded rather than being abandoned at the next change of government.

If we are serious about rebuilding Britain after austerity, we must stop treating politics as a succession of five-year projects. We need the ambition to think in decades, the patience to invest for the future and the political courage to see things through. Hope is not delivered by promises of instant change. It becomes reality when we have the determination to build something that lasts.
