Many older Australians are dying before they can access home support, paying exorbitant fees, and being pushed into hospitals and residential care before they are ready because of a flawed federal government aged care program, a Senate inquiry has found.

The inquiry is examining the impact of the Support at Home program, which launched in November 2025 and has been plagued by complaints of making care harder to access and more expensive. The inquiry was due to finish in November, but has been extended for a further year after receiving almost 300 submissions.

The aged care algorithm, used to assess the home support needs of older people and allocate funding, has been a particular focus of the inquiry.

The inquiry’s interim report, tabled on Thursday, recommended human override of algorithmic assessments be reinstated after evidence that older people are being harmed due to, in many cases, being underassessed and left without adequate support.

The report describes how the algorithm prioritises physical mobility over cognitive decline, meaning people with severe dementia can be classified as “independent” and denied adequate funding. A single point difference on the algorithm’s decision tree can cost an applicant nearly $40,000 a year in funding.

The appeal system can be lengthy and onerous, the inquiry heard, and assessors are barred from overriding any incorrect algorithmic result. It means people are being pushed into residential care homes before they are ready or end up in hospital as their health deteriorates, the inquiry heard.

As of June, more than 106,000 Australians were on the official waiting list for a Support at Home place. In October, the health department reported that 6,436 people had died while waiting for their approved level of funding.

People who are healthy are also left in hospitals for months because they are waiting for a Support at Home assessment, funding allocation, or access to support, the inquiry heard.

The inquiry heard the case of a woman with heart and renal failure who was discharged from hospital as a “medium priority” for Support at Home. She died four months later still waiting for funding to help her eat and administer critical medication.

The Greens senator Penny Allman-Payne, the inquiry chair, said “Labor has clearly botched the implementation of Support at Home and added baffling layers of bureaucracy and complexity, but fundamentally this is a problem of design”.

“Not only are providers jacking up their prices, the system rations and denies care, systematically underassessing need and priority and creating artificial scarcity by withholding packages,” she said.

## Algorithm in focus

In September, freedom of information requests by Guardian Australia revealed health officials from several states and the Northern Territory were so alarmed by the impact of the algorithm that they kept a log of hundreds of cases where vulnerable people were severely under-assessed, sending these cases to the federal government.

Days before the algorithm was rolled out in November, the department told aged care assessment organisations that their assessors would be barred from overriding incorrect algorithmic outcomes, despite assurances that they would have the final say.

The government has stopped short of allowing assessors to immediately correct incorrect algorithm decisions, despite growing calls from clinicians, advocacy groups and politicians throughout the inquiry. Instead, the aged care minister, Sam Rae, said in July that assessors would have the option to escalate cases to a system governor.

The government has also announced an independent review of the aged care classification and prioritisation assessment.

“Many of the adjustments to date appear to be reactive in nature, driven by external pressures – including successive inquiries by this committee – rather than robust monitoring and improvement processes put in place by the government,” the interim report found.

## High prices and delays

The aged care royal commission, which handed down its final report in 2021, found most older Australians want to remain at home and supporting them to safely do so prevents premature decline and strain on the hospital system.

The Support at Home reforms were supposed to help people stay at home longer and give them greater control over their support, with a focus on human rights.

Instead, aged care assessors have described elements of the reforms as “inhumane”.

The inquiry heard funding is being eaten up by exorbitant prices, with reports of prices for some services, including meals and cleaning, tripling under the system.

The program requires people to make payments for these services, but the inquiry heard that because prices had increased so much and people need to pay up to 80% of the cost, their co-contribution is causing financial distress and forcing older people to cut back on essential support.

An example in the report was crutches, which usually cost about $50, rising to $1,800 since Support at Home began as providers increased prices and administrative hurdles were added, with a three-month wait to obtain them.

There are delays at many points of the program, with the report describing how “older people go without, opt out and die without”. There were 4,812 older Australians approved for Support at Home who died before receiving a single service, the inquiry heard.

“My dad passed away on 29 April, two days short of turning 92, and his package arrived three weeks later in May,” one witness told the inquiry.

The shadow health minister, Anne Ruston, said the committee found many of the problems “were either known, or predicted, prior to the rollout”.

“Older Australians and their families have spent a year telling this government that Support at Home is broken,” she said. “Labor dismissed them, and now a Senate committee has confirmed every word.”
