# Andy Burnham faces opposing choices on how to tackle nation's benefits bill: Jayne Dowle

## We wait ages for a much-needed review of benefits then two come along at once.

Last week we had former Secretary of State for Health and Social Care Alan Milburn, arguing that benefit entitlement for NEETs (young people not in education, employment or training) should be curtailed to encourage them to engage with reality.

This week, step forward today’s minister for social security and disability Sir Stephen Timms, whose main point in his own review, Making PIP fit for purpose: A new vision, is that henceforth, all disability benefits should be tailored very specifically to each recipient’s needs.

The differences between the two recommendations sound technical but delve deeper and they promise trouble ahead for Prime Minister Andy Burnham.

![Prime Minister Andy Burnham](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOjNjMTA0ZTRmLWMzNzgtNDAyYy04Mzk5LTc0ZDc1OTFhZmI2ZDo3Njc4ZjQ1Ny0zYjBlLTRiNGEtOGExZC0xZDU5MWYxYmZmMzg=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)


First of all, Burnham is going to have to decide - broadly - which path his government should attempt for younger benefit claimants. This will involve steering very carefully through warring Cabinet factions. Then, he will have to face Labour backbenchers, some of whom are against benefit reform in any form.

Yet, above all, Timms’ point makes perfect sense. At present, the system to claim the main disability benefit of Personal Independence Payment (PIP), is clearly being played by those claiming spurious mental health conditions, whilst those with chronic ill-health, limiting physical disabilities, of facing end of life, are too often subject to humiliating repeat assessments.

However, given that around 4.09 million people in England and Wales were entitled to claim PIP as of July 2026, up by 262,045 (7 per cent) from 3.83 million a year earlier this “tailoring” could be quite a tall order.

Timms makes a strong case for “a fundamental overhaul of the current assessment process, to better recognise people’s varying costs and needs”, whilst prioritising those with the most severe conditions and end-of-life care.

For this cohort, his review recommends a fast track which is less burdensome, so assessment capacity is freed up for other, more questionable cases. And there are countless numbers of those.

PIP, paid weekly at £76.70 (standard) and £114.60 (enhanced) is a non-means-tested, non-taxable cash benefit. It was originally designed to help disabled people who need support with everyday tasks such as preparing food, eating, managing medication, washing, dressing, communicating, reading signs, socialising and budgeting.

The mobility component under enhanced PIP is especially controversial; if an individual qualifies for this, proving that they need help with leaving the house and physically moving around, they may qualify to lease a car under the Motability Scheme.

In response to huge public furore over people obtaining “free cars” through this scheme the government is already clamping down on the rules.

There have been “marked shifts in the nature of disability and ill health” since PIP was created 13 years ago under the Coalition government, Timms argued, writing in The Times ahead of his findings being published. This includes “substantial growth in claims for mental health and neurodiverse conditions as well as changes to wider society and the workplace”.

Under his proposals, if claimants should be found to not have additional costs or care needs after all, Timms recommends that they should be signposted to services and support instead of receiving weekly payments.

He undertook extensive consultation with more than 38,000 people and organisations, during which disabled people and their advocates pushed for a rethink of the current system of points-based assessments and the return of indefinite awards for people with permanent conditions, phased out in 2013.

Research by the anti-poverty charity Z2K shows that last year, there were more than half a million reassessments of people with conditions such as cerebral palsy, learning disabilities and amputations. Others, meanwhile, languish untroubled.

However, there is trouble on the agenda. Two reviews delivering their findings in two weeks, and they disagree fundamentally with each other.

By its nature, Timms’ review makes only broad recommendations. If they are taken up, it will be down to government to implement a system of delivery and set in place new structures for reforms to work.

Also, there will be the big question of deciding whether any changes should apply to only new claimants, or if those already in receipt of PIP will be left undisturbed.

Meanwhile, under his own investigation into tackling the NEETs crisis Alan Milburn is calling for PIP (personal independence payment) to be scrapped for younger claimants. He also argues that the health element of universal credit should be replaced by an additional cost allowance, part of a wider network of job-based help for this age group.

Milburn projects his tough approach would deliver £3.6bn in annual benefit savings over the next decade, which is a quick win comparing to Timms’ recommendation of setting up a new and complex system of checking millions of claims in detail.

What will happen next? Who can say? What is certain is that in terms of proposals to tackle the ever-burgeoning benefits bill threatening to bankrupt Britain, Andy Burnham finds himself with an embarrassment of riches.

Let us hope that this does not end up placing the Prime Minister in an embarrassing position, unable to decide a clear way ahead for any recommended version of radical reform.
