Young people living in the worst housing conditions face a lower likelihood of securing five good GCSEs, including English and maths, or progressing to A-levels, new findings reveal.

According to research conducted for the Sutton Trust, the standard of housing is responsible for 10 per cent of the disparity in GCSE results between students from the highest and lowest-income households.

Drawing on three UK studies, the report highlights how disadvantaged teenagers frequently lack a quiet, dedicated space to complete their schoolwork.

At age 11, over half (52 per cent) of children from the poorest fifth of households shared a bedroom, compared with 7 per cent of those in the wealthiest fifth.

Out of all the conditions evaluated, experiencing homelessness corresponds with the most severe divide in academic achievement.

Teenagers whose families experienced homelessness by the age of 14 were 18 percentage points less likely to obtain five good GCSEs including English and maths compared with their peers.

In light of the findings, the charity is urging the government to evaluate housing policy through its impact on children's academic achievement, health and general wellbeing.

Families with school-age children, especially those approaching their GCSEs and A-levels, could be prioritised in policies to reduce overcrowding, it said.

Nick Harrison, chief executive of the Sutton Trust, said: “The country’s housing crisis is blighting the prospects of a generation, worsened by a chronic failure to build enough homes.”

He added: “The link between a child’s housing situation and their education shouldn’t be underestimated.

“Even the brightest child will struggle to succeed at school if they don’t have a safe and stable learning environment at home.

“Now is the perfect time for the Government to better coordinate housing and education outcomes, particularly as the Office for Equality of Opportunity and a new Class Unit now work closely with the Ministry of Housing.”

Academics at University College London (UCL) found that lacking a quiet place to study predicts lower exam results at age 18 than their peers, despite being level at the end of primary school.

The social mobility charity has called for schools and local authorities to provide young people with access to quiet places to study outside the home.

These could be delivered through extended school opening hours, homework and study clubs, and libraries, the Sutton Trust has suggested.

Report co-author Gill Wyness, professor of economics at UCL, said: “We rightly focus on the role of socio-economic disadvantage in shaping young people’s opportunities, but housing is too often left out of the picture.

“If we’re serious about improving educational outcomes and social mobility, we need to pay much greater attention to the housing circumstances in which young people are growing up.”

Paul Whiteman, general secretary of school leaders’ union NAHT, said: “No child should have their education disrupted because they do not have a safe and secure place to live.

“Yet school leaders see first-hand the impact housing insecurity can have on children and families, from poorer attendance and lower attainment to increased anxiety and challenges with behaviour and wellbeing.”

He added: “Teachers and school leaders do everything they can to provide stability and support, but schools cannot solve these problems alone.”

Pepe Di’Iasio, general secretary of the Association of School and College Leaders (ASCL), said: “Sadly, it is just one of the problems caused by high levels of socioeconomic disadvantage both on the wellbeing of children and on their capacity to learn when they arrive at school cold and hungry.

“It is clear that life is getting even tougher for many struggling families because of the rising cost of living – and the huge increase in the energy price cap will make matters even worse.”
