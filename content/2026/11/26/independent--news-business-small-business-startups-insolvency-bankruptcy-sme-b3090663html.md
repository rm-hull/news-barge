It is often said that most small businesses fail, and it is certainly true that getting one off the ground is hard.

In many cases the idea just doesn’t work in a financially viable way.

For small businesses that actually establish a foothold and make money, however, the picture is more complex - and optimistic.

The smallest businesses can have certain things in their favour relative to larger companies when it comes to survival.

## Chances of insolvency

Research by money.co.uk found the smallest businesses, with either zero (sole trader) or one employee have a lower insolvency rate than the national average for all businesses.

The researchers found 82 in10,000 of these businesses went under versus the national average of 116 in 10,000. The data was pulled from the Insolvency Service’s Business Insolvency Demography statistics.

One possible explanation for this is having no employees means a company can last longer while not making enough money. If a business needs staff and can no longer pay them, there is little choice but to shut up shop.

A sole trader may be able to ride out hard times longer as they can cut back on their own personal spending.

Employees are unlikely to accept a big cut to their finances, whereas a business owner may see it as a sacrifice worth making if they believe better times are coming.

There are also taxes like National Insurance and other obligations involved, such as minimum wage and pension contributions, that can make bigger businesses unsustainable.

For a sole trader, running at a loss means little or no tax to pay, as tax is based on profits, and minimum wage does not apply.

Obviously sole traders cannot run at a loss for an extended period of time, but if they have savings they *can* survive for a while until they drum up more business.

![Smaller businesses have proven adept at thriving](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/08/19/16/58/iStock-977418884.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

An insolvency generally results from a specific bill or set of bills that cannot be paid. A sole trader will often have more flexibility to avoid getting to this point.

As well as size, the age of the business seems to play a role is its survival chances. Money.co.uk found businesses under two years old have the lowest insolvency rate of any age group at 37 in every 10,000.

## Most at risk businesses

The data shows the highest insolvency rates were recorded among businesses with 20-49 employees, and those aged seven to 10 years.

While the Insolvency Service does not provide reasoning for the findings, there are implied explanations.

Businesses of this size can often be caught between a rock and a hard place. They are big enough to have a large wage bill to pay, and invoices coming in from multiple suppliers.

Yet they are often too small to gain favourable rates on financing that large companies often benefit from.

Big companies have tools for raising money that small businesses do not. These include issuing shares or bonds to investors, for example.

They may also have more leverage over their suppliers than small companies, so can negotiate better payment schedules and rates.

The fact that the seven to 10 year age is more vulnerable would seem to be a consequence of it taking that amount of time to grow to a size where there are that many employees to pay, on average.

## 4 top tips to avoid running out of money

Matt Browning, money.co.uk business loans expert, has four top tips for small business owners looking to access finance.

First, use a comparison service to see a cross section of the market, not just your bank. With specialist and challenger lenders now responsible for the majority (68 per cent) of SME lending, a business that only checks with its existing bank may be missing better rates elsewhere.

Secondly, Browning said look at government-backed schemes. Schemes such as Start Up Loans, delivered by the government-backed British Business Bank, are designed to help new, growing businesses get funding.

Thirdly, you should be clear about how much funding you need and what you'll use it for, Browning added. Whether looking to expand, cover a cash flow shortfall or buy new equipment, understanding how much you need and how you will repay it can help you narrow down your options and prepare a stronger application.

The fourth and final of his tips is to check your credit file before you apply. As new businesses often have little trading history, lenders will often look at personal credit files too. Address anything on there that could negatively impact your credit score.
