Maria woke up at 4.40am every day for three years, rousing her two daughters 20 minutes later so they could take two trains and a bus to get to school on time.

Placed in temporary accommodation on the other side of London by their council, the early-years worker and her teenage children spent three hours a day commuting back to their community of 12 years.

The exhaustion and stress took a toll: Maria developed alopecia, losing nearly a third of her hair, and began taking antidepressants for the first time.

Her eldest daughter, who is studying for her GCSEs and plans to study film at university, began showing signs of depression too. Within three months, her grades began to drop.

“She was a very happy girl, always doing very well at school, and then she started to stop caring,” says Maria, whose daughters’ school friends have no idea they are homeless.

“They were arriving home [at 5pm], having a bath or something, and they were so tired that they'd go to sleep in their bed. I had to wake them up: 'Girls, please, you have homework, you have something to do’.”

While the family have since been moved to closer, more suitable temporary accommodation, Maria’s experience highlights how homelessness is hitting young people’s prospects – a link laid bare in new research from Shelter and UCL.

Analysis of over 9,000 young adults from the Millennium Cohort Study – tracking children born across the UK between 2000 and 2002 – shows one in four (26 per cent) who experienced homelessness as children are not in education, employment or training (Neet) in early adulthood, twice the rate of those who were not homeless (13 per cent).

Government figures show homelessness has risen by 60 per cent over the last decade, with a record 177,530 children in England currently in temporary accommodation.

The findings follow Alan Milburn’s landmark government-commissioned review into young people and work, which warned of a “generational fault line” with nearly one million 16- to 24-year-olds now classed as Neet – costing the economy £125bn a year.

The former health minister’s report found that six in 10 of these young people have never held a job, but argued that the crisis is rooted in poverty and disadvantage rather than a lack of ambition.

More than half (58 per cent) of young people who experienced childhood homelessness have no A-levels or equivalent, compared to 38 per cent of their peers, Shelter and UCL’s Centre for Longitudinal Studies found.

Meanwhile, more than one in five (22 per cent) lack five pass-level GCSEs, versus 12 per cent among all others.

Overall, one in 20 (5 per cent) of the Gen-Z respondents reported childhood homelessness, including sofa-surfing and temporary accommodation.

Ri, who was made homeless at 15 while studying for her GCSEs, had to share a single bed with her brother in an overnight placement with no heating or hot water while her mother slept on the floor.

“We moved around a lot and would live out of our boxes for months at a time,” she said, “one time the owners just turned up at our door and we were told we had to go by the weekend.”

![Housing secretary Angela Rayner said: ‘Every child deserves the opportunity to build a better life, and a safe, secure home is vital to this.’](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/24/10/2306627485..?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&trim=0%2C0%2C0%2C0&auto=webp)

Though she later gained a first-class degree and is now settled in a social home, she says the upheaval left her “still scared of moving” as an adult.

As ministers prepare a white paper in response to Mr Milburn’s call for a “whole system reset”, campaigners say that social housing should be central to tackling the Neet crisis.

Mairi MacRae, Shelter’s director of policy and campaigns, said: “Homelessness can tear through every part of a child’s life and leave long-lasting scars. With a record number of children growing up homeless, we must act now to ensure their futures aren’t written off.

"If the government wants to invest in the success of future generations, it must deliver the council housing revolution it promised and unfreeze housing benefit to tackle homelessness. Social homes are key to levelling the playing field – giving children the safety and stability they need to thrive.”

Housing secretary Angela Rayner said: “Every child deserves the opportunity to build a better life, and a safe, secure home is vital to this.

“That’s why we’re ending the unlawful use of B&Bs for families, tackling poor housing standards through Awaab’s Law and putting a new duty on councils to ensure children in temporary accommodation get the support they need at school.

“This comes alongside wider action to tackle child poverty, including expanding free school meals and scrapping the two-child limit – helping give more children security and the best possible life chances.”

It comes after *The Independent*joined forces with charity* EveryYouth* to launch Project Nest – a groundbreaking appeal backed by prime minister Andy Burnham, which will give young homeless people the best opportunity to succeed by providing a secure place to call home.
