A mother whose newborn son died of devastating brain injuries caused by an infection has demanded change after driving her baby 20 miles to hospital when an ambulance didn’t arrive.

Ezrah Pollard was 19 days old when he started showing “red flag markers” of infection.

His parents rang NHS 111 and were told help was coming, but when they chased emergency services an hour later, an ambulance had not yet been dispatched.

It later emerged that there was a four-hour wait for category two calls – the second-highest priority – that night.

It comes as a new coroner’s report said there was an avoidable delay in getting Ezrah antibiotics, with concerns raised that future deaths may happen if no action is taken.

Ezrah’s parents Sarah Doolin and Craig Pollard, from Cornwall, drove their son 20 miles to the nearest hospital, where it was clear to staff he was seriously ill.

He was then transferred to hospital in Bristol, where a CT scan revealed devastating brain injuries.

![Sarah Doolin, 39, is calling for the way newborns are prioritised in terms of ambulance response times following the death of her son Ezrah Pollard in 2022](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/09/25145924-f2d6133e-d83a-4f7b-8fc7-80ca9eec4c61.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Ms Doolin, 39, said that Ezrah was seen by a senior midwife the day before he fell ill in March 2022, who was “thrilled with his progress”.

She had initially thought Ezrah had a cold, but he became less interested in feeding as the day progressed.

“I was still completely unaware of the idea that he could be very sick,” Ms Doolin said.

“I thought maybe he needed a dirty nappy to clear his belly and I was thinking around things like a little cold.

“By early evening things were more alarming. Ezrah’s breathing had changed, and I now know this is called a grunt and it’s a sign of infection. But, to me, it sounded like he was coughing gently.”

Ezrah’s parents called NHS 111 at roughly 8.30pm and were transferred to the South West Ambulance Service.

They were told a paramedic would assess their son at the house and were told to wait for its arrival.

![A coroner criticised delays in getting to the family's home in Cornwall despite the 19-day-old Ezrah showing 'red flag markers' of infection](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/10/01M8B9N9AJ72B3RJ0FFS6X7JN6.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The call was classed as category two, which usually has an average response time of 18 minutes.

The couple chased the ambulance an hour later but it had still not been dispatched “owing to significant operational pressures”, according to a new prevention of future deaths report by coroner Andrew Cox.

Ezrah’s parents drove him to the Royal Cornwall Hospital, which is roughly 20 miles from their home in St Martin, and from there he was transferred to Bristol.

Ezrah died of an infection caused by group B streptococcus (GBS), a harmless bacteria found in between 20 per cent and 40 per cent of people.

During birth it can sometimes pass to the baby, causing serious illness, with symptoms usually starting within seven days.

Ezrah had late onset GBS, which is less common and develops in babies aged between seven days and three months.

An inquest was told there is currently no pathway for babies up to the age of three months to have a category one ambulance response, which is seven minutes on average.

Ezrah was classed as category two despite having “red flag markers” of infection, including “fever, grunting, breathlessness, ‘deathly colour’ and drowsiness”, the report said.

![Ezrah’s parents drove him to the Royal Cornwall Hospital, which is roughly 20 miles from their home in St Martin, and from there he was transferred to Bristol](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/10/01M8B9N6SFF5XR1AT98AZYSWT9.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Ms Doolin told PA: “I would love to see that change. I don’t understand why a baby is not considered category one.

“Newborn babies aren’t ever category one unless they’ve stopped breathing. In which case, by that point, it’s too late.”

According to NHS England, a newborn would be a category one response if they were unconscious, fitting, choking on solids, in cardiac arrest, or suffering major blood loss.

Neither NHS 111 nor the South West Ambulance Service were able to provide Ezrah’s parents with information on why the ambulance was delayed, Mr Cox’s report said.

It stressed that “Ezrah required immediate antibiotics, yet their administration was avoidably delayed”.

At the time, ambulances were taking four hours on average to respond to category two cases.

The report said that even if a category one response is not “considered appropriate” for young babies with signs of infection, parents should be advised to take their child to hospital immediately.

Concerns were also raised about a lack of information about Ezrah when he arrived at hospital, despite his parents telling 111 his history.

Ms Doolin called for better communication, saying she had to “repeat everything multiple times to different services”.

She told PA she feels “hopeful” that Ezrah’s inquest could spark change nationally.

![Ezrah was classed as category two despite having 'red flag markers” of infection, including 'fever, grunting, breathlessness, deathly colour and drowsiness', the report said](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/10/01M8B9N50FPMY7F5MP1J4ZMXM0.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“It would be amazing to see that taken more seriously,” Ms Doolin added. “I remember thinking when I phoned ‘he’ll be priority’, and obviously he didn’t end up being.

“I understood from the inquest that they weren’t even looking at the category two calls because they were too busy looking at category one and trying to make that work.

“To think I made that phone call; it’s almost pointless, because no one looked at it. And I’m sat thinking something’s being done, and nothing’s being done.

“I really do believe in 2026 we could do better. We could do better in terms of clinicians checking the call list right through from category one through onto the other two and three and beyond.”

She suggests this could allow clinicians to make parents aware of delays so they can make their own way to hospital and allow babies to be treated more quickly.

According to NHS England, newborns would be classed as category one.

Ms Doolin, who is an English teacher, and her husband Mr Pollard, 42, have since raised more than £100,000 in Ezrah’s memory.

The couple, who have a three-year-old daughter, are also campaigning to raise awareness of the symptoms of infection in newborn babies.

Ms Doolin said: “Fundraising is an amazing way to put our energy into building a legacy for Ezrah that feels powerful, off the back of such a short life and such a painful and tragic story in terms of his illness.

“It’s our way of trying to do something that’s in the light rather than in the dark.”

Dr Timothy Godfrey, medical director of enhanced and critical care at the South Western Ambulance Service NHS Foundation Trust, offered his “sincere condolences”.

He said that since 2022 “clinical oversight of waiting calls has significantly increased” at the trust.
