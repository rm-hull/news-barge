A man from Northern Ireland who played a central role in the founding of the United States is set to be commemorated in his birth town.

An Ulster History Circle blue plaque honouring James McHenry, who was from Co Antrim, will be unveiled on Thursday at the Pavilion in the People’s Park in Ballymena.

Leaving Northern Ireland at the age of 18 in 1771, Mr McHenry set off across the Atlantic Ocean for a new life, initially settling in Philadelphia—a bustling port and commercial hub on the eve of the American Revolution.

He studied medicine as an apprentice to Benjamin Rush, a signatory of the US Declaration of Independence, and enlisted in George Washington’s forces when conflict broke out.

Captured and held in New York, he was later liberated in a prisoner exchange before going on to rejoin the army.

Mr McHenry served as Washington’s assistant secretary, developing a lifelong personal bond with the man who would serve as the first president of the newly formed USA.

Later joining the staff of the Marquis de Lafayette, he stood by the French commander’s side during the decisive military victory at Yorktown, where American independence was formally secured.

Washington wrote a letter of recommendation for Mr McHenry, describing him as “a man of letters and abilities, of great integrity, sobriety and prudence…of an amiable temper; very obliging, and of polished manners”.

![Fort McHenry, which is today a National Park in Baltimore](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/25/16/01M89EAWKRZPV7R4A6FFMF343A.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

In 1781, Mr McHenry left the army to pursue a career in politics. He was elected to the Senate in Maryland and represented the state at the Constitutional Convention.

He was one of those who signed the founding document on September 17 1787.

As president, Washington made Mr McHenry his secretary of war, and he went on to reform and enlarge the army, in spite of conflict with Congress, who saw it as directly opposed to the interests of the people.

However, Mr McHenry convinced them a large standing army was vital for the defence of America’s sovereignty.

Mr McHenry retired to his estate in Maryland, named Fayetteville after the Marquis, and had a fort named after him in Baltimore following a successful career in business.

Chris Rendo, US Consul General in Belfast, paid tribute to Mr McHenry.

“McHenry’s journey took him from Ulster across the Atlantic, where he played a pivotal role in the founding and early administration of the United States,” he said.

“From serving as a military surgeon during the American War of Independence and a trusted secretary to General George Washington, to signing the United States Constitution and serving as US secretary of war- a legacy permanently remembered in the naming of Fort McHenry- his life exemplified extraordinary vision and public service.

“This Ulster History Circle blue plaque permanently weaves his legacy back into the physical landscape of his birthplace.”

Historian Dr David Hume added: “James McHenry was an outstanding political figure in the early years of the US and someone who connects us to the first President, George Washington, who thought highly of the Ballymena man.

“It is good that he will be remembered in his native town.”

Chris Spurr, chairman of the Ulster History Circle, said the honour is being given in the 250th anniversary year of the signing of the Declaration of Independence.

“James McHenry has the distinction of being not only a Founding Father of the United States but also the sole County Antrim-born delegate to the Constitutional Convention of 1787,” he said.

“In this anniversary year of the American Declaration of Independence, the Ulster History Circle is recognising this statesman and surgeon with a blue plaque in his hometown, and the Circle is grateful to the Mid and East Antrim Borough Council America 250 programme for funding the plaque.”
