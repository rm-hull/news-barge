Powerful storms slamming both coasts of the United States have thrown Thanksgiving travel plans into chaos.

​The adverse weather conditions have led to more than 300 flights traveling from, to, or within the U.S. being canceled, with many more delayed as of Thursday morning.

​A storm on the East Coast is predicted to bring winds of up to 60 mph to New York City, with coastal flooding across the Eastern Seaboard. To the west, a Pacific storm system has led to flood warnings being issued along the coasts of California, Oregon and Washington.

​Terri Zimmerman, 71, was one of the many travelers to experience disruption to her journey on Wednesday. She says that she waited for nearly an hour in a security-check line at Fort Lauderdale-Hollywood International Airport after other travelers headed to the airport early.

![Passengers wait at the departure gates at Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/13/2306323873..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

​“I was just surprised when we walked in to see that kind of line,” she said. “It was 5 a.m. in the morning. I thought we’d be like the first flight out and there wouldn’t be anybody there, but the place was packed.”

​FlightAware recorded 6,996 delays for flights traveling within, into, or out of the U.S. on Wednesday, along with 167 cancellations. As time of publication on Thursday, 365 flights have been canceled and 500 delayed.

​Thanksgiving week is one of the busiest travel periods of the year, with the American Automobile Association estimating as many as 81.5 million people will travel at least 50 miles from their home between November 24 and November 30.

![The storms have caused hundreds of flights to be delayed or cancelled. This image shows the storms at 2.40 p.m. GMT](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/14/24/Screenshot-2026-11-26-at-14-42-59.png?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&trim=77%2C5%2C49%2C0&auto=webp)

​Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy described the week as “the ‘Super Bowl’ of U.S. air travel.”

​“The rest of the holiday travel week will be challenging, with major coastal storms expected to impact the Mid-Atlantic and portions of the Northeast starting tonight,” he wrote on X. “Peak impact -- from heavy rainfall and high wind gusts -- are (sic) expected Thanksgiving Day and potentially continuing into Friday.

​“If you're flying, take it slow on your way to the airport and stay in touch with your airline for the latest updates.”

![King tides have caused severe floods in California](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/13/2026-11-25T220306Z_308390297_RC28VOA2O1HJ_RTRMADP_3_USA-WEATHER-CALIFORNIA.JPG?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&auto=webp)

​Amid stormy weather in Southern Florida, a FedEx cargo jet ran off a runway at Miami International Airport at about 8.40 p.m. on Wednesday. The incident forced the closure of all but one of the airport’s runways.

​“It’s very scary. There’s things that happen and are out of control, and, right now, I can’t blame anyone because the weather is super, super bad and there’s a lot of floods,” a passenger told NBC Miami. “Yeah, flights should have already been delayed already (sic) and not flying out because of the weather.”

​The FAA says that the incident is under investigation and has not yet confirmed the cause.

![The Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade has been delayed due to weather conditions in New York City](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/13/2306356318..?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&auto=webp)

​Meanwhile, weather conditions in the Northeast have disrupted plans for the famous Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade in New York City, with the iconic event moved from Thursday to Saturday as the city braces for expected winds of 60 mph and heavy rain.

​The storm battering the East Coast is also expected to bring some wet snow to the Midwest on Thanksgiving Day, according to AccuWeather.

Over to the west, California has been battling record-breaking king tides that have flooded coastal communities. The king tides - a term that refers to the highest water levels of the year at a location - have been amplified by the El Niño climate pattern and Pacific storms.
