**Saturday mornings mean pitch patrol at Stow FC in the Scottish Borders.**

Before the town's kids can step foot on the pitch, volunteer Tucker MacCall does his rounds with a big black bucket.

The "poo bin", with bags, shovel and sand, is now part of the club's Saturday kit.

"It's getting beyond a joke," he said. "We would normally set up, put the nets up, put the flags out to get the barriers etc ready for the game. Then we have to do a scour of the pitch, to check for dog dirt."

The consequences of not doing this dirty job are frustrating.

"If we start the game, the game will get stopped, the referee will shout for somebody to bring the poo bin on, remove the dog dirt and then get on with the game again," Tucker said.

![Tucker MacCall is wearing a black-and-yellow sports jacket and matching bobble hat. He is standing on a grassy football field, about to pick up a black bucket with equipment in it.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/b997/live/abd9ebd0-e9a4-11f1-b880-15b494ba858d.jpg)

Tucker is one of the Stow FC volunteers who helps clear the pitch for games

Games have almost had to be abandoned because there was so much poo on the pitch.

Tucker said: "If it's found before they land in it, all good and well, but if not, then it's just disgusting and it can cause some serious infection as well.

"I don't know how people walk on a sports area with dogs and don't pick up. If they did it in the house, they would pick it up and disinfect. Why do they think it's fine just to leave it there. It's just wrong."

People in this Glasgow park said they had witnessed owners not picking up after their dogs

The law in Scotland says that anyone in charge of a dog must immediately pick up and properly dispose of its poo when out and about in public places.

Lack of signs is not an excuse.

If you are caught, you can face a fine of at least £80.

These can be handed out by the police or more usually by council enforcement officers.

Stow FC's fight against dog muck is not unique.

BBC Scotland News research has found that the problem is getting worse and fewer people are getting caught.

We received replies from all 32 councils in Scotland and the data showed the number of complaints about dog fouling had risen by nearly a third in the last five years, from 8819 to 11,742.

But during the same time, the number of fixed penalties issued for the offence had dropped from 246 to 164.

Edinburgh and Glasgow accounted for most of the complaints - dog walkers in Glasgow were not impressed. See their responses below.

The local government organisation Cosla said: "Local government takes the issue of dog fouling seriously, it's important that neighbourhoods are clean.

"Councils take active steps to address fouling, however there are real challenges facing local authorities - including the standards for evidence requirement as set out within the legislative framework and the difficult financial decisions that councils have faced over the last decade.

"Communities also have a vital role in encouraging responsible dog owners to pick up after the dogs and dispose of it responsibly to keep our streets and parks clean."

Councils hope dog owners act responsibly in their public parks

The difficulties with enforcement are echoed by the leader of Scottish Borders Council, Euan Jardine.

He told BBC Scotland News: "It is a complicated process. You do have to catch them in the act, then you have to report it, then you have to take them to court sometimes.

"But this is also a matter for the police as well, they can be on top of this. There are ways to do it, people can volunteer, it can go through community councils.

"We'll get people to go out with dog poo bags, with green jackets and they say 'Here, I'm friendly, take a dog poo bag'. No confrontations.

"Unfortunately because of how council budgets are stretched, we can't be on top everything all the time."

The approach in the Borders now includes a specialist education officer. Part of their role is to speak to groups about the problems of dog fouling.

Rose Hogg was visiting pupils at Tweedbank Primary School and said children were great at passing the message on.

"They go home, tell their mums, their dads, anybody who listens, which is the aim – the more we can shape them now, in the future it's potentially not going to be as much of a problem," she said.

"We want to give them that responsibility where they don't even think about it - there's a poo, pick it up, job's done."

The children get to practise picking up pretend "squishy poos" and putting them into the bin.

Education officer Rose Hogg teaches pupils about their responsibilities and they in turn take that home to their parents

"We want to make it fun, because fun is memorable," said Rose. "If we get into their heads now, it then becomes normal, it's not a chore – it's just I have a dog, I pick up the poo, it becomes second nature."

In the current financial climate it is unlikely there will be increased enforcement.

So people like Tucker in Stow can only hope that people get the message and realise the problems they are causing.
