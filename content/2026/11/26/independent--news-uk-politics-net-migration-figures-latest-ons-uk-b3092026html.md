Net migration has plummeted to its lowest level in three decades – outside of the Covid-19 pandemic – fuelled by fewer people coming to Britain for work.

New figures from the Office for National Statistics showed net migration to the UK was an estimated 77,000 in the 12 months to June 2026 – down nearly two-thirds (62 per cent) from 204,000 last year.

The continued fall in net migration – the difference between the number of people moving long-term to the UK and the number of people leaving the country – is being driven by fewer people from outside the EU arriving in the UK to work or study, the ONS said. More Britons are also leaving the UK than coming or returning.

Excluding the Covid shutdown, when international travel fell sharply around the world, it is the lowest annual total since 2007, when it stood at 48,000.

Home secretary Shabana Mahmood hailed the figures, saying it showed the government had “restored control”.

But one expert warned that the government “must make sure it doesn’t overreach” if it wants to achieve its ambition to “reindustrialise” the nation. Jonathan Portes, a former chief economist at the Cabinet Office, also warned that the government’s push to lower immigration was “likely to be bad for growth, productivity and the public finances”.

The figures show that an estimated 738,000 people immigrated to the UK, while 661,000 emigrated.

Overall, more British nationals are estimated to have left the UK in the year to June 2026 (245,000) than moved to the country (109,000), although the number leaving is estimated to have fallen slightly in recent years.

It was a similar picture for nationals from “EU-plus countries” – covering the 27 members of the EU plus Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein and Switzerland – with a higher number emigrating from the UK (124,000) than immigrating (79,000).

By contrast, more people from outside the EU moved to the UK in the year to June (550,000) than left (292,000). Among nationals from non-EU+ countries, net migration was provisionally estimated at 258,000, a drop of 121,000 from last year’s figures.

The ONS said net migration had “fallen to levels not typically seen since the 1990s”.

Sarah Crofts, ONS deputy director of migration statistics, said: “In 1997, movement from inside and outside the EU made a positive contribution to net migration, partially offset by more British people leaving than arriving.

“In 2026, non-EU citizens are the only group driving positive net migration. EU nationals are leaving in greater numbers than are arriving, as is the case with British nationals.

“There are also larger flows of people in 2026, with more than double the number of people leaving and arriving compared with 1997.”

Marley Morris, IPPR associate director for migration, trade and communities, said it was unlikely the figures will remain at these low levels, but said it indicates “the exceptional highs following the pandemic are now firmly behind us”.

“But the government must make sure it doesn’t overreach. Its mission to ‘reindustrialise’ the UK requires an immigration system that helps to recruit workers critical to our industrial strategy,” he said.

“We also need a competitive regime for international students that supports our university sector and helps to boost a critical British export.”

The figures come after the home secretary was forced to scale back her plans to make foreign care workers wait up to 15 years for permanent settlement earlier this month, saying those earning over £25,000 a year would now only have to wait five years.

The changes to the scheme came following significant opposition from campaigners, as well as dozens of Labour MPs.

Despite the drop in net migration, new research from King’s College London found that misconceptions about immigration are widespread among the British public, with only 4 per cent correctly guessing that net migration decreased from June 2024 to June 2025, and almost two-thirds wrongly believing it increased in this period.

The majority of Reform UK supporters wrongly believed net migration rose significantly between June 2024 and June 2025, whilst Conservatives (32 per cent) are next most likely to hold this misperception.

Net migration peaked at 944,000 in 2023, following the pandemic, but has fallen sharply since then.

The jump in migration after the pandemic was driven by a combination of the lifting of travel restrictions following Covid, new humanitarian resettlement schemes for people from Ukraine and Hong Kong, and the introduction of new immigration rules following the UK’s departure from the EU.

The subsequent fall was driven by a drop in the number of people from outside the European Union arriving to study or work, and an increase in people moving out of the country. There were also fewer people arriving in the UK through the resettlement schemes.

The drop in those from outside the EU arriving for study or work was a result of decisions taken under the Conservatives, and continued under Labour, to stop care workers and overseas students from bringing family members to the UK, as well as raising the salary requirements for some visas.

Ms Mahmood said: “This government has restored control. We have brought net migration to 77,000, down 92 per cent from its peak under the Conservatives. Where they failed, we have delivered for the British people.”

Jonathan Portes, a former chief economist at the Cabinet Office, told *The Independent:* “The government is reducing the number of skilled workers who come to the UK, as well as making it much more difficult for them to stay: this is likely to be bad for growth, productivity and the public finances,” he said.

“It is also putting up increasing barriers to international students, worsening the growing financial crisis facing universities, which will be bad for them, for UK-origin students, and local economies.”

Shadow home secretary Chris Philp claimed the fall in net migration was “mainly a result of changes made by the previous Conservative government”.

“We now need to go even further with an annual legally binding inward migration cap, set by parliament, at far lower levels,” he said.
