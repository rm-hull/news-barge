Banks are likely to try to pass the cost of Labour’s £1.5bn tax rise on to customers, the governor of the Bank of England has warned.

In his budget last month, chancellor John Healey doubled the banking corporation tax surcharge from 3 per cent to 6 per cent to raise an extra £1.5bn for cost-of-living support. It will come into force in January.

City analysts have warned that lenders may seek to recover the additional tax bill through pricier mortgages and loans, or by holding back interest rate rises on savings accounts.

Speaking to *The Times*, the Bank’s governor Andrew Bailey said it would not be surprising if banks attempted to “protect their earnings” and maintain target returns by passing on the extra levy.

However, he noted their ability to do so would depend on market conditions and competition across the sector.

“It’s a market economy, so these are decisions banks will have to take,” he said.

“You could understand that they will look to pass it on because they will be targeting a rate of return.

“Now, how successful they will be depends on all sorts of things: market conditions, competition within the industry and so on. It wouldn’t be surprising to find out that they will start by trying to do that.”

Mr Bailey’s warning comes as homeowners face a tighter financial squeeze thanks to rising energy bills and mortgage increases.

Some 6.5 million households will see their mortgage repayments climb over the next three years as existing fixed-rate deals expire.

Typical borrowers coming off fixed tariffs will face an average monthly increase of around £100 – double the £50 jump projected by the Bank in July.

According to the Bank’s latest projections, around 2.7 million households face monthly mortgage increases of between £100 and £500 by the end of 2029, while roughly 430,000 will be hit with rises exceeding £500 a month.

By the final quarter of next year alone, just over one million households currently locked into deals below 3 per cent will see their monthly bills rise by nearly £200.

The Bank raised its base interest rate from 3.75 per cent to 4 per cent earlier this month – the first increase in more than three years – to counter inflationary pressures triggered by the conflict in Iran, with financial markets pricing in further hikes.

At the same time, average household energy bills are set to jump by 26 per cent in January to £2,166 a year due to war-driven wholesale price spikes, outstripping the government’s £100 support payment for households in council tax bands A to D.

Unlike savings accounts – where banks have faced repeated regulatory scrutiny over delays in passing on higher rates – commercial lenders reprice fixed-rate mortgages immediately based on financial market expectations for future base rate rises, meaning home-loan costs began climbing long before base rates moved to 4 per cent.

Before last month’s Budget, banking chiefs had lobbied hard against a windfall levy, warning that UK lenders already face a higher effective tax rate than rivals in New York and Frankfurt.

But Mr Healey pressed ahead, arguing that profitable banks should contribute toward household energy relief, and insisting the doubled 6 per cent surcharge is strictly temporary – expiring automatically in April 2028 after 15 months.

With the higher tax rate set to follow the winter energy price cap rise, borrowers and savers will soon find out whether competition on the high street is strong enough to stop banks passing the bill straight on to households.
