Fresh concerns have emerged about the planet's ability to self-clean as global temperatures rise.

A new study led by the University of York, in collaboration with the University of East Anglia, challenges earlier assumptions that rising emissions of iodine gas from seawater would help break down ground-level ozone, a toxic air pollutant and greenhouse gas.

Instead, the research suggests that iodine’s global air-cleaning effects will plateau and weaken slightly by 2100.

Professor Lucy Carpenter, from the University of York’s Wolfson Atmospheric Chemistry Laboratories, said: "Warmer sea temperatures alter solubility, diffusion and chemistry at the ocean’s surface, slowing down the release of iodine into the air.

"Losing this anticipated surge is not good news for air quality and climate efforts, because it would have been a helpful buffer to offset some of the damage to the atmosphere caused by human activity."

The study found that iodine currently accounts for nearly 11 per cent of global ozone destruction, but this is projected to dip to 10.2 per cent by the end of the century.

Professor Mat Evans, also from York, said: "We thought a warming world would trigger more iodine, which would help create brighter, denser clouds that bounce extra sunlight back into space, but if we now don’t have this surge, we lose some of this natural cooling system."
