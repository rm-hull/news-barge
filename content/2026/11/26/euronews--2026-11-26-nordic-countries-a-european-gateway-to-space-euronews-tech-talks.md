## The Arctic has become strategically important for Europe. But can the region play a central role in helping the EU reach its ambitions in space?

On 20 October, the European Union presented its updated strategy for the Arctic, highlighting the region’s importance for the bloc’s security priorities.

“The Arctic is no longer insulated from the world’s geopolitical tensions,” Kaja Kallas, the EU’s foreign policy chief, said at a press conference. “Our updated Arctic strategy reflects this new reality by putting security firmly at its core,” she added.

The EU first codified its Arctic policy in 2008, which it later updated in 2021, principally to align with the environmental and climate ambitions of the European Green Deal.

At the time, Russia, a key Arctic player, had not yet launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine. And Donald Trump had yet to threaten to annex resource-rich Greenland, before ultimately signing a security agreement with the semi-autonomous Danish territory in September.

The October update reflects these geopolitical shifts and refers to the Arctic as Europe’s “Northern Neighbourhood”, in a bid to stress its strategic importance. Among the topics covered in the document are the economy, defence, energy, the environment and, crucially, space.

But will this strategy change the role of the Nordics in space, or in Europe's overall access to space? In its latest episode, Euronews Tech Talks travels north to find out.

## **A gateway to space**

The Arctic comprises eight countries, three of which are part of the EU (Denmark, Finland, and Sweden) and two of which are close partners of the bloc (Iceland and Norway).

These Nordic countries have played an important role in the space sector since the early days of the space race and are expected to become even more influential thanks to their two launch sites in Andøya, Norway, and Esrange, Sweden.

Andøya saw its first successful orbital launch in September, when a rocket from the German commercial company Isar Aerospace was sent into space, carrying five small satellites and a technology experiment. Esrange is planning its first orbital launch for 2028.

According to René Laufer, chair professor and head of space systems at Luleå University of Technology, Sweden, Esrange and Andøya will not replace Europe’s main spaceport in Kourou, French Guiana, but rather complement it.

“We will see a growth in launch capabilities and launch opportunities, and that means French Guiana will stay busy and additional sites need to get busy because otherwise we cannot address the demand of the market,” he said.

Europe lags behind the United States in the number of satellites it puts into orbit. Still, European governments and private companies are working to launch more, and this’s where the Norwegian and Swedish spaceports could come in hand.

Launching from Northern Europe provides access to polar and sun-synchronous orbits, and having ground stations so close to the North Pole makes it easier to collect data from the satellites.

## **The updated Arctic policy and space**

The Nordic spaceports are also highlighted in the latest update to the EU's Arctic policy.

“What is new now in space, and I think what is also driving a bit of the momentum in northern Norway, at least when it comes to space, is Andøya spaceport, this very first spaceport on the European continent where you can, and already have successfully launched rockets,” said Andreas Raspotnik, director of the High North Centre for Business and Governance at Nord University in Bodø, Norway.

Moreover, according to its revised Arctic policy, the EU aims to procure additional satellites to extend secure connectivity to the Arctic. The bloc also plans to reinforce the EU Space Surveillance and Tracking system with sensors in the Arctic to protect space infrastructure. The EU is also looking to enhance its Earth observation programme for the Arctic and support the development of its spaceports.

“What has happened here with reframing the Arctic, or the European parts of the Arctic, as Europe's Northern Neighbourhood is hopefully a more strategic approach on how to conceptualise, but also on how to take actions in that space,” Raspotnik told Euronews Next.

However, despite the shift in the EU's approach to the Nordics, Raspotnik believes that the practical implications of the strategy remain unclear. “How that [policy] is then executed, how we'll also see or not see high-level interest in the Arctic after October 20th, that will have to be seen.”
