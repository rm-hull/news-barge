## EU member states are discussing a package of new Europe-wide taxes to finance the bloc's next long-term budget. But where is the money coming from? Euronews provides an overview of who will pay for each new levy.

EU leaders are engaged in intense discussion over the bloc's new seven-year budget before the end of the year, with member states butting heads over the overall size, spending priorities and how to finance it.

In terms of new resources Brussels intends to tap into, European Council President Antonio Costa proposed an ambitious mix of EU-wide taxes meant to generate about €60bn in revenue each year.

But who pays the bill? With Costa's compromise, the European Commission circulated an updated estimate of how much each Europe-wide tax is meant to generate, and which countries will bear most of the costs.

Between 2028 and 2034, Germany would be by far the country paying the highest share of EU taxes, with over €13bn in current prices, followed by Italy with €8bn. Despite being the bloc's second-largest economy, France merely ranks third, which explains Paris' strong support for new own resources.

However, the asymmetrical contribution to own resources is best understood in terms of what they would otherwise pay in national contributions based on their Gross National Income (GNI).

Costa's proposal made several adjustments to the EU taxes, mostly to accommodate objections from member states from central and eastern Europe, leaving southern European countries to pay the highest share.

On average, the tax of €2.25 per kilogram of uncollected electronic waste is by far the one expected to yield more revenues, with over €20bn expected per year, despite it being relatively less controversial than the Emissions Trading System (ETS), the Tobacco Tax or the digital levy.

While the e-waste contribution is the largest for most member states, how much each own resource will weigh on the national governments varies considerably, shaping the positioning of the member states.

The revenues expected from each tax are not fixed due to a number of reasons, including the fact that several of them will be adjusted for inflation.

The Commission projects the yields might significantly increase over time, with the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), a tax on imports of carbon-intensive products, expected to more than double its revenues by 2034.

For the tax of €1.25 per kilogram of non-recycled plastic packaging waste, over half of the revenues are expected to come from the EU's four largest member states, France, Germany, Italy and Spain.

Similarly, the four EU countries would also account for over 60% of the revenues coming from an EU-wide digital services tax. France, Italy and Spain already have a digital levy at the national level, meaning they would lose these revenues in favour of Brussels.

The yields of the tobacco excise duty are also highly geographically concentrated, but in this case there is a less linear relation with the member states' population and reflects more national consumption habits and national excise duty levels.

Luxembourg, for instance, would make a significant contribution despite its relatively tiny size, as it sells large volumes of cigarettes and related products to consumers from neighbouring countries thanks to its lower prices.

For the tax on e-waste, which envisages a contribution from member states based on the share of electronic devices produced on their territory that are recycled, over 60% of the revenues would come from Germany, Italy and France, the EU's manufacturing powerhouses. By contrast, the Commission estimates Bulgaria would have to pay zero.

Giving 30% of the revenues generated from the Emissions Trading System (ETS), a carbon market for CO2-intensive industries, would be particularly advantageous for France, which would be in fourth position in terms of contribution, whilst it would penalise countries like Poland and Bulgaria compared to their economic size.

Italy would be the first payer for the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), with Belgium and Poland also punching above their weight in terms of contributions.
