## 'Yorkshire Water is a busted flush and York families deserve answers over chlorine'

Like many families across York, recently, the water coming out of my taps at home has had a strong smell of chlorine and has, at times, been cloudy. In many parts of York, this has been going on for months, with very little communication from Yorkshire Water about what is happening.

Last week, the York Press covered calls from Luke Charters, York Outer’s Member of Parliament, for the Drinking Water Inspectorate to investigate the issue. I agree with that call because, beyond the fact that people have been left in the dark about why their tap water suddenly smells like a swimming pool, many have had to resort to buying bottled water for months.

Yorkshire Water responded by saying that it had made some changes to the way it treats water in York, and that the water continues to meet the required quality and safety standards for drinking water. The exact reasons for these changes, however, remain unclear.

It is not just the water that comes out of our taps, though, that is a source of concern about Yorkshire Water’s privatised management of water in our county. Its track record, on the environment in particular, points to a sheer and brazen disregard for the beautiful natural landscapes that Yorkshire has in abundance.

Last month, the company was given the lowest possible environmental rating by the Environment Agency. Its figures showed that the company was responsible for 260 pollution incidents in 2025, up from 151 the year before.

I’ve sat down with Yorkshire Water bosses on several occasions since I became Mayor, both on my own and alongside the Mayors of South, West and East Yorkshire. They told us clearly and directly that they had plans for improvement and to, quite literally, clean up their act.

All the while, they were recording their highest number of annual pollution incidents since 2011.

Yorkshire Water is a busted flush. As bills go up, customers should rightly expect things to get better. While I will concede that a large programme of investment is taking place, I suspect there could be an even larger programme of investment if shareholders and executives weren’t receiving huge payouts and the company hadn’t accumulated debt at the rate it has.

Nobody in Yorkshire is getting a fair deal from privatisation and the toothless regulators and watchdogs who are supposed to hold them to account. That’s why things must change.

The Government has announced two steps to improve public accountability and, potentially, public control over the water industry, as well as the way water is managed more broadly in England.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/19748266.jpg?type=mds-article-620)David Skaith, Labour mayor for York and North Yorkshire. Image: LDRS

The first was announced in September. New, stronger water laws will be introduced, removing the limits on government share ownership in water companies. The intention, which will be set out in more detail in the forthcoming ten-year plan for Britain, is to give local and regional governments the option of greater public control and potentially ownership of water companies.

The second development, and the reason I was in London earlier this week, is the establishment of Regional Water Councils. I sat down with the Environment Secretary on Tuesday to discuss plans to create eight new water councils, putting communities at the heart of decisions on tackling sewage, flooding and drought, and securing future water supplies.

Each council will produce a regional water plan, which will influence how investment in water infrastructure is allocated. That might mean prioritising flood prevention work upstream to slow the flow, or investing in rewetting peat bogs and supporting land management practices that help water soak into aquifers rather than run off, particularly as climate change places greater pressure on our natural landscapes.

In a place like York and North Yorkshire, work in the upper Dales and Moors to slow the flow of water is crucial. It can help prevent flash flooding by holding more water upstream, reducing the risk of flooding in our towns and cities downstream.

Alternatively, it could mean greater investment and support for farmers to install on-farm reservoirs, which currently face significant financial and planning barriers. The difficulties involved in installing these reservoirs are considerable and affect more than just individual farmers. They have a real impact on food security, the environment and flood management.

It’s time to turn the page on the failings of the privatised water system. I’m committed to working with local leaders, environmental groups, farmers and businesses to make sure that, whatever the future of water management looks like in England, York and North Yorkshire are best equipped to make the most of these changes.
