Interest rates are the primary lever that the Bank of England uses to control inflation or stimulate economic activity.

Recently, the direction has been up, as Governor Andrew Bailey and his colleagues have sought to rein-in inflation by making borrowing more expensive.

This impacts almost everyone in the country to varying degrees, with variable rate mortgage payers and new applicants the most obvious.

But small businesses can also be particularly vulnerable to the impact of rising rates.

## Firms hit from both sides by rising rates

SME firms can face harder trading conditions in two fundamental ways when interest rates rise: the demand side and the supply side.

In terms of demand for their goods or service, higher rates tend to make customers of a small business spend less. This is largely because each person is facing their own higher costs when rates are higher.

Whether it is a mortgage going up or bigger credit card bills, higher rates can mean less money to spend for many people.

The supply side refers to the costs of running the business. This will principally impact a small business through the costs of any variable rate loans already in place or new loans they need to take out. Whenever the Bank of England raises rates, lenders are very quick to reflect this in both their existing loan books and new issuance rates.

Being forced to pay out more money in interest can result in a business having to let staff go, or putting off important investments in new equipment, for example.

For small businesses that have been successful in recent times and built up a nice cash buffer, there is an upside to higher rates.

The money saved in the bank will earn more interest and compound faster. The caveat to that of course is that it is crucial to shop around for a bank account that pays a market-topping rate of interest, otherwise it is throwing money away.

![Governor Andrew Bailey of the BoE](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/06/11/2303165968..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Leaving cash earning paltry rates of interest is a common financial mistake many people make, and some small businesses are certain to be missing a trick here.

Another potential benefit, depending on the specifics on the industry a business is operating in, is it could increase leverage in negotiations with suppliers. When times are tighter, some companies may offer better deals than in boom times.

Being in a position to pay with cash up front rather than in a staggered payment agreement could secure much better prices on all manner of supplies.

## How to deal with higher rates

Cutting costs is the obvious thing to do, but clearly that is easier said than done for most small firms.

Even if a small business owner feels they run a tight ship, there are likely to be at least a couple of ways to save money.

Whether it is by shopping around for new suppliers or renegotiating leases, every little bit saved could make the difference between riding out the higher rates or going under, in the worst case.

One other possibility is selling any equipment that is not essential to raise some extra cash. That will obviously vary greatly depending on the exact nature of the business.

The real key to dealing with higher rates comes in the form of building up a war chest when the business is doing well, before rates rise.

While it can be wise to reinvest money into the business to grow it, throwing everything at growth without saving for a rainy day is high risk, and probably not a good idea for most small businesses.

Likewise, while it may be tempting to take money out of the business to splash out on a new car, or luxury holiday, it is far better in the long run to build up a cash buffer while rates are low and customers are plentiful.
