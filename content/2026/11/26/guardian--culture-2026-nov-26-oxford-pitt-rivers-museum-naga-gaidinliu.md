In 1932, British forces in north-east India imprisoned a 17-year-old girl called Gaidinliu for leading an uprising against colonial rule. While pursuing the freedom fighter through hills and valleys in the homeland of the Naga people, colonial officers also seized some of her most treasured possessions.

They were sent to the University of Oxford’s Pitt Rivers Museum and kept in storage. Now, for the first time, the museum is exhibiting the collection of 27 items, including a basket of Gaidinliu’s notebooks believed to contain her prophetic visions as the leader of a Naga Indigenous religion, clothes, bracelets and amulets.

Also on display are protective ritual objects, taken by the British from Gaidinliu’s hiding places, which they believed she had used against them. These are a stick with a monkey skull used to ward off evil spirits, and small wooden clubs, traditionally used by Naga people to cast an omen on an enemy.

The exhibition, The Afterlives of Captured Objects, raises questions about the colonial extraction of artefacts from Indigenous communities, which were often originally catalogued using prejudiced and discriminatory language, according to the curator, Clare Harris, a professor of visual anthropology.

For example, Harris said Gaidinliu’s notebooks were derided as “meaningless scribbling” by the colonial anthropologist JP Mills, who collected them. But he also recorded that these writings “gave her immense prestige”, which suggests that they may have been seized as part of British efforts to suppress the religious and political movement that Gaidinliu led, called Heraka.

While most of the objects on display were taken by the British after Gaidinliu abandoned them while on the run, two items were more violently extracted. Textiles displayed on mannequins in the show were taken from a pair of male and female sacred clay figures from a Heraka temple in the village of Kambiron in 1931.

The textiles are shown along with a photograph taken by a British officer of another soldier kneeling with the sacred figures, which were destroyed along with the temple.

“By showing the photograph at the same time, you can make the equation visually about what was extracted by the British there,” said Harris. “The British destroy the temple and they destroy the figures, so they take away the potency of those things.”

The show follows a four-year project by researchers from India and the Universities of Edinburgh and Oxford to digitise the Gaidinliu collection and share it with Naga communities in Assam, Nagaland and Manipur. Their craftspeople have used the images to revive and revitalise traditional crafts such as basket weaving and textile making, with some of their creations included in the exhibition.

Arkotong Longkumer, a professor of anthropology and modern Asia at the school of divinity, University of Edinburgh, who co-led the project with Harris, said attempts were also being made to translate Gaidinliu’s notebooks in an effort to reconstruct a new Indigenous language for the Naga people.

He said: “There are some symbols which are recognisable within stories and myths. They may be untranslatable in a conventional sense but they are being used to try and construct a language system.”

The exhibition also aims to raise international recognition of Gaidinliu, who has long been revered as an important leader in the resistance movement that led to Indian independence. After her release from prison in 1947, she became head of a political movement advocating for recognition of a Naga homeland.

![Rani Gaidinliu’s statue, adorned with garlands, stands among flags against a cloudy sky.](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/40f87ce4ee31d6cbef795ee9f5c4f17228a09808/0_0_1600_1066/master/1600.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

Alila Chang, one of Gaidinliu’s grandnieces, said her family felt a deep sense of pride about the exhibition. She said: “These objects are connected to our family’s history and the struggles and sacrifices of our people as well. We want the world to know her struggles and for what reason she fought.”

Chang, who has come to Oxford for the exhibition’s private view, also shared a more personal recollection of Gaidinliu as a “very affectionate grandmother”.

“I remember [her] giving us sweets,” she said, adding how Gaidinliu, who died in 1993, would pray for her and her sibling when they went to school in the morning.

“She will always be in our hearts, but I think she belongs to the world now.”
