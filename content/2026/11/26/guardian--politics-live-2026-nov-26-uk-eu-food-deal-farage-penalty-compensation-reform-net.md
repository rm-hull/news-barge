How Mandelson tried to influence Starmer's September 2025 reshuffle

Pippa Crerar

Pippa Crerar is the Guardian’s political editor.

The files release today show Peter Mandelson trying to get involved in Keir Starmer’s reshuffle in September 2025.

In a short exchange of WhatsApps on 5 September 2025, Mandelson told Starmer’s chief of staff at the time, Morgan McSweeney, to “solve your Darren issue” – referring to Darren Jones.

Referring to the then science secretary, Mandelson wrote: “Are you taking peter Kyle away ….?”

In a second message, he said: “You should solve your Darren issue and put him at DSIT (Department for Science, Innovation and Technology).”

Around 45 minutes later, he asked where McSweeney was and added “I am in upstairs meeting room”.

Jones was not made science secretary. Instead he was made chief secretary to the PM.

Kyle was moved in the refshuffle. He became business secretary, and Liz Kendall replaced him as science secretary.

The reshuffle took place was prompted by the resignation of Angela Rayner over her tax affairs. In April this year McSweeney, who at that point had resigned, taking responsibility for the flawed decision to appoint Mandelson as ambassador to the US, told the foreign affairs select committee that Mandelson had not masterminded the reshuffle, though he confirmed he had sent unsolicited suggestions by text.

“He texted me his thoughts,” he told MPs. “I have to say that on reshuffle day I get texted a lot of thoughts from a lot of people”.

McSweeney told the committee that he hadn’t responded to any of Mandelson’s suggestions – which the release appears to confirm – but had been in touch with him at the end of the day. “I think we seized that day quite well,” the messages reveal he told him.

The former No 10 chief also told MPs he “might have” bumped into Mandelson in Downing Street that day, but he could not recall. The newly released messages show that the former ambassador told McSweeney exactly where in the building he was. It is unclear whether the two men actually met.

Record high number of children in temporary accommodation 'shameful milestone', says Shelter

The number of children living in temporary accommodation has reached a new record high, as research suggested children who have been homeless are twice as likely to end up as so-called Neets, the Press Association reports. PA says:

There were 178,690 children living in temporary accommodation – a form of homelessness which can include hostels, refuges and bed and breakfasts (B+Bs) – at the end of June, according to the latest official government data.

This was up by 3.7% from the same time last year when the figure stood at 172,370, and it was branded a “shameful milestone” by housing charity Shelter as it was the 19th consecutive quarterly rise in child homelessness.

The latest data was published as a report from Shelter and University College London (UCL) suggested around one in four (26%) young people born in 2000-02 who experienced homelessness as children are not in education, employment or training (Neet), compared to 13% for their peers who were not homeless as a child.

Shelter warned action must be taken to ensure children’s futures “aren’t written off”.

Mairi MacRae, the charity’s director of policy and campaigns, said: “Homelessness can tear through every part of a child’s life and leave long-lasting scars.

“With a record number of children growing up homeless, we must act now to ensure their futures aren’t written off.”

Philp and Jenrick criticise 53% increase in settlement grants - even though this is due to 'Boriswave' when they were ministers

Today’s immigration figures (see 9.38am) have created a problem for rightwing politicians who have spent years arguing that net migration is too high.

One response, illustrated by Nigel Farage (see 10.49am), has been to switch to emigration, and to complain that too many Britons are leaving the country (because of the government’s failings, it’s implied).

But the Conservatives have illustrated another approach. While Chris Philp, the shadow home secretary, has issued a statement saying net migration is still too high (see 10.17am), he has also issued a much longer news release highlighting different figures – those in a Home Office report showing that grants of indefinite leave to remain were up 53% in the year ending September 2026. The Tory news release says:

Today Home Office figures have once again exposed the extent of Labour’s immigration failure in the starkest possible terms.

One migrant is being granted permanent settlement roughly every two minutes under Labour, with nearly a quarter of a million handed the right to stay in the year to September 2026. This is the highest since records began in 2013 and 53% up on the year before. Every one of those grants is permanent, bringing full access to the benefits system and, in time, a British passport.

Robert Jenrick, the Reform UK Treasury spokesperson, has made the same argument, with different figures. He posted this on social media.

An obscene \*544,732\* people obtained leave to remain last year.

Over half a million foreigners - mostly low skilled, low wage and from third world countries - are now entitled to British benefits.

Only Reform UK will block this disaster.

Both figures are right. Around 241,000 people were granted indefinite leave to remain (ILR) in the year ending September 2026 (the figure Philp is quoting). The Home Office says that is the highest annual figure since 2010 (not 2013, as the Tories said). But if you also include people granted immediate settlement (ILE – indefinite leave to enter, like ILR, but for people applying from outside the UK, not inside) and settled status under the EU settlement scheme, the total is around 545,000. That is the number Jenrick was using.

What is surprising, though, is that Philp and Jenrick feel confident making this argument. People who come to the UK on work visas have to wait five years until they can apply for ILR, and so the increase is mostly explained by the huge rise in the number of people coming to the UK in the so-called “Boriswave”. Many of these were people on work visas. Philp and Jenrick were both ministers during this period – with Philp serving in the Home Office in 2021, and Jenrick moving there in 2022.

According to the Home Office, the large rise in the number of ILR applications was “largely driven by more grants to individuals who held leave on skilled worker (including health and care worker) and British national (overseas) (BN(O)) routes, immediately prior to their settlement application”. The BN(O) visa is for people from Hong Kong. This was also another decision taken by the last Tory government – it set up this visa route for people escaping Hong Kong after the Chinese passed an oppressive national security law – but it was a move that had cross-party support.

Figures for people getting ILR Photograph: Home Office

Commenting on these figures, Nuni Jorgensen, a researcher at the Migration Observatory at the University of Oxford, said:

The high number of ILR applications results from the levels of immigration that the UK saw in the years immediately after the pandemic. Citizenship applications have risen both because more migrants have become eligible and because a larger share of those eligible are choosing to apply.

In his news release, Philp criticised Shabana Mahmood, the home secretary, for recently watering down her plans to force migrants to wait longer for ILR. He said:

Scared of their leftwing backbenchers, Labour have backed down on settlement reform just as grants hit a record high. Labour are handing out the right to stay with no serious grip on who gets to settle here.

But even the watered-down Labour plans are still stricter than the ILR rules that were in force when the Tories were in office.

Burnham says more reform needed to ensure 'vested interests' aren't running Westminster, as new Mandelson files released

Andy Burnham has suggested that the revelations in the Peter Mandelson files have strengthened his conviction that further change is needed to the way Westminster operates.

Speaking to reporters in the Downing Street garden, Burnham said:

I don’t think we have yet seen the change that this system down here needs.

I’ve come back in as something of an outsider into the system and I see a lot of things that I think need to change to improve the political culture of this country, and to strengthen the public interest when it comes to all of these issues.

And I will address those in the 10-year plan for Britain that we bring forward next month.

You know, it will involve political reform, public service reform, because I think there’s a lot further to go until people can have confidence that the Westminster and Whitehall system is run on the way that they think it should be run, and that vested interests aren’t running large within the system, so these are precisely the issues that we will address in next month’s 10-year plan.
