## A new legal study suggests Brussels could circumvent the EU’s unanimity requirement by designing a levy on oil and gas companies’ excess profits around climate or energy policy objectives rather than revenue raising.

The EU could introduce a new windfall profit tax on oil and gas companies without requiring unanimous backing from all EU countries, as long as the measure is designed primarily to advance climate or energy policy and not to raise public revenue, according to a new legal study.

The findings could potentially reopen a politically sensitive debate over who should pay for the EU's energy crises just as EU leaders gather at a summit in Brussels on Thursday and Friday to discuss the bloc's multi-annual budget for 2028-2034.

The closure of the Strait of Hormuz, renewed fuel price increases and record profits for energy companies have revived calls for an EU windfall tax. The European Commission has rejected such an EU-wide tax, citing legal challenges. It ultimately reverts the matter for capitals to decide on their own.

In the study commissioned by German environmental organisations Germanwatch and Klima-Allianz Deutschland and conducted by Baldon Avocats, several possible legal routes for Brussels to impose a levy on fossil fuel companies were identified.

The legal study examines how the EU could require EU countries to introduce, collect and administer such a levy, with revenues potentially flowing either to national treasuries or the EU budget.

## Avoiding unanimity

A striking finding that could pave the way for a windfall profit tax is that the Commission could potentially avoid the unanimity requirement normally associated with EU taxation by using existing treaty provisions covering environmental protection or energy policy, according to the study.

However, the tax must genuinely pursue an environmental or energy goal as its main purpose. If its main intention is to raise and redistribute money, it would be classified as a fiscal measure, triggering the unanimity requirement.

The lawyers argue that the design of the levy would be crucial.

“The label given to the instrument (‘excess profits’ or ‘windfall profits tax’, ‘solidarity contribution’) carries limited weight for the characterisation of a measure as primarily fiscal. What matters most are the aim and content of the measure,” reads the legal study.

## A question of framing

A climate-focused mechanism could target profits generated specifically by fossil fuel activities, discourage further investment in polluting operations and direct revenues towards climate mitigation or adaptation.

An energy-focused version could finance energy efficiency, security of supply and measures to reduce bills for consumers.

However, framing the measure solely as a climate or energy levy would not be sufficient to bypass national vetoes. Its actual goals, tax base and allocation of revenues would determine its legal classification.

Another possibility would be to introduce the tax as part of a broader package of environmental or energy measures, with the overall purpose of the law determining its legal basis.

## **The 2022 precedent**

The study also identifies a more immediate route akin to that used by the EU in 2022 following Russia's invasion of Ukraine.

That mechanism required EU countries to impose a temporary contribution of at least 33% on exceptional profits made by companies in the crude oil, natural gas, coal and refining sectors. It applied to profits exceeding by more than 20% the average profits recorded over the previous four financial years.

Under EU law, the Council can adopt emergency economic measures by qualified majority, avoiding the need for unanimous agreement, the study noted.

The lawyers argued that a new temporary contribution could follow the same model, provided they can demonstrate exceptional circumstances and the need for emergency intervention.

The study also sees as plausible establishing a permanent EU crisis framework that could be activated temporarily whenever exceptional economic or energy circumstances arise.

However, the legal precedent is not yet settled. Energy companies have challenged the 2022 contribution before EU and national courts, questioning its legal basis and compatibility with fundamental rights.

EU Court of Justice's Advocate General, Athanasios Rantos, backed on 22 October the validity of the contribution under EU law. The Court of Justice is expected to deliver its ruling in 2027.

## **Who gets the money?**

Beyond whether the tax can be introduced, who gets the money could become another major political battleground.

The study proposes allowing revenues to remain in national budgets while restricting their use through EU legislation, using the revenues to create a new EU budget own resource, or assigning the proceeds to a dedicated European fund.

The first option would broadly replicate the 2022 arrangement, giving national governments control over collection while requiring spending to follow goals established by EU law.

The second would be more complicated, according to the lawyers. Establishing a new EU own resource requires unanimous approval by EU countries and ratification at the national level, potentially bringing back the very veto problem that supporters of the tax would seek to avoid.

But the third option could provide an alternative. The lawyers argued that revenues could be assigned to a dedicated EU fund through secondary legislation without creating a new own resource.

Spain's proposal for a European Climate Resilience Levy on fossil fuel profits, with revenues directed towards a dedicated Climate Adaptation Fund, is one example flagged in the study.

The lawyers stress that the mechanism would require national authorities to levy and collect the tax. Direct collection by the EU itself would be difficult under the existing treaties.
