A former fashion boss is suing a north London council for £110,000 after they banned him from chopping down oak trees in his own garden, which damaged his £1 million home.

Richard Martyn, a former supplies manager for Burberry and a retired business consultant, moved into his sprawling six-bedroom home in The Reddings, Mill Hill, nearly 40 years ago.

He claims the two large oak trees in his back garden – one of which is over 80 feet high – have caused damage which cost more than £104,000 to remedy through underpinning and other repairs due to their roots working under the foundations of an extension at the back of his house.

Mr Martyn is suing the London Borough of Barnet, on his insurers’ behalf, for £110,000 to compensate him for the damage done to his extension plus interest.

His barrister, David Thomas, told the Upper Tribunal in London that Mr Martyn was left with little option but to pay for the works when council planning officials refused his plea for permission to fell the two trees because of their "special amenity value" to the neighbourhood - a decision Mr Martyn labelled “pure madness” outside court.

However, the council is disputing liability and insisting that chopping down the oaks would not have prevented problems, as uprooting them would have caused destructive earth movement around the property through “heave” damage.

![Richard Martyn, formerly a supplies manager for Burberry and a business consultant](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/9/12/Richard-Martyn.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Mr Martyn explained how the first signs of subsidence at his property came in November 2017 when his young grandson discovered a worrying crack in the living room.

By the following year, he was engaging experts to assess symptoms of root damage to his extension, and in March 2018 a specialist diagnosed internal and external crack damage “indicative of vegetation-related subsidence”.

An “arboricultural report” carried out in April 2018 named the two oaks - one 45ft and the other over 80ft - as the “dominant vegetation” causing the subsidence, and recommended their removal.

But the council went on to refuse Mr Martyn’s application for consent to fell the oaks, which are subject to a tree preservation order, due to their “special amenity value”.

His barrister told specialist tribunal judge Mark Higgin: “It seems beyond dispute that the oaks caused the damage to the property.

“Equally, it does not appear to be in dispute that the risk of further damage to the property - and the need for sub-structure works at the property - were foreseeable if the tree preservation order application were refused.

“The London Borough of Barnet elected not to allow consent for felling, given the oaks’ perceived special amenity value. This is their right, but one that it's submitted triggers the claimant’s entitlement to compensation.”

![Mr Martyn claims the oaks caused damage which cost more than £104,000 to remedy through underpinning and other repairs](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/9/22/Richard-Martyn-oak-tree.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Pruning the trees was an inadequate solution to stop the trees wreaking havoc on the foundations, claimed Mr Thomas, adding that an engineer who examined the site in May 2019 found that the property’s movement was directly linked to the “seasonal water demands of the nearby vegetation causing clay shrinking subsidence”.

But defending the claim, the council's barrister James Neill argued they should not be liable to pay, as the trees’ removal in itself could well have caused major damage, had they agreed to let them be chopped down.

“The evidence of both engineering experts indicates that there was a risk, should the trees have been felled and removed in their entirety, of heave once the trees had been removed, i.e. that ground movement would have been caused by the removal of the trees," he said.

“Given that heave risk, the council submits that underpinning would have been necessary in any event, even if the consent had been granted and the trees removed.”

Mr Martyn’s barrister told the judge that the extension foundations were excavated to a depth of 1.3 metres, which was deeper than the minimum depth generally recommended on “shrinkable soils”.

Nor would Mr Martyn have been on the alert for any potential root damage from the oak trees - the smallest of which is 45 feet from the house - since the two trees were not “particularly close” to the building and his extension foundations were deeper than normal.

Although aware of “historic subsidence” in the area, Mr Martyn had done what he could to prevent the risk of any potential root damage by “paying extra for deeper foundations to the extension”.

Tackling the question of what caused the subsidence, Mr Thomas claimed that the “arboricultural” experts agree that the oaks caused the upheaval and that “removal of the oaks would have allowed the property to stabilise”.

Other factors such as soil “heave” were insufficient to have caused major subsidence problems, he argued, adding: “there is no evidence that the extension would have required underpinning even if the oaks had been felled”.

The judge has now reserved his decision in the case until a later date.
