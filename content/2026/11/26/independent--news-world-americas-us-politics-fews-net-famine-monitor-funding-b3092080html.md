Dozens of employees at the Famine Early Warning Systems Network received 30-day termination notices on December 1, three people with direct knowledge of the issue said.

The cuts mean the U.S. government's famine monitor will be unable to produce its next food security update. More than half of the monitor's field workers will lose their jobs by the end of the year because of a lack of funding, six people told Reuters.

The monitor will be left unable to help fend off famines made more likely by El Niño and aid cuts, they said. An email from the monitor to aid officials, sent on Tuesday and seen by Reuters, said the December update would not be published.

![An aerial photograph from the plane shows a camp for the internal displaced people in Baidao, Somalia](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/10/2026-11-25T200926Z_672974991_RC20ZKA6AN0U_RTRMADP_3_USA-TRUMP-HUNGER.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The reason given was that the State Department had not confirmed funding for the fiscal year that began in October, forcing staff to "initiate contingency closeout actions".

"We will endeavor to remain as engaged as possible during this period of disruption and anticipate that we will soon ⁠be able to confirm funding and restart full operations," the email said. The update is crucial for countries on the brink of famine such as Sudan, Yemen, South Sudan and Somalia.

It was expected to include details of the areas hit hardest by drastic food shortages. Early indications of a famine help humanitarian agencies and policymakers assess and deploy lifesaving relief measures to prevent further deaths from hunger.

The anticipated terminations and the fact that the December report will not be published have not been reported before.

## No plan to close down monitor

The December report is one of three such updates produced in a year. People Reuters spoke to said they had no details on how and when funding and reporting might resume, or whether any jobs might be reinstated. A spokesperson for FEWS NET asked Reuters to direct all questions regarding its funding to the State Department.

![The shuttering of the US Agency for International Development was detailed in a memorandum named: 'USAID's Final Mission'](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/28/12/2253193345..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

A State Department spokesperson told Reuters that they did "not intend to close down FEWS NET or reduce its deliverables, which are funded through April 2027.” The spokesperson did not respond to questions about funding for the full fiscal year, which ends on September 30, 2027.

Washington-based FEWS NET is one of only two global hunger monitors; the other is the UN-backed Integrated Food Security Phase Classification, or IPC. It works in more than 30 countries. Around 200 people of many different nationalities collect and analyze data for FEWS NET, most of them field staff based in regional hubs or country offices.

Additional staff run the website and information storage. In October, termination notices had already been sent to 20% of FEWS NET staff following months of uncertainty about the monitor's financial future, the six people said.

Four of the people said staff at the hunger monitor warned the State Department as recently as last week that its operations would have to cease without financial guarantees. An internal email dated July 24, seen by Reuters, ordered a pause on travel and recruitment, citing a cash crunch.

## Contractor draws up closeout plan

In the past, funding for the hunger monitor, which was part of the now-shuttered U.S. Agency for International Development, would have been approved before the fiscal year began on October 1, the people said.

![People gather for a "Save the Civil Service" rally hosted by the American Federation of Government Employees (AFGE) outside the U.S. Capitol on February 11, 2025](https://static.independent.co.uk/2025/06/06/19/29/GettyImages-2198372338.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

They added they did not know whether the lack of funding stemmed from internal bottlenecks and poor management or reflected a deliberate decision by the Trump administration to downsize or close FEWS NET.

The spending for FEWS NET's field operations is channeled through private contractor Chemonics, which traditionally pays the monitor's expenses upfront before being reimbursed by the U.S. government. The six people said Chemonics has continued to cover FEWS NET expenses for months while it awaited funding approval from the State Department.

The contractor sent a closeout plan to the State Department earlier this month to wind down operations, they said. Chemonics did not respond immediately to a request for comment.

## Questions from Congress

Aid workers have struggled to respond to surging needs after widespread government funding cuts, including those from the United States, the world's largest donor, forced organizations to cut their workforce and operations. The monitor aims to forecast hunger crises 6 to 12 months in advance.

![A wounded child receives medical treatment at a hospital in Yemen's third city of Taez, after reported shelling on residential areas by Huthi rebels](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/10/1244378142..?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

It was established in 1985 following a deadly famine in Ethiopia that shocked the world and highlighted the need for early warning systems. It began operations during former President Ronald Reagan's second term and had received bipartisan support until last year.

Last year, the shutdown of USAID took it offline for five months. It resumed work in late June last year. Earlier this year, the State Department ordered the monitor to stop publishing reports on crisis-hit Somalia, Yemen and Afghanistan, without explanation, according to a July 24 internal email seen by Reuters.

The suspension, lifted in September, prompted criticism from Gregory Meeks, the top-ranking Democrat on the House of Representatives' Foreign Affairs Committee. Meeks told Reuters that the State Department was putting "millions of lives at risk as it delays funding for FEWS NET".

"The Department should stop stalling and allow FEWS NET to do what it does best — provide analysis to prevent famine abroad,” he said. The State Department did not respond to a request for comment on Meeks' remarks.
