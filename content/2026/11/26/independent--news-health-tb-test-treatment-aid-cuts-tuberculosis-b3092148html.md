Efforts to make tuberculosis a disease of the past are faltering as the impacts of global aid cuts are being felt, campaigners have said at a major global conference.

Patients diagnosed with tuberculosis in Nigeria are being forced to delay treatment because crucial drugs are out of stock. In Zambia, a community organisation supporting TB detection and care will shut its doors after nearly 20 years.

Examples of aid cuts threatening progress against the deadly illness were abundant as scientists, policymakers and activists met in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, for The Union World Conference on Lung Disease.

More than 10 million people fall ill with TB each year, and a million die from the disease - making it the leading infectious cause of death globally.

Some $22 billion (£16.8bn) per year is needed for TB prevention, diagnosis and treatment in low and middle income countries, according to internationally agreed targets. However in 2025, only $6.57bn was available.

“The financial situation is perhaps the greatest immediate threat to progress,” said Dr Kavindhran Velen, chief scientific officer at the International Union Against Tuberculosis and Lung Disease (The Union).

Countries like the US and UK have made deep cuts to their aid budgets over the past two years.The Global Fund to Fight Aids, TB and Malaria only raised $13bn for its 2026-2028 funding round, against an $18bn target.

The impact of falls in TB aid spending has not been evenly distributed, according to the World Health Organisation’s annual report on tuberculosis, which also warned effects “may be delayed or not yet visible”.

Some countries saw total funding fall, while in others governments were able to dig deeper into their own budgets to mitigate the cuts. New agreements with the US government actually meant funding increases in some countries, the report said. However, Lucica Ditiu, executive director of the Stop TB Partnership, said that even if some countries have seen funding remain fairly stable, what was being funded had changed.

“If there is an area that suffers a lot, it is the funding for communities and civil society,” she said. Grassroots organisations have not only been vital in delivering services to vulnerable and hard-to-reach communities, but also in campaigning against stigma and for the human rights of people affected by the disease.

That advocacy work is now harder to fund, Ms Ditiu said – adding that she was also concerned that attention to TB might be crowded out by broader issues such as global conflict and economic uncertainty.

Dr Temitope Adetiba of the Institute of Human Virology Nigeria presented research at the conference that showed case detection in Kano, Nigeria’s most populous state, had in fact more than doubled between 2024 and 2025.

“There’s a certain extent to which we can be squeezed and will still be able to respond positively,” he said.

But he added that the impact of TB in the country – driven by factors such as malnutrition and conflict – was not decreasing: “When you keep having the burden sustained, and the resources dwindling, it means you are facing an uphill battle.”

There are already warning signs of progress being lost, Dr Adetiba said. “We now have a waiting list of patients that have been diagnosed and can't be placed on treatment, which never used to be the case. And the longer an infectious case has to wait before he begins treatment, the more he can transmit to others.”

Testing turnaround times are also increasing, he said, with shortages of the cartridges needed to run specialist molecular tests.

CITAM+, a community organisation working with people affected by TB and HIV in Zambia, set up in 2007, will be forced to close its doors completely next month, its executive director, Carol Nyirenda said: “I can’t afford to pay the rentals, I can’t afford to pay the workers.”

Before Donald Trump cut funding for the United States Agency for International Development (USAID) in 2025, impacting the organisation, it engaged more than 500 volunteers across four provinces who, for a small stipend, carried out work such as contact tracing and supporting people through the process of TB diagnosis and treatment, or encouraging people to attend screening.

“What's happening now is [the government and most of the donors] are only funding what they call life-saving activities,” said Ms Nyirenda. “So all right, you bring all these vaccines, all these good equipment, but people are staying at home. They're not coming to us. What is the point? Those are wasted investments.”

However, there were some bright spots at the conference. Novel technologies highlighted at the conference included new vaccines against TB now in late-stage trials, and tiny tabletop machines that can be taken anywhere to test for TB with swift results, which could help the issue of people staying at home.
