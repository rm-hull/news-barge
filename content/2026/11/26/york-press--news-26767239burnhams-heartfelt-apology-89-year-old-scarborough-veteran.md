A SCARBOROUGH veteran who was told as a young soldier that he would not live to see his 50th birthday has been personally apologised to by the Prime Minister after decades of campaigning by Britain’s nuclear test veterans.

Edward Luther, now 89, was stationed on Christmas Island during Britain’s nuclear weapons testing programme in 1957 and has spent years seeking answers about health problems suffered by both him and his family.

His case was raised in the House of Commons by Alison Hume, MP for Scarborough and Whitby, during a statement on a new independent panel established to examine the experiences of veterans involved in the UK’s nuclear testing programme.

The panel will be chaired by former Bishop of Liverpool James Jones, who previously led the Hillsborough Independent Panel.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21769957.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Alison Hume MP Asks Prime Minister About Scarborough Veteran. Parliament Live

Speaking in Parliament on November 25, Ms Hume said: “I thank the Prime Minister for his commitment to truth and justice for our nuclear test veterans.

“My constituent Edward Luther was stationed on Christmas Island in 1957.

“Aged just 20, he was told on the way over that he would not make it to 50. At 89 years old, he is still waiting for answers to medical issues that both he and his family have suffered.

“He told me that he feels abandoned and dismissed.

“Today, I hope that he feels heard.”

She also urged the Government to ensure surviving veterans were invited to a planned national memorial event, noting that Mr Luther had received his Nuclear Test Medal through the post rather than at a formal ceremony.

In response, Prime Minister Andy Burnham delivered a direct apology to the veteran.

He said: “Can I say a heartfelt sorry to Edward, who has been left in a completely unacceptable position by the country he served so well?”

The Prime Minister added that a planned national memorial service would be intended to give veterans the recognition they deserved.

“I can give my hon. Friend that assurance, because the national memorial service I mentioned in my statement is entirely intended for that purpose – that we get a moment to put things right as best we can, and give people the recognition that they rightly deserve.”

Around 22,000 British servicemen, including many national servicemen, were involved in nuclear weapons tests in the Pacific during the 1950s.

Veterans have long argued that exposure to radiation during the tests led to serious health problems affecting both them and their descendants, claims that have been disputed by the Ministry of Defence.

The newly established panel is expected to operate under the Hillsborough Law framework, which creates a legally enforceable duty of candour on public authorities during investigations and inquiries.

The Government hopes the review will finally address longstanding questions raised by veterans and their families about what happened during Britain’s nuclear testing programme.
