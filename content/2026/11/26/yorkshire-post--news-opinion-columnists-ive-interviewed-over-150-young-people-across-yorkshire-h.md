# I've interviewed over 150 young people across Yorkshire - here's what concerns me about Alan Milburn's plans: Aniela Wenham

## A central ambition of Alan Milburn’s review into young people and work is to give local areas greater responsibility for coordinating employment support. But that will depend not only on helping young people into work, but also on the opportunities available where they live.

Over the past nine years, I have interviewed more than 150 young people in Scarborough, Bradford and York about their experiences of poverty, inequality and growing up. Their experiences show how much the opportunities available to young people can depend on where they live. While Milburn recognises these geographical inequalities, there are important questions about whether his proposals can improve employment prospects in places where secure, adequately paid work is limited.

Young people we spoke to in Scarborough drew attention to the difficulties of finding secure, well-paid work in a town heavily reliant on tourism and hospitality. Robin, 17, described how jobs often disappear after the summer: "Yeah, it's very seasonal. If you're looking for jobs, they're mainly going in the summer, then you just get kicked out". Josh, 18, raised a similar concern: "once that seasonal period’s done, what are you meant to do?".

Their experiences highlight the difficulties of building financial security when work is temporary and opportunities for reliable, year-round employment are limited. Temporary contracts and insufficient hours can leave young people churning between work, underemployment and periods without work.

These challenges exist alongside considerable deprivation in the region. The 2025 Indices of Deprivation show that 31 per cent of residents in the Scarborough and Whitby area live in neighbourhoods among the 20 per cent most deprived nationally, compared with just 6 per cent across North Yorkshire. Inadequate income can also make it harder to afford transport, housing or training, creating further barriers to employment.

This makes Milburn's proposed benefit reductions especially concerning. Social security can help young people find and sustain employment, rather than discourage it. Reducing benefits for young people already at greater risk of poverty and destitution, when existing support often does not stretch far enough, is not progressive policy. As the Institute for Fiscal Studies warns, "Many young people with health conditions would get less in benefits, and only some of them would be able to offset that loss by moving into work”. Financial security and meaningful employment should not be treated as competing priorities.

In York, our research has highlighted different but related pressures, particularly the relationship between employment, housing costs and young people's ability to remain in the communities where they have grown up. As Hannah, 17, explained: "I'd love to stay in York, I've always said this… I've grown up here, everything's here for me, but then it's expensive; I know people are struggling to get houses." Having a job is only part of what young people need to become financially independent and build secure futures.

Milburn's proposals for locally led Youth Opportunity Services, better coordination between employment, education and health, and longer-term funding are welcome. But giving local areas responsibility for employment support is not the same as giving them the powers and resources to improve local economies. Delivering these reforms will also require effective partnerships and institutional capacity, particularly where devolved structures are relatively new. Without adequate investment, differences in local capacity could reinforce rather than reduce geographical inequalities. And regional strategies must recognise that places such as Scarborough and York face very different challenges, even within the same combined authority. Improving employment services cannot substitute for sustained investment in good-quality local jobs.

Our research has also shown how young people can feel disconnected from local regeneration, especially where tourism appears to take priority over community needs.

Young people must have a meaningful role in shaping regeneration and local economic priorities, not simply be consulted about them. Ultimately, success should be measured not only by fewer young people classified as NEET, but by whether they can access secure jobs, adequate incomes and financial protection that support their transitions into adulthood and enable them to build futures in the places they call home.

Aniela Wenham is Professor of Social Policy at the University of York
