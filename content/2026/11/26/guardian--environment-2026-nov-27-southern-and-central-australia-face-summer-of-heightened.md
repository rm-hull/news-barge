Large parts of southern and central Australia face elevated fire risk this summer, authorities say, while heatwaves and extreme temperatures are likely nationwide.

Most of Victoria, eastern Tasmania and large parts of South Australia, Western Australia, New South Wales and the Northern Territory face heightened bushfire risk – driven by high fuel loads, low soil moisture and forecast hot and dry conditions – according to the seasonal outlook from the Australian and New Zealand Council for Fire and Emergency Services (AFAC), released on Friday.

The AFAC chief executive, Rob Webb, said nearly every state had areas of above average risk heading into the hottest part of the year. He urged people to make sure they had a plan and to talk to family and neighbours.

**“** Know what you’re going to do and know where you’ll get your information – don’t wait till the fire’s there,” he said.

In Victoria, the dire outlook followed back-to-back years of devastating fires and record-breaking heat. Recent rains had spurred abundant grass growth in places, which could dry rapidly, creating the potential for grassfires as the weather warms up, AFAC said.

Last summer, more than 440,000 hectares of land burned across Victoria – an area larger than the Black Saturday fires of 2009. That followed major fires in the Grampians national park in 2024-25.

The state’s emergency management commissioner, Tim Wiebusch, warned that bushfires wouldn’t be the only risk Victorians would have to deal with.

“We’ve also seen in recent times the storm risk here in Victoria. We’ve seen flash flooding, we’ve seen low levels of riverine flooding, but we’ve also seen earthquakes, and we’ve seen the risk of thunderstorm asthma,” he said.

“All Victorians need to prepare for a mixed bag of emergencies over our high-risk weather season as we come through the summer months.”

In Tasmania, residents on Flinders Island were already contending with an out-of-control blaze, after a warm and dry year, and rainfall well below average.

“Tasmania has been particularly dry,” Webb said. “The fuel is there to burn, it’s just a matter of whether we get the weather conditions, the windy conditions, that allow fires to become established.”

Climate councillor Dr Andrew Watkins said a complex combination of factors – including the strong El Niño, a positive Indian Ocean Dipole, and influences from Antarctica – were combining with long-term warming to create tinderbox conditions in parts of the country.

Global heating, fuelled by the burning of fossil fuels, was already having an influence on fires across the world, said Watkins, adjunct professor at Monash University’s School of Earth, Atmosphere and Environment.

“South-eastern Australia is one of the most bushfire-prone places in the entire world,” he said.

“Nowhere on that map is low risk. Everyone needs to prepare, and unfortunately climate change is going to mean that’s the norm for the future.”

Hotter than usual days and nights were likely nationwide between December and February, which could lead to extreme temperatures and heatwaves, according to the Bureau of Meteorology. North-western Australia and eastern Tasmania were forecast to be drier than usual in summer, while above average rainfall was expected for Queensland and north-eastern New South Wales. Oceans around Australia were mostly much warmer than usual, with the temperature expected to rise over coming months.

In its summer outlook, the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority was forecasting a high risk of widespread coral bleaching, with marine heatwaves up to 2C warmer than average expected.

The Australian Marine Conservation Society has called for an emergency response plan for the reef, which could include temporary protections for critical reefs to restrict certain activities, like removing coral.

“Everyone is very worried,” said Dr Lissa Schindler, the AMCS’s campaign manager. “The Great Barrier Reef is heading into another dangerous summer, with El Niño threatening to intensify marine heatwaves across the whole World Heritage Area.

“The warning lights are flashing red. We need to be throwing everything that we can at this, to give the reef a fighting chance.”
