The environment minister, Murray Watt, has acknowledged concerns raised by scientists and celebrity campaigners, including Hugh Jackman, about a contentious Woodside offshore gas proposal in northern Western Australia, saying he “absolutely understands” why they think it could have “very serious impacts” on nature.

The federal government is expected to make a decision on whether to approve the Browse gas development near Scott Reef, about 300km off the Kimberley coast, under national environment law early next year.

Scientists and WA state regulators have documented the risk of leakage and oil spills near the reef, which is home to more than 1,500 species, many of them unique to the area. They include endangered green turtles and migratory whales.

Watt said from “day one” of the project there had been a “level of concern about what the environmental impacts would be”, and the national environment agency had to assess whether they could be managed.

“I don’t think that Hugh Jackman or any of the other people campaigning against this project are wrong to be concerned because, clearly, the project at an early stage was considered to potentially have some very serious impacts,” Watt said, noting that the former government in 2019 “decided that the Browse project was likely to have significant impacts on various environmental matters”.

More than 150 Australian artists, including Jackman, Rebel Wilson and Jessica Mauboy, last week backed a Greenpeace-led campaign against Woodside’s proposal, signing an open letter and urging the government to block it.

Browse, which was first proposed by Woodside in 2018, would involve gas being piped about 900km to the North West Shelf gas export plant, which last year had its life extended to 2070 despite concerns of its potential impact on world heritage-listed Murujuga rock art.

WA’s independent environmental watchdog sharply criticised the proposal in 2023 in a draft letter, warning it might be impossible to reduce the risk of an oil spill to an acceptable level, and that plans to protect marine life near Scott Reef would be ineffective.

“I absolutely understand people’s concerns about the potential environmental impacts from big projects like these,” Watt said.

“What the EPA’s got to do is get across in much more thorough detail and go through the scientific evidence to assess what those impacts would be.”

Saturday marks one year since Labor’s reformed Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation (EPBC) Act was legislated, but Watt said that all projects being assessed under the environment laws before 1 December would be assessed under the previous rules. That included Woodside’s Browse proposal. No fossil fuel development proposals have been rejected since the legislation was passed.

Watt said the new rules would set a higher bar for fossil fuel projects proposed after 1 December though the laws do not include the effects of greenhouse gas emissions on the climate as grounds to block a development.

“I think there are stronger tests that need to be passed by all projects – fossil fuel and other projects – as a result of our reforms,” Watt said. “The EPA, I think, will be running a very close eye over fossil fuel projects in the future to make sure that they do meet stronger tests under the reforms.”

Watt defended the government’s treatment of native forestry logging under the changes made last year. Under the revamped laws, an effective exemption for native forest logging from the EPBC Act was removed.

But conservationists have been concerned that decisions on logging could still be left to pro-forestry state governments, which argue the industry does not harm the environment, under bilateral agreements that hand decision-making powers to state governments. Tasmanian Greens senator Nick McKim described this as a “sneaky way to keep the chainsaws running”.

Watt said the bilateral agreement would still require the state to abide by stricter federal rules.

“At the time we passed the reforms, I made the point, our government’s policy is not to end native forestry but we do want to see it become more sustainable,” Watt said.

“We have to accredit whatever environmental framework the Tasmanian government applies to forestry, which means that it has to meet our standards in a way that they’ve never had to do before.”
