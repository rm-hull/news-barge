The UK is seeing fewer frosty days in winter and more frequent extreme heat in the summer as weather patterns shift with climate change, the Met Office has warned.

Across the country, the average number of days where forecasters recorded an air frost – when the air temperature falls to 0C or below – has decreased by around a quarter in the last decade, from 38 days between 1961 to 1990 to around 28 days between 2016 and 2026.

Forecasters said we are seeing “more frequent and severe extreme weather events” and warned that climate change is “shifting the baseline” of the country’s climate. But they said while the number of frosty days is falling, wintry threats are projected to “remain key elements of the forecast for many years to come”.

"Our summer is getting wider and our winter is getting smaller," says Dr James Pope, climate scientist at the Met Office, told the BBC.

"The driver of this is that the whole global climate is warming. And that impacts on the UK. Some areas went from their wettest ever winter to a severe drought via a record-breaking summer for the whole country."

![Met Office data shows the number of days recording an air frost in the winter has fallen dramatically](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/11/26/8/21/WinterFrostDays-768x432.png?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Data collected by the Met Office shows it is not just the country’s winters that are changing. The UK is seeing an average of almost five fewer air frost days in spring and almost three in autumn, the forecaster said.

The new figures also show how the country is also getting hotter in the summer. The number of 25C summer days across the UK has more than doubled, from an average of around approximately four days per summer during 1961 to 1990 to almost nine days in the last decade.

These changes are even more extreme in southern England, where the number of winter days recording an air frost has decreased from an average of around 33 days per winter during the period 1961-1990 to almost 23 in the last decade.

The number of summer days in the south of England of 25C has more than doubled from an average of just over eight days every summer between 1961-1990 to more than 16 in the last ten years.

## What does this mean for the country?

The changing climate has a number of impacts across the country, some of which became stark during this year’s record-breaking summer.

Five heatwaves between May and August left the majority of the country in drought and sparked wildfires and travel disruption. Hosepipe bans were brought in for millions of households, and a recent UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) report linked more than 5,000 deaths to the heat in England this year.

![Grass turned brown as five heatwaves scorched the UK (Annabel Lee-Ellis/PA)](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/01/07/e25efb681dc039216f7e16078e50b312Y29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzg4MzMwNTMz-2.85570477.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The exceptionally hot and dry spring and summer left farmers at “breaking point” as the extreme heat impacted yields and left the country at risk of food shortages.

Extreme temperatures have also put pressure on the UK’s wildlife, according to scientists, who warned a warmer climate will see certain invasive species thrive.

## What does this mean for future summers and winters?

The Met Office has issued some stark projections for the UK’s summers and winters to come.

If the world warms by 2C, the forecaster said the number of days over 25C in the summer could increase to around 15 days. Similarly, the number of winter days recording an air frost across the UK could reduce further to around 24 days per winter.

These projections are even more extreme in southern England, which tends to see the hottest temperatures in the country.

Met Office chief executive, Penny Endersby, said: "The weather affects every aspect of our national life, and increasingly so with more frequent and severe extreme weather events resulting from our changing climate.”
