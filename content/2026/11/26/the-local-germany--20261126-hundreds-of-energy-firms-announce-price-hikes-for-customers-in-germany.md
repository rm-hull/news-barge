Hundreds of regional energy providers in Germany have recently announced new prices. So far, Verivox had counted as many as 361 gas and 329 electricity increases at the time of writing.

Earlier this month, the comparison portal had already reported that household gas tariffs could rise by as much as 20 percent next year.

Here’s what the price increases mean in practice – including examples for households in Berlin, Hesse, Bavaria and Hamburg – and what you can do to keep costs under control.

**What is the ‘basic rate’?**

The announced price rises mainly hit households on the "basic rate" – *Grundversorgung* in German, sometimes also translated as the "basic tariff" – which is the default tariff you land on if you've never signed a contract of your own with an energy supplier.

Every part of Germany has a default supplier, which is typically the company serving the most households there. If your bill or contract says *Grundversorgung*or possibly* Basis Privatstrom*, then you’re on the basic rate – which is often the most expensive way to buy energy.

The Federal Network Agency estimates that just over a fifth of households in Germany get their electricity this way, and just over one in six get their gas this way.

**Why prices are increasing**

The increases are mostly due to take effect between December and February. Based on the announcements so far, Verivox puts the average gas rise at about 14 percent. For a single-family home using 20,000 kilowatt-hours (kWh) a year, that means around €370 more a year.

Electricity is rising by about eight percent on average, which is roughly €128 more a year for a three-person household using 4,000 kWh.

The main reason for the increase in gas prices is simple. Wholesale gas prices have nearly tripled since the start of the year. Energy bills tend to react slowly to market prices, so many households are only feeling this now. Higher grid fees are also pushing up electricity prices.

There is some possible relief. The government plans to cut electricity tax to the EU minimum for 2027.

This isn't yet reflected in the announced prices. Assuming the law passes – and the saving is passed on to customers in full – a 4,000 kWh household could expect to save about €93 a year.

Because prices are set locally, households in different parts of Germany will be affected differently depending on the local default supplier. Some suppliers are also increasing prices beyond the basic rate.

Here's how prices are changing in some German states:

**Berlin**

Gas and electricity rates are both going up in the capital.

Electricity provider Vattenfall has said it will raise its basic electricity price on February 1st, from 37.93 to 42.44 cents per kWh. That's about €8.27 more a month for a typical household using 2,200 kWh.

Similarly, gas supplier Gasag recently announced a rise of 2.98 cents per kWh from January, and a higher monthly base charge for the first time in 20 years. That's reportedly just under €10 a month more for people using gas for cooking and hot water, and about €32 a month for those heating an average flat with it.

Vattenfall says most Berlin customers are already on cheaper special rates, which are protected by their contract's price guarantee.

**Hesse**

Prices here depend heavily on which town you live in.

Frankfurt's municipal utility Mainova has said it will hold its basic gas price at 12.76 cents per kWh from January 1st. Its electricity price is going up though, from 38.78 to 39.10 cents per kWh. That's about €32 more a year for a household using 2,500 kWh.

Further south, Entega, which supplies Darmstadt and the surrounding area, has announced a rise in its basic gas rate from 12.86 to 15.24 cents per kWh. That's an increase of more than 16 percent. Its electricity price is also going up by 3.5 cents to 42.48 cents per kWh.

In central Hesse, Stadtwerke Gießen is raising its gas price slightly, while in the east, RhönEnergie Fulda reportedly plans to keep gas prices stable.

Some providers in Bavaria are putting prices up, but others are holding them.

Lechwerke, which supplies parts of Bavarian Swabia, has said it will raise its basic electricity price on January 1st, from 35.24 to up to 40.42 cents per kWh. That's about €130 more a year for a household using 2,500 kWh.

Stadtwerke Ulm/Neu-Ulm, which straddles the Bavaria–Baden-Württemberg border, has announced an increase of about €7.43 a month for the same type of household.

The picture is better in Augsburg. Stadtwerke Augsburg says it will keep both its electricity and gas prices unchanged. In the Allgäu, Allgäuer Überlandwerk has also promised to hold its electricity prices and plans a new tariff with a price guarantee, reportedly up to five years for an extra €1 a month.

**Hamburg**

In Hamburg Vattenfall has said it will raise its "Hamburg Basis Privatstrom" basic electricity rate on February 1st, from 38.95 to 43.63 cents per kWh. That's about €117 more a year for a household using 2,500 kWh. The monthly base fee stays at €16.55.

According to t-online, the extra annual cost ranges from about €56 for a single person in a flat to around €220 for a family in a house that heats its water electrically.

Households with electric water heaters use more power, so they feel the rise more.

**How to switch from a basic rate**

If you're currently getting energy at the basic rate, switching off of it could save you several hundred euros a year, according to consumer advocates.

Even basic-rate providers usually offer cheaper tariffs, so you may not need to change company at all.

First, check whether you're on the basic rate. Then compare tariffs, ideally from your current supplier and from rivals.

Don't just go for the lowest headline price. Check any price guarantee – and make sure you understand the implications of any bonus conditions.

Leaving is easier than you might expect. You can cancel a basic-rate contract at any time with two weeks' notice.
