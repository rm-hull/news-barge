## Good morning. Leo Cendrowicz here with your Thursday newsletter. We have almighty punch-up on our agenda today, as EU leaders gather in Brussels to brawl over their seven-year budget – a slugfest that almost certainly won’t be resolved by the end of the summit.

**Brace yourselves.** EU leaders gather in Brussels this morning for what promises to be one of the angriest summits in years. There is precious little goodwill in the room, no obvious broker capable of bridging the differences and virtually no expectation of a breakthrough. The meeting could spill into tomorrow, although it is hard to see what another night of haggling might achieve.

As Jorge Liboreiro reports, two of the EU’s most divisive issues are heading for a collision: the next seven-year budget and the financing of Ukraine’s war effort. Technically separate, they have become politically intertwined. And nothing brings out the worst in European solidarity quite like an argument about money.

**Nobody’s happy.** European Council President António Costa has lopped €204 billion off the Commission’s original €2 trillion budget proposal for 2028-2034. His reward? Complaints from virtually every corner of Europe.

The German-led frugals, who now prefer to call themselves the “modernisers”, want deeper cuts to agricultural subsidies and regional development funds, prioritising defence, technology and competitiveness instead. The 17 Friends of Cohesion, predominantly southern and eastern countries, think the cuts have already gone too far.

France, despite securing concessions on farming subsidies and a proposed digital tax, is also deeply unimpressed. Paris wants to preserve spending while avoiding an increase in its national contribution – a particularly tricky equation given the parlous state of French public finances.

**The elephant in the room.** Marine Le Pen is not attending the summit, but her presence will be felt. The far-right frontrunner in next spring’s French presidential election has threatened to slash France’s contribution to the EU budget. Emmanuel Macron insists negotiations cannot extend beyond December. Unfortunately, imposing a deadline is rather easier than meeting it.**Enter Ukraine.** Russia’s relentless attacks have left Kyiv facing a €70 billion budget shortfall next year. The Commission is proposing another €45 billion loan, financed through joint borrowing, but Hungary, Slovakia, Bulgaria and the Czech Republic want no part in it.

An exasperated Friedrich Merz is threatening to veto the entire EU budget unless all 27 countries agree to support Ukraine. Slovak Prime Minister Robert Fico calls it a “textbook case of blackmail”.

The Commission’s suggested escape route would allow reluctant countries to contribute to the €20 billion earmarked for Ukraine’s civilian needs without participating in the €25 billion military component.

**Guns versus butter.** One of the more alarming aspects of the budget wrangling is that, for all the grand rhetoric about European sovereignty and military readiness, defence spending is also taking a hit. EU Defence Commissioner Andrius Kubilius warns that the proposed €39 billion cut to defence and space funding – a reduction of 30% – would jeopardise crucial pan-European programmes.

Former Commission President José Manuel Barroso weighed in, pointing to the glaring contradiction between European leaders’ declarations on defence and their willingness to pay for it. With Russia still waging war in Ukraine and Donald Trump proving an increasingly unreliable ally, the timing could hardly be worse.

**Something to agree on?** Leaders will also discuss Christine Lagarde’s successor at the European Central Bank, with Spain’s Pablo Hernández de Cos emerging as the favourite.

But the real measure of success by tomorrow will be whether a budget deal in December looks remotely achievable. The danger is that everyone leaves Brussels even angrier than when they arrived.

Meanwhile, the European Parliament is up in arms over Costa's proposed 10% cut to the MFF, threatening to reject the deal if it is struck by EU leaders. Vincenzo Genovese has the story from Strasbourg.

For more on this, follow our live blog on the EU summit.

## In other news

**With friends like these.** So much for transatlantic trade peace. Donald Trump is proposing another round of tariffs on 14 major trading partners, including the EU, ostensibly to combat industrial overcapacity. European goods could face duties of 15% on top of existing most-favoured-nation tariffs, potentially rising to 20%. Brussels strongly rejects Washington’s premise, pointing out that Europe is itself a victim of global overcapacity. For Europeans who thought last year’s painstakingly negotiated trade deal offered some protection against Trump’s tariff whims, it’s another rude awakening.**Who picks up the tab?** As EU leaders haggle over the next seven-year budget, Luca Bertuzzi and Kamuran Samar have put together a brilliant explainer on who would actually pay the proposed new EU taxes, complete with illuminating charts. The package could raise €60 billion annually, with Germany shouldering the biggest bill. Electronic waste, rather than Big Tech or tobacco, is the biggest money-spinner. Essential reading for understanding the national interests behind the budget horse-trading.**New faces, old dilemmas.** Serbia’s absence from yesterday’s EU-Western Balkans summit has postponed a crucial test of its new leadership. The student movement that ousted Aleksandar Vučić is making encouraging noises about democratic reforms but remains decidedly coy about aligning with EU foreign policy. Sanctioning Russia, a move opposed by 67% of Serbians, and normalising relations with Kosovo remain particularly thorny issues. Brussels is still waiting to see whether Belgrade’s geopolitical compass has shifted.**Trouble in the marriage.** The European Parliament’s Socialists and Liberals have suspended coalition talks with the centre-right EPP after it once again teamed up with the far right, this time to water down environmental safeguards, Vincenzo Genovese reports. They want written guarantees that Manfred Weber’s group will stop flirting with parties further to its right. Among the potential casualties of the spat: Roberta Metsola’s re-election as European Parliament president in January.**Taxing times.** Brussels could slap a windfall tax on oil and gas giants without giving national governments a veto. A new legal study suggests the EU could sidestep unanimity rules if the levy genuinely serves climate or energy policy rather than simply raising revenue. With fuel prices soaring, fossil fuel companies enjoying bumper profits and EU leaders squabbling over the next budget, the timing is particularly intriguing.**Quantum leap.** Having largely missed the boat on AI, Brussels is determined not to lose the next technology race. The Commission unveiled its Quantum Act yesterday, alongside a new fund designed to mobilise €50 billion in investment across critical technologies. Europe has a strong scientific pedigree in quantum but struggles to turn research into commercial success. There’s also a looming headache: quantum computers could crack today’s encryption systems.**Membership with strings attached.** Getting into the EU is hard enough, but Montenegro could find itself under special scrutiny even after joining. The Commission wants to extend its monitoring of reforms beyond accession, alongside a 10-year probation period during which serious breaches of EU values could trigger the suspension of voting rights. It’s all designed to prevent another Viktor Orbán, although some member states question whether Brussels really needs another policing mechanism.**Follow the powder.** Brussels wants to establish whether Irish-made alumina is feeding Russia’s war machine. Rather than banning exports of the raw material used to make aluminium, the Commission is proposing a reporting mechanism to track shipments to their final destination. Kyiv is disappointed, while Dublin fears hundreds of jobs could be at stake. An Irish investigation over the summer failed to settle the matter.**Political ippon?** French judo legend turned presidential hopeful Teddy Riner has questioned France’s military support for Ukraine, saying that he did "not understand" why France was sending troops there. The five-time Olympic gold medallist asked whether Ukraine was even in the EU.
