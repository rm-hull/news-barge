# Maximilian Riedel: An eye-opening wine session in Harrogate

## We all pay a lot of attention to the wines we buy and drink, but what about the glasses we drink from? If you have a dinner party, can you muster a table full of matching glasses, or are there a few that are slightly different because of breakages after a late-night washing-up session? Do you perhaps use a glass for the first wine, and then pour a different wine into the same glass?

Now, after sitting through a fascinating talk by Maximilian Riedel, I am almost too ashamed to set the table anymore. He is the 11th generation of his glass-making family, and at last week’s tasting in Harrogate, he demonstrated the need to have the correct glass shape for each grape variety.

I have a huge stock of glasses; many of them are from Riedel, but I don’t have enough of any one shape to set a table for eight people.

The only matched set I have is the Riedel glasses I acquired when I was studying for wine exams and now use for tastings – and those never get put out for dinner parties.

Maximilian gave two presentations. The morning session brought together 120 of Yorkshire’s sommeliers and wine professionals and demonstrated that it really is vital to match the shape of the glass to the grape variety you are pouring. The evening presentation, for 170 wine enthusiasts who came from miles around, showed off a completely different range of glasses but was run along the same lines.

I attended the first session, and it was totally impressive. After a quick history of Riedel and the fact that Maximilian’s grandfather started the process of designing glasses to suit particular wines, we started tasting.

With four glasses from the Veloce range in front of each of us, we poured the same wine into three of them. The wines had all been selected by the Riedel team from The Harrogate Fine Wine Company. Owner Andy Langshaw doesn’t just have a terrific range of wines; he also stocks Riedel glasses.

The first wine into the glasses was Newton Johnson Southend Chardonnay 2024 (£22.99) from South Africa, and we first looked at the wine in a glass that has been specifically designed to suit a Chardonnay wine. Maximilian took us through the tasting, making sure that no one got ahead of the group. We had to take the tasting slowly, looking at colour, aroma and then taste.

![Maximilian Riedel explores the fine aromas of a wine, when served in the correct glass   © Christine Austin](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOjQ4YTliMjk1LTc5YjUtNGJiNy1hMmY4LWZjZTIxZjg0MDA4NTpiMGMzMDcxOC0wOGU0LTQ0M2ItYjE4NC1lZDQxODczNTFhZGY=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)


The wine in the Chardonnay glass shone with complex aromas, and the taste had a creamy style and texture. Maximilian pointed out that the glass doesn’t need you to tilt your head as you drink. The rim is wide enough to enjoy the wine, and it hits the palate at the right point.

We moved on to the Riesling glass, much the same height, but narrower in style. It was as if someone had taken all the aroma away from the wine. It was dull, and then as we tasted it, the angle of my head had to change. The wine hit the tip of my tongue, but it didn’t taste like the same wine. There was a bitter streak and a green note on the palate.

Then we tried the wine from the Pinot Noir glass. This looked remarkably like the Chardonnay glass, but it was different in proportion. Once again, the aromas and the flavours of the wine were different.

It was almost as if Maximilian was a magician controlling the taste of the wine depending on which glass it was in.

Next, we moved on to a Pinot Noir, and Wente Vineyards Riva Ranch 2022 (£29.99) from California went into three different glasses. First the wine was poured into the Chardonnay glass, then the Pinot Noir glass and finally the Cabernet glass.

In the Pinot Noir glass, the wine showed red fruit cherries, linden berries, vanilla and spice. In the Chardonnay glass, it lost a lot of red fruit and acquired bitter notes, and then in the Cabernet glass, there was a strong nose, darker flavours, but not as much fruit. They really were different, and by this time, I was a total convert.

I was mentally rearranging my kitchen cupboards to accommodate a whole range of new glasses. But then we got a warning that you need to be careful where you store your glasses. Apparently, some cupboards give off an aroma of lacquer which gets taken up by the glass and carries through to the wine.

The final wine, a Cabernet Sauvignon 2023 from Vergelegen in South Africa (£21.99), was poured into three different glasses, but only the Cabernet glass delivered its best flavours.

The message was clear. If a restaurant pours wine into the correct Riedel glass, then the wine will taste better, and customers will enjoy it more. They may buy a second bottle. And this principle works at home too. The right glass will make the most of your wine, and your guests will enjoy the evening more.

But all this pleasure does not come cheap. Glasses from the Riedel Veloce range come at between £50 and £60 a pair. If you want to serve champagne followed by a white wine and then a red, you need three glasses per person, multiplied by however many people you can sit around your table.

A recent improvement, in particular the Veloce range, is that the base of the glass is discreetly engraved with the name of the grape variety that the glass has been designed for. My cupboard full of assorted Riedel glasses has no such identification, so I never know whether I am pouring a wine into the right glass.

But Christmas is coming, and this is the time to invest a little in the nice things of life. Champagne glasses might be the place to start, since flutes are now out of fashion, and your fizz should be in a wider glass, more egg-shaped than a flute.

Then, as the budget allows, you could buy some Cabernet glasses, which are the best-selling line, although clearly, they don’t work for Pinot Noir. And while working on your collection, check whether your cupboards are good enough for your new glassware.
