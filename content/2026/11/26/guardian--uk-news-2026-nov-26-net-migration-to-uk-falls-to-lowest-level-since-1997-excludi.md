Net migration across the UK has fallen to its lowest level since 1997 with the exclusion of the years of the Covid pandemic.

Figures released by the Office for National Statistics and the Home Office show that the difference between people arriving and leaving the UK was ⁠77,000 in ⁠the ​year ‌to ‌June ‌2026.

The ONS figures cover the year ending June 2026, down from an estimated 171,000 in 2025 and a peak of 944,000 in 2023. Annual net migration to the UK in 1997 was 48,000.

UK net migration is also for the first time in the “tens of thousands” since that target was set but never fulfilled by David Cameron’s government in 2010. An estimated 738,000 people immigrated to the UK in the year to June while 661,000 emigrated, according to the data published on Thursday.

The 62% fall over the past 12 months – from 204,000 in the year ending June 2025 – results from lower immigration. Net migration has fallen more than 90% since the peak of 944,000 in the year ending March 2023.

Immigration fell by 17% over the year to June 2026, driven by fewer non-EU citizens being granted permission to work, study, bring family members to the UK or apply for asylum.

Keir Starmer’s policies last year made care workers and some mid-skilled jobs ineligible for work visas, suspended family reunion for refugees and toughened restrictions for universities sponsoring international students.

Emigration remained relatively high but fell by 4% (-28,000) compared with the year ending June 2025.

Most people leaving the UK to live in another country were non-citizens who previously migrated for work or study, including those who moved to the UK as part of the “Boriswave” from 2021 onwards when immigration was unusually high.

Reacting to the figures, the home secretary, Shabana Mahmood, said Labour’s policies should be credited for the drop. It will be seen as a boost to Andy Burnham’s plans to keep hold of “red wall” seats in any future general election.

“The Conservatives lost control of our borders. Year upon year, they promised they would bring net migration down to the tens of thousands. Instead, they let it reach close to a million a year, overseeing four decades worth of migration in their last four years alone.

“This government has restored control. Where they failed, we have delivered for the British people,” she said.

Burnham said of the figures: “I think we are finally, as a country, getting into the position that the public has long wanted to see.”

Asked about the UK’s future relationship with the EU and whether freedom of movement should be up for discussion, he said the Channel crossings crisis “arose significantly in the post-Brexit years when we lost that ability to collaborate with our European partners, and we’ve had to work hard to catch up again … So, no, I don’t think you can say that Brexit did anything other than weaken control of immigration in this country.”

The drop has been driven by policies pushed through by Labour and Conservative governments.

Home Office visa data from January to June 2026 shows visa grants to international students fell by 38% (-39,200) and grants to skilled workers including those in the health and care sector and their dependants also dropped by 38% (-26,300) compared with the same period in 2025.

The end of social care visas has caused numbers to fall from 108,000 at their peak to just 1,400 last year.

Migration remains a key political issue, with Labour’s 2024 manifesto promising to reduce net migration, and aiming to “smash the gangs” involved in irregular migration and bringing asylum seekers across the Channel in small boats.

Burnham’s arrival in No 10 has signalled a shift in the way the government views legal migration. Some Labour insiders say that policy is now being driven by economic demands rather than a fear of the rise of Nigel Farage’s Reform UK, which has suffered a dip in the polls.

The Conservatives claimed their policies under Rishi Sunak’s administration were responsible for the drop, including restrictions on family members that could be brought over by care workers and changes stopping dependants moving to the UK with international students.

Chris Philp, the shadow home secretary, said: “The fall in net migration is mainly a result of changes made by the previous Conservative government. We now need to go even further with a legally binding annual cap, set by parliament, at a far lower level.”

Ben Brindle, a senior researcher at the Migration Observatory at the University of Oxford, said net migration could drop even further next year, and may have a “fiscal cost”.

“We are entering an unusual period where immigration has come down and may still have further to fall.

“Falling net migration is not automatically a problem for the UK economy, especially in the long run. Falling asylum migration is expected to benefit public finances. On the other hand, the number of migrants on skilled work visas has fallen even more over the past year, and that is expected to have a fiscal cost,” he said.

Sunder Katwala, the director of the British Future thinktank, said the figures “will come as a surprise to most of the public” who still tended to believe that immigration was rising. “It must now be time for the political and media debate to catch up and start a new phase of the debate about what happens next,” he said.

Other data published by the Home Office showed the number of asylum seekers living in hotels was at the lowest level since records began in 2022, but there was a 9% rise of those in “dispersal” accommodation – privately managed houses, flats or rooms in properties of multiple occupancy. There has been a drop in the number of new asylum applicants and Channel crossings are down 39% year on year, with 23,983 people having arrived in the UK in small boats in 2026.
