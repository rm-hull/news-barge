EU leaders braced for clashes over Ukraine aid at budget talks

Jennifer Rankin

Brussels correspondent

EU leaders are bracing for a clash over long-term financial aid for Ukraine, amid an effort to finalise high-stakes budget talks by the end of the year.

Arriving in Brussels on Thursday, the German chancellor, Friedrich Merz, reiterated his view that all EU member states should support a €45bn (£38bn) loan for Ukraine for 2027.

German chancellor Friedrich Merz attends a European Union (EU) summit in Brussels, Belgium. Photograph: Dursun Aydemir/Anadolu/Getty Images

“When the European Union provides funding, it does so as the European Union – and that union consists of 27 member states, not 23 or 24,” he said.

Slovakia, Hungary and Czechia, net beneficiaries, want to opt out of the loan. Slovakia’s prime minister, Robert Fico, who has maintained close ties to the Kremlin, has accused Merz of blackmail.

But Merz’s stance has won widespread support, especially among western European, Nordic and Baltic states.

Some governments, however, which support Merz’s goal have reservations about his tactics, fearing the public threat to block cohesion funds could make it harder for recalcitrant governments to climb down.

The discussion on support for Ukraine has become entangled in negotiations over the EU’s next long-term budget, which will set the bloc’s spending priorities for 2028-2034.

European parliament president Metsola says MEPs will 'not rubber-stamp' EU budget deal

Back to the negotiations in Brussels, European parliament president Roberta Metsola told EU leaders that they cannot expect MEPs to automatically approve a deal on the bloc’s next long-term budget, insisting that parliament’s priorities must be reflected in any agreement.

“Let’s see what the (European) Council finds as a landing zone when all the red lines are overcome.”

The final long-term budget must be approved by a majority of MEPs, meaning at least 361 of the European parliament’s 720 members. The vote will come after negotiations between parliament and the European Council, once EU member states have agreed on their position.

European Parliament president Roberta Metsola speaks during a media conference at the EU summit in Brussels. Photograph: Marius Burgelman/AP

On Ukraine, Metsola stressed the importance of continued EU support for Kyiv and its wider implications for European security.

“It’s not only solidarity, it is an investment in the future of Ukraine and our, Europe’s, very own security. The stakes go well beyond Ukraine’s borders, and we must never forget that,” she said.

Zelenskyy warns Russia preparing ‘new massive strike’ as Ukraine seeks urgent air defence supplies

Turning briefly to Ukraine, president Volodymyr Zelenskyy has warned that Russia is preparing for a “new massive strike” against the country, with energy facilities, logistics infrastructure and essential services expected to be among the targets.

“Unfortunately, the Russians are already completing preparations for a new massive strike,” Zelensky wrote on X.

He said Ukrainian intelligence indicated that the country’s energy infrastructure would again be a primary target, as repair crews continue working around the clock to restore electricity supplies following Russian attacks on “substations, overhead power lines, and the entire power grid”.

A firefighter works to extinguish a fire at a house following a Russian air attack in Kharkiv, Ukraine, on Thursday. Photograph: Andrii Marienko/AP

Zelensky added that he was awaiting an update from Ukraine’s foreign ministry on discussions with international partners over additional air defence supplies, adding that interceptor missiles for Patriot and Nasams systems were a “key priority”.

His warning comes as EU leaders meet in Brussels to discuss continued financial support for Ukraine, alongside negotiations on the bloc’s next long-term budget. The European Commission has proposed a further €45bn loan for Kyiv in 2027, but member states remain divided over how the money should be provided, with Germany pushing for the entire bloc to participate (see post at 9.15am).

In a video address yesterday, Zelenskyy said there was “no good news” from the US on accelerating air defence deliveries, saying they were “still delayed”.

Legal text underpinning last Friday's EU-UK 'reset' deal is out

Lisa O’Carroll

As promised, the draft legal text underpinning last Friday’s EU-UK reset is out.

Joint press conference after the end of the EU-UK Summit in Brussels last week. Photograph: Nicolas Economou/NurPhoto/Shutterstock

The 80 page legal text of the food and drink agreement outlines the rules for the creating of a “common sanitary and phyto sanitary area” which will be “governed by the legal acts of the union” to which the “UK shall dynamically align and simultaneously apply”.

Under the deal, the UK will have a “consultative” role in EU legislation but no vote.

“The United Kingdom shall have no voting rights” even in discussion on emergencies such as disease outbreaks, the text makes clear.

The consultation includes a “preliminary exchange of views” in a new joint EU-UK committee.

Experts in the UK and member states will be “informally consulted” instead, a move that may not satisfy food industry bodies that have argued that British regulators should be involved in all the technical meetings ahead of drafting of EU laws.

The adoption of the three main elements of last Friday’s reset including the food and drink agreement, will not take place until a separate agreement is reached on the level of tuition fees EU students will pay in the UK in a youth mobility scheme.

There will also be consultation with experts when laws are implemented to ensure the drafts on both sides of the channel align.

But the text specifically excludes a vote for the UK at higher level meetings

“The United Kingdom shall be invited to attend, as an observer, only those meetings or parts of meetings of the committees and expert groups” when the application of EU laws are addressed with the exception of “emergencies” which would include disease outbreaks.

The document includes a caveat that it is “published for information purposes only,” and “may undergo further modifications.” It “will be final upon signature,” it added.

Details of two treaties at heart of UK’s EU reset to be published today

Alexandra Topping

Political correspondent

Details of two treaties at the heart of Andy Burnham’s post-Brexit reset plans will be published on Thursday alongside the bill that will implement the changes into UK law.

Andy Burnham clasps hands the EU’s Ursula von der Leyen and António Costa in Brussels last week. Photograph: Simon Dawson/No 10 Downing Street

The treaties signed by the UK and the European Union at last week’s summit will have a 10-year termination clause that would mean if one party were to end the agreement, they would have to pay to rebuild border checks infrastructure, the BBC reported on Thursday.

Any termination clause could bring the longer-term future of the deal into doubt, after the opposition leader, Kemi Badenoch, described it as a “terrible deal” that she would scrap. Nigel Farage has suggested he would refuse to pay, with the Reform UK leader promising to renege on the deal if he were to win power, arguing that Burnham “cannot bind a future British government”.

At a major summit in Brussels last week the UK and EU announced three deals, on food and drink, emissions trading and a youth mobility scheme, which will allow 100,000 young Britons to live, work, study and travel in the EU, with reciprocal access for Europeans in the UK.

Two of the three legal texts will be released on Thursday, covering the food and emissions trading deals, alongside the government’s bill to enact the agreement. The government is also expected to publish assessments of the impact on different sectors and regions of the country .

But uncertainty around the trio of deals remains, with fears they could be derailed in six months unless the UK allows European students to pay domestic-level fees to study at British universities.

Marine Le Pen,the far-right frontrunner in the French presidential election next year, has blasted “underhanded political manoeuvring” as she described the push to get a deal on the next multiannual budget this year, well ahead of the French vote.

While she backed France’s attempt to increase agricultural funding, she said “the reasons have nothing to do with defending our farmers,” but with rushing the deal before she can get (still onlypotentially) elected in 2027.
