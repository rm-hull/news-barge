EU leaders are bracing for a clash over long-term financial aid for Ukraine amid an effort to finalise high-stakes budget talks by the end of the year.

Arriving in Brussels on Thursday, the German chancellor, Friedrich Merz, reiterated his view that all EU member states should support a €45bn (£38bn) loan for Ukraine for 2027. “When the European Union provides funding, it does so as the European Union – and that union consists of 27 member states, not 23 or 24,” he said.

Merz has previously threatened to veto the bloc’s next seven-year budget unless all countries sign up to the Ukraine loan, which would be a painful outcome for member states that are net recipients of EU funds.

Slovakia, Hungary and Czechia, net beneficiaries, want to opt out of the loan. Slovakia’s prime minister, Robert Fico, who has maintained close ties to the Kremlin, has accused Merz of blackmail.

But Bulgaria, which had been against the loan, said on Thursday it was ready to contribute as long as its share was not linked to weapons.

“Bulgaria is going to ‌take part in the loan of 20bn [for] non-military support ​for Ukraine,” Bulgaria’s prime minister, Rumen Radev, said as he arrived at the summit. “We have a ⁠huge historic diaspora of Bulgarians living ​in Ukraine. Our wish is ​to concentrate our support ​in that ‌area. We are ​going ​to contribute, but no money from Bulgaria for weapons,” he said.

The European Commission last week proposed a €45bn EU loan for Ukraine to fill a funding black hole caused by Russia’s relentless attacks. EU officials proposed €25bn for military spending and €20bn for Ukraine’s civilian needs, such as schools and hospitals.

Arriving at the summit, Czechia’s populist prime minister, Andrej Babiš, was in no mood for compromise. “Cohesion is really the goal of the European Union and [the] Czech Republic. We will fight for this money,” he said, referring to the EU’s cohesion funds, which are intended to help poorer regions catch up with richer ones.

Babiš said his country needed a highway to Austria and more hospitals. “Financing of Ukraine should be negotiated separately. And the most important is the life of our citizens,” he said, adding that the EU should “do everything” to support peace in Ukraine.

But Merz’s stance has won widespread support, especially among western European, Nordic and Baltic states. Sweden’s prime minister, Ulf Kristersson, said: “It’s not acceptable to simply kidnap the support for Ukraine by saying that we will accept the European Union support for Ukraine, but we will not pay for it ourselves. That is not a reasonable way of thinking.”

However, some governments that support Merz’s goal have reservations about his tactics, fearing the public threat to block cohesion funds could make it harder for recalcitrant governments to climb down.

The discussion on support for Ukraine has become entangled in negotiations over the EU’s next long-term budget, which will set the bloc’s spending priorities for 2028-2034.

EU leaders are discussing a €1.78tn proposal presented by the European Council president, António Costa, last Saturday, which represents a 10% reduction on the original €2tn plan from the European Commission.

EU officials want to finalise a budget deal by the end of the year before a sequence of big elections in 2027 complicates finding an agreement. The far-right leader Marine Le Pen, who is the frontrunner in next year’s French presidential elections, wants to slash France’s contribution to the budget and cease funding Ukraine.

The Irish taoiseach, Micheál Martin, said it was important to get a budget agreement before the end of the year as “the prospects of doing it in the first half of 2027 are very dim”.

Separately, Germany is seeking to insulate EU funding for Ukraine from a Le Pen victory: Berlin wants to ensure future decisions on funding Kyiv via the EU budget are made by a majority of EU member states. The current system requires unanimity, enabling one country to veto a decision.

Others have pushed back against allowing French elections to dominate the EU budget timetable, and have said making Le Pen the focus gives her ammunition to make a case that the EU is against her political movement. “We should be aware of a self-fulfilling prophecy,” said one senior EU diplomat. “Let’s not be paralysed by elections happening in six months.”

Arriving at the summit on Thursday, Costa said all leaders needed to approach discussions with an “open mind, a constructive approach and a sense of compromise” as he called for the budget to be approved in a “timely” way, adding: “That means by the end of this year.”

The budget has to be agreed unanimously, but countries are deeply split. In one camp are the so-called frugals – net contributors to the EU budget such as Germany, the Netherlands and Sweden – which want to reduce the overall size and shift spending priorities to defence and competitiveness, rather than farm subsidies and regional funds.

In the other camp are 17 countries in southern, central and eastern Europe, which want to ensure the flow of EU funds to traditional policies and oppose cuts.

France sits in neither camp, but has vowed to protect the common agriculture policy (CAP), which benefits French farmers. Maintaining farm payments would make it harder for Le Pen to unravel the budget. On the eve of the summit, the French president, Emmanuel Macron, said “not a single euro of the CAP must be withheld from France in the next European multi-annual budget”.

Le Pen welcomed press reports that Paris was pushing for increased agriculture spending, but said Macron’s position had “nothing to do” with defending French farmers. “The objective would be to dissuade us – if the French entrusted us with the responsibility to govern – from defending French interests in European budgetary negotiations” she wrote on X.
