## The European Commission last week unveiled a €45 billion loan to help Ukraine amid Russia's nonstop airstrikes. But how is the EU affording all this support?

The proposal by the EU executive for an additional loan to help Ukraine address its growing wartime budget deficit is one of the key topics over which EU leaders are butting heads at the EU summit this week.

On the table is a €45 billion support loan, which Brussels says will help to plug an estimated €70 billion funding black hole in Kyiv's 2027 budget caused by Russia’s relentless attacks. The money also aims to strengthen the country's defence capabilities and ensure the continuation of basic public services.

Since the start of the war in 2022, the EU and its members have given Kyiv nearly €245.5 billion in overall support.

So, how does the bloc pay for this loan? **James Thomas** dug into the matter with**Jacob Funk Kirkegaard**, a senior fellow at the Bruegel think tank, in this episode of Euronews Verify.

The EU gets the money through borrowing on the financial markets. This is guaranteed by the EU's budget headroom — the difference between the maximum amount the EU can call on from its member states and what it needs to finance the budget.

Important here is that the debt appears on the EU's ledgers, not the member states'.

"Because the EU is a treaty-based organisation, the EU can issue debt that doesn't show up on the Member States' balance sheet. It shows up on the EU's balance sheet," Kirkegaard said.

## What about using frozen Russian assets?

The proposal is a "limited recourse" loan, meaning that, in theory, Ukraine will only have to pay back the initial amount once it receives reparations from Russia.

The plan also includes a provision confirming that the EU reserves the right to repay it using frozen Russian assets, instead of Ukraine, pointing to the windfall profits generated by the frozen funds, such as interest and investment returns.

The EU has frozen about €210 billion in Russian Central Bank assets since 2022, according to a European Parliament briefing, with tens of billions in private Russian assets also frozen across the bloc.

"I believe that, since the prospects that a nuclear-armed country like Russia will ever actually pay war reparations [are small], either the EU is refunded by these immobilised Russian assets, or the loan is ultimately paid by European taxpayers," Kirkegaard noted.

## Why is the loan dividing the EU?

Because citizens may end up footing the bill, leaders are clashing over this latest financial aid package. Some governments are wary of the optics of approving fresh cash for Ukraine at a time of high energy bills, tight budgets and general discontent.

But Kirkegaard noted that the €45 billion support loan being discussed is a pittance compared to the size of the EU economy.

"We are talking about something like 0.2% of EU GDP, which is literally a drop in the bucket," he said. "It is also a drop in the bucket compared to EU social expenditures by governments, etc. It's even a drop in the bucket compared to the size of the regular EU budget, which is easily, on an annual basis, counted in hundreds of billions."

He added that the cost of not supporting Ukraine is much higher.

"If you think through what the true cost in the long run for Europe would be. To arm ourselves, to be able to deter Russia if it had subdued Ukraine, the cost of that rearmament in Europe would be a factor of 10 [higher]," Kirkegaard concluded.

## Are Black Friday deals paying off?

Also in today's episode, online shoppers are questioning whether the Black Friday deals popping up everywhere are quite what they seem.

Some consumer research suggests they have a reason to be sceptical, but not all offers are illusions. **Tamsin Paternoster** breaks down what makes a good deal.

One key element is widening the period of making comparisons.

"More than half [of the deals] were even cheaper on another day in November before Black Friday itself," Florian Kriegel, Price Expert at idealo, told Euronews Verify. "So it's not about the one day anymore or the weekend. It's about the whole November."

Under EU rules, retailers announcing price reductions generally have to use their lowest price from the previous 30 days as the reference, to stop shops from raising the price just before a sale.

In a sweep of 314 online traders during last year's Black Friday sales, around 30% displayed inaccurate price reductions.

One way to avoid feeling scammed? Only buy what you really want or need.

"Consumers need to avoid the main mistake, which is allowing the price reduction advertisements to drive their purchasing decisions," said Patrycja Gautier, Head of Consumer Rights at the European Consumer Organisation (BEUC).

"So they should decide really in advance what they need, monitor the prices beforehand and compare the offers calmly without being pressured."
