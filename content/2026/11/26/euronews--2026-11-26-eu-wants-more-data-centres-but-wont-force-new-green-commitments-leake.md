## The EU is preparing a voluntary agreement to accelerate data centre expansion as it seeks to triple capacity within five to seven years, despite growing concerns about electricity consumption and pressure on power grids.

A leaked European Commission draft accessed by Euronews reveals that the EU wants national governments, operators and energy companies to agree on measures to make new data centres easier to build and connect to electricity networks.

But rather than imposing binding sustainability requirements, the Commission is proposing a framework countries can adapt to their needs.

The document, "EU model for tripartite agreements on the sustainable integration of data centres into the energy system", describes the proposed commitments as a "toolbox rather than a single integrated package that needs to be adopted as such".

Countries could pick and choose measures covering grid access, renewable energy, storage, flexibility and the reuse of excess heat from data centres to warm buildings. Crucially, the draft states that "participation in local tripartite agreements is voluntary" and that the commitments "do not create any legal obligations for the signatory parties".

In parallel, Brussels is developing separate rules and standards to improve data centres' sustainability, raising questions about their compatibility with voluntary industry participation.

**Europe's growing appetite for data centres**

Data centres are facilities packed with servers that store, process and transmit digital information. They power everything from online banking and cloud services to AI, which demands huge computing capacity.

The leaked draft puts the EU's installed data centre capacity at approximately 12 gigawatts in 2025. According to International Energy Agency estimates, capacity stood at around 52 gigawatts in the US and 28 gigawatts in China that year.

The Commission sees expanding capacity as key to digital sovereignty. But MEP Benedetta Scuderi questioned whether this would strengthen Europe's autonomy, particularly if new infrastructure is developed by American technology companies.

"We know that we have very scarce resources for building data centres: energy, water and land. We need to be sure that what we build is what we need, that we don't go beyond that, that these data centres are not oversized or unnecessarily large-scale, and that they support our digital sovereignty. We cannot sell out our natural resources to American Big Tech because that would be of no benefit to us."

Data centres require huge supplies of electricity and cooling systems that can consume vast amounts of water. According to the Commission's draft, they already account for around 2.5% of EU electricity consumption, while their concentration near major hubs is increasing pressure on local grids.

That challenges Europe's energy transition, which also requires electricity for transport, buildings and industrial decarbonisation.

**The draft's plans for centres and member states**

The Commission is trying to balance these priorities through several energy and digital policy initiatives, including the Cloud and AI Development Act. Existing EU environmental and energy-efficiency rules already require data centres to report on sustainability, including energy efficiency and water use.

The leaked draft also mentions a common EU sustainability rating scheme to make facilities' environmental performance easier to compare. It proposes a collaborative approach through tripartite agreements, bringing together public authorities, data centre operators and energy companies to negotiate arrangements for individual projects or broader national and regional development plans.

According to the draft, the aim is to support "faster rollout of data centre projects, improve their visibility, improve local acceptance, and add value to the energy system and the community".

For member states, this could mean designating areas for new data centres based on grid capacity, environmental factors and opportunities to reuse excess heat. Authorities would also be encouraged to coordinate electricity, heating and digital infrastructure planning, streamline grid connections and prevent speculative projects from hogging scarce capacity. The draft suggests checking whether projects have permits, financing and sufficient technical readiness before granting grid access.

Data centre operators would share electricity demand forecasts and project updates with grid operators and consider flexible connection agreements. These could speed up grid access in exchange for limiting electricity consumption at certain times. Operators would also be encouraged to invest in clean power, sign long-term power purchase agreements and shift computing workloads to periods when electricity is more readily available.

Waste heat is another major focus, as heat generated by servers could be recovered to warm homes, schools and hospitals through district heating networks. The draft encourages operators to publish heat-reuse readiness plans, explore connections to local heating networks and negotiate long-term commercial deals where feasible. Governments could help through infrastructure planning, heat purchase agreements for public buildings and funding to cover upfront costs.

**Voluntary commitments versus environmental ambitions**

The proposed framework does not require every new facility to finance enough additional renewable energy to cover its consumption. Instead, it proposes that member states "explore options for pursuing the time matching of clean energy supply" with data centre demand, leaving the intervals, shares and implementation timelines to national authorities.

Scuderi warned of the consequences of increasing electricity demand in already strained energy systems.

"If you put a huge increase in electricity demand on an already pressured electricity system, what will happen is that we will have a collapse of the system. We will have a huge increase in prices, and we will not know where to get electricity for things that are strategic, such as industry and households.

"When you are asking for a certain number of megawatts for a data centre, we need to know where we're getting this electricity. This needs to come mostly from renewable energy.

"We need to require data centres to produce a certain amount of renewable energy, and the increase in the number of data centres needs to be sustainable in terms of electricity production, without an increase in emissions and without relying on fossil fuel-based electricity production."

While the EU's Energy Efficiency Directive already contains requirements for assessing waste-heat recovery opportunities, the draft's additional commitments would remain voluntary.

It also allows for commercial and technical constraints: where no heat recovery agreement is reached, operators could proceed with facilities designed to accommodate future heat exports. The framework would encourage earlier planning and cooperation without guaranteeing that recoverable heat will reach nearby communities.

While the document presents sustainability as a central objective, it also explicitly seeks to make data centre development faster and more predictable.

**The Commission's role**

Despite the voluntary nature of the agreements, the Commission plans to retain a central coordinating role, having developed the model with input from authorities in 18 member states and 14 industry associations representing data centres, electricity networks, renewables and storage.

The first local agreements could begin in 2027, potentially alongside data centre acceleration zones under the proposed Cloud and AI Development Act. Brussels would monitor progress annually until at least 2035, share best practices and regularly update the Energy Council and, where appropriate, the European Parliament.
