Prof Jason Arday said he was not a liar after accusations of plagiarism emerged

**A group of academics is calling for an independent investigation into the appointment of Jason Arday, who resigned from his University of Cambridge roles following allegations of plagiarism.**

The professor of education has also faced accusations of fabricating or exaggerating elements of his CV. He has defended himself and his work.

Attention has now turned to Cambridge's handling of concerns about Arday, who became its youngest-ever black professor in 2023, as well as his upcoming memoir.

Priyamvada Gopal, professor of postcolonial studies at Cambridge and among those calling for an independent probe, said the university was facing "massive reputational damage" over the affair.

"This is reputational damage that has upset colleagues across disciplines, across the political spectrum," she told BBC Radio 4's Today programme on Friday.

"I've been quite struck by the number of people who are very upset by what has happened. So I don't think it is an option for Cambridge to just sweep this under the carpet and not take any action at all."

Cambridge announced on Wednesday that it had begun an investigation into "new information" about Arday's academic qualifications and appointments, having already said it was looking into allegations of plagiarism.

But Gopal said the university should not be "marking its own homework", adding that the group of academics of which she is a part had suggested a mix of senior academics at Cambridge and elsewhere to conduct an investigation.

Accusations of plagiarism in Arday's work were first voiced by Nathan Cofnas - a US researcher whose role at a Cambridge college was terminated after backlash against his view that under a true meritocracy black people would "disappear from almost all high-profile positions outside of sports and entertainment".

He highlighted that plagiarism detection software had found several tracts of Arday's PhD dissertation to be copied verbatim or lightly rephrased from prior academic works by other authors.

Liverpool John Moores University (LJMU), which awarded Arday's PhD, has said it investigated the allegations of plagiarism last year and dismissed them, saying they were likely the result of "honest and reasonable error".

It told the BBC on Friday that its position remains unchanged in light of recent events and that his PhD "still stands".

Cofnas also noted that a retired professor had previously raised concerns about Arday's subsequent academic articles, which led to two journals issuing correction notes, and raised questions about some of his claimed achievements, including extensive marathon running and raising millions for charity.

Several universities at which Arday claimed on his CV to have had academic positions have since denied him having those roles.

The controversy has attracted a range of opinions, including within academia, about whether he had been scrutinised unfairly because of his race.

Arday has admitted errors in his work but denied plagiarism, saying his autism and a lack of adequate supervision early in his academic career may have contributed to those mistakes. He also denied lying about his achievements.

He said his decision to step down should not "be mistaken for an acceptance of the narratives that have surrounded me", which he has previously said were "racially motivated".

Gopal said universities were "being targeted in particular by a lazy and false division between academic excellence and diversity and equality on campus", which was "really part of culture wars rather than any part of university life".

She and her colleagues found it "unusual" that Arday had been appointed to a "very senior, very prestigious" post early in his career - and that he had been put forward in the media with "frankly a bit of a target on his back".

Gopal also said any investigation should review "whether the university was involved in any way or any level with efforts to deter journalists from reporting on the allegations".

This came after it emerged the Metropolitan Police investigated Times Higher Education (THE) reporter Jack Grove over a complaint of harassment after he contacted Arday concerning allegations of plagiarism last year.

The Telegraph also reported that Arday hired law firm Carter-Ruck to send a letter to THE which implied that the "apparent attack" on Arday might be "racially motivated".

The Met closed the case and Commissioner Sir Mark Rowley apologised for the investigation, telling LBC on Thursday that the complaint should have been "screened out earlier on as not requiring investigation".

Cambridge stated on Friday that it would "continue to investigate the circumstances around Jason Arday's appointment and tenure". A spokesperson declined to comment further when approached by the BBC.

## Great and unfortunate things

Among the claims by Arday that have been viewed as outlandish is one, made in an interview with the Guardian, that he was paid "1.4 million" for his memoir, Great and Unfortunate Things, due to be released next week. He did not specify the currency.

While not inconceivable, publishing insiders cast doubt on a figure such as Arday being able to attract such a large advance payment.

Publisher Simon & Schuster has said it plans to press ahead with the publication despite the controversy surrounding Arday.

But the Atlantic reported on Thursday that there were discrepancies between an advance copy of the memoir and the proposal for the book from 2024 it had obtained.

Among the differences, the magazine said the proposal gave his suicide attempt as happening before the Covid pandemic, whereas in the memoir it occurred during, and that the proposal detailed Arday being hit by a car as a teenager before "miraculously" reawakening, an episode omitted from the finished book.

It comes after the Telegraph reported that Arday had claimed to have already published a book entitled Being Young, Black and Male: Challenging the Dominant Discourse.

Palgrave Macmillan, which was attributed to its publication, told the newspaper it had accepted a proposal for the book but did not publish it.

The BBC has contacted Simon & Schuster and the Good Law Project, which has publicly represented Arday during the plagiarism row, for comment.
