Privacy commissioner says smart glasses and wearable tech may require new laws

Australia’s privacy commissioner, Carly Kind, has said the arrival of smart glasses with cameras, such as Meta’s Ray-Bans and Kmart’s Anko glasses, will “fundamentally alter our experience of interpersonal interactions” and requires consideration if new laws are required.

In a blog post on Friday, Kind said:

The proliferation of smart glasses – and their future peers, such as wearable pins or advanced earbuds – would fundamentally alter our experience of interpersonal interactions, in both private and public spaces … without knowing we could be filmed, recorded or photographed at any time.

Kind said Australia’s Privacy Act only applies to businesses and government agencies, and there should be consideration on whether new laws are needed.

As Australia’s privacy regulator we are giving serious consideration to the issues raised by surveillance wearables and monitoring their market presence in order to understand if scrutiny and intervention is required or warranted.

We have engaged with one entity on at least two occasions this year to further understand the technical specifications of surveillance wearables on offer.

In a nod to the public backlash to the glasses, Kind said public trust in tech companies is “extremely low” and the bar for establishing a social licence for new tech “will be high”.

New Victoria premier pushes on with work-from-home laws

The Victorian premier, Ben Carroll, who took over as leader after an internal revolt against Jacinta Allan, will continue pursuing the laws she championed, AAP reports.

The legislation would give full-time, part-time and casual workers the right to work from home two days a week on a pro-rata basis where reasonable.

The bill has already passed the Labor-controlled lower house and will soon be debated in the upper house as the November state election approaches.

It has attracted staunch opposition from business lobby groups, which have met with Carroll since his elevation to premier. But a Victorian government spokesperson confirmed on Friday the government’s position had not changed.

Among the more contentious elements of the bill is a requirement for employers to pay any reasonable costs to allow an eligible employee to work remotely. This may include essential equipment and secure access to work systems.

Can Australia make housing affordable while avoiding a major market crash?

The Reserve Bank has raised interest rates and the property market is in retreat. So far, so normal.

Or is it?

There is a sense that this year’s fall in home prices feels different from previous episodes. The data tells another story.

Over the past four decades there have been seven property market downturns of varying length and depth, according to analysis put together by Shane Oliver, AMP’s chief economist.

They are often triggered by interest rate hikes, which make home loans more expensive, but they also occur during crises and policy changes.

Former ABC executive says Race Around the World clip about Rinehart not a breach, but worthy of apology

Alan Sunderland, a former editorial director at the ABC, has said he was not surprised the broadcaster’s ombudsman found an episode of its Race Around the World program did not breach its editorial standards related to harm and offence.

The program included a four-minute documentary that ended with crude and offensive comments about mining magnate Gina Rinehart, including a line about “mixing her guts up nice and smooth, to make one big sausage roll”. The ABC managing director, Hugh Marks, later apologised for the episode amid Rinehart’s threats of legal action, and the broadcaster edited the footage to remove the offending lines.

Sunderland said there was a “long history of latitude” at the ABC for content, “particularly with satirical entertainment programs where people ascertain that the depiction of violence, whatever it is, was clearly meant to be humorous”:

It is not per se a breach of standards to depict violence where nobody would reasonably in good faith think that it was an actual direct threat of violence.

Still, Sunderland said he believes it was probably right for Marks to apologise:

I think the ABC needs to take account of public tastes on this. Public tastes move and shift over questions of what is and isn’t offensive, but I think there was a pretty common acceptance, even from the film-maker herself, that it was inappropriate. And I think they did the right thing to make that call, regardless of whether it breached editorial standards or not.

Marles maintains gambling reforms about striking a ‘balance’ over ‘complex’ issue

Richard Marles, the deputy prime minister, said the government still intends to get its gambling reforms past parliament despite opposition from the Greens and the Coalition, who both argue that they do not go far enough.

Marles spoke to RN Breakfast, echoing the government line that they are the “strongest reforms in respect of gambling advertising that have ever been put forward”. He went on:

I mean, we are doing more in terms of breaking the link between gambling advertising and sport, more in terms of protecting our kids from being exposed to gambling advertising on TV and radio at the times when they would be watching and listening. We’re doing more in terms of supporting BetStop, which helps people who identify to restrict their own gambling habits.

So this is really important legislation and we’ll work with the parties across the parliament to get it passed.

When asked about criticism of the legislation, Marles said gambling was a “complex” issue with differing views.

It’s also an area where you need to strike a balance and we believe we’ve done that … We’re not saying that people should not be able to engage in betting but there needs to be a balance. And it’s important.
