An official investigation has been launched into charities in England and Wales donating funds to illegal Israeli settlements in Palestine.

The Charity Commission will aim to establish the amount of charitable funds going to illegal settlements, whether the spending was in furtherance of their purposes and any regulatory action that may be necessary.

The investigation follows the Guardian’s reporting about the issue. Last year, it revealed that two UK charities had transferred approximately £5.7m to the Bnei Akiva Yeshiva high school in Susya, in the Israeli-occupied territory.

In June, the Guardian published details of a formal complaint made by the Labour MP, Melanie Ward, to the regulator in which she said that 32 charities in England and Wales had donated at least £28m to Israeli settlements that are illegal under international law. The Guardian understands that the inquiry is a direct response to Ward’s complaint.

Announcing the investigation, which will initially focus on eight unnamed charities, Stephen Roake, assistant director, high risk compliance at the commission, said: “Serious allegations have been made about UK charities operating in illegal Israeli settlements in Palestine, so our first step is to establish the facts. Only then can we determine if regulatory action is required, based on our findings and the evidence we see.

“Our fact-finding will also help us develop regulatory guidance for charities, which will to help ensure public trust and confidence in charities.”

Government contracts to require job creation instead of green targets under new rules

Alexandra Topping

Ministers will scrap the requirement for companies to prove their green and progressive values before they are awarded government contracts, and instead ask firms to prove they will help young people back into work.

As part of a government push to reduce the number of people not in education, employment or training (Neets) in the UK and boost regional growth, public sector buyers will double the consideration they give to whether companies will get more people into jobs, rather than if they can show they are improving social and environmental wellbeing. Government procurement contracts account for about £90bn of taxpayers’ money spent each year.

Louise Haigh, the first secretary of state, said the move would help small businesses who often find themselves locked out of contracts because of the requirements, which big businesses know how to get around.

“Every pound of taxpayer money should be spent in a way that benefits local communities – creating good jobs and giving young people the skills they need for the future,” said Haigh. “For too long, securing government contracts has been a tick-box exercise that fails to deliver the positive change that people feel in their pocket and see in the places they live.

“We are doing things differently. These new rules will ensure the £90bn that is spent each year through government contracts supports British jobs, skills and people in every postcode.”

BP’s chief executive has urged Andy Burnham to make use of the UK’s oil and gas resources even as the oil company prepares to sell its North Sea business after 60 years in the ageing basin.

In a conversation with the new prime minister, Meg O’Neill said the country should prioritise using energy produced in the UK, “where we generate jobs, we generate tax revenue, we generate all those additional positive impacts”.

BP’s endorsement of UK oil production came days after the 117-year-old company signalled a plan to exit the North Sea after six decades, as part of a wider plan to streamline its business.

O’Neill said North Sea investments were “not competitive” within BP’s portfolio, while the company reported its highest quarterly profits since the first year of Russia’s war on Ukraine because of rising oil and gas prices caused by the Middle East crisis.

BP’s quarterly profits more than doubled to $5.73bn (£4.27bn) in the three months to the end of June, as the conflict in the Middle East continued to disrupt energy exports from the Gulf.

O’Neill added that BP had already received several unsolicited approaches to buy its North Sea fields and she was “very optimistic” that the assets would “continue to be profitable in the hands of a future owner”.

Labour members ‘have tougher view on welfare than you might think’, poll suggests

Aletha Adu

Andy Burnham has inherited a Labour membership that is sceptical about increased welfare spending, supportive of higher defence spending, and ranks the climate crisis as one of the country’s biggest challenges, according to new polling.

The survey of 788 members between the end of June and the end of July shows them ranking health (43%), the economy (42%), cost of living (39%) and climate change (29%) as Britain’s most important issues, ahead of welfare benefits (7%) and immigration (6%).

Despite the internal rows within the parliamentary Labour party (PLP) on welfare spending and general public pushback, 47% of members surveyed by ThinkLabour believe the government is spending too much on welfare, compared with 21% who believe it does not spend enough.

When asked whether they would prefer the government to fund its current welfare bill differently, 52% said taxes should be raised. Additionally, 70% of Labour members said only high earners should be taxed while 24% supported higher taxes on everyone to fund the current bill.

The survey reveals members overwhelmingly backed more spending on social care, with 73% saying the government should spend more on this through higher taxes, either on higher earners alone or through general taxation. It found 68% of members wanted Britain to spend more on defence, and among the supporters for higher defence spending, 61% backed paying for this through higher taxes.

Minister defends early release of PC Andrew Harper's killers amid possible legal challenge

Good morning and welcome to the UK politics blog.

Ministers continue to defend the release of PC Andrew Harper’s killers as part of the government’s early prisoner release scheme, despite a potential legal challenge from the Police Federation.

Justice minister Jake Richards acknowledged the “unimaginable pain” of the deceased officer’s family but said “difficult decisions” must be made because law and order could “collapse” without a functioning prison system.

About 1,000 fewer prisoners will be released early after Andy Burnham’s intervention to exclude those convicted of rape, serious child sexual offences and grooming from the scheme.

Asked to respond to Police Federation chief Aileen O’Connor’s threat of legal action, he told Sky News:

Well, look, we understand that people are angry. The Harper family are, of course, angry. The police are angry too.

We’re angry that we’re in this situation where we face the unenviable position of having to make sure that we have a sustainable prison system, which is so vital to a functioning law and order system in our country, but also that we do right by victims as far as possible.

And the prime minister has looked again at the scheme. He said he wants to do more than he has been able to do, and that we wanted to take further action to try and ease the unimaginable pain that the Harper family are feeling every day, but we also have to balance that by ensuring that we have a functioning prison system.

He added:

Because without a functioning prison system, it means it’s difficult for police to make arrests, it’s difficult for our courts to try cases, and it could mean to the collapse of law and order in this country.

We cannot be in that situation, so that is the challenge that we face as government ministers and the prime minister faces ultimately.

Richards’ defence of the policy came as a Police Federation chief told Times Radio she feels “sick to her stomach” over the possible early release of PC Harper’s killers.

Aileen O’Connor, chairwoman of Thames Valley Police Federation, previously wrote in The Sun newspaper that the organisation “will throw everything at this through a proper legal challenge from KCs, barristers, you name it.”

She added on Wednesday:

I feel sick to my stomach, if I’m really honest with you. I’ve had a gut wrenching feeling in my stomach the past couple of weeks with this, and it doesn’t go away.

And arguably, I would say that many of my members across Thames Valley also have this gut wrenching feeling, even at the prospect of these two killers being released in the space of weeks, potentially.
