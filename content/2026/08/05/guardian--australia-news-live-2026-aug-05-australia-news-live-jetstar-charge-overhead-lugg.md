Investors rushing to buy homes via superannuation before ban, mortgage broker says

Australians are racing to borrow to buy investment properties though their self-managed super funds before the government bans the practice next week.

Labor’s reforms to investor tax breaks became law in June with the Greens’ help, on the condition SMSF loans be banned from 10 August, as you can read here:

Loan Market, a mortgage brokers group, says it received hundreds more SMSF loan applications than normal in the month ahead of the ban.

Applications for SMSF property loans rose about 30% from July 2025 to June 2026, after the budget reforms made super one of the least-taxed ways of investing in property.

Applications then rose 326% in July 2026 to hit record volumes, after the ban was announced, Loan Market reported.

About 1.2 million Australians have self-run super funds, which are an alternative to industry super that typically incur high fees, and housing made up only about 6% of their combined wealth. The government estimated just 4,000 people took out SMSF investment loans each year but the Australian Finance Industry Association estimated more than 16,000 were written in 2025-26.

The rush suggests there is still demand for property investment despite a broader fall as prices slide. Lending to all housing investors rose 0.8% in June, or 10.4% annually, its highest rate since 2015, according to Reserve Bank data. Lending to owner-occupiers rose 0.5% in the month.

NSW police required to switch on body cameras under new policy

New South Wales police officers carrying out operational duties will be required to wear and activate body cameras under a new policy.

The change follows a key recommendation from the Law Enforcement Conduct Commission’s review of the force’s body-worn video policy and practice, prompted by a series of serious incidents in which officers failed to activate available cameras.

Under the new policy, officers must activate their body-worn cameras in most operational situations, unless doing so “is reasonable impracticable or an exemption applies”.

If an officer is not wearing a camera, fails to switch it on, or turns it off during a job, they will have to provide an explanation.

NSW police. Photograph: AAP

Chief commissioner Peter Johnson welcomed the change, calling it “a very important development” that should “increase public confidence” in the force. He said:

The knowledge that interactions between police and community members are being recorded helps to keep both the officer and the community safe. The use of body worn cameras also promotes officer accountability by enabling the NSW Police Force and the Commission to effectively scrutinise officers’ actions where there are allegations of police misconduct.

The NSW police force says it has now implemented most of the commission’s 15 recommendations.

Victorian corruption watchdog finds ‘concerning’ conduct by Andrews government but no evidence premier was blackmailed in tabled report

Benita Kolovos

Victoria’s anti-corruption watchdog has tabled a long-awaited report into the former Daniel Andrews government’s dealings with the United Firefighters Union and its secretary Peter Marshall.

The Operation Richmond report did not find that any individuals engaged in corrupt conduct, but it found that senior members of the Andrews government engaged in “serious and concerning” conduct in their dealings with Marshall when negotiating pay deals for the Country Fire Authority and Metropolitan Fire Brigade, and their later amalgamation into Fire Services Victoria.

This included “actions contrary to policy, procedure and codes of conduct, deliberate attempts to conceal and mislead, and the misuse of information.”

The Independent Broad-based Anti-corruption Commission (Ibac) found the government made an “undisclosed deal” with Marshall, “to the exclusion of other key stakeholders,” which allowed him to “exert extraordinary influence over the negotiations” for both the CFA and MFB enterprise bargaining agreements.

Ibac also found that Marshall was granted “privileged access” to responsible ministers.

The final release of the findings comes after years of legal attempts by the UFU to keep the report secret, including a high court challenge. The investigation first began in 2018 following a tip-off that Marshall “blackmailed or otherwise compromised” Andrews “to change his position on the CFA EBA”. Ibac found no evidence of blackmail and did not substantiate the allegation, which both Andrews and Marshall denied.
