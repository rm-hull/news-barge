A gardener accustomed to the sprawling landscapes of Tuscan estates and iconic British gardens like Sissinghurst is now demonstrating how even the smallest urban spaces can become vital havens for biodiversity.

Thomas Rutter, whose professional career has spanned prestigious locations including Lowther Castle and Iford Manor, cultivates a thriving 5m x 1m balcony garden on the fourth floor of his Tottenham flat.

Rutter's personal project serves as compelling evidence that people with limited outdoor areas can significantly contribute to local ecosystems.

"Any small space, whether it’s a window ledge or a crack in the wall or a balcony, or something larger like a small terrace, all have value," explains the 35-year-old, whose latest book, *The Ethical Gardener*, champions this very approach.

He highlights simple yet effective methods. "I have a small number of pots on some saucers which are full of water. I can’t tell you the number of birds that have visited my balcony in the last week for water."

![This year, plants which have thrived on Thomas Rutter’s balcony, include two varieties of fennel which have been covered in pretty yellow flowers](https://static.the-independent.com/2026/08/04/07/31171245-c506c0b7-9a6a-47fc-a0b2-08f75d387368.jpg)

Despite his inner-city location, the impact is clear. "It’s very industrial where I am and the number of hoverflies and insects that are passing through, even with just a small handful of pots on a very small balcony in Tottenham, is quite remarkable."

His top suggestions for helping biodiversity in small gardens include:

**Choose suitable plants for small spaces**

This year, plants which have thrived on his balcony, which is south facing but in a wind tunnel, include two varieties of fennel which have been covered in pretty yellow flowers.

“Pollinators love them, but they have moved gently with the wind and because they have a filigree leaf they haven’t been too affected by the heatwave.”

Other winners have included micro agastache, species pelargoniums and Verbena bonariensis, which can move in the wind and are not so far removed from prairie plants.

![Verbena bonariensis are a recommended plant to grow on your balcony](https://static.the-independent.com/2026/08/04/07/31171351-3edf1282-c6bf-4a84-b569-463baea4acc4.jpg)

“Achillea has done quite well, which you sometimes see on the side of the road or in meadows, but actually in a pot that has done remarkably well, and a few salvias.”

Rudbeckias in pots lengthen the season, flowering later on in summer and hopefully into autumn.

“It’s important to have things that flower a little earlier, in May and June, like the salvia, then thinking about what could flower in August, September and October,” he advises.

**Consider evergreens**

They might not be as showy or colourful as other plants, but they do have worth for biodiversity, he points out.

“If you thought about growing ivy vertically, trailing it across the end of a balcony or covering a wall, you only need one pot to do that. And ivy has wonderful food for pollinators in autumn, but also habitat, sheltering insects over the winter, or nesting birds in spring.”

**Think about one tree**

A large pot should be able to accommodate a tree, he offers.

“It’s great because it provides a structure and a bit of shade, which can protect younger plants underneath it as well when the summer gets quite challenging.

“If you’re thinking about spring blossom, there’s nothing that will benefit those early-to-rise pollinators more. It might be a crab apple or a cherry tree, and you’ll find masses of pollinators around them in early spring.”

Other good candidates for pots include amelanchiers, which don’t mind a bit of shade and produce pretty blossom and can easily be kept in check, while elaeagnus or arbutus also bear fruits as well as blossom.

![A large pot should be able to accommodate a tree, Thomas Rutter says](https://static.the-independent.com/2026/08/04/07/31172640-072cc779-d16f-4913-a693-572e8dba22f7.jpg)

**Create interest with underplanting**

Underplant an evergreen tree with colourful annuals thinking about what will fill that gap for about three months.

“They’re not going to last longer than a few months and you have your colour and joy, but when they are done, the tree is still there,” he says.

Alternatively, you could choose bulbs.

**Try to add water**

Obviously you’ll have to be careful if you are thinking about water on a balcony because of weight restrictions and drainage issues, which you’ll need to check with your landlord or management company.

“But even if it’s a small birdbath or a saucer under your pot which is taking the excess from watering, it’s surprising which birds will find a balcony to get a drink,” he says.

![Placing a bird bath on your balcony can help attract birds](https://static.the-independent.com/2026/08/04/07/31172928-76adfca7-144f-409f-9db4-eb5d2fde0e8d.jpg)

**Leave a messy corner**

If you have space such as a small terrace with a few metres, leave a small messy corner, for instance a few pots that have gone a bit wild or a shrub that you don’t cut back, which will provide sheltering spots for wildlife.

**Become a beacon for wildlife**

Cascade trailing plants over your balcony using window boxes which just slot on to the edge of your railings, he suggests.

Even if you are a few floors up, pollinators will find them. But check with your management that you have permission to do that.

*The Ethical Gardener* by Thomas Rutter is published by Pavilion on 13 August, priced £16.99.
