Morning opening: Hungary 'within millimetres' of shutting down its nuclear plant as region fights extreme weather and droughts

Jakub Krupa

Hungary has come “within millimetres” of having to shut down its nuclear power plant overnight,prime minister Péter Magyar said, as low water levels in the Danube continue to pose challenges for countries in south-eastern Europe.

Paks Nuclear Power Plant with the partially dried-up Danube riverbed in Dunaszentbenedek, Hungary. Photograph: Bernadett Szabó/Reuters

“Around 1:30 a.m. we were a few millimetres away from completely shutting down the Paks Power Plant, but then the water level stopped falling and by morning it had risen 1.5 centimetres. The last turbine is currently operating safely,” Magyar said on social media.

The plant already operates at a reduced capacity of just over 10% after the Danube fell to a record low, making it impossible to cool it properly.

Energy minister István Kapitány said on Tuesday that a number of energy saving measures succeeded in lowering energy consumption to keep the grid secure.

A number of businesses have made significant adjustments to their normal ways of working to save energy consumption during the peak hours. For example, a grocery chain Lidl changed its baking shifts, and turned off its drink fridges – despite temperatures expected to get close to 40C. Electric car chargers in their parkings can only be used until 5pm, HVG reported.

Over in Romania, the government celebrated that a two-centimetre rise in water levels after yesterday’s spectacular controlled explosion, intended to divert the flow of the Danube to help cool its Cernavodă nuclear plant.

The plant manager told local media that it should help keep the plant running during the next few days, but more interventions are planned today, with plans to sink old barges to further divert the flow of water.

Temperatures are set to surpass 40C in large parts of Romania today, again.

Elsewhere, I will keep an eye on the much-awaited meeting of EU interior ministers who will discuss last week’s migration crisis in Ceuta amid questions about the EU’s relationship with Morocco and the steps needed to secure the bloc’s external borders.

People wait in line to receive food and beverage from the Red Cross, following mass crossings on foot and by sea from Morocco into Spanish territory, in Ceuta, Spain. Photograph: Violeta Santos Moura/Reuters

They are meeting at 10am Brussels time, and we should hear from the EU’s migration commissioner, Magnus Brunner, in the early afternoon.

EU 'monitoring very closely' energy crunch in Bulgaria, Hungary and Romania

Meanwhile, the European Commission said it was “monitoring very closely” the situation in Bulgaria, Hungary and Romania as the region faces an energy crunch caused by the ongoing heatwave and drought.

A drone view shows sand banks on the Old Danube river ramification, in Rasova, Constanta, Romania. Photograph: George Calin/Reuters

Energy spokesperson Anna-Kaisa Itkonen said “the low Danube levels will clearly have an impact on nuclear generation along the route,” and this “concerns several member states.”

The commission was ready to “convene an electricity coordination group as soon as there is a need for that.”

“Generally speaking, climate risks, extreme weather events, have already been embedded in many of our policies related to energy transport already for some time, so we will obviously include, include these aspects in any of our future work as well,” she said.

We are expecting to hear from the EU’s migration commissioner, Magnus Brunner, in the next hour as he will brief journalists on today’s meeting of the bloc’s interior ministers on Ceuta.

I will bring it live for you here when he shows up.

Morocco says it warned Spain about 'a problem' with Spain's top court ruling on migration

Back to discussions on Ceuta, now under way in Brussels (10:08), a senior Moroccan official claimed that Morocco had warned Spain that a recent Spanish court ruling that migrants arriving by sea would not face immediate removal was going to create “a problem,” AFP reported.

Spain’s Supreme Court ruled on 8 July that migrants arriving by sea should not be returned as quickly as others and Madrid has blamed criminal gangs for spreading misinformation about the ruling.

“We started engaging with the Spanish side around 22-23 July, when the networks began talking about this,” the Moroccan senior official, speaking on condition of anonymity, said on Monday night.

“We discussed this ruling and its impact. We explained that this ruling was going to create a problem. And it did create a problem,” he said.

A group of migrants approach the border post to make their way back to Morocco, escorted by Spanish infantry soldiers, in the Spain’s north African enclave of Ceuta. Photograph: Henry Nicholls/AFP/Getty Images

The official also rejected any suggestion that Morocco was to be blamed for the crisis, saying “at a certain point, the Moroccan law enforcement forces could no longer confront the migrants because the numbers were very large,” and because “the pull effect was very strong,” he said.

“Today, it is up to Spain to find answers to the challenges posed by this ruling,” he said.

For what it’s worth, Spain responded to the ruling in recent days, installing new border containment structures that would provide them a legal claim to use an exemption provided in the court’s ruling and allow to send migrants back across the border.

Magyar praises people's solidarity amid energy crunch, but warns of 'hardest days ahead'

Meanwhile, Hungary’s prime minister Péter Magyar praised the solidarity of Hungarians after the country faced “the most difficult and critical night” amid a heatwave energy crunch.

Hungarian prime minister Péter Magyar (L) and acting president Ágnes Forsthoffer hold a joint press conference at the Sandor Palace in Budapest, Hungary. Photograph: Balint Szentgallay/NurPhoto/Shutterstock

In a video on Facebook, Magyar repeated that the Paks power plant was just “millimetres away” from being shut down overnight, but the water levels started rising again as of this morning.

He praised the “solidarity” shown by Hungarians when reducing their energy consumption, and thanked people working to stabilise the country’s energy grid in these challenging circumstances.

“Tonight’s turn of events gives us cause for optimism, but the hardest days are still ahead of us,” he warned, as Hungary faces a few more days of extreme temperatures.

“Scorching heat of over 40C, drought, and further hardships await, but light now can be seen at the end of the tunnel as cooler weather arrives from Friday, and hopefully much-needed rain in the upper areas of the Danube,” he said.

Temperatures in Budapest are expected to get close to 40C later today.

Meanwhile, a 4.3 magnitude earthquake was reported in the Italian city of Pisa, which was also felt in Livorno, Lucca and Massa Carrara in northern Tuscany, Italian media reported.

The National Institute of Geophysics and Volcanology reported the earthquake had its epicentre 7km west of Pisa.

Tuscan president Eugenio Gianisaid the authorities were checking across the region to see if there’s any damage. He later added that while “hundreds” of calls to emergency services were made, there are currently no reports of injuries.

Last Thursday, a 4.7 quake was also reported in the Campi Flegrei caldera, west of Naples.

Ceuta leader accuses Morocco over ‘atrocity’ of mass border crossing

Ashifa Kassam in Madrid, Okba Mohammad in Ceuta and Lisa O’Carrollin London

A growing chorus of voices has accused Morocco of helping to fuel the crisis in Ceuta, with the leader of the Spanish enclave describing last week’s border crossings as an attack on Spain’s territorial integrity that was seen as being “if not orchestrated, at least encouraged or permitted by Morocco”.

Migrants hold signs reading "asylum" in Spanish while waiting in line to receive food and beverage from the Red Cross, following mass crossings of migrants on foot and by sea from Morocco into Spanish territory, in Ceuta, Spain. Photograph: Violeta Santos Moura/Reuters

Spanish officials said on Monday that an estimated 69,500 people who had arrived late last week in Ceuta, a tiny Spanish territory in north Africa, had returned to Morocco in recent days. About 2,500 people who had made the crossing were believed to still be in Ceuta.

The extraordinary influx had overwhelmed the city of 84,000 people and set off a political crisis across the EU as some member states laid blame on Spain.

In Ceuta, leaders pointed to the broader picture of what had transpired. “It’s an atrocity,” said Juan Jesús Vivas, the long-serving conservative leader of the territory.

“There are 88 bodies in the morgue, and there are more on the other side of the border.”

He said local people watched the events play out with a mix of sadness, worry and anguish.

“People see this not as a migration crisis; it was fundamentally an attack, a violation of Spain’s territorial integrity,” Vivas told El País newspaper. “People felt that this, if not orchestrated, was at least encouraged or permitted by Morocco.”

He drew a parallel with May 2021, when Rabat loosened border controls, allowing 10,000 people to cross into Ceuta over the course of two days, during a diplomatic standoff over Madrid’s decision to allow a Western Sahara independence leader to be treated for Covid-19 in Spain.

This time around, analysts have suggested that Spain’s recent rapprochement with Rabat’s arch-enemy Algeria could have triggered a response from Morocco.

EU ministers to discuss bloc's response to Ceuta migration crisis today

Jennifer Rankin

in Brussels

Over in Brussels, after a weekend of backbiting over the response to the mass border crossings in the Spanish enclave of Ceuta, EU ministers will seek to present a more unified front – and signal tougher security at Europe’s frontiers.

Migrants wait in line to receive food and beverage from the Red Cross, following mass crossings of migrants on foot and by sea from Morocco into Spanish territory, in Ceuta, Spain. Photograph: Violeta Santos Moura/Reuters

EU home affairs ministers are meeting at 10.30 CET via video conference to assess the bloc’s response to the arrival – and quick departure – of tens of thousands of people at the Spanish territory in north Africa. Around 100 people are estimated to have died on both sides of the Spanish-Moroccan border.

Von der Leyen wants to double down on five areas: strengthening cooperation with non-EU countries to prevent migration; stronger external borders; implementing early warning systems; dismantling smuggling networks; and boosting returns of people with no right to stay.

At a meeting of senior EU diplomats on Monday, several countries stressed the importance of preventing sudden movements of people, calling for greater use of intelligence and monitoring of disinformation and misinformation.

While Madrid’s envoy voiced his government’s frustration with the response from some member states, others rejected suggestions that they had failed to show solidarity over the sudden influx of arrivals.

“There is some concern that the regularisation of 1.2m people can be used to paint a picture that the door is open.”

This issue is set to run. And EU migration policy in general, including controversial plans for return hubs, will be high on the agenda in the autumn, including at the EU’s next scheduled summit in October.
