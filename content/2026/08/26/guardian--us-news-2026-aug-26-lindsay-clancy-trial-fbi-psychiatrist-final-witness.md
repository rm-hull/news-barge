Testimony finished in the Lindsay Clancy trial on Wednesday, as a final witness for the prosecution laid out that he believed the Massachusetts mother knew right from wrong when she took the lives of her three young children before attempting to commit suicide in 2023.

Closing arguments are expected to begin on Thursday. The jury will then start deliberating.

An FBI psychiatrist, Dr Gregory Saathoff, testified in court in Plymouth that he does not believe Clancy’s account that she heard a voice ordering her to kill the children – five-year-old Cora, three-year-old Dawson and eight-month-old Callan – as her defense team maintains that this occurred while she was suffering from the relatively rare condition of postpartum psychosis.

The issue is the crux of the case: Clancy’s mental health and whether, as prosecutors contend, she knowingly murdered her children or, as the defense argues, she was at that moment outside the realms of reality and, in legal terms, lacks criminal responsibility for her actions. She has admitted to killing the children, but not to murdering them.

Saathoff was asked by prosecutor Jennifer Sprague whether he believed a voice told Clancy where to kill the children, how to kill them, what to use to do it and in what order to kill them.

![A man in front of a microphone](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/906058aa9a6241658d1ed2fa8bc529b927124a03/0_0_3000_2000/master/3000.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

“Having no instructions as to how to do it, but being able to execute those decisions … that is surprising that she’s able to accomplish all of this without any other kind of direction or plan,” Saathoff testified.

Saathoff testified that Clancy’s ability to accomplish everything she did without direction indicated she “had control, control of the sequence of what she was doing throughout”.

“In the small amount of time that Miss Clancy had in the home without her husband, it was important, in order to carry this out, as well as her suicide [attempt], to do this quickly, methodically and alike,” he added.

Clancy has admitted to strangling her children with exercise bands in the family’s basement while her then husband was out on errands, but pleaded not guilty to a crime, by reason of insanity.

The case has brought national attention to the court as jurors heard dueling explanations for Clancy’s actions over almost five weeks of testimony. Her defense claims that postpartum psychosis, exacerbated by undiagnosed bipolar disorder and a combination of anti-anxiety and sedative medications, led to the killings. Supporters who claim she was failed by the health system last week rallied outside the courthouse.

In recent days, prosecutors have refined their argument that Clancy was a high-functioning mother and a perfectionist who felt she was losing control and planned to take her own and her children’s lives.

![a man in suit speaking in courtroom](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/c26a255f2ff0c8d8d50f4acf56698ae20bd80587/0_0_4913_3000/master/4913.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

Clancy knew right from wrong when she killed her children, Saathoff testified. “The planning involved, prior to the strangling of her children, I thought was significant,” he said.

He pointed to Clancy’s visits to a pediatrician’s office on the day of the killings and later making lunch for her children and building a snowman with them. “The activities of that day were controlled. Throughout the day she was methodical and quite controlled and organized,” he said. “There was a lot to do in terms of killing her children but also [attempting to kill] herself.”

Saathoff said on Tuesday he found it unusual that Clancy said the voice she was hearing stopped once she had killed the children, and that the voice was constant rather than intermittent. “Committing the act is not curative of the voice,” Saathoff said, adding that in his experience he had “not ever seen that” happen.

Under cross-examination by defense attorney Kevin Reddington, Saathoff said he had treated only two women experiencing postpartum psychosis “in the late 1980s and also during the 1990s” when he worked at a state hospital.

He acknowledged that the mental health progression from postpartum depression to psychosis can be “rapid” and the latter can look similar to regular depression, along with sleep problems and suicidal ideation.

Saathoff also acknowledged that changes in a woman’s hormone levels after giving birth can affect neurotransmitters in the brain and lead to “intrusive and hard-to-control thoughts and hard-to-control behavior”.

But in a closing moment of testimony, Reddington read from the prosecution’s opening statement, which said Clancy had “made a calculated decision” and “was a woman who no longer liked the life she thought she wanted”.

Prosecutors accused Clancy of having chosen “to manipulate her providers” and “seek out a quick and easy fix when she was feeling depressed and anxious”, adding: “When she didn’t get what she expected, she chose the alternate route.”

Reddington asked Saathof if he supported that summation. Saathof said he would not “I think that statement that you read really minimizes what [Clancy] was going through,” he said.

Jurors also heard from Saathoff that Clancy had complained about a lack of sleep and feeling numb when she went to an emergency room about a month before the killings. But the Women and Infants hospital in Rhode Island turned her away from a postpartum day program.

Jurors will hear closing arguments, probably on Thursday, before being given jury instructions and sent to deliberate.
