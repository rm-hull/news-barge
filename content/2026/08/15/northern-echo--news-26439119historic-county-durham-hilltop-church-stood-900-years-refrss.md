A hilltop church in a County Durham village has stood watch over the surrounding countryside for almost 900 years.

The historical site has links to medieval bishops, a leper's squint and one of the county's most celebrated historians.

The Church of St Michael and All Angels sits on high ground south of the village of Bishop Middleham, its elevated position and clutch of hillside gravestones drawing the eye up Church Street long before visitors reach the door.

## Norman foundations on a hilltop site

The building dates back to the early 12th century and features an unusual Norman chancel with a horseshoe arch, built around 1155, making it one of the oldest surviving structures in the parish.

It is presumed to date to the original layout of the village, and its historic importance is reflected in its Grade II listed status.

Although the church's origins lie in the Norman period, much of what visitors see today reflects centuries of alteration and repair.

Thirteenth-century additions brought aisles, a north porch and an extended chancel, while the north aisle was rebuilt in 1802.

A further phase of restoration followed between 1842 and 1846, carried out in memory of the county historian Robert Surtees of Mainsforth by his widow.


## A church built for bishops

In the Middle Ages, St Michael's stood beside a fortified manor house that served as a residence for the Bishops of Durham, reinforcing the settlement's status as one of their favoured retreats; two bishops are known to have died there.

That connection to the diocese's most powerful figures is still visible today in the coat of arms of the Bishops of Durham, mounted below the church's bellcote, which may originally have come from the nearby castle.

Rather than a conventional tower, the church uses a bellcote, giving it a lower profile than many parish churches of its age, though its hilltop position and churchyard still make it a prominent local landmark.

## Medieval features and a notable resting place

Inside, the church retains several striking historic features, including a medieval font carved from Frosterley marble, a distinctive dark limestone quarried in Weardale, and a rare surviving leper's squint, a narrow opening once used to allow those with contagious illness to view services without entering the building.

The churchyard and interior also hold close links to one of County Durham's most significant historians.

Robert Surtees, who lived nearby at Mainsforth and wrote extensively about Bishop Middleham and its castle in his History of Durham.

It was published in 1823 and is buried in the churchyard, and several memorial tablets to the wider Surtees family of Mainsforth Hall can be found inside the church.
