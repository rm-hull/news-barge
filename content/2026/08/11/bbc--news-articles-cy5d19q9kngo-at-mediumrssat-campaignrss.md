**A man who suffered permanent eye damage by watching the 1999 solar eclipse has warned people to use protection when watching Wednesday's astronomical event.**

Peter Smith, from Taunton, viewed the solar eclipse 27 years ago wearing a borrowed welder's mask having thought it would provide protection from the sun's rays.

But the next morning he noticed flashing in his eyes alongside headaches and later discovered both of his retinas had been damaged. He is now asking people to use appropriate eye protection to enjoy the eclipse safely.

Smith said: "Make sure its got the right protection and filters to stop any damage because any damage you get, it'll never heal. It's not like cutting your hand."

Smith said he still feels the effects of looking at the eclipse directly.

"Even now there is a residual impact. When I'm tired sometimes a flashing dot might come back, and it's not nice when it does.

"If you're going to go and watch it, make sure that everything you've got is correct."

## More from Somerset

Sarah Farrant, an optometrist from Taunton, warned that viewing the eclipse without protection could damage the retina and macular at the back of the eye.

Farrant said: "The partial eclipse in the UK really means that people will want to look at the sun, but there's still enough to cause damage even when the sun is at its dimmest here in the UK.

"If they're not safe when they are viewing the sun, you can end up with what we call a 'solar burn' at the back of the eye and that can have a lifelong lasting impact on the central part of your vision."

## How to view the solar eclipse safely

For those wanting to watch the eclipse, the safest way to do so is by using certified eclipse glasses that meet the ISO 12312-2 safety standard, or by viewing the eclipse indirectly.

Looking at the sun directly with naked eyes, sunglasses, unfiltered cameras, binoculars or a telescope can cause significant damage.

A kitchen colander is one way to indirectly observe the eclipse. To do so, hold it above the ground with your back to the sun. The holes in the colander will project a grid of crescent suns onto the ground.
