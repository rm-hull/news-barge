# What the death of Jason Arday shows us about society - Daxa Patel

## The recent death of Professor Jason Arday warrants a period of reflection and pause. The Chancellor of Cambridge described the atmosphere surrounding him as a racist feeding frenzy. This is a phrase that should make us ask what kind of society we have become and why we appear to have lost the ability to recognise the humanity of those placed under the glare of public scrutiny.

This death is not only a private tragedy. It is a mirror held up to the nation. It forces us to confront the way we speak about people, the way we judge them, and the way we allow public commentary to escalate into something that resembles a witch hunt. The details of any individual case will always be complex. We need to examine the climate that surrounds these events and the behaviour that becomes normalised when people gather online to express outrage, suspicion, or triumph.

This is not the first time the country has witnessed a public figure driven to despair by relentless scrutiny. Many will remember the case of Dr David Kelly, the weapons expert whose name became entangled in the political turmoil surrounding the Iraq war. His body was found in woodland after he had been subjected to intense media pressure.

The circumstances were different, yet the pattern was familiar. A man became a target. A man became a vessel for the anger and speculation of others. The human being at the centre of the storm was lost beneath the noise. His family were left to carry the weight of a tragedy that had unfolded in full public view.

We have seen similar dynamics in political life. When Sir Keir Starmer was forced to resign as Prime Minister, the speed and ferocity of the commentary created an atmosphere that felt less like democratic accountability and more like a spectacle. It was as though the country had become addicted to the drama of downfall. The commentary was not always grounded in fact or fairness. It was often driven by people with their own interests and their own desire to be seen.

Social media has accelerated this trend. It has created a space where information and speculation sit side by side without clear distinction. It has created a space where people can express judgement without accountability. It has created a space where the suffering of others becomes a form of entertainment.

There are limited safeguards to separate truth from fiction or to encourage restraint. It is a culture that can dehumanise a person to the point where they feel the only way out of the nightmare is to end their life. We cannot begin to imagine what they will have endured to feel so alone and so vulnerable.

Journalism is meant to be a profession rooted in truth. It is meant to carry responsibility and to recognise the weight of its influence. When a life ends, we must ask whether the scrutiny that preceded it was proportionate. We must ask whether the commentary was fair. We must ask whether the public interest was served. We must ask whether the behaviour of commentators and institutions contributed to a climate that became unbearable for the person at the centre of it.

The question that sits beneath all of this is simple. How can we say that the end of a life is justifiable or acceptable. We cannot. A life is not a storyline. A life is not a vessel for the anger or curiosity of strangers. A life is a life. When public behaviour contributes to the destruction of that life, something has gone profoundly wrong.

There is an ugly curiosity in the way some people watch the suffering of others. It echoes the darkest periods of history. In the past, witch hunts were conducted in the name of morality or purity. People were accused, judged, and punished without evidence. Crowds gathered to witness the downfall of those who had been selected as targets. The rituals were brutal. The logic was flawed. The consequences were fatal.

We tell ourselves that we have evolved. We tell ourselves that we are more informed and more civilised. Yet the behaviour we see today suggests that the instinct to hunt has not disappeared. It has simply changed form. It now takes place on screens rather than in village squares. It now uses commentary rather than torches. It now relies on algorithms rather than superstition. The underlying impulse remains the same. It is the impulse to judge without understanding and to condemn without restraint.

The death of Professor Arday should not be absorbed into the cycle of news and forgotten. It should be a moment of reckoning. It should prompt institutions to examine their practices. It should prompt journalists to reflect on their responsibilities. It should prompt the public to consider the consequences of their words. It should remind us that the line between scrutiny and cruelty is thin and easily crossed.

If we wish to call ourselves a civilised society, we must learn to step back from the frenzy and recognise the humanity of those placed under scrutiny. We must value truth over spectacle and speak with care.

Daxa Manhar Patel is a leadership coach, author and solicitor.
