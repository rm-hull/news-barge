AFTER Yorkshire endured another period of hot, dry weather, firefighters and conservationists are warning that most moorland fires are caused not by natural events, but by people.

According to West Yorkshire Fire and Rescue Service (WYFRS), around 98 per cent of wildfires and large outdoor fires are linked to human activity.

Mick Lead, wildfire lead for WYFRS, said: "Nationally, 2025 broke the records that were set during the heatwaves of 2022. We saw well over a thousand wildfire and moorland fire incidents.

"Based on the data we've been looking at, 2026 is currently well on track to become another record-breaking year for wildfires."

   

*(Image: WYFRS)*

## Barbecues, cigarettes and fireworks

Firefighters say some of the biggest risks come from seemingly everyday activities.

Disposable barbecues, discarded cigarettes, campfires and fireworks all feature heavily in wildfire prevention campaigns.

However, Wildfire lead Mick Rhodes explained that, although this is what they suspect, it would be incredibly hard to determine the actual cause without significant manpower.

He said: "It's very difficult to ascertain the exact cause of many moorland fires because these incidents can cover hundreds, if not thousands, of acres. It's not always possible for a fire investigation officer to walk an entire moor and locate a discarded cigarette, a firework, a glass bottle or a disposable barbecue."

Mr Rhodes added: "We don't want to stop people enjoying the countryside. I enjoy spending time on the moors myself. But we do know that disposable barbecues, campfires, smoking materials, fireworks and other naked flames can all have a significant negative impact."

Yorkshire Wildlife Trust's peatland restoration officer Lyndon Marquis said many fires are entirely preventable.

He said: "These fires are often described as wildfires, but they don't start themselves. They are caused by people, whether that's through a discarded cigarette, a barbecue or, in some cases, deliberate arson."

On proposals for a disposable barbecue ban, he added: "I think certainly amongst our world and people and restoration of us across the peatland restoration community across England, we would support a ban on disposable barbecues simply because it will be that much harder for this kind of issue to happen."

## Easy access and deliberate fire-setting

Another factor is the accessibility of many South Pennine beauty spots.

Mr Marquis said: "One issue in the South Pennines is accessibility. In places such as the Yorkshire Dales, getting onto the moors often requires a significant walk. In parts of the South Pennines, you can park very close to the uplands and be on the moor in a matter of minutes."

Fire services also say a significant number of incidents are started deliberately.

Mr Rhodes said: "Unfortunately, a substantial number of these incidents are also deliberate. Some fires are started intentionally by people who either don't understand or don't care about the consequences of their actions."

He added: "We have seen evidence nationally of individuals deliberately igniting fires on open moorland, including through the use of fireworks. It's difficult to understand why somebody would do that, but we know it does happen."

## Why the damage lasts so long

Beyond the immediate flames, experts say the long-term consequences for wildlife and peatland can be severe, and the greatest concern is often damage to peatland.

He said: "Peat forms incredibly slowly, at around a millimetre a year on average. In colder upland environments it may be slower still. If you've lost a metre of peat, you've potentially lost around a thousand years' worth of natural accumulation.

"That's not something that can simply be replaced within a few years."

Mr Marquis added: "You've got a compound effect where a site has been on fire and it's not really getting a chance to recover before it catches fire again."

## A preventable problem

Both organisations agree that public education remains key to reducing fires.

Mr Marquis said: "Certainly, public education has got to be one of them."

Mr Rhodes added: "Our messaging forms part of the national Be Moor Aware and Let's Learn Moor campaigns, which focus on issues including disposable barbecues, campfires, discarded cigarettes, fireworks and litter such as glass bottles."

He said: "We're trying to make people understand that their actions can have serious consequences, both for the landscape and for the emergency services that have to respond."
