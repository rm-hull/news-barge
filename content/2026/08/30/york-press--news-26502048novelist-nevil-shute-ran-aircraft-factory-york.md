## Before Spark York: Inside the Piccadilly aircraft factory run by a famous author

Airspeed Ferry - built in its Piccadilly factory York. Picture from Air Museum.

  *IVAN MARTIN reports of the life of Nevil Shute, the famous novelist who ran an aircraft factory on York’s Spark site*

IN recent times the Spark development in Piccadilly has attracted much comment and publicity.

Entwined within the comments is the recognition that the plot concerned was at one time an aircraft factory.

However, few may know that the man behind that factory was the famous author Nevil Shute.

Just another literary figure to be added to those with strong links to York.

  Spark in York's Piccadilly


At Howden, near Selby. in the 1920s, large inter-continental passenger airships were being built by the large engineering firm Vickers.

The chief engineer of those projects was Barnes Wallis (later of the Second World War dam-busters fame), and deputy to him was a young aeronautical engineer, Nevil Shute Norway (later known simply as Nevil Shute).

It was this appointment that led Shute to live in York, taking up residence at The Yorkshire Club, then at 5 St Leonard’s Place, opposite the Theatre Royal.

  Nevil Shute 

*(Image: NQ)*

In 1930 their early inter-continental airship the R100 successfully crossed the Atlantic to Canada and back.

But their later R101 airship on its maiden voyage in October 1930, bound for India, crashed in bad weather at Beauvais. France, killing all 48 passengers and crew.

It followed an earlier tragedy in August 1921 when their R38 crashed near Hull killing 44 crew and signalled the end to airship building at Howden.

By the end of 1931, Shute had created his own aircraft manufacturing firm, Airspeed Ltd. with local directors which included aviator Amy Johnson (you can visit the Amy Johnson Museum, at Sowerby Park, near Bridlington).

The year 1931 was an eventful one for Shute. He had previously met a young York doctor Frances Mary Heaton, who he married that year, the couple then taking up residence in Bootham.

The firm’s workshop was in Piccadilly (lately known as Reynard’s bus depot) and at first they produced small gliders, but then turned to making larger aeroplanes with powered flight.

  Old picture of the Airspeed factory at the former Reynards garage site in Piccadilly, now home to Spark. Photo from Air Museum..


However the workshop premises were not big enough to enable full assembly of these aircraft. So the wings and fuselage were taken separately to a private airfield at Sherburn-in-Elmet, where the aircraft could be fully assembled and test flown before being delivered (this airfield was home to the Yorkshire Aeronautical Club of which Shute was a founding member).

This practice was very time consuming, costly and not commercially viable or suitable for mass production.

The aircraft Airspeed produced were technically very sound (Shute received a national award for his designs of retractable undercarriages, later to be used in the Hawker Hurricane and Spitfire) and were in great demand by the Air Ministry.

The Airspeed Oxford was required as a training aircraft for bomber crews, but the Piccadilly plant could not cope with the order numbers (eventually over 8,000 Oxfords were built).

  RAF Airspeed Oxford


So in early 1933, with an eye on the dark political and military future in Germany, Airspeed was ‘taken over’ by De Havilland Ltd.

By the summer of 1933, Airspeed had left York and relocated to larger premises next to an airfield on the outskirts of Portsmouth. Shute, with his wife and now new family followed, leaving York along with the firm from which he would later resign in 1938.

  Airspeed Ferry - built in its Piccadilly factory York. Picture from Air Museum.


During the Second World War, Shute worked for the Royal Navy, while Airspeed returned to its glider roots and produced the large troop-carrying Horsa glider, which was used extensively during D-Day and Operation Market Garden, Arnhem.

Films of those two campaigns (The Longest Day and A Bridge Too Far) clearly show the Horsa glider in use.

  Airspeed Horsa glider. Picture credit to The Army Flying Museum


In 1946, Shute and his family emigrated to Australia were he pursued his great passion for writing novels, for which he would become internationally famous.

His novel, A Town Called Alice would be later adapted for a film of the same name and achieve critical acclaim.

His autobiography, Slide-rule, warmly recalls his time in York.

Nevil Shute Norway died in Melbourne, Australia, on January 12, 1960.
