## Featured in this article

There’s something oddly satisfying about a perfectly labeled spice rack or a pantry with bins that specify exactly what they contain—even if it’s plain as day that the “pasta” bin contains boxes of dried penne. Taking a label maker to the most clutter-laden corners of my home helps me feel organized. And frankly, feeling organized externally often helps me feel organized internally, too.

With that in mind, I researched, selected, and tested some of the top home label makers to see how intuitive different devices were to use and how well their labels stuck to my kids’ dresser drawers, toy bins, water bottles, and lunchboxes. Read on for my thoughts about the best machines for various needs. Just beware that household organizing with a label printer can get addicting and lead to joy-inducing clean spaces.

## Also Tested

**Nelko P21 for $22:** This adorably pocket-sized, affordable, direct thermal label maker is easy to keep at the ready (or even in a tote bag for on-demand label-making emergencies), but actually using it is kind of a lot of work. Its companion app offers plenty of fun templates, but a lot of them are behind a paywall. I only discovered this when I printed a “Pasta” label for a pantry bin, and it came out with a watermark-like overlay. Once I made my own simple label, I struggled to calibrate the label tape to get the pre-cut stickers appropriately centered for my design. Even after I did figure it out, I realized I had to recalibrate the tape for each subsequent print. That’s because there’s no perforation or internal cutting mechanism, so you have to pull out the label tape a bit after it’s printed, which then moves the rest of the pre-cut roll of labels off-center. If you don’t remember to recalibrate, your next label will print off-center as well. I’m sure with enough trial and error, this process would become second nature, but the learning curve for the Nelko was nonetheless the steepest of all the label makers I tried.

## FAQs

What Are the Different Kinds of Label Makers?

The top label makers I’ve included in the list use either thermal transfer printing or direct thermal printing. Direct thermal printing works by heating special paper, which is a process that yields labels that are less durable under various conditions and also more likely to fade over time. Thermal transfer printing works by melting ink from a ribbon cartridge onto a label. These labels tend to be more durable and longer-lasting. Thermal-transfer label makers also typically require slightly larger, more expensive machines.

Are Bluetooth or Traditional Label Makers Better?

Neither is inherently better, but each offers its own benefits. Traditional label makers have on-device keyboards where you type your label and customize font sizes, styles, symbols, and borders. Bluetooth-enabled label makers work by pairing the device with a mobile app on which you design your label, and then print it remotely. Often, these apps feature a wider variety of templates and font options than traditional label makers. Apps also typically show a more accurate preview than LCD screens on label makers themselves. Some label makers, like the best overall Brother PT-n25bt, offer both on-device and app-enabled designing.

Can You Use Label Maker Labels on Clothing?

It’s not recommended unless the label tape in question specifically mentions being able to work with fabric, like the TZe fabric label tape for the P-touch Cube Plus. Some label tape does claim to be heat-resistant and water-resistant, but even in this case, the label’s adhesive quality is likely to degrade after a bunch of cycles in the washer and dryer.

How I Tested These Label Makers

I considered popular options, and I received some testing units as samples from the company and bought others. I tested the functionality of each device by creating labels with an on-device keyboard, designing labels in a companion app and printing via Bluetooth compatibility, or both, depending on the label maker in question’s capabilities. I tested the durability of the top machines’ labels by sticking them on various surfaces, seeing if they could get wet and handle heat without fading or losing their adhesive strength over the course of at least one week, often longer.
