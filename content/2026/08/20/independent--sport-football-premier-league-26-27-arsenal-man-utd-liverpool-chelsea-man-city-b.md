As the Premier League’s wealthier clubs have tried to work out how close they are to a 2026-27 title challenge, one calculation has caused excitement. Some of their analyses predict that winning the league may require just over 80 points. That would be the lowest since 2016, a period with a few parallels to now.

The low estimated figure is largely down to the general evolution of the division since then… but also one distinctive end point. Pep Guardiola, English football’s singular most influential figure since Sir Alex Ferguson, has gone.

And with him has gone the guarantee that Manchester City will always be up there, ready to go on a run, ensuring you almost certainly have to finish above them to win the title.

You knew exactly what was going to happen otherwise.

And sure, Enzo Maresca’s new side may end up as good a team since this remains a lavishly funded Abu Dhabi project who are likely to spend another £200m before the window closes. But we just don’t know. The uncertainty is deepened by the likelihood the City case finally concludes this season. The club insist on their innocence. Yes, we’ve heard all this before, with Wednesday’s annual pre-season briefing marking the fourth successive year that Premier League chief executive Richard Masters insisted he couldn’t comment.

The potential outcome nevertheless forms yet another unknown in this campaign, only further fostering the sense of a new era. If so, it would appropriately be a first such break since the other bookend to Guardiola’s City career: his beginning, back in 2016.

In that summer, the Catalan was one of many big-name coaches to arrive, heralding “the league of star managers”.

Now, benches will be populated by numerous relative unknowns. It says much that Mikel Arteta is maybe the only “big beast”, since he is the only current manager to have won the title. This similarly marks the first ever Premier League season where none of Ferguson, Arsene Wenger or Guardiola are managing.

Numerous similar stats emphasise this.

Fabian Hurzeler, just 33, is the joint-fourth longest serving manager, along with Regis Le Bris.

Over half the coaches, totalling 11, are currently going through their first pre-seasons with these teams.

It is remarkable turnover.

That probably perpetuates a theme of last season and the increasing influence of executives, but it’s also not to argue these coaches are “nobodies” or “randoms”.

You have to be very good to even get to the Premier League given its current status. Those in coaching circles are especially enthused about Newcastle United’s Matthias Jaissle, who moves from Al-Ahli to replace Eddie Howe.

As Masters did say on the eve of the new season, however, “there’s lots of new in the Premier League”.

Really, it’s hard to think of a season with so many unknowns.

It’s both emotionally thrilling, if also more difficult to make sense of.

The number of new managers means we have so little existing knowledge to lean on.

For years, after all, we’ve known what Guardiola was capable of. We’ve known so many similar idiosyncrasies to rivals like Jurgen Klopp.

Now, there’s less of that than ever, with even some recent truths complicated.

Andoni Iraola’s football may be among the most stirring in the Premier League, but is it as replicable at a bigger club, with Champions League demands and a shallow squad?

Brighton have long been one of the most intelligent clubs but will European football dull their edge? Michael Carrick has restored stability to Manchester United but is that enough when you are now more than the stand-in?

The effect of an exhausting World Cup only offers another variable.

Much of this does admittedly amplify the Premier League’s long-time unique selling point, that anything can happen.

That feels truer than ever before, principally because a shift like this alone has never happened.

It is arguably all the more timely, too, since this is the first season of new financial rules. They are termed Squad Cost Ratio and Sustainability and Systemic Resilience if not exactly jumpers for goalposts.

Masters argued on Wednesday that the intention is to improve the Premier League’s famed competitive balance but counter-arguments pervade that they will only end up serving wealthy clubs and wealthy owners.

The Premier League could do with continuing last season’s capacity for the unexpected. Two areas of the table may be instructive.

One is at the bottom, where Ipswich Town, Hull City and the resurgent Coventry City currently look less equipped than recent promoted teams. Or maybe that will pose another surprise.

Another area is the top five, and the notorious Champions League in-out cycle that Aston Villa and Newcastle United have got into.

Europe is again set to prove a great differential. With nine clubs involved, those with free midweeks could have a significant advantage. Tottenham Hotspur’s eye-catching spending suggests they sense opportunity, while Chelsea consequently look real challengers for the title under Xabi Alonso. The latter is exactly what happened in 2016, too, Antonio Conte benefitting from no Europe in the way Alonso, who may also operate a back three, might.

A rare serenity within Chelsea’s squad is the other side of more chaos outside it, from ongoing rival agitation at the lack of punishment in recent disciplinary cases to the latest noise that Todd Boehly and Mark Walters are going to sell their stakes.

That takeover subplot is linked to wider developments in the US, as Walters’s business empire faces state scrutiny, having sold off the Los Angeles Lakers in a record-breaking deal thought to be worth £9bn. All of this at the same time that a consortium involving Jeff Bezos has bought into Liverpool.

This World Cup does seem to have pushed football into an even more elevated sphere of high politics, with the Premier League naturally at the top. On Wednesday, Masters evaded a question on whether the game’s institutions can possibly withstand such forces.

The deepening investment also comes when there’s a sudden wrinkle in the Premier League’s immense global marketability.

The departures of Guardiola and Mohammed Salah have left it low on “stars”; of players capable of the unknown. Erling Haaland almost stands alone, even if players like Alexander Isak and Martin Odegaard are now in rising form.

It still represents such an incongruity, given how the Premier League spends billions more than any other league. Are there many opposition players you’d be excited about visiting your stadium? Does this consequently represent “waste” or a lack of value for all that money, especially amid last season’s debate about the quality of the football?

While the Premier League is now attempting to lessen “grappling”, Masters and others would insist there’s nothing to solve as regards recruitment. What good are the stars of Real Madrid, Barcelona and Paris Saint-Germain, after all, if they just make their domestic leagues competitively unappealing? The unpredictability of games trumps the unpredictability of individuals, while helping to create stars, too. Salah didn’t arrive with such a profile.

It is nevertheless within all of these unknowns that certain “knowns” become all the more persuasive, especially with the teams that have a perceived stability.

There is opportunity for Brentford. Leeds United can kick on, too.

Above all, there are the champions. Along with so many predictions that Arsenal will become a rare team to retain the title, their first since 1935, many analyses have them winning it with 85 points or less; something we saw last season. They have a new assurance from that, that they finally know they can win it.

Or, perhaps it’ll be the theme of this season to offer something nobody has seen before.
