Cue *At the Castle Gate*. Almost as enduring as the BBC itself,* The Sky at Night* is one of the longest-running TV programmes anywhere. First shown in 1957, it's like a hand reaching out from the past, albeit with butter-fingers. Many of the episodes, somewhat pleasingly, have been lost forever, the romantic legacy of a bygone era.

We can only imagine what was contained within them.

And that rather neatly encapsulates what *The Sky at Night* stands for. The science, the explainers, the expert insight is illuminating and educational, but at its heart is something altogether more nebulous, even sentimental: that feeling when you're gazing at the stars and your mind drifts so deeply into the outer bounds of the universe that it's all you can do to remain upright.

The late Sir Patrick Moore missed one episode between the show's inception on April 24, 1957 and his death on December 9, 2012, and it's a tribute to the amateur astronomer that it's taken four avuncular experts at the top of their respective fields – Maggie Aderin-Pocock, Pete Lawrence, Chris Lintott and George Dransfield – to pick up the mantle.

Come for the untold stories, the facts, the deep dives on subjects as divergent and open-ended as extra-terrestrial life, space weather and astronaut training, but if there's anything you take away from it, let it be curiosity and the urge to pick up a telescope or a pair of binoculars every once in a while.
