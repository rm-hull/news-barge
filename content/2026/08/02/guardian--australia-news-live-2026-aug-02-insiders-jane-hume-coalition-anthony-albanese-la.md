Concept images of controversial Victoria Park precinct revealed ahead of 2032 Brisbane Olympics

Ben Smee

The Queensland government has released new concept images showing Brisbane’s broader Victoria Park precinct after the construction of the 2032 Olympic stadium and aquatic centre.

Victoria Park is Brisbane’s largest inner-city parkland and a site of significance for First Nations elders.

It is a controversial choice as home to Brisbane’s upcoming Olympics, leading to protests from community members concerned about the loss of community green space and areas of cultural importance.

The images – along with a master plan for the broader parkland site – show plans for two new pedestrian bridges across the inner-city bypass. The road severs the site from Spring Hill and the rest of the Brisbane CBD and had been a significant constraint for planners.

Artists impressions of the Victoria Park precinct, in the Queensland government’s Victoria Park Draft Legacy Plan. Illustration: Supplied by the Queensland Government

It also proposes flooding existing sports fields on the site to expand the York’s Hollow wetland.

The government says the precinct will “become a world-class destination that celebrates Brisbane’s unique outdoor lifestyle while creating a vibrant place where people can gather, play, connect and experience unforgettable moments for generations to come”.

It says about two-thirds of the existing park will remain public green space.

‘Starting to take off’: bird flu mass mortality likely

A mass mortality event, potentially triggered by bird flu, mirrors what’s happened as the deadly disease spread overseas, environmental advocates warn.

AAP reports, more than 80 dead and sick greater crested terns have been found at an island called Baudin Rock in South Australia.

Authorities say it is likely to be the first mass mortality event from the deadly disease.

The Invasive Species Council’s chief executive, Jack Gough, said Australia was still at the very early stages of the H5 outbreak, but it could spread quickly.

It seems the virus is starting to take off, mirroring the experience in South America.

[That is:] early detections, then rapid increases in numbers and spread, leading to mass mortality events in wildlife.

Bird flu has caused significant illness and deaths in poultry, wild birds and mammals in Asia; Europe; North and South America; and the Arctic and Antarctic, according to Wildlife Health Australia.

Over 600 bird species and more than 100 mammalian species, including wild marine and land mammals, have been affected.

‘More than just objects’: Australian booksellers raise alarm over ‘horrific’ destruction of rare titles to feed AI

Vendors and rare books experts are increasingly worried that these artefacts may be under threat of destruction by generative AI companies seeking to feed their ravenous technology with new texts.

Stephanie Convery writes the fears stem from a copyright lawsuit brought against AI company Anthropic in the United States by book authors. Unsealed court files revealed earlier this year that the company was engaging in the practice of “destructive scanning” – buying physical books, slicing off the spines to more efficiently scan the pages, then pulping the remains – and had sought to keep it quiet. The judge ruled this was lawful under US copyright law, as it amounted to “transformative use”.

The practice is now understood to be widespread among generative AI companies, and recent reports suggest intermediaries are stepping up to facilitate the process.

Australian secondhand booksellers are wondering if they have been caught up in the AI supply chain after they recently experienced waves of orders that did not fit with usual customer patterns.

Nation-first fully integrated bionic arm to hit shelves

A university startup has developed Australia’s first fully integrated bionic arm, giving hope to users who have given up on heavy, painful prosthetics, AAP reports.

Cost, weight and accessibility changes means 35–40% of prosthetic users in high-income countries end up rejecting their devices.

But after speaking to dozens of people with limb difference to identify key challenges over 10 years of development, University of Melbourne-led startup Meablex have developed a bionic arm that could change the experience of prosthetics for users.

It features soft and flexible materials that weigh about half what a conventional prosthetic does.

It’s also been designed with an adjustable socket to make the prosthetic easier to fit, and is more reliable to control than others currently on the market.

Australian scientists have created a revolutionary lightweight bionic arm. Photograph: Christopher Hopkins/AAP

Senior research engineer Dr Alireza Mohammadi told AAP:

Our technology combines a lightweight 3D-printed prosthetic hand with a new sensorised socket that detects subtle muscle movement, rather than relying only on traditional electrical muscle signals.

He hopes the device can enter the market next year, following clinical trials and regulatory approvals later this year.

We’re not just looking at Australia, we are thinking more globally.

It will definitely affect many lives and many people that can use this device.
