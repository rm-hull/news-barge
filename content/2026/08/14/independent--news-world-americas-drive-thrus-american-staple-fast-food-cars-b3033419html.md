To properly frame this history, it helps to look at two separate narratives. Few pairings are as naturally intertwined in modern culture as two quintessential American staples: fast food and cars.

These major cultural phenomena first converged in the early 20th century through the emergence of drive-in eateries. This nostalgic concept, famously depicted in mid-century pop culture, featured staff, delivering meals directly to parked vehicles to be eaten off window-mounted trays.

The true turning point arrived during the late 1940s. Depending on historical accounts, the modern drive-thru originated either at Red’s Giant Hamburg in Springfield, Missouri, in 1947, or at a California In-N-Out Burger a year later. By contrast, Europe did not welcome its first recorded drive-thru until 1985, in Dublin.

![The concept rapidly expanded into a flourishing industry across the United States by the mid-1960s](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/08/14/19/America_25__Objects_The_Drive_Thru_73516.jpg)

The concept rapidly expanded into a flourishing industry across the United States by the mid-1960s, inspiring fresh variations. Jack in the Box introduced a model dedicated entirely to drive-thru orders, omitting indoor dining and parking entirely while using a mechanical clown speaker box. Meanwhile, Western residents may remember Wienershnitzel's distinctive A-frame buildings, which featured driving lanes cut directly through the structure.

The innovation sparked unexpected knock-on developments. Restaurant menus shifted heavily toward sandwiches, fries, chicken strips and other portables that could be consumed on the go. The cars themselves adjusted over the years to accommodate larger and larger drink holders. What sociological (and nutritional) impact this has all had on the actual consumers would be the subject of a much longer essay.

The innovation hasn’t stopped. Modern drive-thrus have evolved into tightly coordinated multi-lane affairs. At Chick-fil-As around the country, actual humans with electronic tablets now stand – breathing in exhaust fumes all day – to ensure a seamless ordering process with no mechanical speaker garbles.

The drive-thru, with its distinctly American creative spelling, has become woven into the fabric of the culture. It has been enshrined in the Smithsonian Museum of American History and has played a memorable role in recent and successful Presidential campaigns.

As one historian put it, somewhat pointedly: “The drive-in was less a pure novelty than an expression of great American passions that go hand in hand: speed, efficiency and, sometimes, laziness.”
