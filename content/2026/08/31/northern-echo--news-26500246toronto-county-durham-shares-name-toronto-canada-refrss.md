Just a mile or so north-west of Bishop Auckland lies a small County Durham village with a name far more commonly associated with one of Canada’s biggest cities.

Toronto is a former mining settlement tucked into a loop of the River Wear, and its unusual name has long prompted curiosity from people passing through the area.

While it may seem an unlikely name for a quiet County Durham village surrounded by fields and countryside, its origins can reportedly be traced back to the height of the region’s coal-mining era.

The story goes that in 1859, local coal owner W.C. Stobart was visiting Toronto in Ontario when he received news that coal had been discovered beneath land he owned back home.

*(Image: GOOGLE)*

To mark the discovery, the new colliery was named Toronto, and over time the name was adopted by the small community that grew up around it.

The result was a County Durham village sharing its name with a major Canadian city thousands of miles away.

Toronto’s history is closely tied to the coal industry, as was the case with many communities across the Durham coalfield.

The colliery around which the settlement developed has long since disappeared, but the village still retains much of the appearance associated with its industrial past, including rows of traditional brick-built terraced homes.

Despite its mining heritage, Toronto today has a distinctly rural setting.


The village sits on raised ground within a bend of the River Wear, with agricultural land and open countryside surrounding much of the settlement.

It has always been a relatively small community, with a population of just 399 recorded in the 2001 Census.

The Canadian city from which the village took its name has an even older linguistic history, with Toronto commonly traced to the Mohawk word “tkaronto”, often translated as “where there are trees standing in the water”.

Toronto is also not the only County Durham place name with an international flavour.

Elsewhere in the county are places including Quebec, while Washington in the North East also has its own famous transatlantic associations.

Such names are reminders of a period when international travel, trade, industry and empire could all leave their mark on local communities.

In Toronto’s case, however, the explanation is particularly neat — a local coal owner travelling in Canada, news of a coal discovery back in County Durham, and a decision that left a global name permanently attached to a small former mining village near Bishop Auckland.
