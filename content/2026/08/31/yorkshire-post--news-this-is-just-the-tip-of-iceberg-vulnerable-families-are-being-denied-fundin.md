# 'This is just the tip of iceberg': Vulnerable families are being denied funding, says lawyer

## Families across Britain are facing potentially catastrophic care costs after being wrongly denied NHS funding which is supposed to support people with major, long-term healthcare needs, according to a Yorkshire lawyer.

James Urquhart-Burton, Continuing Healthcare expert at Leeds-based Winston Solicitors told The Yorkshire Post that flaws in the application of the NHS’s Continuing Healthcare (CHC) scheme are forcing relatives of people with chronic health conditions to face a traumatic process, at a time when declining eligibility figures have not been fully explained.

CHC is NHS-funded care for adults whose needs are primarily health-related. If found eligible, the NHS funds all assessed care costs, Mr Urquhart-Burton said. If not, individuals may face substantial means-tested social care charges, which means it is one of the most “consequential decisions” in health and social care.

However, Mr Urquhart-Burton said that eligibility rates are falling at a time when completing referrals have increased and reviews by Integrated Care Boards (ICBs) – the organisations responsible for managing the local NHS budget – and their external providers sometimes unjustly result in previously awarded funding being withdrawn.

At the end of December 2024, the total number of people deemed eligible for CHC funding – for both standard and fast-track CHC – was 52,008, falling 9.1 per cent from 57,216 at the end of June 2017, according to a Nuffield Trust report.

In December 2024, the report said that the number of people assessed as eligible ranged from 20 to 95 per 50,000 adults between ICBs - an almost fivefold difference.

Eligibility for standard CHC has fallen, with less than a fifth (18.6 per cent) of assessed individuals found eligible in October-December 2024, compared with just under a third (31.2 per cent) in April-June 2017.

Mr Urquhart-Burton said the need for CHC comes at the worst of times for any family, but the process they face to receive the monies thousands of them are legally entitled to is gruelling.

He added: “It can be lengthy, complex and at times inconsistent. We regularly encounter cases where decisions are later overturned on appeal, or where retrospective reviews identify individuals who should have been assessed for CHC during their lifetime. That inevitably raises questions about whether the process always identifies eligible individuals when it should.”

He said issues surrounding the provision of CHC are very well and recently documented. The topic was debated in Parliament in March, while a Nuffield Trust report in September 2025 “grimly dubbed it an all or nothing scheme but despite this, still nothing has been done to fix it”.

The Nuffield Trust report called for “urgent action” to improve consistency and fairness in how CHC operates, adding: “There are concerns that financial pressures are playing a key role in the operation of CHC, with all parts of the system facing significant pressure to keep costs down.”

The Trust, Age UK and the National Audit Office have also highlighted “persistent concerns” about a lack of public awareness, complex assessments and the financial and emotional stress on families linked with the process of applying for CHC.

Mr Urquhart-Burton, who has specialised in Continuing Healthcare claims for 15- years, reports that in recent years, there have been growing numbers coming to him to appeal rejected claims or fight for retrospective monies following a loved one’s death. Much of that work arises from serious concerns that the guidance is being applied correctly, or that an assessment was never even carried out when it should have been, he said.

“The NHS is meant to provide healthcare for all, but when it comes to CHC, we’re finding vital care funding refused or withdrawn in circumstances where the decision does not appear to be properly supported by the clinical evidence,” said Mr Urquhart-Burton.

“We’ve also witnessed an increasing number of decisions being made that do not clearly align with the National Framework for CHC.”

He said: “People who come to us for advice have realised that CHC is incredibly difficult to access. Your loved one may have very complex needs but still faces a battle to receive CHC support.

“If you’ve got a parent who needs to be assessed, you really need to educate yourself about how this process works. We regularly advise families who have been told their loved one is not eligible for CHC, only for questions to arise later about whether the assessment was conducted properly and in accordance with the National Framework.”

Since 2007 we’ve had a National Framework for assessing CHC eligibility – most recently updated in 2022 – but evidently, the criteria aren’t being applied consistently across England, he said.

“This is likely to be – at least in part – a training and consistency issue among NHS staff. For instance, as an advocate, I will attend CHC assessments around the country. One day I might be representing a client in Surrey, and another day in West Yorkshire. My personal experience is that there remain significant variations in how the criteria are applied across England and between individual assessors.

“ICBs will not all have the same budget or the same population size in their area. The criteria requires assessors to look at the nature, intensity, complexity and unpredictability of an individual’s needs before exercising professional judgement on eligibility; which inevitably creates scope for variation.

“The number of referrals into the system has increased, which could be due to greater awareness from families and professionals, as well as an ageing population. However, that increase has not been matched by a corresponding increase in the number of people being found eligible for CHC, and that’s where the concern lies.”

“At the moment, our experience on the ground is that people in West Yorkshire are not being assessed as promptly as they should, and case decisions are slow to arrive. But, as a lawyer working around the country, I see similar systemic issues everywhere.

“The CHC assessment should be multi-disciplinary, and the professionals who make the eligibility recommendation should be individuals who have personal knowledge of the patient’s needs, where possible.

“We’re increasingly seeing ICBs refuse to allow knowledgeable professionals to take part in making the recommendation, and multi-disciplinary teams making clear recommendations to the ICB, only for individual “verifiers” to reject those recommendations, and substitute their own decision on eligibility.

“The decision families ultimately receive is increasingly no longer truly multi-disciplinary in nature, but instead a decision made by an individual decision-maker. Families are having to deal not only with a catastrophic health event and profound deterioration of the health of a close family member, compounded by the worry of cost and how the care will be paid for.”

“In the worst cases, the potential consequence is that people live out the remainder of their time in a care home, leading to significant family wealth consumed by care fees.

“A bad decision can ruin a family emotionally and financially. There is no sugar coating that. Whilst I would never advocate for a “tick box” approach to assessing an individual’s needs, the eligibility criteria must be simplified.

“People are calling for a complete overhaul of the system. At the very least, the criteria should be clear and consistently applied for everybody, and there should be collaboration between the NHS and key stakeholders, to develop a national standard for principles of assessment and training assessors so there isn’t this regional variation.”

Mr Urquhart-Burton said recent examples have included a case where the initial screening assessment showed the patient required a full eligibility assessment for CHC, but the assessor incorrectly interpreted the report they had produced. As a result, a full assessment wasn’t carried out and the incorrect report was then relied upon as the benchmark for subsequent annual reviews. The patient died without ever receiving a proper assessment, he said.

He also cited a case where an Integrated Care Board accepted that a patient had been wrongly denied NHS Continuing Healthcare funding for part of the time she was receiving care, but refused funding for an earlier period despite evidence that the patient’s needs remained complex. It has taken three months for a reimbursement offer to be received from an ICB and the family are expected to appeal the decision relating to the earlier period.

“This is just the tip of the iceberg – the full extent of which is scandalous; especially when looked at in the context of an ageing population that will undoubtedly lead to the numbers qualifying for CHC growing significantly over the next few years.”

Responding to the concerns raised by Mr Urquhart-Burton, a Department of Health and Social Care spokesperson said:

“No family should face distress or financial hardship because they cannot access the care their loved one is entitled to.

“Local Integrated Care Boards are responsible for decisions about providing funding for NHS Continuing Healthcare. Any measures to manage cost pressures should not impact someone’s eligibility to access NHS Continuing Healthcare.

“NHS England provides detailed assurance and oversight of Integrated Care Boards, including comparing NHS Continuing Healthcare eligibility and referral rates, as well as investigating outliers for drivers of unwarranted variation.”
