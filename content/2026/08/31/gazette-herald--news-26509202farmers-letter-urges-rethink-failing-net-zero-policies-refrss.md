Another week, another letter or two about our changing climate and the problems it creates. Once again, we are presented with statistics and arguments that can feel a long way removed from the everyday reality of those simply trying to make a living and get through another day.

I am not an expert in statistics, but I am someone living with the real, day-to-day consequences of failing government net zero targets and policies.

Dr Williams and Mr Potter (letters 19 August) appear to me to be a long way removed from the reality of the damage that can be caused by policies which may sound reasonable on paper but have very real consequences on the ground.

Let’s be clear: I agree that the climate is changing. I get it. We see changes ourselves. But net zero targets must not trump everything else. They must not become an excuse to damage livelihoods, food production, land or communities in pursuit of targets at any cost.

Mr Potter talks about what farmers must be aware of: reduced yields, feed availability, loss of fertility and rising input costs.

Well, Mr Potter, farmers are very aware of these things. Those of us who work outside every day, in and alongside the natural environment, understand its changes in a very practical way. On our own farm, we have been reducing our fossil fuel use for almost 30 years. We do what we can, locally and practically.

We are not waiting for someone to tell us that the environment matters. We already know it does.

That is why I struggle with policies which can take some of our best and resilient farmland out of food production while at the same time warning farmers about the challenges of heatwaves, droughts, reduced yields and food security.

And I question policies which increasingly favour large corporations and wealthy investors at the expense of the small, hardworking farming businesses that actually produce our food and manage our countryside.

There is nothing wrong with wanting to reduce emissions. But the pace and scale of change must be realistic, proportionate and properly considered. We cannot simply pursue ever more ambitious targets without asking who pays the price and what unintended consequences follow.

Most of us are doing our best for the environment around us - the place where we live, raise our children and contribute to our communities. Practical action locally is worth far more than arguments about who has the most impressive statistics or the strongest opinion.

Perhaps it is time to hear from some fresh voices too. Our community has plenty of people with experience and views worth sharing. Let’s hear some different perspectives and invigorate these pages with a proper debate, rather than simply revisiting the same arguments.

Emma Sturdy

Old Malton
