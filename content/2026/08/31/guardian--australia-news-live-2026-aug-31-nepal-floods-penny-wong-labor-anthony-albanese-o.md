NSW may not be able to prosecute large-scale water theft

The New South Wales water regulator has cast doubt over its ability to prosecute potential water theft due to uncertainty about the validity of licences governing billions of litres of water entitlements in the state’s north.

It follows a successful legal challenge brought by a group of irrigators in the state’s north-west which found 11 floodplain harvesting licences issued by the former Coalition government were invalid.

The NSW land and environment court found the government had failed to follow correct procedures related to fair consultation with landholders. The case, known as Emu Rider, has raised questions about the validity of the process used to issue hundreds of floodplain harvesting licences.

NSW police appeal for information after body of newborn found in Sydney

NSW police are appealing for information after the body of a newborn baby was found in Sydney’s south-west today.

Officials said emergency services were called to the suburb of Auburn amid reports the baby had been found in a bin. Officers attended and discovered the body of a baby boy.

Police have established a crime scene and commenced an investigation.

They hold serious concerns for the welfare of the baby’s mother and are urging her to attend the nearest hospital to receive urgent care. Police are also asking for anyone with information about the incident, or who was in the area at the time with any relevant footage, to contact police.

A majority of Australian youth lack confidence distinguishing news fact from fiction as they increasingly source information from artificial intelligence, AAP reports.

The 2026 News and Young Australians Report, released on Monday, found just 47% of children aged between 10 and 17 felt confident they could spot disinformation online.

A third of the 1,027 kids surveyed had shared a news story on social media to later find it was false.

Queensland becomes first state to mandate licence requirements for ebikes and e-scooters

From Monday, Queensland has become the first state to make ebike and e-scooter riders hold at least a learner driver’s licence, with new rules kicking in on top of age limits and a statewide police blitz seizing hundreds of non-compliant devices, AAP reports.

Riders must now have a valid learner’s licence to use electric-powered cycles and e-scooters on Queensland roads and paths, with limited exemptions for supervised 12 to 17-year-olds, medically exempt riders and people in designated off-road recreation areas.

“We always said we would make the tough decisions to keep the community safe; our laws get the balance right between cracking down on those who do the wrong thing, while backing those Queenslanders who do the right thing and delivering sensible exemptions,” the state transport minister, Brent Mickelberg, said.

Police have already issued more than 1,700 fines and seized almost 400 illegal devices since 1 July under Operation Yankee Surety, and will now start checking licences during street intercepts.

Government not planning to ban import of smartglasses, says Rowland

Luca Ittimani

Michelle Rowland says the government is not immediately planning to ban imports of smartglasses.

The Greens and others have called for the camera-enabled wearable technology to locked out of the country.

The attorney general has told reporters the calls to limit the technology were “valid”. She said:

Firstly, I want to acknowledge the very deep concerns. We are not specifically in this consultation consulting on an import ban. … that would have consequences across government, including across trade, foreign affairs, and other potential legal obligations that can apply internationally.

She said the government’s reforms today would still impact on the smartglasses, with digital platforms prevented from storing recordings and data from the glasses if it was not “fair and reasonable” to do so.

Ray-Ban Meta AI glasses, which have in-built cameras and AI. Photograph: Vuk Valcic/Zuma Press Wire/Shutterstock

Rowland said she commended local governments who were blocking the glasses’ use, for example at public pools, and said the federal government needed to use the powers that it had.

Let’s be clear, we’re talking about the harmful behaviours and regulating the harms. That’s what governments seek to do. In terms of an import ban, as I said, we are not ruling anything out, but we are utilising what we have at our disposal right now.

Attorney general outlines planned privacy reforms including 'right to erasure'

Luca Ittimani

The attorney general, Michelle Rowland, is announcing planned new privacy reforms at a Canberra press conference. She has told reporters:

We know that Australians are increasingly concerned about how their data is being collected, used, and shared, particularly as AI and other emerging technologies such as smart glasses become more accessible. People should not have to choose between protecting their privacy and participating in the digital economy.

She said the government was planning to introduce:

A “fair and reasonable” test to limit companies to collecting and using only the personal information they need;

A right to erasure, so people can ask digital platforms to destroy their stores of personal information;

Stronger standards for consent and clearer information when people agree to share personal details;

Limits on businesses buying and selling personal information without clear permission;

And a new ID Lock service through MyGov, designed to let Australians block, unblock, and monitor the use of their eligible identity documents for verification.

Victorian prisoners to be sent out to repair roads under Labor’s pothole plan

Victorian prisoners will be put to work on road maintenance as part of Labor’s plan to fix the state’s potholes if re-elected.

The state’s premier, Ben Carroll, will outline the plan on Monday off the back of an announcement on Sunday that the government would be increasing the road maintenance budget by $352m to fix 500,000 potholes by the end of January.

Carroll will declare that if Labor wins the November election, it would work with Corrections Victoria to develop a pilot program to put supervised, minimum-security prisoners to work performing roadside maintenance.

The plan would include having inmates take over “basic” duties such as mowing lawns, controlling weeds, removing graffiti and picking up rubbish, allowing more skilled crews to focus on fixing potholes.

The government said the work of prisoners “will not replace paid jobs” but instead “supplement the workforce where there are skill and capacity shortages”.
