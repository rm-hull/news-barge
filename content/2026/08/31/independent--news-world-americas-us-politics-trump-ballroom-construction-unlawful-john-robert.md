A 5-4 conservative majority on the Supreme Court ruled Monday that construction on a new $400 million White House ballroom can proceed, reversing lower court orders that had temporarily halted aboveground work on the project.

Chief Justice John Roberts dissented from the decision, writing that the ongoing construction was “likely unlawful.”

Roberts joined the court’s three liberal justices in opposition, arguing that work on the facility had moved forward unchecked for nearly a year despite significant legal flaws.

“Construction of the ballroom has proceeded apace for the better part of a year,” Roberts wrote. “That construction is likely unlawful.”

Despite Roberts’ warning, the majority threw out the challenge against the Trump administration on procedural grounds. The court concluded that the plaintiff, the National Trust for Historic Preservation, lacked the legal standing required to pursue a federal lawsuit.

“Today, we do not pass upon the legality of the government’s East Wing project,” the court stated in its unsigned opinion. “We conclude only that, based on the submissions before us, the government is likely to prevail in showing that the Trust lacks ... standing to challenge the project in federal court.”

By deciding the matter purely on standing, the ruling leaves unresolved the core constitutional dispute: whether a president possesses the unilateral authority to alter White House grounds without explicit authorization from Congress.

While administration attorneys point to existing provisions in federal law allowing routine executive maintenance and property improvements, the National Trust contended in court filings that long-standing federal statutes mandate express congressional approval for major structural construction on public land in the capital.

The historic preservation group had grounded its claim of standing on a declaration by board member Alison Hoagland, a Washington resident and preservationist. Hoagland asserted that passing the construction site caused her personal, professional and aesthetic harm.

The Supreme Court majority explicitly rejected that argument, writing that “mere offense, disagreement, or distaste does not qualify as a concrete and particularized injury.”

The ruling removes the final legal barrier for a 250-person construction crew operating on 20-hour daily shifts to finish the structure. Government filings indicate the development — which includes an extensive subterranean facility designed for secure medical and operational needs — was already 65 percent complete as of late August.

Although White House officials maintain that the $400 million price tag is fully covered by private donations, that claim continues to face intense scrutiny, as court records reveal Secret Service funds have already been tapped to finance site security upgrades.

Monday's order replaces an interim administrative stay issued by Roberts on Aug. 21. It overturns previous rulings by both a federal district court judge and the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit, which had permitted underground security work to proceed while blocking aboveground construction pending a trial.
