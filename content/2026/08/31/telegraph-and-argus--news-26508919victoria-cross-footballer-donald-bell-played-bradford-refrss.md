A new book sheds light on the life of a Bradford Park Avenue footballer who was the only English professional player to be awarded the Victoria Cross.

Donald Simpson Bell, the only English professional footballer to be awarded the Victoria Cross during the First World War, is the subject of a full-length biography by Manchester-born writer Iain McMullen.

The book tells the story of Bell’s life, from his early years in Harrogate to his time as a schoolteacher, footballer and soldier.

It draws on previously unpublished letters, diaries and photographs from the Bell family archive.

He went on to play for Newcastle United and Bishop Auckland before signing professionally with Bradford Park Avenue.

When the First World War began, he enlisted in the Army rather than joining the Footballers’ Battalion. He served with the 9th Battalion of the Yorkshire Regiment, better known as the Green Howards, and was commissioned as a second lieutenant.

His football background came in useful: contemporary accounts emphasised his speed, fitness and athleticism, qualities that mattered enormously in the sudden close-quarter assault for which he became famous.

## The Horseshoe Trench action

Horseshoe Trench was a German position near La Boisselle and Contalmaison on the Somme battlefield in northern France. It was around 1,400–1,500 metres long, on higher ground and curved in shape — hence its name. On 5 July 1916, four days after the disastrous opening of the Battle of the Somme, Bell’s battalion was among the troops ordered to capture it.

During the advance, a German machine gun opened severe enfilade fire — firing along the length or flank of the British line — and threatened to halt the assault with heavy casualties.

Bell acted on his own initiative. Accompanied by Corporal Colwill and Private Batey, he worked forward through a communication trench. When they were close to the gun, the three men dashed across open ground under intense fire.

Bell reached the position, shot the machine-gunner with his revolver, then used Mills bombs to destroy the gun and its crew. He also threw bombs into the adjacent trench. The official VC citation concluded that his action “saved many lives and ensured the success of the attack”

Sadly, he was killed in action five days later during a similar act of gallantry at Contalmaison.

Mr McMullen’s book explores Bell’s early life, football career and military service, as well as the lasting impact of his sacrifice.

## Football honours a hero

It also examines how the footballing world has honoured his memory over the years.

A former journalist and long-standing member of the Western Front Association, Mr McMullen has spent nearly 40 years researching the First World War.

His work on footballers who served during the conflict led to the creation of the website footballandthefirstworldwar.org in 2013.

During the war’s centenary, he worked closely with the National Football Museum, the Professional Footballer Association and the Commonwealth War Graves Commission.

He has also advised media outlets including the BBC, Sky Sports, The Daily Telegraph and The Mirror.
