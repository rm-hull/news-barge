A dramatic gathering of some of the North East’s most striking birds has been captured at RSPB Saltholme after a marsh harrier sent flocks of waders and ducks into the air.

Northern Echo Camera Club member Pat Blewitt photographed avocets and black-tailed godwits taking off alongside teal as the bird of prey passed overhead at the Teesside nature reserve this week.

The encounter brought together several species with notable conservation stories in one scene, with the black-tailed godwit currently on the UK Red List of birds of highest conservation concern.

  The rare birds over RSPB Saltholme 

*(Image: PAT BLEWITT/CAMERA CLUB)*

Avocets, meanwhile, are one of British conservation’s most recognisable success stories.

The elegant black-and-white waders, known for their distinctive upturned bills, disappeared as a breeding species in Britain before recolonising Suffolk in 1947.

They have since spread into other parts of the country with the help of protected wetland habitats, although the RSPB says their continued success remains dependent on careful management.

The species is currently Amber-listed rather than Red-listed and famously appears on the RSPB’s logo.


Black-tailed godwits are larger waders, distinguished by their long legs and straight bills.

During the breeding season they develop striking orange-brown colouring, while birds seen later in the year tend to have more subdued grey-brown plumage.

Their Red-list status means they are among the UK birds considered to be of greatest conservation concern.

The bird responsible for sending Pat’s gathering skywards also has a remarkable history.

Marsh harriers are large birds of prey usually seen quartering low over wetlands and reedbeds, holding their wings in a distinctive shallow V as they search for small birds, mammals and other prey.

The species suffered severe historical declines in Britain before recovering, and the RSPB says its future in the UK is now more secure than at any point during the last century.

It remains Amber-listed and is protected as a Schedule 1 species under the Wildlife and Countryside Act.

A marsh harrier passing overhead can cause an immediate reaction among wetland birds, which may take flight together when a potential predator approaches.

Interestingly, a remarkably similar scene has been recorded around Teesmouth before.

An RSPB Durham Local Group report from 2016 described a marsh harrier putting up a group of teal during a visit to the Saltholme area.

RSPB Saltholme, near Stockton, is particularly well suited to such encounters.

Its extensive wetlands support a wide variety of wildlife, with the reserve itself highlighting avocets among the species visitors can encounter there.

For Pat, however, the visit provided something more memorable than simply ticking individual species off a list.

In a matter of moments, avocets, threatened black-tailed godwits and teal were all airborne together as one of Britain’s recovering birds of prey swept across the reserve – offering a striking snapshot of the wildlife now found on Teesside.
