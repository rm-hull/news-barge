## YouTube's new "exposure-based model" counts a view from the first frame, across shorts, long-form videos, podcasts and livestreams, inflating creators' headline numbers without changing how much they actually get paid.

YouTube is about to make every video on the platform look far more popular overnight, and it will not cost the company a cent.

The platform is shifting to what it calls an "exposure-based model" for views, applied across every format, including shorts, long-form videos, podcasts and live streams.

Under the new system, a "view" counts the moment the first frame plays, even if someone is just hovering over a thumbnail.

The upshot is simple — the big number under every video is about to go up, probably fast, and it will be counted the same way everywhere on the platform.

The change kicks in for any video uploaded after 24 August, which will show the new first-frame count from the outset.

Videos already on the platform keep their existing tally until then, after which future views get counted under the new rules.

YouTube frames this as a tidying-up exercise, more "consistency" across the platform, no content slipping through uncounted and less confusion about what a view actually represents.

But there is a useful precedent for what "consistency" actually means in practice.

YouTube already ran this experiment when it switched its short videos over to first-frame counting back in March last year, and the results were immediate: view counts on Shorts jumped by roughly 70%, according to YouTube's own figures. Seventeen months later, the company is rolling the same trick out everywhere else.

It is also just catching up. Instagram and TikTok have counted views this way for years, and creators who post across all three platforms have long watched their YouTube numbers look comparatively anemic by contrast.

This closes that gap, on paper if nothing else.

Here is the catch: none of it will put an extra cent in creators' pockets, since YouTube is not changing how it calculates ad payouts. It will keep a separate metric, now branded as "engaged views" or someone watching for several seconds, the old standard, specifically for that purpose.

Inflated headline numbers will largely be show while the money still flows according to the old method.

YouTube says the shift came from creator feedback that the tangle of different view metrics made it hard to gauge true exposure.

That claim is plausible, though the timing also works in YouTube's favour.

Most creators do not primarily earn through YouTube ad revenue: according to marketing platform IdeaEquity, up to 70% of creator income comes instead from brand deals and sponsorships, where view counts function as a bargaining chip rather than a direct source of payment.

Which is the real function of this change.

Bigger numbers give creators more leverage to walk into brand negotiations, but none of that leverage comes out of YouTube's pocket.

The platform gets to hand out a popularity boost that costs it nothing and hands the bill for actually paying creators more, if there is one, to advertisers and brands instead.
