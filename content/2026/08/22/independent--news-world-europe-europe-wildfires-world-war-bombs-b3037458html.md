Scorched landscapes across Europe have exposed a potentially lethal threat lurking underground.

Rusted bombs and mines from the First and Second World Wars are now endangering emergency crews responding to the wildfires across the continent.

High heat has caused several weapons to explode in France, Belgium, Germany and the Netherlands. In the Dutch province of Limburg, a bomb-disposal team was called out to inspect woodland near the site of heavy 1944 combat threatened by flames.

Exacerbated by climate change, the blazes and detonations show that Europe’s "climatic luck" is ending, said Sarah Njeri, a peace and conflict scholar at London’s School of Oriental and African Studies. Consistent temperatures and predictable rain previously kept hidden weapons dormant.

"Climate change is now doing the work of unburying and reactivating what was previously stable," Ms Njeri said. Blazes can ignite vintage munitions and clear away the dense brush concealing them.

Heavy rainfall in January spurred vegetation growth that turned to kindling when summer heat arrived. Coupled with drought, low humidity and stiff winds, the temperatures created optimal conditions for fire, according to a World Weather Attribution study published in July.

![Members of a bomb disposal unit, Dieter Schwaetzler, left, and Rene Bennert sit next to an 1.8-tonne Second World War bomb after defusing it in Frankfurt, Germany, in 2017](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/08/22/07/Europe_Wildfire_Ordnance_1163_.jpg)

As they advanced, the flames moved over territory that has long been known to hold unexploded weapons, a lethal legacy that lingered long after the guns of the World Wars fell silent.

In a forest in tinder-dry France, a massive wildfire outside the southwestern city of Bordeaux uncovered a cache of what is believed to be Second World War-era ammunition.

In the eastern Belgian province of Liege, Governor Hervé Jamar said that “a few sporadic detonations" were recently heard and that emergency services were adapting to account for munitions buried in fire areas. He said that no fire crews were in danger, but local media published photos of a firefighter handling a rusted bomb.

During the Second World War, booby traps, bombs and mines were sprinkled throughout the province's dense peat-bog forest, which is known as the High Fens.

In the first four years after Germany’s surrender in 1945, the munitions killed six civilians, said Bernard Collard, a local historian who runs the Truschbaum Museum near Belgium's border with Germany that houses a collection of found objects from the war.

![Bernard Collard runs a metal detector over a field in the Elsenborn area of Krinkelt, Belgium](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/08/22/07/Europe_Wildfire_Ordnance_35895.jpg)

“When you go into a forest like this, you are not sure where the explosives are,” he said.

The fire in the High Fens, which stretches across the border with Germany, is smouldering atop a much-bloodied battlefield where Nazis fought Allied troops in the famed Battle of the Bulge.

The fire is even closer to the sites of two lesser-known skirmishes along Elsenborn Ridge and in the Hurtgen Forest, which lasted 88 days and saw about 33,000 American and 28,000 German troops killed.

## ‘It’s part of our history’

Mr Collard is authorised to scan the former battlefields, which are now forests, pastures and farmland, with a metal detector. He has found mortar shells, machine guns, helmets, cigarette cases, and once, a human skeleton.

“You never know what you might come across, and you shouldn’t take it lightly,” he said while standing in a field that was once a blood-soaked site. Nearby, blueberry bushes covered old foxholes, trenches and deep craters from artillery fire.

Mr Collard frequently works with the Belgian de-mining squad. “It’s part of our history. We live with it,” he said.

Unexploded ordnance is even more dangerous around wildfires because heat can trigger the explosives, said Jonas Tepe, a spokesperson for North Rhine-Westphalia, just across the border in Germany.

![Smoke rises from a field scorched by a wildfire near Waimes, Belgium](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/08/22/07/Europe_Wildfire_Ordnance_88897.jpg)

The Belgian Ministry of Defence said it receives requests from the region about every week to assist in disarming explosives, yet it does not consider the area a “hot spot” compared to elsewhere in Belgium. Such requests are even more common in the west, where farmers in towns such as Ypres, which is infamous for harrowing trench warfare in the Second World War, call the annual tilling of the soil “the iron harvest” because it turns up so many war remnants.

Every year across the nation, Belgium’s Explosive Ordnance Disposal and Destruction Service responds to more than 3,000 calls and disposes of 200 to 250 tons of munitions left over from both world wars.

## Drought also poses a threat

Drought has also helped to lay the past bare by shrinking rivers and lakes to reveal old weapons.

In Slovakia, the Danube River fell and exposed two naval mines that police pulled out of the water and buried in a trench for a controlled demolition. Authorities in Hungary closed Budapest's Margaret Bridge after a bomb dating from the Second World War emerged at its base in receding water.

As the Rhine dried up, German authorities also found a Second World War-era bomb.

Christina Greene, a researcher at the University of Arizona who, with Ms Njeri, wrote an article on Europe’s wildfires and unexploded ordnance risk, said “the legacy of war keeps creeping up” because of climate change.

“It's reliving the legacies of war and conflict in a way that we haven't had to deal with in a while in certain places."
