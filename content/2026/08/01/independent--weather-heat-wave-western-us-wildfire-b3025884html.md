A dangerous heat wave is gripping parts of the Western U.S. this weekend, threatening to shatter temperature records and significantly escalating wildfire risks across already parched or burning regions.

A formidable ridge of high pressure, commonly known as a heat dome, is trapping intense heat close to the ground from Arizona to Montana, though meteorologists anticipate it will be relatively short-lived.

Forecasts from the National Weather Service on Saturday predicted Las Vegas would hit a scorching 115 degrees Fahrenheit (46 degrees Celsius), marking its hottest day of the year.

Phoenix is expected to reach 116F (47C), while El Centro, California, could see a record-breaking 118F (48C).

Even parts of Montana are bracing for extreme heat, with Great Falls forecast to reach a record 104F (40C).

In Las Vegas, Saturday's projected high was 10 degrees above normal, with dangerous conditions expected to persist through Sunday, when temperatures are forecast to remain at 114F, according to meteorologist Morgan Stessman.

The severity of the situation is compounded by overnight lows remaining in the upper 80s to low 90s, preventing bodies from recovering.

"There's just no relief overnight," Stessman warned, urging residents to stay indoors and hydrated. While the August record for Las Vegas stands at 116F (47C), the all-time high of 120F (49C) was set in July 2024, which also marked the hottest summer on record.

In Montana, the entire eastern side of the state was under an extreme heat warning Saturday, with Helena expected to match or surpass its record of 102F (39C).

This current heat wave, however, is not as severe as the mid-July event that saw some areas, like Billings, break all-time records at 111F (44C).

Forecasters are particularly concerned that this heat wave, combined with the drying effects of previous high temperatures, will exacerbate wildfire risks across the West.

In Montana, where fires are already active and high winds are predicted, "We’re definitely worried about that," said meteorologist Jeff Kitsmiller, noting that most of the state was under a red flag warning.

Nevada is also experiencing heightened risk, with the heat dome causing relative humidity to plummet to single digits in many areas.

The U.S. Forest Service has issued an enhanced wildfire risk warning through Sunday for most of Southern California, excluding the coastline.

Beyond immediate weather patterns, climate change, driven by the burning of coal, oil, and natural gas, is contributing to more frequent, intense, and prolonged heat waves.

These conditions, in turn, lead to more severe droughts and fuel larger, more destructive wildfires. This year's weather is also significantly influenced by El Nino, a natural warming of the equatorial Pacific that alters global weather patterns and spikes temperatures.

Experts predict the current El Nino could be among the most intense since tracking began in 1950, with United Nations officials warning it has already broken early-stage intensity records.
