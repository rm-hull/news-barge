Reflecting pool damage was caused by 'botched' renovation, not vandalism, prosecutors say as they ask to drop charges

In a court filing on Friday, the office of the US attorney in Washington DC, Jeanine Pirro, moved to dismiss charges against DavidHearn, a former Olympian her office had accused on vandalizing the Lincoln Memorial reflecting pool.

In asking to dismiss the criminal indictment, Pirro’s office told the DC Superior Court that “it was not until after the return of the indictment,” that the Department of the Interior “provided additional documents” to prosecutors “indicating that damage to the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool in June 2026 was the result of flawed installation by the contractor, Atlantic Industrial Coatings (“AIC”), and the rush to complete the project prior to events associated with the America 250 celebration in the weeks surrounding Independence Day 2026.”

“It was not until after these documents were produced,” Pirro’s office added, that her office “first became aware of information showing that the damage was the result of a botched installation and not vandalism as initially represented by DOI.”

Former Olympic canoeist David Hearn, second from left, listened as his lawyer, Norm Eisen, spoke after he pleaded not guilty on 9 July to allegedly damaging the Lincoln Memorial reflecting pool. Photograph: Rod Lamkey/AP

“The Trump administration’s case against Davey Hearn should never have been brought,” Hearn’s lawyers, Norm Eisen, Mary Dohrmann and Steve Levin said in a statement. “Its dismissal today does not erase the abuse of government power in arresting and charging a patriotic American who did nothing wrong. The government’s approach was ready, fire, aim. The administration owes Mr. Hearn an apology.”

The government’s request to dismiss the case against Hearn said that when Pirro inspected the pool on 17 July, “as a defense expert inspected the now-drained Reflecting Pool” that she “immediately noted extensive damage throughout” the pool and requested “all records regarding the installation of the lining.”

“These documents indicated a rushed and flawed installation process, with repeated failures of the lining during the installation process, and extensive peeling of the lining throughout the Reflecting Pool,” the filing added.

Trump sows fear about migration by pointing to video from Europe, again

In a social media post sent from Air Force One on Friday afternoon, Donald Trump drew attention to video of tens of thousands of migrants entering the Spanish enclave of Ceuta from Morocco the day before.

“What’s happening with Spain,” Trump wrote, characterizing the arrival of migrants in the enclave on the North African coast that has been a Spanish possession since 1580 as an invasion, “happened in the United States during the Sleepy Joe Biden Administration, and it will happen again, only worse, if the Dumocrats ever again gain power.”

Trump illustrated his warning about the US-Mexico border with video of migrants crossing the border from Morocco into the Spanish territory that Morocco claims as its own, and calls “occupied”.

Migrants tried to cross from Morocco into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta on Thursday. Photograph: Antonio Sempere/AP

This is not the first time that Trump has sought to sow fear about the US southern border by using video of migrants in Europe.

In January 2016, Trump released an ad in support of his first presidential campaign that featured images of migrants surging across a border fence as a narrator promised that Trump would “stop illegal immigration by building a wall on our southern border that Mexico will pay for”. That video, however, was not recorded on the US-Mexico border, but in another Spanish enclave on the Moroccan coast, Melilla, in 2014.

Trump’s closing ad for that campaign, released in November 2016 also featured footage of migrants in Europe, passed off as video of immigrants streaming across the border from Mexico into the United States.

Near the start of that ad, as the candidate could be heard complaining of “massive illegal immigration,” thousands of people were shown walking along a highway.

As I reported in 2016, however, that video was not shot along the southern border of the US — where Trump had promised to build a great wall — but in Hungary, at the height of the 2105 migrant crisis, and it showed Syrian refugees, desperate for safe passage to Germany, marching out of Budapest.

Several hundred refugees from Syria left Budapest, Hungary in September 2015 after deciding not to wait for trains to Germany and instead set out on foot for Austria. Photograph: Antonio Olmos/The Observer

That video was lifted, without permission, from a social media account of Nabih Bulos, a foreign correspondent who had accompanied the refugees.

“When this footage was taken, thousands of refugees were on an odyssey through the Balkan corridor and Europe to escape the cataclysm ripping their country apart,” Bulos told me at the time. “As a son of two Palestinian refugees who benefited from Jordan’s largesse, a naturalized American welcomed to the country even after 9/11, as well as a working conflict journalist, the last thing I would want this footage to be used for is to embody Trump’s xenophobic, repugnant message.”

Earlier this year, the Republican-led House appropriations committee referred to the dispute over the two enclaves in a report, in which it noted “that the Spanish-administered cities of Ceuta and Melilla are located in Moroccan territory and remain the subject of Morocco’s longstanding claim.”

The committee added that it “supports efforts by the Secretary of State to encourage diplomatic engagement between Morocco and Spain on the future status of Ceuta and Melilla.”

The US government released its anxiously awaited plan for how to manage the Colorado river crisis on Friday, outlining cuts in water supplied to California, Arizona and Nevada.

The proposal from the US Bureau of Reclamation spares Colorado, Utah, New Mexico and Wyoming from facing mandatory cuts, for now. But it is a temporary fix as the states that rely on the river continue to fight over a long-term management plan for the dwindling resource that supplies huge swaths of the western US.

The proposal is the federal government’s answer to a years-long stalemate between the seven states responsible for forming a plan on how to distribute the deep cuts needed to pull the system back from the brink. It comes just months before the framework that currently governs the essential and imperiled waterway is set to expire.

“This framework provides the flexibility to respond to changing hydrologic conditions while preserving the opportunity for the Basin States to continue working toward durable, consensus-based solutions,” said Doug Burgum, the US interior secretary, in a press release.

Under the 10-year plan, the Lower Basin states could face deep cuts of up to 3m acre-feet (3.7bn kiloliters) a year – up to 40% of their collective total – “subject to hydrology”, according to the press release by the Department of the Interior. That’s about enough water annually to serve more than 25 million people.

The plan would also allow annual releases of between 5m and 12m acre-feet from Lake Powell, the basin’s second-largest reservoir. Under the 10-year federal framework, water management decisions will be made every two years.

The federal government is expected to release a record of decision in the coming week that will detail operations under the plan in the short term, but it is likely they will closely follow a proposal issued by the lower basin states earlier this year that would shave 3.2m acre-feet from their combined total over 2027 and 2028.
