THE street-lamps of the ocean; and behold,

O’erhead the banners of the night unfold;

The day hath passed into the land of dreams.

O summer day beside the joyous sea!

O summer day so wonderful and white,

So full of gladness and so full of pain!

Forever and forever shalt thou be

To some the gravestone of a dead delight,

To some the landmark of a new domain.

In his 1874 poem A Summer’s Day by the Sea, Henry Wadsworth Longfellow calls the summer day “so full of gladness and so full of pain”. This means that experiencing something wonderful also hurts because we know it cannot last. Beauty and loss come from the exact same place.

So we must enjoy this long dry summer we have been having, as like all things it will pass into memories before we know it.

Watering has been a task in the heat, day after day and sometimes twice a day. The result of frequent watering results in the leaching out of nutrients, particularly those that are potted and in containers. The result will poorer growth. So we have to feed more when water more. This can be in the form of a dry general fertiliser such as Growmore 7-7-7, a highly effective general purpose NPK granular fertiliser designed to promote strong, healthy plants, trees and vegetables.

Or, as I favour, Phostrogen All Purpose Plant Food is a water-soluble fertilizer used for lawns, seedlings, houseplants, fruits, and vegetables. It can be scattered evenly over the soil at a rate of 70g per square metre (2oz per square yard).

I put a few pinches around the plants in my window boxes. Don’t forget to keep up with dead heading.

Feeding is key in the greenhouse especially cucumbers who are ‘gross’ feeders. Tomatoes should be well set with fruit and good high phosphate tomato feed will encourage fruit growth.

Problems such as blossom end rot and splitting of the fruit are brought on by intermittent watering - so don’t let them go dry at the roots. Shading helps, as I said last month it keeps the temperature down during spells of hot weather.

Shade can be in the form of a liquid mixed and applied with a brush or with a green fine mesh put over the glass as I do.

Intermittent watering is another issue that can be avoided by not letting the plants dry out. If they do go dry don’t give them a large amount of water all at once - give a little and then give more later. This will prevent the skins from splitting, which is another common problem.

Now is the time to propagate shrubs with semi-ripe cuttings taken in August and September from new growth that has developed over the spring and summer and just started to harden up (but not yet turned woody).

Shrubs such as Berberis, Camellia, Ceanothus, Erica, Hebe, Viburnum and many other types can be taken from growth 2in to 3in long (5-7cm) and inserted into a mix of 50/50 multi-purpose and horticultural river sand and placed in a cold frame to root. With the prices of even small specimens by the big garden centres rising ever year it now pays to root cuttings.

Bradford is now ahead of the game in horticulture, with its spend of 3.5m in the city centre and 300k each year it is making the city centre better and better. And the winning of the Pineapples Award (awarded to Bradford for the city centre transformation) shows that our parks matter. However I would like to see more colour, as at Lister Park, in the city centre itself.

Talking of the value of colour, the city of Bradford can be proud to be the first place in the UK to be hosting the International Flower Carpet which has its premier in Centenary Square by City Hall from September 24-27.

Such great events have taken place in continental Europe, but not in the UK.

Local artist Shiraaz Ali is the man creating the flower carpet which is to use 300,000 Dahlia flower heads to make the work of art. I have always regarded gardeners as artists in their own right; as in their work they have always created great beauty, when given the chance.

I recall in 2014 Huddersfield’s Bradley Nurseries team when the Tour de France passed through. The team put on a French Garden in St George’s Square in Huddersfield right by the railway station. It featured 1,500 sunflowers, 1,700 lettuces, 700kg of vegetables, and live farm animals, including a Jersey cow named Bridget, as well as sheep, pigs, and chickens, on loan from Cannon Hall Farm. A fine example of gardeners’ art.

The flower carpet in Bradford will be talking aspects of Bradford’s industrial and textile story, and it will be over 31 yards wide, hexagonal in shape and will involve 40 people in creation. It will be lit up at night with sound to form a magical atmosphere.

The use of sounds has been used in Singapore’s vertical gardens covered in unique flora. They host a free, daily light and sound show called Garden Rhapsody - but this will be the first time the use of sound as being used in this way in the UK. Another first for Bradford.

We can all eagerly await for this wonderful creation - one not to be missed.
