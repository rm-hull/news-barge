# Keith Short: Meet the fascinating Sheffield 'Irish Triangle' artist you may have seen doodling in pubs like The Grapes

## Keith Short can be found with a pint and a sketchbook in the corner of The Grapes – and his pictures on the walls. Christopher Hallam meets him.

A pint of “sump oil” (read: Guinness) on the table, quietly sketching in the corner - the unassuming nature of Keith Short may mean some folk don’t notice him at all.

Over the years though, his sketches and paintings have adorned the walls of some of Sheffield’s favourite watering holes, and they certainly do catch the eye.

The Dog & Partridge, Fagan’s and The Grapes have all proudly displayed Keith’s art, although the Dog & Partridge is the only one without one on display at the moment.

Former landlady Ann Flynn took Keith’s work with her when she left the Dog & Partridge after 40 years to focus on her other pub The Grapes on Trippet Lane – one that is forever intertwined with the Arctic Monkeys legacy and the city’s musical story.

Originally from Cork, Ireland, she moved to Sheffield in 1954 when her family moved to the city when her father was seeking work.

Working in pubs from the age of 18, she took over the Dog and Partridge in 1970. From 2001 until 2010, she ran both the Dog and Partridge and The Grapes, both on Trippet Lane, until leaving the Dog and Partridge in 2010.

The Grapes – which is said to sell more Guinness than any other pub in Yorkshire - is now immortalised in history for hosting the band’s first ever gig on June 13, 2003, when its members were just 16-years-old – though the room which was once used for gigs was converted to living accommodation years ago.

It is location of our catch up with Keith and when I arrive he is sitting in the corner scratching away on his current sketch.

It turns out to be one of Arctic Monkeys frontman Alex Turner, which is quite apt considering where we are.

The interior of The Grapes, a much-loved Sheffield institution, is decidedly old-school. Aged and worn but packed with soul, a warmth and an endearing aura.

What led to Keith drawing in these pubs?

He said: “I got into this by accident. It’s just a follow on from what I was doing earlier, sitting in quiet and then being immersed in what was happening around me.

“I got drawn into the singing and the music of it all. The pictures came later.”

Just over three decades ago, Keith was impacted by a traumatic experience in such a way that he became a recluse, avoiding social interaction.

It was through the urging of his wife – a loving marriage of 57 years – that he began to get out again.

He would find a quiet corner of the Dog & Partridge, where he would sup a pint and quietly sketch.

Keith believes the community helped relieve his sorrow and ‘drew’ him out of it.

“I’ve tended to hide away, I go in a little corner, get my little pad out and draw a little picture,” he said.

"But I love these people. This area, the triangle, there’s a culture there with the people. Loads of musicians, it’s almost embryonic this area. It’s sort of where they first start doing what they do and doing it well.”

One portrait in the pub is of John Dowling, a man who meant a lot to Keith.

“I wouldn’t have sung in public if it wasn’t for John,” Keith says with a forlorn sigh.

Also known as ‘Big Jack’, John was an Irish and Sheffield legend who had an amazing career as an ultra-distance walker and holds the record as theonly person to have walked both the length of Ireland and from John O’Groats to Land’s End.

Brilliant though he was for his athletic endeavours, John unlocked something in Keith.

“God bless him. He got me singing,” said Keith.

Keith was speaking while looking at one of his pictures on the wall. It depicts a man, worn and depleted, a deep loss etched into his face. The eyes are orbs of perpetual sadness. It is John.

It was sketched after John’s daughter Theresa had died following a cancer battle. Keith managed to capture the emotional toll on a man who in that moment was broken.

Now John is remembered with reverence and almost a tear.

“Each one of these people is expressing themselves, in their way,” says Keith about the people who have been the subjects of his drawings in the pub.

He tells the story of a woman who came in with her husband, who is battling dementia.

Keith’s pictures helped elicit a recollection of memories from him long thought lost. “I didn’t realise that other people would get something out of this,” Keith says.

After his own pain and struggles, Keith has been enriched by the people in the Irish community.

He feels a sense of belonging in this group, like he has found his place, singing in unison with spiritual sisters and brothers.

“In a way, I’m trying to tell the world that I’m still worth something.

“I come in here, have a few jars and then I can sing.

“Then when I sing, I say to the world ‘I am worth something’. That’s what I’m doing with the pictures.”

His art is now part of the fabric and soul of The Grapes and Fagan’s.

“To me, these people are giants,” he replies.

“They don’t just let me walk amongst them, they welcome me amongst them and that’s fabulous.”

You can see Keith Short’s pictures and art for yourself at The Grapes on Trippet Lane and at Fagan’s on Tenter Street.
