Foreign affairs minister Penny Wong has confirmed the number of Australians missing in the devastating Nepal floods has increased overnight from 35 to 39.

In a statement, Wong said the government was “rapidly mobilising consular and humanitarian support in response to the catastrophic floods along the Nepal-China border, including through a $5 million assistance package for affected communities.”

We understand at least 39 Australians are among those reported missing. Australian officials are working urgently to confirm their welfare and provide assistance to those affected.

She said the government would send an initial team of three humanitarian response specialists “within the next 48 hours'” to help Australian staff already on the ground.

The extraordinary scale of this disaster and the remoteness of the affected region mean immense challenges lie ahead for the response and recovery.

Coalition fully supports government’s support for Nepali flood response

Ted O’Brien, the shadow minister for foreign affairs, said the Coalition fully supports the government’s support of Nepal after the massive flooding in which 39 Australians are missing.

O’Brien spoke to RN Breakfast this morning, saying he supported the level of assistance given so far:

The government does have the opposition’s support and I believe it is responding in the right way. Of course, this is an absolute tragedy. And first and foremost, on behalf of the Coalition, I just want to express my absolute devastation and to send a message of love and thoughts to all Australians of Nepalese or Tibetan heritage and any Australians who still have family members and loved ones missing.

The government is in the driver’s seat. Of course, this is a very difficult set of circumstances and information is coming in very quickly … No doubt there will be further information as time goes on. But the commitment already provided through various agencies, the Coalition supports.

NSW begins H5 bird flu vaccination program for vulnerable species

NSW has begun a vaccination program for vulnerable species of birds to protect them against H5 bird flu.

The state said a mixture of populations in captivity and wild populations will be vaccinated, including little penguins in Manly and on Barunguba Montague Island. Taronga Conservation Society has also given Carnaby’s black cockatoos and red-tailed black cockatoos the initial dose of the two-dose vaccine course.

Other wildlife facilities will also join the vaccination program to ensure as many species as possible can be covered.

The vaccine requires two doses, administered three to four weeks apart. The NSW premier, Chris Minns, said:

While no government can stop the virus from spreading, vaccination programs like this one rolling out at Taronga Zoo are part of our efforts to help protect some of Australia’s most beloved native birds.

Four additional missing Australians were travelling separately to tours: Wong

The foreign affairs minister, Penny Wong, says the four additional Australians confirmed missing overnight in the devastating Nepal floods were travelling separately to the tours.

Fifteen Australians on the Himalayan Glacier Adventure were reported missing, while Kathmandu Holiday Tours and Travels said one of their customers was Australian.

“We understand an additional four Australians who are unaccounted for were travelling separately to these tours,” she told ABC’s Radio National.

Wong said a further increase in the number of Australians missing was an “unfortunate possibility”.

“I know this is an extremely distressing time for those who have loved ones unaccounted for and we are thinking of them,” she said.

Wong said there were a total of 43 consular cases.

Voluntary assisted dying laws have been passed in the Northern Territory after debate over a gag clause barring doctors from raising end-of-life options with patients, Australian Associated Press reports.

The rights of the terminally ill bill passed on a conscience vote in the NT parliament late last night, with the gag intact.

The territory became the first Australian jurisdiction to make voluntary euthanasia legal in 1995, but the laws were overturned in 1997 by the federal government under John Howard.

All six states and the ACT have since passed their own laws allowing it.

The federal veto was only lifted in 2022, restoring the NT’s right to make its own assisted dying laws.

The NT bill requires that only people predicted by doctors to have 12 months or less to live would have access to assisted dying.

In the NT parliament yesterday government backbencher Tanzil Rahman spoke against the gag clause that would bar healthcare professionals from initiating conversations about end-of-life options with patients.

Elderly patients could be bedridden and dying in an aged-care home in a one-clinic town and it would not be compassionate to prevent them talking to a doctor about end-of-life options, he told parliament on Thursday.

“If the facility says no, that’s it,” Rahman said.

“This cannot be a bill for the rights of the terminally ill if we restrict conversations in these premises.”

The opposition leader, Selena Uibo, also argued against residents of aged care institutions being denied information, saying they must be allowed to make informed choices about end-of-life options.

Nepalese community grieves after flooding disaster

The small Himalayan town Keshav Kadel migrated from was not the first to be hit by the floods ravaging his native Nepal, Australian Associated Press reports.

Sitting in the Chitwan district, it is further along the Trishuli River than those more directly in the firing line, such as Gorkha and Rasuwa.

Keshav Kadel , centre, with other members of the Nepali community in Melbourne. Photograph: Jesse Thompson/AAP

The distance afforded residents more time to flee to higher ground, but it has not been spared the dead.

“What’s happening in my town is that bodies are now flowing in the river,” he told AAP.

“They have slid all the way down and people are pulling bodies from the river.”

At least 360 people have died and hundreds more remain unaccounted for, including 39 Australians, since they began on Wednesday.

Prayers for those affected by the disaster in Nepal at Diggers Rest. Photograph: Jesse Thompson/AAP

Kadel was among members of Nepalese community who gathered yesterday at the Australian Nepalese Multicultural Centre at Diggers Rest north-west of Melbourne, which he serves as vice president.

Families gathered for prayers and held a minute’s silence for those lost to the floods, laying candles beside a hand-crafted display of dyed rice reading “pray for Nepal”.

The diaspora was “united in grief, hope and prayer,” secretary of the centre Sudhir Shakya told the event.

“Behind every number is a person – a mother, a father, a son, a daughter, a relative, a friend, and someone deeply loved,” he said.
