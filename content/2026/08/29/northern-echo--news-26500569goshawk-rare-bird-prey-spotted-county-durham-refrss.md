A County Durham woman was left stunned when one of Britain’s most elusive birds of prey suddenly appeared in her garden after swooping down to attack a pigeon.

Northern Echo Camera Club member Tina Taylor spotted the goshawk at her home in Spennymoor within the last month.

She captured the unusual visitor after it landed on her fence.

Goshawks are notoriously difficult birds to see and are normally associated with large areas of woodland rather than suburban gardens.

This makes the close encounter particularly unusual.

The RSPB describes goshawks as secretive woodland birds which are “very unlikely” to appear in gardens in the way their smaller relative, the sparrowhawk, frequently does.

  Goshawk in Spennymoor, County Durham 

*(Image: TINA TAYLOR/CAMERA CLUB)*

Tina’s visitor had apparently arrived dramatically.

It swooped into the garden in pursuit of a pigeon before settling on the fence.

The behaviour is typical of this powerful predator.

Goshawks hunt using speed, surprise and extraordinary agility.

They pursue prey including pigeons, crows, squirrels and rabbits and manoeuvre through woodland at high speed.

The Wildlife Trusts has nicknamed the species the “phantom of the forest” because of its secretive nature and ability to move rapidly among trees.

Adults are striking birds, with broad wings, a long rounded tail, powerful yellow legs and a prominent white stripe above the eye.

Females can reach around the size of a buzzard and are considerably larger than males.

Eye colour can range from yellow and orange to a deep red depending on the bird’s age and sex.

Seeing one at all would once have been impossible in Britain.

Goshawks were persecuted to extinction as a breeding species in the UK by the late 19th century, with deforestation and deliberate killing among the causes of their disappearance.

Their return began during the second half of the 20th century.

The modern population is thought to have developed largely from falconry birds which escaped or were deliberately released during the 1960s and 1970s.

Since then, the species has staged a substantial recovery.


The British Trust for Ornithology says goshawks have expanded their distribution dramatically over recent decades.

Its BirdFacts data gives a formal breeding estimate of 620 pairs for 2013 to 2017, while more recent evidence cited by the organisation suggests there could now be around 1,200 breeding pairs across the country.

Northern England is among the species’ UK strongholds, alongside Wales and parts of Scotland, although goshawks remain thinly distributed and can be exceptionally difficult to spot.

They have been seen in County Durham before, including in Weardale, where the mixture of mature woodland and more remote countryside offers the sort of habitat generally associated with the species.

A previous Northern Echo Camera Club photograph also captured a goshawk in the county in 2021.

Despite their recovery, the birds continue to receive legal protection.

Goshawks are a Schedule 1 species under wildlife legislation, giving their nests and breeding birds additional protection, while they are currently Green-listed under the UK's Birds of Conservation Concern assessment.

The RSPB says persecution and nest robbery nevertheless remain threats.

For people hoping to see one, late winter and spring traditionally offer the best opportunity, when goshawks can sometimes be watched performing spectacular aerial displays above woodland.

Tina did not need to venture into the forests of County Durham, however.

Instead, one of Britain's most secretive birds of prey brought the spectacle straight to her Spennymoor garden – with an unsuspecting pigeon apparently on the menu.
