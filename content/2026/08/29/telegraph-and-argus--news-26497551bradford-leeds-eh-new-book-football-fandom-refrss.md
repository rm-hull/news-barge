‘DO you wanna go to the pictures?’

‘Sorry Alison, I can’t, I’m playing football.’

May ’89, the Beautiful South released Song for Whoever. The lyrics mentioned my ex-girlfriend, Alison. But without knowing it, my first love wasn’t her; it was football.

Any chance we got to kick around outdoors, with whatever we had, like a half-blown-up Mitre, a tennis ball or even a ping pong ball, we took it. We’d spend afternoons in Ladyhill Park or on the road, ignoring any NO BALL GAMES signs, playing make-shift football until our Mums called us in for the hundredth time for our now-cold corned-beef hash tea. Even indoors, football followed us in, with tabletop Subbuteo or the classic Binatone plug-in TV console. For many of us back then, football was all that mattered.

From playing it to reading about it, magazines that won over school books included Shoot, Roy of the Rovers, and Match. Many of us collected Panini stickers, hoping to get our favourite players, including Keegan, Dalglish, Lineker, and Zico, to fill our albums. We’d swap any doubles we got with schoolmates or, like in my case, trade our way out of playground strife with our best stickers.

Then, of course, watching football was a must; we’d wait up late for Match of the Day on Saturday nights.

Glued to the 16-inch TV, which only had three channels, we’d watch long-haired players like George Best whose sideburns were longer than rulers, wearing team shirts that had no sponsors.

  George Best - impressive side burns 

*(Image: PA)*

Jimmy Hill, the presenter, helped change the game by championing three points for a win.

Growing up in the ’70s and ’80s, we didn’t notice the details; what mattered was that our team, whoever it was, had to win.

And then there was the banter that started in the school playground.

‘Who do you support?’

‘Liverpool, who else?’

‘Liverpool, never heard of them.’

One person who’d certainly heard of Liverpool was contributor and City fan Lee Marron, who witnessed the fourth-division Bantams beating the first-division Mighty Reds at Valley Parade in 1980.

  Jimmy Hill did so much for TV football 

*(Image: PA)*

‘In those days, the Kop was great. When you were allowed to stand, there weren’t many seats. It was a great night. What an atmosphere. The City fans were singing very loudly.

Ray Clemence was in net for Liverpool, and if I remember correctly, Bobby Campbell scored the winner near the end. Bradford won 1-0.’

Most will remember how they chose their team: their dad’s, their mates’, or whoever was hoovering up the trophies.

My old man, who shared my love of football, once said to me: ‘Lad, ye ’av to follow yer home team. Ye ’av to follow City.’

But when I was still undecided, this was easier said than done. My two best school mates were staunch Leeds fans.

‘Come on Crooty, are you our mate or what?’ they’d say, ‘You have to follow Leeds!’

That journey took some figuring out. Other fans, like contributor Paul Finney, followed their team from the get-go and stuck with them. Finney, a Leeds supporter, explains in the book that his hero was Vinnie Jones, who’d just signed for Leeds in 1989.

Finney’s dedication to the player and the team was shown by having a V shaved at the back of his head, just like his icon. He said: ‘Imagine how I felt when I saw him coming out of Freeman, Hardy & Willis on Briggate in Leeds.’

  Watching a match from the terraces 

*(Image: Steve Daniels)*

From a Leeds high street to a Bradford pizza restaurant on Upper Piccadilly, Finney wasn’t the only one to meet his footy idol. Another contributor, City fan Dave Burden, had an encounter in the gents of Mama Mia in the ’90s. There, by chance, Burden met the former City captain, Peter Jackson, and chatted to him for around half an hour. His family didn’t believe him when he returned and told them.

‘I think our Dave has had far too many shandies!’

He was trying to convince them otherwise when Jackson turned up at their table, leaving them as stunned as Burden had been earlier.

It was also former City captain Peter Jackson who wrote the foreword to the book. He tells readers that back then, players made time for fans.

‘Back in the day, the bond between players and supporters was different; with no mobile phones or selfies, just ordinary lads playing football. After games, we’d meet fans in pubs and socialise together. That camaraderie was something special.’

Noel Blake, who played for City and Leeds, wrote the afterword. He echoed the player-fan connection and spoke about giving spare tickets away to the teams’ supporters who would travel far from home to support them. ‘Leeds and Bradford supporters were quite open to come and speak to you, and in those days, we mixed with supporters. So you got to know people.’

Another football fan and contributor, Nicky D’Agostino, bumped into former City player and manager Stuart McCall at Hollins Hall Golf Club in Shipley. It left him with a long-lasting memory of a ‘decent football man who always had time for people’.

The football book is full of stories from the ’80s and ’90s, with a toe-poke back into the ’70s. It taps into nostalgia and includes plenty of photos. You’ll find lots of banter, from the home terraces to the away games. It swerves the bother and celebrates the beautiful game.

Whether it’s with schoolmates, workmates, or family members, there will always be rivalry. But growing up, I discovered that you could support different teams and still be mates.

So, with all this in mind, what else could I call the book but BRADFORD OR LEEDS, EH?: Valley Parade, Elland Road, and Back Again.

The book is available on Amazon and at other outlets.
