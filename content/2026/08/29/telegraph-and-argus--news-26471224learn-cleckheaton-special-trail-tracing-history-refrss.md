SPEN Valley covers an area in the centre of the West Yorkshire conurbation jokingly called Cleckheckmondsedge because of the quirky names of its villages and small towns.

One of these is Cleckheaton. It’s now a popular residential area with plenty of trees, but that’s not always been the case. Once upon a time Cleckheaton was a medieval village with tofts, crofts and strips of field surrounded by common land where residents grazed their animals. Around 200 years ago, the last traces of open-field farming were cleared away. Cleckheaton became a smoky Victorian mill town with a railway, grandiose Non-Conformist chapels, enormous spinning mills with tall chimneys, and sophisticated textile engineering, to the extent that it was the world ‘capital’ of card clothing manufacture. In the 20th and 21st centuries it’s transformed itself again.

On the way, Cleckheaton has lost many interesting, substantial buildings.

Spen Valley Civic Society decided to expand and upgrade a 1990s pamphlet about the town and asked its author Cleckheaton-born local historian Dr Gillian Cookson for help. She kindly agreed, and the 24-page booklet Old Cleckheaton has been published by Spen Valley Civic Society.

With several maps and numerous photos, it takes you on a tour through Cleckheaton’s history - it allows you to walk around the town centre and understand how streets and buildings developed as manufacturing, engineering and the textile industries changed.

The book includes the following historical aspects of the town:

*Cleckheaton Spenborough Town Hall

Elymas Wadsworth came to Cleckheaton in 1860 and started a worsted spinning company. As the business grew he moved to the gigantic Broomfield Mills at Moorbottom and soon took over the entire complex, employing more than 600 people. He involved himself in the town’s life and was Cleckheaton’s first representative when the new West Riding County Council was created in 1889.

The need for a civic building big enough to cater for large meetings and public functions became pressing in 1885 when the Redistribution of Seats Act made Cleckheaton the electoral centre of the new Spen Valley Parliamentary Division of the West Riding.

Elymas was elected Chairman of the Building Committee. Public subscriptions poured in and a design by architects Mawson and Hudson of Bradford was adopted and built.

Sadly, Elymas Wadsworth never lived to see it completed. In spring 1891 an epidemic of Russian Influenza swept the country. Elymas was one of 101 victims in Spen Valley in one week ending May 22, 1891.

He was buried in Cleckheaton’s Old Cemetery after a funeral attended by hundreds. At the opening ceremony of Cleckheaton Town Hall in February 1892 a brass plaque was unveiled in his name. His family donated the town hall’s fine clock with its three faces and distinctive chimes, still helping residents to keep track of time. At the top of the clocktower is a copper cupola, with a weather vane featuring a cheeky fox.

*Memorial Park

The site of Cleckheaton Memorial Park was the original village green. It’s now a pleasant green space surrounded by 21st century traffic.

In its centre is the war memorial. Designed by artist H Derwent Wood, it was sculpted by Sir George Frampton. Two figures are seated beside the main column: at the front is a male figure called Valour Triumphant, and at the rear a female figure called Burden of Grief.

The sculpture’s surrounding memorial walls bear the names of 469 Cleckheaton men and one woman who died during the First World War. The woman was Lizzie Jowett, a nurse in the Voluntary Aid Detachment who nursed sick and wounded soldiers for three years before she died in November 1918.

In 1952 a further 221 names were added for those who died during the Second World War. In 2000, 35 names were added and the memorial re-dedicated in June 2009.

Notable Cleckheaton characters from the past include:

*George Anderton

George Anderton was an enlightened entrepreneur and important figure in Cleckheaton’s development. He started as a weaver, but by 1825 he had patented his own design for a special combing machine for worsted fibres. He built a factory on Bradford Road, Cleckheaton, in 1835, re-named Victoria Mills in 1837 to honour Queen Victoria’s coronation.The company produced worsted yarns from high quality long fibres, used in carpets, upholstery, and worsted coatings.

George formed a Local Board, the forerunner of a local council, which brought piped water and gas to Cleckheaton for the first time. He pioneered Cleckheaton’s first British School in 1836.

In 1837 he founded Spen Valley’s first Mechanics Institute, which provided education to men, and later became the Technical Institute, the forerunner of Whitcliffe Mount Secondary School.

George was instrumental in getting the railway to come through Cleckheaton. His family invested £4,260 in the Lancashire & Yorkshire Railway in 1840, the equivalent of £500,000 today. The Manchester to Bradford route opened in 1848 with a new station in Cleckheaton.

*Obadiah Brooke

Obadiah Brooke founded his Cleckheaton tannery in 1712 and handed the business on to subsequent generations of Brookes.

By 1795 the family had bought a large area of land and named their works Crow Trees. It developed to include the Brooke family’s large house, the tannery and a gatehouse.

The company produced leathers for saddlery, shoes and industry.

Brooke’s Tannery appears to have ceased trading between 1901 and 1908. All that remains of the firm’s premises is the gatehouse, which from 1904 to 1908 was used by two budding motorcycle manufacturers called Joah Phelon and Richard Moore.

*Phelon and Moore

Joah Phelon was a Cleckheaton wire drawing gauge and die maker and a keen cyclist. He designed and built a motor car in 1896, but it crashed on its maiden run, so he turned his skills to developing a motor cycle from a workshop on Heaton Street Cleckheaton.

In 1904 he went into partnership with Richard Moore, a mill engineer. Phelon and Moore Ltd moved to a small factory at the junction of Bradford Road and Valley Road, Cleckheaton, formerly Obadiah Brooke’s gatehouse. Their designs were reliable and well-engineered, and in 1910 they moved to larger premises at Horncastle Mills.

Phelon & Moore were the sole suppliers of motorbikes to the RAF and its predecessor the Royal Flying Corps during the First World War, and also supplied the British Army, the Russian Army and other overseas forces.

In 1923 the Cleckheaton Cat was born when Phelon & Moore launched their famous Panther model. Its success at the Isle of Man TT races and other competitive trials enhanced the Panther brand, which thrived even in the Depression. The lightweight 250cc Red Panther model was ‘the sensation of 1933’, costing just £29 17s 6d .It could be afforded by working people, hence it provided freedom and reliable transport. With a sidecar, it offered families the opportunity for days out and holidays.

By the 1960s the Mini car and Japanese motorbikes led to the company’s decline and production ceased in 1966. But Panthers live on, lovingly maintained and restored, and the Panther Owners Club pantherownersclub.com has an international membership.
