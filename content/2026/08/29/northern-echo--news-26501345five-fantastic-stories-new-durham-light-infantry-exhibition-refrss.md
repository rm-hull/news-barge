LIGHT was cast last weekend on what would be in Durham’s new gallery and exhibition space when The Light opened to the public.

It is in the former Durham Light Infantry Museum which controversially closed in 2016 but has now been reborn with a stunning display of light installations in the top floor gallery and a permanent exhibition on the first floor showing off some of the 15,000 items in the DLI collection now housed at The Story.

  The Light, Durham Picture: SARAH CALDECOTT 

*(Image: Sarah Caldecott)*

Since the DLI was formed in 1758, it has been involved in practically every major conflict except Waterloo and Gallipoli, so its members have had a ringside seat for more than 250 years of world history.

Yet its global stories are of a local nature because so many of its men were recruited from the towns and villages of the county.

Rather than being about battles and campaigns, the new exhibition sets out to tell the personal experiences of those men.

  Gillian Kirkbride, Durham County Council’s heritage venues and collection manager 

*(Image: Sarah Caldecott)*

"This exhibition is trying to give you a little break in that idea of the idealised soldier who is just in a trench in the First World War wearing khaki," says Gillian Kirkbride, Durham County Council’s heritage venues and collection manager, who has the DLI badge dangling from her ears.

“Actually, they’re so much more than that – they’re artists, musicians, astronomers who even discovered the planet Uranus,”

“And there’s an awful lot of brotherhood and camaraderie, an awful lot of light to counteract the dark which is what we’re trying to get through in this exhibition.”

  Gillian Kirkbride, Durham County Council’s heritage venues and collection manager, with her DLI earrings 

*(Image: Sarah Caldecott)*

With Gillian's help, we’ve selected five from the countless stories featured in We Are The Durhams…

  The Enigma codebreaking machine on display in The Light, Durham 

*(Image: Sarah Caldecott)*

  **BREAKING THE ENIGMA**

NEARLY 10,000 codebreakers, translators and other people worked at Bletchley Park, near Milton Keynes, on top secret Ultra intelligence work trying to understand the enemy’s Enigma Code.

When they cracked it, they shortened the Second World War by at least two years.

All those workers were housed in camps within Bletchley Park, one of which, the Shenley Road Military Camp, was commanded by Lieutenant Colonel George Fillingham, of the Durham Light Infantry, who had won a Military Cross while serving in the First World War with the regiment for his “conspicuous gallantry”.

  Lt-Col George Fillingham, of the DLI, who played a role in breaking the German Enigma Code. 

*(Image: © Reproduced with the permission of the Trustees of the Former Durham Light Infantry Regiment and the DLI Collection.)*

In his honour, one of the rotating code-breaking machines, on loan from GCHQ, is one of the first exhibits in the new exhibition.

Fillingham was an impressive figure with his full moustache, walking stick and unpredictable temper – he liked to be saluted by his men.

And yet he had an affection for those in camp, organising boxing competitions and ballet performances.

As he had not been given clearance to enter Bletchley Park, he probably did not know the secrets contained within, but he still played a vital role in breaking the Enigma Code.

  The cheeky Christmas postcard Lt Michael Farr, himself a serial escaper, sent home in November 1941 on display in The Light 

*(Image: Sarah Caldecott)*

  **ESCAPE FROM COLDITZ**

LIEUTENANT MICHAEL FARR, 21, from a family of distillers and brewers, joined the 2nd DLI in January 1939, but was captured during the retreat towards Dunkirk on May 27, 1940.

He spent the rest of the war trying to escape.

One of his first attempts involved a raid on the camp commandant’s wine cellar; another saw him successfully tunnel out and cross the River Danube before being recaptured.

Now regarded as a hardliner, he was sent to Colditz Castle, the top security prison.

“They regarded it as their duty to try and escape,” says Gillian.

“They had a huge amount of construction skills between them so everyone had a role, and he made wine while he was in there called Jam Happy – it was black and would make you pass out.”

He worked on the construction of the famous glider in the castle's roof that was only abandoned when the war ended and, once liberated, he worked for the family firm in Plymouth making Hawker's Pedlars Sloe Gin.

  Lt Michael Farr, of the DLI, who was imprisoned in Colditz where he was involved in the most celebrated escape attempts of the Second World War 

*(Image: © Reproduced with the permission of the Trustees of the Former Durham Light Infantry Regiment and the DLI Collection.)*

He was awarded the MBE in 1946 for his escape efforts and before he died in 1993, wrote his autobiography entitled War and Wine.

The exhibition includes a cheeky Christmas postcard he sent home on November 22, 1941, showing Santa trying to escape as well as a 1974 Airfix model of the Colditz glider.

In Farr’s honour, a DLI gin is being made – although it may not be black.

  Pte Thomas Willock's glass eye on display in The Light 

*(Image: Sarah Caldecott)*

  **WATCH ME PINT**

THE most unusual exhibit is a glass eye which belonged to Private Thomas Willock, of Gateshead, who lost his own eye while fighting on the Somme with the DLI during the First World War.

Before the war, Germany had been the main manufacturer of optical glass and so when war broke out, and demand increased, there was a major shortage of glass eyes.

The Army Spectacle Depot was created to manage the problem, and between December 1916 and August 1919, it distributed more than 22,000 eyes – one of which ended up with Thomas.

  Pte Thomas Willcox, of the DLI, who had a glass eye 

*(Image: © Reproduced with the permission of the Trustees of the Former Durham Light Infantry Regiment and the DLI Collection.)*

“His granddaughter, Brenda, recalled visits to the Ferry Boat Inn (in Fatfield),” says Gillian.

“He would tell her to sit quietly when he went to speak to someone, and he would remove his eye, place it beside his beer, and instruct it to ‘watch me pint’.

"When he died in 1956, she, as a seven-year-old, quietly removed it from his coffin, didn’t tell anyone else and kept it in a box.

“Every Remembrance Day she would hold it along with his Silver War Badge.”

  The High Wood Cross, made under fire on the Somme in memory of fallen Durhams in the First World War 

*(Image: Sarah Caldecott)*

  **ON THE FRONTLINE**

AT the centre of the exhibition is the High Wood Cross that was made on the Somme by DLI soldiers while still under fire during the appalling killing days of autumn 1916.

Suspended on chains around it are 126 dogtags, made by volunteers, with the names of the 126 Durhams who fell at High Wood between September 15 and October 1, 1916.

The chains sway in the breeze created by visitors so, with a projection of images from the trench behind the cross, it is very moving.

“We have taken the deliberate decision to have the cross on open display, not in a glass case, so people can make that personal connection,” says Gillian.

“We have many accounts from DLI soldiers that say how the Germans stopped firing while it was put up on the battlefield – we can only hypothesise about why, whether it was out of reverence or because they weren’t sure what was going on, but the cross represents that moment in time, what the frontline was like.”

High Wood was a notorious patch of ground that was so heavily fought over that it became a place of shattered tree stumps, deep shell holes and churned up mud and human remains.

The dogtags are probably all that remains of the Durhams – the 6th battalion, for instance, recruited from Bishop Auckland, Barnard Castle and Spennymoor – as their bodies were never recovered.

The cross was commissioned by Lt Roland Bradford, who won the Victoria Cross during this action, and designed by Captain Robert Mauchlen, who also left some remarkable paintings of the battlefield.

He placed the DLI badge on the top of the cross, and had “Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori” (“it is sweet and fair to die for one’s country”) painted on the top step, while the second step bears the inscription: “Erected in affectionate remembrance by their friends who fought with them and who will ever keep their memory green.”

The cross was placed on the top of the Butte de Warlencourt – the small hillock that the Durhams expended countless lives capturing – until 1926 when it came back to Britain and ended up in St Mary’s Church, Gateshead.

It was saved when the church caught fire in 1979 and now forms the emotional centrepiece of The Story exhibition.

  Helena Rosa Duncombe Shafto, of South Bailey, Durham, who did more for the DLI than anyone else 

*(Image: Sarah Caldecott)*

  **MRS SHAFTO'S LOSS**

“OUT of horrific loss and great personal grief, she gave so much back and did so much for the DLI,” says Gillian, standing in front of a portrait of Helena Rosa Duncombe Shafto, of the famous Spennymoor family.

“When she died in 1938, a DLI wreath went ahead of her coffin and the regiment said that no person had done more for it than she had.”

Mrs Shafto’s eldest son, Lt Charles Duncombe Shafto, was killed at Spion Kop serving with the 1st DLI during the Boer War in 1900, and her second son, Captain Arthur Duncombe Shafto, was killed at Mons in northern France in 1914.

  Mrs Shafto was renowned for her efforts to comfort Durhams being held in German PoW camps during the First World War 

*(Image: Chris Lloyd)*

She threw herself into organising relief efforts for imprisoned soldiers, collecting food parcels and knitting socks, and she became the central figure for PoW welfare in Durham.

Returning prisoners frequently credited her with helping them survive captivity.

After the war, she carried on raising money and helping wounded veterans, both those with physical injuries and mental wounds, sitting on the boards of local hospitals and the asylum.

For her efforts she was made the first Honorary Freewoman of Durham and was awarded the OBE.

In 2024, a blue plaque was placed on her home in South Bailey, Durham.
