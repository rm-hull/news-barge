The Senate homeland security and governmental affairs committee voted to hold Anthony Fauci in contempt of Congress after a bitter hearing last week, where the retired public health official invoked the fifth amendment for fear of self-incrimination and possible prosecution by Republican lawmakers, who have routinely vilified him for his handling of the Covid-19 response.

This came after several attempts from some of the panel’s Democratic members to table today’s vote, all of these were rejected by the committee’s Republican majority.

Rand Paul, the committee’s GOP chair, will now seek a full vote in the Senate, or possibly send the resolution directly the justice department. The DoJ will then determine whether to pursue criminal charges.

Democratic senator Maggie Hassan, of New Hampshire, said while there is a “real need” for a “serious bipartisan examination into what went well and what went wrong during our country’s response to the pandemic”, last week’s hearing was ultimately designed to “target a single doctor and dedicated public servant, who has testified before Congress many times before, and they wanted to blame him for everything that went wrong during the pandemic”.

She also defended Fauci’s decision to pleas the fifth amendment, adding that he had “little choice but to invoke his constitutional right to not walk directly into a trap”.

During his opening remarks in today’s hearing, Republican Senator Rand Paul insisted that the vote to hold Anthony Fauci in contempt of Congress was not about his “opinions”, “policies” or “anything he said from a podium six years ago”.

“We are voting on whether a witness who has received the benefit of a sweeping federal pardon can be ordered by this committee to answer questions and then defy that order without consequence,” Paul said.

A reminder that even though Fauci received a presidential pardon as Joe Biden left office, anything the former public health official says after 19 January 2025 could expose him to certain perjury charges that Paul has pursued. As a result, Fauci invoked the fifth amendment 111 times throughout last week’s questioning from lawmakers.

My colleague Jessica Glenza reports that there is debate among experts about how far the fifth amendment extends. Some argue that once a person accepts a presidential pardon, they cannot invoke the fifth amendment for federal crimes. Without that right, Congress could hold a witness in contempt, which can lead to fines or – in the extreme – jail time. Others have argued that the right extends as long as there is a valid threat of prosecution, particularly by states.

In the Senate today, a vote to hold Anthony Fauci is scheduled for 8.30am ET in the homeland security and governmental affairs committee.

The Republican chair, senator Rand Paul of Kentucky, is hoping the panel’s GOP majority will vote in favor of the resolution, after Fauci, the former director of the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Disease (Niaid), consistently invoked the fifth during a rancorous hearing last week.

However, to guarantee holding Fauci in contempt of Congress, Paul will need 60 votes in the upper chamber before sending a criminal referral to the Department of Justice.
