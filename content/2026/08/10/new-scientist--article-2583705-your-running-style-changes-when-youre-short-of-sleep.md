A lack of sleep doesn’t just make it harder to motivate yourself to go out running; it also changes the way you move. Sleep-deprived runners don’t stiffen their leading leg as much before impact, which increases the time their feet are in contact with the ground and reduces the number of steps per minute.

“You need to actively prepare your leg for ground contact,” says Maura Seynaeve at KU Leuven in Belgium. “When you’re sleep-deprived, you have less alertness, and you are less present in the moment. At least, that’s my hypothesis.”

Previous studies have looked at how sleep deprivation affects running performance, the risk of injuries and physiological factors such as heart rate and oxygen consumption. But this is the first to look at how it affects the way people move, says Seynaeve.

She and her colleagues had 11 women and 10 men each do two runs on a treadmill on different days, one week apart. The runs consisted of intervals of increasing intensity, with speeds based on an earlier test of each runner’s fitness.

Before one of the runs, the volunteers weren’t allowed to sleep at all, while they slept as normal before the other run. Their movements were monitored with a waist-mounted accelerometer and a motion-capture camera.

Running styles vary a lot between individuals, so the researchers didn’t find any changes distinctive enough to tell from a single run whether any random individual is sleep-deprived. However, for any one individual, they found significant changes between the two runs. When sleep-deprived, the runners took between 1 and 3 fewer steps per minute at the same speed, for instance.

Sleep deprivation also increased the time the runners’ feet were in contact with the ground, with more time known as grounded running. This may not sound like a bad thing. “People who have a lot of injuries are advised to run more grounded, because the load is reduced on their joints,” says Seynaeve.

But other studies have found that a lack of sleep increases the risk of running injuries. For instance, a study last year found that runners who rated their sleep quality as poor were 36 per cent more likely to be injured. Possibly this is a result of changed loads on different parts of the body, says Seynaeve. “We see that, for example, the knee is loaded much more, so this could maybe lead to more knee injuries.”

“Yes, if sleep deprivation changes how you move, that may be one way it could induce injury risk,” says Michael Grandner at the University of Arizona College of Medicine.

“This study is very well-designed and executed,” says Hannah Dimmick at the University of Denver, Colorado, who has looked at how running styles change as people tire. There are some similarities, because both tiredness and sleep deprivation reduce cadence, but the two studies aren’t directly comparable, she says.

“While this may have been outside the scope of this specific paper, I’d be interested in whether sleep deprivation might potentially exaggerate the effect of acute fatigue on running biomechanics,” says Dimmick.
