For this morning’s First Edition, I spoke to our senior international correspondent, ***Julian Borger***, about the latest incursions, and how Israeli extremists are trying to create a new reality in Jerusalem. That’s after the weekend roundup.

## In depth: ‘To erect structures, even if they’re temporary, is a red rag to a bull’

![Al-Haram al-Sharif site in Jerusalem’s old city – one of the most contentious places on earth.](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/07a11e9c8e5ef3e553b3ecdcceffac0040b5b145/1376_0_6880_5504/master/6880.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

The al-Haram al-Sharif site in Jerusalem’s old city, known to Jews as the Temple Mount, is one of the most contentious places on earth. It’s governed by a fragile agreement that gives Muslims exclusive rights to pray and worship there, while Jews pray at the Western Wall below. But Israel’s messianic right, which has gained a powerful foothold in Netanyahu’s coalition government, is fixated on the site as a symbol of its political project. Their vision of building a new Jewish temple on one of Islam’s holiest sites – once a fringe idea – has become increasingly widespread.

Since 2023, Jewish religious extremists have made numerous visits to the 35-acre compound which is home to both al-Aqsa mosque and Dome of the Rock, the holiest sites for Muslims in Jerusalem and one of holiest sites for Muslims in the world. “At first, you had groups of perhaps 20 Jews, mostly settlers, who were escorted by the police to the far wall of the compound to pray discreetly,” Julian tells me. “If you were just a casual visitor, you wouldn’t necessarily see them”. Over the last two years, however, the groups have grown bigger and more audacious. On 23 July, they sang the national anthem, and prayed and performed rituals in the centre of the compound, while Israeli police – who report to Ben-Gvir, who was in attendance – set up tents to provide shade to the worshippers.

“To erect structures, even if they’re temporary, is a red rag to a bull,” Julian says. “The longstanding fear is that Israelis would knock down the Dome of the Rock or the mosque, or rebuild a [Jewish] temple on the site. And these tents were part of a gradual attempt to push the boundaries.” Eight Arab nations swiftly issued a joint statement condemning “mass incursions by settlers led by extremist Israeli ministers”. Last Wednesday, the Jordanian authorities, who administer the site, held an emergency meeting of Arab and Islamic nations to discuss these risks, and afterwards, the Jordanian authorities condemned Israeli violations and warned that they constituted a “blatant provocation” to Muslims worldwide.

“All they can do is issue strong statements, saying, ‘don’t do this’,” says Julian. The Waqf, the Jordanian-appointed foundation that manages the site, is “defenceless”, he explains. In the run-up to Ramadan this year, Waqf sources told the Guardian that five of its staff had been detained by the Shin Bet, Israel’s counterintelligence service. Meanwhile, Israeli police have begun actively recruiting extremist activists to serve in the Temple Mount Unit, the force responsible for policing the compound. According to Haaretz, the deputy commander of the police’s Temple Mount Unit published a callout to officers on social media and in WhatsApp groups associated with West Bank settlers. “Anyone who wants to take part in implementing sovereignty is welcome to contact me,” he wrote.

**Why is the site so significant?**

The site’s history is constantly enlisted in attempts to justify the present. Muslims know the raised compound in the south-east of Jerusalem as al-Haram al-Sharif (the Noble Sanctuary), or simply al-Aqsa, after the mosque located at its southern edge. Next to the mosque, at the centre of the compound, is the Dome of the Rock, one of the oldest surviving Islamic places of worship. It’s on this site the prophet Muhammad is said to have ascended to heaven.

At the base of the compound is the Western Wall, a section of retaining wall that survived the destruction of the second Jewish temple in 70CE. The Temple Mount is also the holiest site in Judaism, the site of two temples which were built and then destroyed by invaders in ancient times.

Access to the site has been delicately managed for centuries. After the six-day war in 1967, when Israel captured East Jerusalem from Jordan, the Israeli government agreed that Jordan would continue to be the site’s custodian. Since then, the “status quo”, as it is known, has sought to prevent violent conflict by circumscribing the rights of Muslims and Jews to access the compound. While Jews are permitted to visit the site at specific times, they cannot pray there. Many Jews opt not to visit the site, out of fear of treading on holy ground while ritually impure.

“The status quo represented a historic compromise. Jews got the Western Wall. Muslims got Haram al-Sharif,” Julian says. “But the site has always been a detonator, sitting right there in the centre of the old city, with strong religious beliefs crammed together in a small area.”

**How has this erupted in the past?**

It’s not uncommon in the region for holy sites to be carefully managed with intricate arrangements. Elsewhere in the Old City, the Church of the Holy Sepulchre is divided up between six Christian denominations, with its keys held by two Muslim families. At the Cave of the Patriarchs in the occupied city of Hebron, a mosque and synagogue share one building.

Past Israeli moves to challenge the status quo at al-Haram al-Sharif have been incendiary, sparking deadly violence. In 2000, then Israeli opposition leader and future prime minister Ariel Sharon made a visit to the site in an attempt to demonstrate Israeli sovereignty, sparking a chain of events that helped ignite  the second intifada. More recently, Hamas gave the name “al-Aqsa flood” to its attack on Israel in October 2023 that killed 1,200 Israelis and triggered the war on Gaza, claiming it was provoked by Israeli violations at the Jerusalem mosque.

**What’s the end goal of these incursions?**

Some Jewish extremists have begun marching under the temple flag – a star of David overlaid with a picture of the (as yet nonexistent) third temple they hope to build. “You see these flags on the bulldozers that are flattening neighbourhoods in Gaza, and soldiers wearing patches with a picture of the third temple on them,” Julian says.

The rise of the third temple movement comes at a time of surging attacks on Palestinians in the occupied West Bank, with the scale of violence prompting urgent calls for intervention from the heart of the Israeli establishment.

Earlier this year, dozens of Israelis, including two former prime ministers, former heads of its security services and former judges, signed a “final warning” threatening legal action against their government over its support for “Jewish terrorism” in the occupied West Bank. They argued the actions of the government had isolated Israel internationally and put its security at risk.

“There’s a feeling that the far-right side of Netanyahu’s government is now creating unnecessary, long-term security problems for Israel,” Julian says. That extends to attempts to change the status quo at al-Aqsa.

Netanyahu has publicly backed Jewish prayer at the site, claiming that changes pushed by Ben-Gvir were coordinated with him. “This isn’t the stuff of radicals any more,” Julian says. “It has government approval.”
