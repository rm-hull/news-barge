Australian faith in Aukus ‘stark staring bonkers’: Turnbull

Ben Doherty

Australia’s blind faith in the Aukus submarine deal is “stark staring bonkers,” the former prime minister Malcolm Turnbull has told a public inquiry, arguing the asymmetric deal with the US and UK will likely result in no submarines at all for Australia:

There is a cargo-cult mentality in Canberra about these Virginia-class submarines where people clearly feel that if they acknowledge that there is a real risk that they won’t arrive, that will prevent them arriving: so you have to keep on believing.

Turnbull – the prime minister whose deal to buy submarines from the French group Naval was torn up by Scott Morrison in favour of Aukus – has long argued the Aukus agreement has always been irretrievably lopsided in the US’s favour.

Giving evidence to a public inquiry into Aukus in Sydney, Turnbull focused on the three second-hand Virginia Class submarines the US is proposing to sell to Australia from 2031 (the first tranche of Aukus’ Pillar One).

Turnbull said the US’s capacity for building submarines has remained stubbornly laggard and that its navy would not have enough boats to sell to Australia.

The US is prevented by its own legislation from selling any submarines to Australia if that would “degrade” its own fleet. It currently needs to nearly double its submarine production rate, from 1.2 to 2.3 a year.

Mortgage applications fall 20% at Westpac since budget

Westpac has delivered a $1.8bn quarterly net profit even after mortgage applications fell due to a combination of higher interest rates and Labor’s property tax changes.

The bank said in an update this morning that mortgage applications were down 20% between mid-May and the end of July, representing the immediate period after Labor announced changes to negative gearing and the treatment of capital gains.

The tax changes have largely dented demand from investors, to the benefit of owner-occupiers.

Mortgage applications were already falling ahead of the May budget due to the Reserve Bank’s string of interest rate hikes, designed to subdue inflation.

Anthony Miller, Westpac’s chief executive, said ongoing housing undersupply would support prices.

The undersupply of housing combined with population growth is expected to partially offset the impact of higher interest rates and recent federal government policy changes on the housing market.

Overall, the bank recorded a 2% lift in lending during the June quarter, helping it increase its net profit result.

NSW begins roll out of smart rental bonds, allowing transfer of bond from one rental to the next

The NSW government is rolling out a new scheme of smart rental bonds, which will allow some renters to transfer their bonds from one rental to the next when they move house.

The scheme will end the need to save up thousands of dollars for a new bond before the old one is returned, and will begin with a phased rollout for renters in Parramatta, the Central Coast or Penrith local government areas.

The scheme will ultimately be rolled out across the state by the end of the year.

Renters will be notified when a new landlord or agent lodges a bond request online. They can then choose to pay their new bond upfront or use a smart rental bond to move it to the new rental for a $25 fee.

Any difference in bond is either paid by the renter, or refunded if it is cheaper and there is no agreed claim on the bond. If the landlord at an original property makes a claim, the NSW government will pay the landlord directly and the renter will then pay the government.

The premier, Chris Minns, said in a statement:

Over two million people in New South Wales are renters – one of the highest proportions of the population ever living in rental accommodation.

For a lot of renters, moving home can mean finding another $3,000 or $4,000 before you’ve even had your old bond returned. That’s a huge financial hurdle for families already doing it tough.

Smart Rental Bonds removes that barrier by letting renters transfer their bond instead of paying for two at once.

Health minister says bulk billing rate has increased to 84%

Josh Butler

The federal government is trumpeting “big benefits” from its Medicare investments, announcing bulk billing rates across the country are up nearly five percentage points since last year.

The health minister, Mark Butler, said the national bulk billing rate for the June quarter had increased to 84.1%, announcing increases in every state and territory.

Bulk billing was up 5.7% in Victoria (to 85.7%), up 6% in South Australia (82.5%), up 4.1% in NSW (87.5%) and up 13% in the Northern Territory to 90.8%.

Other states saw smaller increases, with the ACT coming in last, with bulk billing still lagging at 59.3% – by far the lowest rate nationally.

Butler said the Labor government’s Medicare investments had helped raise that rate.

“Across the country more and more GP practices are becoming fully bulk billing clinics meaning all patients need is their Medicare card, not their credit card,” he said.

NSW deputy premier asks for moratorium on take-home assessments for students amid AI review

Prue Car, the NSW deputy premier, has asked the NSW Education Standards Authority (Nesa) to impose a ban on unsupervised take-home assessments during a broad review of AI’s impact on student learning.

Car said she has asked Nesa to “urgently” examine artificial intelligence and move to protect student learning amid growing evidence the technology is affecting student’s cognitive development. Half of students’ HSC result is based on school-based assessments, some of which is finished outside the classroom.

She said in a statement:

Artificial intelligence has enormous potential to support teaching and learning, but it should never replace the thinking, creativity, and hard work that education is designed to develop.

The purpose of schooling extends beyond producing an assessment task. It is to build knowledge, judgment, resilience, and critical thinking over time. If students rely on AI to bypass that learning process, we risk undermining both their education and the integrity of the HSC.

Subject to Nesa’s advice, any interim changes would be applied from the beginning of term 4.
