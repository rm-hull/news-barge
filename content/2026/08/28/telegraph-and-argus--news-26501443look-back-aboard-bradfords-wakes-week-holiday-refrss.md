BACK in the day, the first two weeks of August meant Wakes Week, or Factory Fortnight as folk called it.

Bradford’s mills and factories shut up shop and the workers, along with their families, took a much-deserved holiday.

My family, like many others in the region, did just that.

And of course, many would take the opportunity to head for where they could breathe in holiday air and feel sand between their toes. While some folk headed west to Blackpool, others headed east to Bridlington.

  Hilderthorpe Road coach park, Bridlington, 1972. Image: Terry Walker 

*(Image: Terry Walker)*

Stifling-hot coaches, or sharras as they were called, headed from the Bradford Interchange or outside Norfolk Gardens Hotel on a journey that took around two-and-a-half hours.

Right from the get-go, there was a holiday atmosphere, with cans of beer for adults and cold pop aplenty for the kids, as happier-than-life holidaymakers played cards and games, ate packed lunches, and sang along to Walking on Sunshine-type songs to pass the time.

  Jason with his brother and gran on Bridlington beach, 1980.


Our family’s regular holiday destination was the seaside town of Bridlington on the East Coast of Yorkshire. On arrival in ‘Brid’, as everyone seemed to call it, after a whip round for the driver, folk would clamber off the sharra and lift their heavy suitcases, pre-wheelie ones. There was the flutter of excitement when you smelt the sea air and heard the squawking seagulls.

You’d hear barrow boys greet the trippers with a cheerful, “Where ye goin’ to, mister?” While others hollered, “Mind yer backs!” as they carted holidaygoers’ suitcases to the many B&Bs peppered all over the town.

  Aunties Edie and Mary, left, in Blackpool for Wakes Week in 1962.


Brid had plenty on offer; it boasted broad, golden sandy beaches that stretched for miles, leaving plenty of space for eager tourists. You had your Polaroid camera loaded with 24 or 36 snaps to last you the week or fortnight.

  Jay and his older brother with two donkeys, Tiny and Beulah, which they used to walk on Bridlington beach in 1980 

*(Image: Jay Croot)*

On sunny mornings, families rose early to claim the best patch of sand, lugging armfuls of rented seaside equipment. Deckchairs were around 50p to hire, and a windbreaker cost a quid. It was a common sight to see dads hammering their sturdy windbreakers into the sand and positioning their deckchairs for the day, facing the sparkling grey North Sea.

  A family photo from Blackpool Wakes Week.


The seaside town buzzed with young and old, drawn to the video game arcades, where you could play 10p Pac-Man, Space Invaders and Pole Position. It was fun galore for the kids, with 50p jetty boat rides and bouncy castles. Adults would enjoy the nightly variety shows at the grand Bridlington Spa and the heaving disco bars and pubs, like the Harbour Lights.

The days that followed were spent on the beach, filled with donkey rides and dipping toes in the sea. To keep our energy up, we had sand-filled, triangle-shaped Spam sarnies, washed down with cartons of pop, and finished off with sweet, soft ice cream.

Time for a quick swill back at the B&B, trying to wash off all the sand that managed to find its way into every gap and crevice on ourselves and our clothing. We’d open the sash windows, shake the beach towels and bash our shoes on the windowsill, but no matter how hard we tried, there was always some sand left.

Then out for some local culinary delights: locally caught deep-fried fish and thick chips soaked in salt and vinegar, eaten out of newspapers or trays with wooden forks. And not forgetting the best hot doughnuts ever at Wendy’s Waffles on the harbour, golden and crisp and smothered in sugar. You’d hear the mums saying, “Blow it first, luv, it’s too ’ot to eat.”

Of course, the kids didn’t listen, biting into them while eagle-eyed seagulls waited for the chance to nab a crumb or two.

Whether walking around, sitting on the sand or swimming, it was like Bradford-on-Sea. You’d often spot a neighbour, a fellow worker or a school chum; sometimes it was just a wave, a quick ‘ey up’ or a catch-up while taking a stroll around the harbour.

For our clan, Brid often meant a family gathering. After a long day on the beach, we’d all end up together in the Half Moon pub. While Dad and my uncle Alan knocked back the 80p pints, Tetley’s or Webster’s, their nectar of choice, they’d have some banter with the locals.

Dad: “Same again, Alfred, and get one for yoursen.”

Alfred, the barman: “Cheers, Dennis.”

Dad: “I luv Brid. Have ye lived here all yer life, Alfred?”

Alfred: “Well, not yet, Dennis!”

Mum and my Aunty Eileen sat in the corner on the velvet seats, sipping their Babychams and humming along to Save All Your Kisses For Me, which pumped through the jukebox speakers. My older brother, cousins Peter and Jacqui, and I dined on Seabrook beefy crisps and lemonade in the kids’ room. Every now and then, we’d sneak into the adult section and be pocketed out of our shekels as we played Tic-Tac-Toe on the pub’s one-armed bandit, which was strictly prohibited for under-18s.

During the day, my brother and I had jobs, leading donkeys up and down the beach. At night we’d lie in our bunk beds in our digs, our B&B on Belgrave Road, full of fizzy pop and sunburn, listening to the distant hum of the amusement arcades and arguing over who got the top bunk, making sure we’d put enough 50p pieces in the hungry electric meter.

Back then we wore flares and tank tops like the kids we saw on Top of the Pops, and hoped the Polaroid camera caught at least one photo where our eyes weren’t shut.

Those Bridlington summers were simple. Sunshine, sea air, family, friends, hot Wendy’s doughnuts, fish and chips, and the smell of salt and vinegar on our fingers.

Happy Days!
