WHILE volunteering at the Hanson Academy Museum, one surname surfaced again and again: Symes.

In the archives were Henry Symes, a First World War casualty; his sister Jane, a teacher and suffragette; and Charles, recognised from a Hanson school photograph at the request of curator Brian Russell.

What emerged was the story of a Bradford family whose lives were shaped, sometimes shattered, by war and social change.

Henry Symes (Senior) was born in Manningham in 1862 and worked as a rate collector. In 1884 he married Lydia Hannah Angel of Guernsey, and together they raised five children: Henry, Jane Elizabeth, James Stanley, Florence May and Charles Betts.

Despite being 50-years-old, Henry (Snr) enlisted on October 4, 1914 with the Prince of Wales’s Own (West Yorkshire Regiment). His service record describes him as “5 feet 8.5 inches, grey eyes and grey hair.”

He rose from private to sergeant, served in France in 1917, and was medically evacuated with exhaustion aboard the St Patrick. When declared unfit for overseas service, he was assigned home duties, supervising German prisoners of war at Croxton Park. Repatriation of POWs did not begin immediately after the Armistice; for most, it followed the signing of the Treaty of Versailles in 1919.

The eldest son, Henry, was born on February 17, 1885 and attended Hanson School. In 1901 he was a stuff warehouse worker, but later worked in Shanghai. The Bradford Daily Argus of 19 May 1915 reported:

‘Mr Henry Symes, late of Shanghai and formerly of Bradford, has obtained a commission in the 11th Battalion, York and Lancaster Regiment. For a number of years Mr Symes has held the responsible position as head of the textile department of a large English firm at Shanghai. He has had seven years’ of service in the volunteer corps, and was educated at Hanson Secondary School, Bradford.’

  War memorial at Shanghai Race Club, bearing Henry Symes' name. 

*(Image: Sharon Knott)*

Passenger records show him travelling second class, boarding at Shanghai in April 1915.

His surviving military papers are sparse, but his medal card records that he disembarked at Mudros on November 22, 1915 aboard RMS Olympic, sister ship to Titanic and Britannic. The Olympic featured luxury amenities including a Turkish bath and gym. It was requisitioned for war service, carrying thousands of troops; it was named “old reliable.” Henry served with the 6th Battalion; he fought in Gallipoli, defended the Suez Canal, and later moved to France.

The Yorkshire Evening Post on October 12, 1916 reported: ‘Second-Lieutenant Henry Symes, York and Lancaster Reg., killed in action on Sept. 30, was the son of Sergeant H Symes, West Yorks, who was previously in the offices of the Bradford Corporation. Lieut. Symes was formerly a representative in China for a Manchester firm, and saw active service during the trouble in Shanghai. He returned to this country in 1915 and served on several fronts.’

Henry was killed on September 30, 1916 at Schwaben Redoubt on Thiepval Ridge during the Somme offensive. He has no known grave and is commemorated on the Thiepval Memorial alongside 72,310 others. His name also appears at the Shanghai Race Club War Memorial and on Hanson Academy’s original First World War memorial.

Bradford Pals suffered devastating losses on July 1, 1916, with 1,770 men killed, wounded or missing at the Battle of the Somme. A staggering figure that roughly matches the number of pupils and staff at Hanson Academy.

James Stanley was born on March 20, 1889. It is unclear whether he attended Hanson. He married Nellie Walker in 1916 at St Peter’s, Shipley. He enlisted on June 21, 1917 with the Royal Naval Volunteer Reserve as a chief motor mechanic. Though he survived the war, he suffered a severe injury described as “bilateral loss of left knee,” leading to discharge, a King’s Certificate, and entitlement to pension and gratuity. By 1921 he was working as a sheet metal worker for his father in law. He died in 1960 aged 71.

Charles Betts, born on March 2, 1897, attended Hanson - confirmed by the football team photograph in which fellow player Herbert McKell later fell in the war.

  Charles Betts Symes reported as wounded. Image: Leeds Intelligener


Charles joined the Northumberland Fusiliers and served in France in 1915 at just 18-years-old. He was honourably discharged due to wounds and awarded the Silver War Badge. He married Kathleen M Gilyard in 1923, a schoolteacher whose father owned a stationery shop on Darley Street. Charles died in 1961.

  Charles B Symes on his wedding day. Image: Leeds Intelligencer


Jane Elizabeth, born June 24, 1886, gained a BA in 1908 and taught at Hanson Girls’ School until 1943. She was active in the suffragette movement, alongside many colleagues. In 1912 she delivered a spirited speech on “equal pay for equal work,” including the memorable line:

“Did they realise it, that employers paid for work done, not responsibilities? What if a teacher on the eve of his marriage went to his Education Committee and said, ‘Tomorrow I am going to be married, and I shall have a wife to keep. You will, of course, double my pay’.”

Her humour, imagining a male teacher demanding double pay because he was about to marry, was met with laughter. Jane never married and died in 1984 aged 98.

Florence May, born May 11, 1893, appears in the 1911 census with her parents and no occupation listed. She married Thomas Banks, a bank clerk, in 1918; his address on their marriage certificate was RAF Aerodrome, Driffield. The couple later moved away from the area. Florence died in 1984.
