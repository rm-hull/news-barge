Trump signs order to rename Lake Ontario as 'Lake America'

Donald Trump summoned the press pool to the Oval Office to witness him signing an executive order that directs the Department of the Interior to rename Lake Ontario as “Lake America”.

This comes after trade talks between Canada collapsed over the weekend and led to a fray of tariffs between Washington and Ottawa.

Trump claimed the name change was something “I’ve been thinking about for a long time”.

“Canada treats us very badly,” the president said. “People oftentimes they’ll ask me who’s the worst to deal with on trade, and I said that’s easy. Canada, they are the worst, and they feel entitled, and just we can’t have that.”

A 50% tariff on several Canadian imports kicked in on Saturday, with Trump threatening another round of 50% duties on Canadian steel, trucks, cars and auto parts to take effect in the new year. Mark Carney, Canada’s prime minister, hit back with 25-50% tariffs on hundreds of US goods.

This, of course, is not the first time the president has made renaming a body of water a priority. At the beginning of his second term in office he issued an executive order to rename the Gulf of Mexico to the “Gulf of America”.

Trump appeared to joke that there could be more to come: “We have a Gulf and we have a Lake – now all we need is an ocean. Maybe we’ll have to change the name of the Atlantic and/or the Pacific. Maybe we’ll change them both.”

Trump posts presidential rankings with himself at the top and 11 previous presidents missing

Two minutes before Air Force One landed in Houston on Thursday, Donald Trump posted a visual ranking of US presidents on his social media platform that described him as the “greatest” president, in a list that omitted, without explanation, 11 of his predecessors.

As critics of the president quickly pointed out, the illustrated ranking of presidents was an altered version of one published in Life magazine on 1 November 1948. The original illustrated an article about the rankings compiled by the Harvard professor Arthur M. Schlesinger Sr. who polled 55 historians, journalists and political scientists and asked them to rate presidents in one of five categories: great, near great, average, below average and failure.

In the article published the day before the 1948 election, Schlesinger explained that he had left out three presidents, William Henry Harrison who died after a month in office; James Garfield, who was shot four months after his inauguration; and Harry Truman, who was still serving out the term his predecessor Franklin D Roosevelt had been elected to before his death.

The date of the illustration explains why another eight presidents are missing from the list that puts Trump at the top.

Dwight Eisenhower, John F Kennedy, Lyndon Johnson, Richard Nixon, Gerald Ford, Ronald Reagan, George H W Bush and George W Bush all, of course, were elected after 1948.

But the graphic was updated to add not just Trump, who was two years old when the original was published, alone in a new category “greatest” but also to add to the list of “failures” at the bottom the names and images of Barack Obama, Joe Biden and Jimmy Carter.

The only other president to serve since 1948 to be added to the list was Bill Clinton, who, perhaps by dint of having socialized with Trump, made his way into the “average” category, along with Herbert Hoover, whose reputation has fallen since 1948.

The original Schlesinger list was topped by six presidents: Abraham Lincoln, George Washington, Franklin Roosevelt, Woodrow Wilson, Thomas Jefferson and Andrew Jackson. Only Lincoln was rated that way by all 55 of the experts.

The image of Trump taking a Sharpie to a map, and then having a more polished version of his alterations presented to the media in the Oval Office, echoed an incident during his first term, when he insisted that a hurricane might hit Alabama, and then refused to admit that he had been wrong.

In that case, when Trump invited the press corps into the Oval Office, on 4 September 2019, he showed a forecast map that he had altered with a black marker, presenting it as proof that he had been right and the National Weather Service wrong, when he warned residents of Alabama earlier that week that they were in the likely path of Hurricane Dorian.

In 2019, Donald Trump held up a chart that showed the original projected track of Hurricane Dorian from the National Weather Service that he had altered by drawing a black line on it to suggest that Alabama might be hit. Photograph: Jonathan Ernst/Reuters

In a section of her memoir on “Sharpiegate”, Trump’s then press secretary, Stephanie Grisham, confirmed that Trump had made the alteration himself.

Before calling in the media, Grisham wrote, Trump had expressed anger that the map, then a week-old projection of the storm’s likely path “sitting on an easel by his desk”, showed the hurricane threatening Florida and Georgia but not, as he’d tweeted, Alabama.

Trump then took a Sharpie from his desk, Grisham recalled, and drew a black line to include Alabama in the path, insisting to his staff that this was the original projection for the storm.

Grisham, whose book is littered with the remains of half-truths and outright lies she’d told on Trump’s behalf, claimed that Trump was right about the threat to Alabama, despite the fact that none of the forecast cones predicting the storm’s path ever included the state.

Fired Stars and Stripes journalists sue the Pentagon, and Hegseth, for wrongful termination

Three journalists who were fired by the Pentagon from a US government-funded news outlet sued the US Department of Defense in federal court in Washington DC on Thursday arguing that their dismissals were illegal and violated their First Amendment rights to free speech.

The three journalists were all employees of Stars and Stripes, which their court filing calls “the U.S. military’s independent news source since the Civil War with a long history of editorial independence, free from news management or censorship by military command.”

The news outlet’s former publisher, Max Lederer, editor-in-chief, Erik Slavin, and Middle East correspondent, Lara Korte, allege, in the language of the complaint, that the defense department and the defense secretary, Pete Hegseth, “are attempting to fire Plaintiffs for speaking up as private citizens in support of the press’s freedom to cover military operations, its need to reach all servicepersons, and in retaliation for their publication of a news report on deteriorating conditions aboard the U.S.S. Abraham Lincoln during its extended tour of duty in the Mid-East.”

Slavin says he was dismissed for supposed “insubordination” during an interview with CBS News in which he objected to potential censorship by the US military. Korte took part in the same interview.

“The spark that moved Defendants to act was a news report published by Stripes on August 11, 2026,” the suit alleges. “The article broke a major story about deteriorating conditions aboard the U.S.S. Abraham Lincoln. It reported that the aircraft carrier’s 5,000 Sailors have endured food and water shortages, disrupted mail service, long workdays, and little time off for months on end, and that the long deployment and physical hardships were impacting Sailors’ physical and mental health”.

The day after that article was published by Stars and Stripes, the suit contends, “Defendants ordered Plaintiff Max Lederer, Stripes’ Publisher, to present Notices of Separation to Mr. Slavin and Ms. Korte. The notices charged them with Insubordination for the statements they made five and one-half weeks earlier on CBS”.

“Stripes’ historical editorial independence is critical to its core mission of gathering and providing unbiased, credible journalism to the U.S. military community, particularly servicemembers and their families stationed overseas,” the suit also says.

“Lederer did not deliver the notices,” the suit explains. “Instead, he decided then and there to retire, and informed the Stripes’ staff via email. Mr. Lederer took this step because he did not want to be part of Defendants’ scheme to illegally punish Mr. Slavin and Ms. Korte for exercising their First Amendment rights.”

Lederer was dismissed shortly after announcing his impending retirement, effective at the end of September. He said the Pentagon had installed a new deputy publisher, an active duty service member, under him at the newspaper without his prior knowledge.

Ontario is an indigenous name that predates both the US and Canada, Canadian prime minister explains

Canada’s prime minister, Mark Carney, points out on social media that the name of the lake Donald Trump presumed to rename on Thursday comes from the language of the indigenous Wendat people.

“The name Lake Ontario comes from the Wendat word Ontari’io, which, appropriately, means ‘the lake is beautiful, the lake is big’, Carney wrote. “The name is more than 400 years old, predating both the Confederation of Canada and the Declaration of Independence of the United States of America.”

“We know that America is changing,” Carney added. “Their trading relationships, their foreign policies, their national monuments, their hydronyms. Canadians also know that naming reality means calling it Lake Ontario – then, now and always.”

The province of Ontario, founded in 1867, took its name from the lake.

The name “Canada” likely comes from the Huron-Iroquois word “kanata,” meaning “village” or “settlement.” In 1535, two Aboriginal youths told French explorer Jacques Cartier about the route to kanata; they were actually referring to the village of Stadacona, the site of the present-day City of Québec. For lack of another name, Cartier used the word “Canada” to describe not only the village, but the entire area controlled by its chief, Donnacona.

Trump administration officials are seeking to radically accelerate the deportation of hundreds of thousands of Haitians who were previously living in the US under the aegis of a humanitarian program, according to documents obtained by The New York Times.
