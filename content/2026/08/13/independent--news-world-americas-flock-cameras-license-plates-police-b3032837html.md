Surveillance technology firm Flock Safety announced platform modifications on Thursday, aiming to ease privacy concerns and address documented abuses by law enforcement officers, following mounting pressure from lawmakers, civil liberties groups, and citizens nationwide.

The company operates an extensive national network of automated cameras that record vehicle license plates and distinguishing details every day. Thousands of police agencies across 49 states can search and share this data across jurisdictions during active investigations.

Law enforcement officials credit the system as an essential tool for recovering missing persons and tracking violent crime suspects.

However, critics argue the pervasive tracking constitutes unconstitutional warrantless mass surveillance. Dozens of municipalities and police departments have ended their contracts with Flock over fears the system could be used for immigration enforcement or unauthorized personal tracking, following numerous reported instances of officer misconduct.

In an interview, Flock CEO Garrett Langley stated that key platform updates will convert previously optional safeguards into mandatory features for all users by Jan. 1.

Under the new policies, every police client must implement an audit tool designed to flag unusual search activity.

When irregular behavior is detected, the user's account will be locked out pending an internal review, according to details provided by the company ahead of Thursday's announcement.

Flock, which emphasizes that clients own the captured camera records, is also reducing its standard data retention period from 30 days to seven. Data can only be retained longer if designated as evidence connected to a case number.

![](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/08/13/21/2026-08-06T054718Z_372625750_RC2QSMAFF12M_RTRMADP_3_TECH-FLOCK.JPG)

Law enforcement users will also be required to enter a record management system code linking each search to a specific case before running queries, a requirement civil liberties advocates have long demanded, Langley said. Emergency overrides will be automatically flagged for administrative review.

Clients will also be able to choose which specific offense types—such as homicide or arson—outside agencies are permitted to search within their data, enabling departments to block external queries related to immigration enforcement.

Langley said this adjustment gives individual communities and departments control to deploy the platform in a manner "consistent with community values."

Skeptics reacted doubtfully to the measures, suggesting they appear designed to alleviate growing bipartisan political anger while still leaving room for potential misuse by police.

The American Civil Liberties Union noted in a blog post that the shortened retention timeframe could be "a step in the right direction," but described the other updates as "retreads" of inadequate safeguards.

Robert Frommer, a senior attorney at the Institute for Justice—a public interest law firm leading high-profile legal challenges against the technology—called the changes "window dressing" from a firm in "panic mode."

"This is window dressing that doesn’t address the fundamental problem, which is that police officers are the ones deciding who and when to search, and that should be done by judges with real warrants," Frommer said.

Andrew Guthrie Ferguson, a professor at the George Washington University Law School focusing on policing and surveillance, described Thursday’s changes as "better than the opposite," while calling for further oversight in the form of "sustained democratic engagement with the rules and judicial checks on access at a minimum."

Ferguson remarked that he was surprised by the "growing community backlash" targeted at Flock specifically, given that automated license plate readers are sold by multiple vendors. However, he observed that Flock and the opposition movement have "captured people’s sense that maybe they don’t want to be surveilled all the time."

More than 50 agencies or local jurisdictions have canceled, suspended, or rejected contracts, or turned off their cameras since the start of the year, according to DeFlock, a grassroots organization tracking license plate reader technology. Cameras nationwide have also suffered vandalism.

In Congress, Republican representatives introduced at least two bills in July aimed at limiting the technology's use.

Ian Adams, an associate professor of criminology at the University of South Carolina researching Flock, noted that concerns surrounding law enforcement data misuse are not new.

"Anyone with policing experience could have reasonably foreseen that what have been termed as ‘curiosity searches’ by officers, searches for private reasons not related to police work, were going to be a problem this technology faced," Adams said, pointing out that systems like the FBI’s Criminal Justice Information have faced similar issues.

Law enforcement experts noted this reflects a persistent tension in democratic policing—balancing tools officers say help solve crime against public privacy rights.

"It's a balancing act. A community has a legitimate interest in how information is used, but it also has a legitimate interest in the effectiveness of a police department in preventing crime," said Chuck Wexler, executive director of the Police Executive Research Forum, a Washington-based nonpartisan think tank. "I think a balance can be struck, but it's more likely to come from department policy than company changes."

Flock, headquartered in Atlanta, Georgia, frequently features success stories on its website, such as locating missing seniors and recovering stolen vehicles. The platform has also featured in high-profile cases, including the search for a suspect in a fatal shooting at Brown University and the arrest of a former North Carolina police officer accused of threatening a mass shooting at a Louisiana festival.

At the same time, instances of abuse have drawn widespread scrutiny. A recent report by The Washington Post identified nearly 50 instances of police officers charged or accused of using the cameras for unauthorized searches, frequently to track romantic partners or family members.

Just this week, six employees—including four officers—at the Savannah Police Department in Georgia were fired after being accused of searching for friends and relatives and allowing an outside officer to use city cameras.

The Savannah department noted the misconduct was uncovered through Flock’s voluntary audit feature.
