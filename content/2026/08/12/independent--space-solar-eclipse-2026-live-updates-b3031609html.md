The UK and parts of Europe are about to see a solar eclipse, as the Moon moves in front of the Sun and blocks out its light.

People around the country are preparing for the rare spectacle – with travellers taking holidays, and the glasses that are required to see it safely quickly selling out.

While experts have urged the public to try and see the major astronomical event, they have warned that people should do so safely. Looking straight at the Sun, even during an eclipse, can cause significant damage to the eyes and even to phones, and viewers are warned to only do so using special equipment.

Follow here for all the latest and live updates on the times, places and best ways to see the eclipse, as well as the most stunning images from the rare display.

## How, when and why to see the solar eclipse

Here's a helpful rundown from Daniel Brown, an astronomy expert at Nottingham Trent University, on what exactly is going to happen today.

“Deep partial eclipses of this scale are uncommon from the UK, and the next comparable event will not occur for many years. Even though we won't experience totality in the UK, this is still a wonderful opportunity to experience a remarkable astronomical event. With a bit of planning and some clear skies it should be a memorable evening for anyone who takes the time to look up safely,” he said.

“For this eclipse, totality – the length of time the Moon will cover the Sun – could last up to one minute and 40 seconds. It will begin in the UK at 6.06pm in Scotland and 6.17pm in Cornwall, when the Sun is only 22 degrees above the horizon. It will end at 8pm in the North and 8.06m in the South, with the Sun just five degrees above the horizon. Maximum coverage will occur at 7.02pm in the North and 7.16pm in the South.

“Because the maximum coverage will be 89% in the East and 95% in the West, for most of the UK we will notice a marginal dimming, similar to an impending thunderstorm. One of the most striking sights will be the shape of the remaining visible Sun, which will appear as a thin crescent with its points, or ‘horns’ facing downward.

“This isn't the easiest eclipse to observe because the Sun will be close to the horizon and cloud cover could easily get in the way. However, that low position also creates some fantastic photography opportunities. Think about how you might include a landmark, building or landscape feature in your photographs. If conditions are right, you'll be able to capture a beautiful crescent Sun above the horizon.

“It is important to observe safely, never to look directly at the sun with the naked eye, never use binoculars, telescopes or cameras without the appropriate solar filters, and to use certified eclipse glasses or indirect viewing methods such as pinhole projection.”

## Don’t use electricity during the solar eclipse, Octopus Energy warns

Hopefully, when the eclipse arrives you’ll be out and watching it anyway. But if you aren’t, please don’t use your washing machine, says Britain’s biggest energy supplier.

The eclipse will lower solar-powered electricity generation just as people get off work and go home, when electricity demand often surges.
