Prosecution’s case against the alleged Bondi terror attacker expands

The prosecution’s case against Naveed Akram for his alleged role as a gunman in the Bondi terror attack now includes 12 additional electronic devices and a report on an explosive device.

Commonwealth prosecutor Danielle New outlined what is included in the case to the Downing Centre local court on Wednesday before seeking a two months extension to serve the brief of evidence.

New said the case now included statements from protected witnesses, and:

An additional 12 electronic devices [and] an improvised explosive device testing report are due and need to be reviewed.

New told the court this would be included in the brief alongside “significant data” extracted from seven other electronic devices, extensive medical records, and 800 witness statements.

Judge Hugh Donnelly approved the extension for the brief of evidence, noting it was a “significant matter” and was moving “steadily”. It’s now expected to be served on 28 October.

Akram, 24, and his father, 50-year-old Sajid Akram, allegedly killed 15 people after opening fire at a Hanukah festival at Bondi beach on 14 December.

Akram, who survived a shootout with police, was charged in December with 78 offences, including 15 counts of murder and one count of committing a terrorist act that investigators allege may have been “inspired by Isis”. Sajid Akram was shot and killed by police at the scene.

Wilkie contacts Nacc over gambling bill, claiming government ‘improperly influenced’ by wagering lobby

Josh Butler

Independent MP Andrew Wilkie has alleged that “individuals in the Federal Government” had been “improperly influenced by individuals in the private sector” in formulating the gambling legislation, writing to the National Anti-Corruption Commission with his claims.

We’ve contacted the office of the communications minister, Anika Wells, for response.

Wilkie, the Tasmanian crossbencher, claimed he had “referred” the issue to the Nacc. The anti-corruption body is, of course, independent and chooses which issues it will investigate and, while any person can write to the body, there is no obligation to investigate.

In his letter, shared with media today, Wilkie said he was not making “any allegation against any specific individual”. But he claimed he was writing to “express my concern that individuals in the Federal Government, when formulating gambling reform policy, have been improperly influenced by individuals in the private sector, most obviously but not exclusively through donations, gifts, hospitality and personal relationships.”

This has resulted in policy so clearly at odds with research, public opinion and the public interest, that the implementation of such policy could be characterised, in my opinion, as serious corruption in the Commonwealth public sector.

Wilkie attached a newspaper article noting large sums of donations which Labor had received from betting agencies.

Wilkie wrote that while governments “have the right to accept donations, be lobbied and make their own policy decisions”, he claimed “individuals in the Government are acting so egregiously in response to individuals in the private sector that all reasonable limits on government behaviour have been exceeded.”

“If this doesn’t ‘cross the line’, then I dread to think what would,” he said.

Victoria to vaccinate thousands of little penguins against H5 bird flu

Petra Stock

Victoria will begin vaccinating about 5,000 little penguins at Phillip Island and St Kilda from next week to protect them against the deadly H5 bird flu, in what is believed to be the world’s largest vaccination program involving wild birds.

While vaccinating wild birds against the disease was challenging, the fact that the penguins returned home every night to Phillip Island and St Kilda made it possible to deliver the two doses necessary over a two-week period.

We can’t stop H5 bird flu circulating in wild birds, but we can take practical steps to protect vulnerable species like our little penguins from its impacts.

We know how people love little penguins, we know how iconic they are to Victoria.

Catherine Basterfield, the chief executive of Phillip Island Nature Parks, said trials had already been undertaken, which showed no adverse effect in the birds. “It is a world first, to propose to vaccinate wildlife in this scale,” she said.

The vaccines were being supplied by the federal agriculture department, and would cover only a proportion of the penguin population of about 40,000 birds. Doses have arrived at Phillip Island and are ready to be administered from early next week, pending final regulatory approvals. The birds would be microchipped at the same time.

James Todd, the state’s chief biodiversity officer, said vaccines weren’t a “silver bullet”, and wouldn’t be suitable for many of the hundreds of species at risk from the virus.

The announcement followed the detection of a positive H5 bird flu case on a rocky part of Phillip Island. However there have not been any cases found in little penguins.

Australia signs statement with US, UK and others on ballistic missile testing

Australia has joined the US and dozens of other nations including the UK, France, Germany and the Philippines, in calling on all relevant countries to provide advanced notice of ballistic missile launches.

Why does this matter? The joint statement of 40 nations follows a series of launches in the Pacific, including a ballistic missile test by North Korea, and a nuclear-capable missile test by China last month.

Only a few Pacific nations were signatory to the statement, including Palau and the Marshall Islands.

The statement says countries should provide 24 hours notice before the launch of intercontinental ballistic missiles, submarine-launched ballistic missiles, and space launch vehicles.

Conducting nuclear-capable ballistic missile tests without sufficient advance notice and detail is dangerous and disrupts the peace and security of relevant nations in proximity to these tests.

It increases the risk of miscalculation, undermines global stability and security, and is incompatible with the responsibilities that come with possessing long-range missile capabilities.

Victorian parliament has voted to abolish group voting tickets (GVTs) to elect its upper house.

The state was the only remaining jurisdiction with the system, in which voters can only choose one party above the line on the ballot paper, with their preferences then allocated by the party if it’s knocked out during counting.

GVTs have been criticised by experts including Antony Green because they allowed backroom deals between parties to flourish, enabling some candidates to get elected with only a minuscule number of primary votes.

The electoral amendment (miscellaneous matters) bill 2026, which passed the upper house 33 votes to 4, will replace GVTs with a new above-the-line voting system that requires voters to number at least five groups in order of preference. Those preferences will then flow to candidates in the order they appear below the line for each party or group.

The MPs who did not support the bill were: Shooters Fishers and Farmers MP Jeff Bourman, Animal Justice Party MP Georgie Purcell, Legalise Cannabis MP Rachel Payne and Libertarian David Limbrick. These minor-party MPs will be most affected by the change and face the prospect of losing their seats at the November election as a result.
