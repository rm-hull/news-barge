In Wisconsin, David Crowley narrowly leads Democratic socialist Francesca Hong

Results in the Wisconsin governor’s race are very close, with David Crowley, the Milwaukee county executive, narrowly leading Democratic socialist Francesca Hong.

Crowley, endorsed by outgoing governor Tony Evers, made a last-minute full-court press across the state, emphasizing that he believes he is more poised to win statewide and beat Republican Tom Tiffany in the general.

Polls had shown Hong with a strong lead, but often showed a sizable amount of undecided voters. With many candidates running, some dropping out and then Crowley re-entering the race, the polls likely didn’t accurately capture the race.

Crowley’s team wrote a memo saying as much in the days leading up to the primary, stating that the polls would likely be off as they were in Michigan, where they showed Abdul El-Sayed with a large lead over Haley Stevens for US Senate. El-Sayed won the race, albeit with a closer margin than polls predicted.

Matt Little rose to the top of a three-way race between current and former state lawmakers to win the Democratic nomination in Minnesota’s second congressional district, the seat currently held by Angie Craig, who is running for US senate.

Little, who patrolled immigration officers during Operation Metro Surge, defeated state senator Matt Klein and state representative Kaela Berg. He had received the endorsement from the state Democratic party via the delegate process.

The second congressional district is seen as a swing district, though it went for Kamala Harris in 2024. Cook Political Report rates it as a likely Democratic seat, though national Republicans are likely to make a play to flip it.

Bronin talked to the Guardian last year for a piece on Democrats challenging older members of Congress in the aftermath of Joe Biden’s 2024 exit from the presidential race over age and mental fitness concerns.

Voters in parts of Alabama have voted for the second time this primary season

Some Alabama voters took to the polls today for the second time this primary season after the state’s Republican-controlled legislature reconfigured four of the seven districts.

The redrawn district map came after the supreme court dismantled a key section of the 1965 Voting Rights Act and several southern states moved to redistrict and consolidate Republican power.

In June, the supreme court ruled that Alabama could use a redrawn congressional map that eliminates one of the state’s two majority-Black districts in this year’s midterm elections.

The new map poses steep challenges for Democratic representative Shomari Figures, who was first elected in 2024 after the creation a new district that included the mostly Black cities of Mobile and Montgomery. But the district no longer includes Mobile, Figures’s hometown.

State representative Rhett Marques, a Republican and a small-business owner who became part of the the new district, won the primary to challenge Figures in November.

Judge blocks USPS from implementing Trump mail-in voting restrictions

As we watch for election results tonight, we’re also following news that a federal judge blocked the US Postal Service (USPS) from implementing part of Donald Trump’s executive order on mail-in voting before the November midterm elections.

The president’s order had enabled the USPS to refuse to deliver ballots to states that did not comply with directives to turn over their voter rolls to federal officials.

US district judge Indira Talwani in Boston expanded on an earlier order from June that had prevented the administration from implementing restrictions in 23 mostly Democratic-led states.

Voting rights groups, joined by 23 states and the District of Columbia, sued the administration to stop the executive order, arguing that the US constitution does not authorize the president to issue orders governing the administration of elections.

ICE plans to spend up to $20m to purchase thousands of gloves that can deliver painful electric shocks for officers and agents by March, according to a notice published Monday by the Department of Homeland Security.

The devices, called Glove (Generated Low Output Voltage Emitter), are manufactured by Compliant Technologies LLC of Lexington, Kentucky.

According to the manufacturer, “the discomfort associated with the stimulation is usually effective in bringing individuals into compliance in less than three seconds and leaves no burns, marks or scars”. The company has advertised the device as a “humane, low-optics, de-escalation solution for many situations facing our men and women in uniform”.

The gloves have previously been used in settings such as jails in the US.

Jenn Rolnick Borchetta, deputy project director on policing at the American Civil Liberties Union, told the Associated Press that she questioned the use of the devices for civil immigration enforcement.

“ICE spent the last year showing this country they are too quick to use force. Now they will be able to deploy electric shocks with the slight push of a button that maybe nobody else can see them do,” she said. “Introducing gloves that can so easily be used to deliver terrible pain in encounters is a recipe for harm to the public.”
