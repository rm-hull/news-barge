Probation chief says he’s ‘not fully confident’ service will cope with early prisoner releases

Good morning. Andy Burnham is chairing a meeting of the government’s Cobra emergency committee this afternoon to discuss the heatwave and the climate crisis.

About time too, some people will say. One of them is Zack Polanski, the Green party leader, who has been calling for this and who posted this on social media in response.

Good.

Now we need action. Tax fossil fuel giants to pay for firefighting helicopters. A cool space in every community. A maximum working temperature.

And no new oil and gas extraction in the North Sea. We cannot negotiate with the science.

We are running a separate heatwave/climate live blog, and most of the coverage of the politics relating to this will be there.

But the Cobra announcement won’t deflect all attention from the prisons crisis. Yesterday Burnham said that he was “increasingly confident” that the new review of the early release scheme he has ordered will result in the killers of PC Andrew Harper not being let out. That has not silenced his critics. As Peter Walker, Rajeev Syal and Caroline Davies report, 50 police leaders, including every chief constable in England and Wales, have signed a letter urging him to do everything in his power to stop the killers benefiting from a programme designed to free up prison space, saying there is “a profound strength of feeling across policing” that this should not happen.

The Conservatives are claiming that the changes in approach to this issue adopted by No 10 over the past week – denounced as a U-turn in the Daily Express splash, and described as “chaos” in the Times – are “re-traumatising” victims. And there are new warnings about what will happen when the early release scheme does start.

Martin Jones, the chief inspector of probation for England and Wales, has said he is “not confident” the service will cope when the new round of releases starts in the autumn. In an interview with the BBC, he said:

Ultimately I’m not fully confident that the probation system will be able to cope with this massive work and there’s a risk that things will go wrong.

Probation services are already under unprecedented pressure.

They’re managing a caseload of 258,000 cases, that’s nearly three times the number that are in our prisons, and obviously the early release scheme will be increasing that pressure considerably.

In her interviews this morning, Anna Turley, the immigration minister, said the government was still negotiating with Napo, the probation union, in the hope of averting the strike action its members have voted for.

Asked about this, she told Sky News:

We’ll continue to negotiate with them.

“The justice secretary last year put in over £700m of extra money to support probation services. They’ve also had a pay increase this year.

So it’s really important that we look to make sure that they’ve got the support that they need. We’ll continue that negotiation. The justice secretary I know will be continuing to work with them.

Minister says poorer places have taken 'fair share' of asylum seekers, after PM suggests affluent areas should take more

Yesterday Andy Burnhamsuggested that more asylum seekers would need to be housed in more affluent areas in future. Unsurprisingly, this has not gone down well at the Daily Mail.

Mail splash Photograph: Daily Mail

In an interview on BBC Breakfast this morning, Anna Turley, the immigration minister, said that areas like Blackpool (where the Grand Metropole Hotel was until very recently being used to house asylum seekers – see 9.24am) have already taken their “fair share” of migrants.

Backing up the point Burnham made yesterday, she said:

I think this is about fairness, and we won’t be asking anyone to take more than they can.

It’s about their fair share, and we have to look across the country at what is appropriate for areas to take, what is the infrastructure, what is manageable with the local community. So this is about fairness.

She also defended the government’s intention to switch from using hotels for asylum seekers to putting them on industrial sites or former military bases instead. She said:

The priority for us now is making sure that we look after asylum seekers in a secure place, in a place that is much more appropriate and where we limit the impact on local communities.

Home Office says another 13 hotels no longer being used for asylum seekers

The Home Office has announced that 13 hotels in England are no longer being used to house asylum seekers. They include the Grand Metropole Hotel, Blackpool.

The government says this means 44 asylum hotels have closed so far this year. The number still being used is below 160, it says.

Anna Turley, the immigration minister, said:

For too long, communities have watched hotels at the centre of their towns and cities become symbols of a broken asylum system.

We are turning that around by ending the use of asylum hotels for good and moving people into larger, basic accommodation like former military sites.

Here is the full list of the 13 hotels no longer being used for asylum seekers: Ramada Jarvis Hotel, Loughborough; Chimney House Hotel, Sandbach; Britannia Ashley Hotel, Hale; Gold Thorn Hotel, Wolverhampton; Grand Metropole Hotel, Blackpool; Nottingham Hotel, Nottingham; Grosvenor Hotel, Stratford-upon-Avon; Staycity Apartments Deptford Bridge, Lewisham; Needham House, Hitchin; Holiday Inn, Bridgwater; Holiday Inn Express, Dunstable; Dubrovnik Hotel, Bradford; and Best Western Brook Hotel, Norwich.
