Healey tells Labour money New Labour had in 1990s 'is simply not there now'

The Labour MP Liam Byrne still gets criticised for the note he left in the Treasury after the 2010 election to his Tory successor saying “there is no money”. The note (often wrongly remember as saying there is no money left) was a joke, written when Byrne expected to be succeeded by a Tory with whom he had a friendly relationship, but the Lib Dem David Laws got the job instead. Laws gave it George Osborne and David Cameron and they spent years quoting it as alleged proof that Gordon Brown had bankrupted the country.

In his speech today John Healey used a similar line. I did not quote it earlier, so here it is.

Now I know the nineties is having a bit of a revival,

To Andy’s excitement, Oasis are going back on tour.

To Angela’s joy, Gladiators is back on Saturday night TV.

But I have to level with you, Conference.

The money New Labour had in the nineties is simply not there now.

Economic growth under Gordon was more than double the Tory years that followed.

And Britain has it harder now.

We had 14 years of flatlining Tory growth and living standards – when Cameron’s austerity gutted our public services.

When Johnson’s Brexit hammered our exports.

And Truss’s mini Budget crashed the economy and collapsed confidence in Britain’s fiscal strength.

They lost control of Britain’s finances.

They left a legacy of sky-high debt costs – for families and for government.

Rolls-Royce gives details of £300m investment announced by Healey

John Healey said Rolls-Royce is today announcing an investment worth £300m in the UK. (See 12.37pm.) It will cover manufacturing and engineering facilities in Derby, Bristol, Glasgow, Rotherham and Warwickshire.

Left to right: Alan Campbell, leader of the Commons; Angela Smith, leader of the Lords, and Lucy Powell, deputy Labour leader, listening to Healey’s speech. Photograph: Christopher Thomond/The Guardian

John Healey not letting anybody leave the Labour conference hall with any doubt he’s committed to the fiscal rules. But zero detail as to how he’ll do it.

John Healey‘s speech as chancellor so far has been a bit like an after dinner speech with nice anecdotes and rhetoric but little to say. But perhaps the most important thing to underline to the members - “the money is simply not there.”

Healey is stressing at considerable length that he will adhere to fiscal discipline in his forthcoming budget. It is redolent of Gordon Brown’s “prudence with a purpose”’

Healey says his priority is to revive economic growth on a foundation of fiscal discipline. The challenge will be to avoid the kind of tax rises to deliver those stronger public finances without choking off enterprise and growth. Not that simple

John Healey talking a lot about hope but basic message to Labour is there’s no money left - and won’t be without growth: ‘The money New Labour had in the 90s is simply not there now... Labour isn’t just here to cut the cake in a different way, we have to bake a bigger cake’

John Healey: “The money New Labour had in the 90s- just isn’t there now.” The political preparation for a budget Labour really won’t much like starts now. Sits uneasily with this.

“Without growth, there is no social democracy,” says John Healey, citing Tony Crosland. He’s right – and that’s why Labour needs to be far more radical on planning reform/Europe/AI adoption to boost growth.

Healey trying to play down tax rise fears by saying he wants growth to provide the extra money. But he knows that will take time, hence his warning that they don’t have enough money as when he was last in the Treasury. Echoes Burnham from last week, we can’t do everything we want to do. Happy vibes meet economic reality next month and we’re all still living off the (small) victory coat tails of Labour replacing their PM without too much of a hiccup and seeing a baby poll bounce ...

Andy Burnham congratulating John Healey after his speech. Photograph: Christopher Thomond/The Guardian

There is no other party in British politics that relishes its own past as much as Labour. Even when they liked their previous leaders, the Conservatives were not inclined to go on about them at party conference. The liberals have to go back more than 100 years to recall their glory days, and even the most ancient Lib Dem peer doesn’t have a memory that good. Other parties are short on hero figures in history. But Labour, in some respects, is the most conservative party on the block – constantly harking back. Only among this tribe would a tie once worn by a chancellor more than 20 years ago gain the status of a relic.

So, in that sense, Healey’s speech was perfectly crafted – rich with sentimental references to the party’s history, and the nation’s too. But wallowing in nostalgia will only get you so far, and at times it felt this was serving as cover for the fact that Healey did not have a lot new to say. That is not necessarily the chancellor’s fault; he was a surprise appointment in a new government, and a lot of issues remain unresolved.

There were a smattering of smallish announcements in the speech. But the main takeaways were messages that we have already heard from the government, but which Healey felt were important enough to repeat to the Labour conference: that the cost of servicing debt is crippling (see 12.20pm), that he’s fully committed to the fiscal rules (see 12.25pm), and that welfare reform is a “moral duty” (see 12.31pm). This platform is hard to distinguish from Rachel Reeves’.

Perhaps the most interesting line came at the end, after Healey referenced Tony Crosland (another Labour history lesson). Healey said:

That’s the lesson for our generation. And Labour isn’t just here to cut the cake in a different way.

We have to bake a bigger cake and make sure that it’s fairly shared.

This is a much wiser lesson than Boris Johnson’s cakeism. But it begs the question, how? On this, Healey did not really provide answers.

Healey stresses importance of growth in speech peroration

Healey ended his speech with a passage about growth.

It was 70 years ago at a Labour conference that Tony Crosland published his book on the future of our party [the Future of Socialism].

He wrote it then believing that economic growth will take care of itself, and that Labour’s job was simply to share out the proceeds.

Twenty years later he realised he’d been wrong. The growth doesn’t just happen. We have to work for it. We have to create the conditions for it and without growth there is no social democracy. You can’t fund ends without the means.

And that’s the lesson for our generation. And Labour isn’t just here to cut the cake in a different way.

We have to bake a bigger cake and make sure that it’s fairly shared.

So whether it’s the cost of business or the cost of living, the only long term answer is growth. Good growth in every postcode, a dual mission from the Treasury and No 10 North.

Healey said London should not have to “shoulder the economic burden alone”. The government wanted growth in every postcode, he said.

Growth wasn’t just a graph or spreedsheet, he said. It was about whether a family could afford to go on holiday.

At the start of his speech, Healey talked about having a miner’s lamp on a shelf at home and he came back to that as he ended his speech.

When you leave this conference, remember that miner’s lamp, those workers didn’t carry it for themselves. They carried it for those beside them. They carried it for their community. They carried it for the ones who came after.

That is our job now, to light the way for a generation that’s been told there is no way through.

Good work, fresh hope, a new age of industry and the solidarity that has always carried this country forward.

Healey said the government would not let AI leave workers behind.

AI is the greatest innovation of our time. It’s a general purpose technology that will simply transform our society at every level.

Every business, every community, every hospital, every school, everywhere can benefit from adopting AI.

It’s at the centre of a groundswell of new world-leading British tech companies, and it’s the driving force with which this new age of industrialisation can be built.

Yes, we need to face the serious risks to national security, to personal data and to our children’s well-being, and it will change our labour market in fundamental ways.

But I give you this commitment. We won’t let AI leave Britain’s workers behind, and we will make sure that Britain wins in this AI revolution.

Healey says he will revive union learning fund axed by Tories

Healey gives credit to the GMB union for getting the shipyards investments “over the line”.

He says he is proud of the partnership with unions. And he defends having a union figures on the Bank of England’s court, saying “Britain’s economic success is built on the success of working people”.

He also says he will re-establish the union learning fund, a scheme set up by the Tony Blair government to fund workplace learning but axed by the Tories.

I’m proud of the fact that last time 180,000 workers every year got new training through that fund, and we’ll do that again.

Better skills, better prospects for British workers – because of the decisions taken by this Labour government.

Healey says Rolls-Royce is today announcing investment worth an extra £300m in its British factories in Derby, in Bristol and Glasgow, and in Rotherham.

And he says that is an example of what he means by proposing a “new age of industrialisation”. (See 7.54am.)

Healey announces new local apprenticeships service

Healey says, as a downpayment on the Milburn report, he is announcing a local apprenticeships service

He says it will be “led by mayors, targeted at these young people, delivering thousands more apprenticeships for these young people with local teams – a bit like football scouts for apprenticeships – local teams who will go to local firms that have never taken on a trainee and say ‘We’ll help you do it.’

Every mayor will be able to take part.

And there will be £100m in funding for the scheme, funded by savings from the DWP.

Healey says it's 'not progressive to allow bigger and bigger benefits bill'

Healey says the Alan Milburn report on Neets, out soon, will show the scale of the challenge facing young people.

He goes on:

Our social security system must always be there for those who can never work.

But for everyone else, benefits should be a bridge to work, to wider participation, to a better life, not a trap that people can’t escape.

And, conference, it is simply not progressive to allow a bigger and bigger benefits bill, and a system which offers an income but doesn’t offer a future.

So once again, as a Labour government, it falls to us to act.

And this is more than a just an economic must. This is a moral duty.

I refuse to write off a million young people like the Tories have done.

John Healey speaking at the Labour conference. Photograph: Phil Noble/Reuters

On day one as chancellor I said my first duty is fiscal discipline, and I’ll tell you why.

It underwrites every promise this government makes on growth, on jobs, on national security and on public services. We can’t succeed without it.

And that’s why the prime minister and I are united in meeting the fiscal rules, balancing the books with a buffer against uncertainty, controlling borrowing to bring down inflation, and reducing long term pressures on our finances, and confidence.

That commitment is so important. There is nothing progressive about losing control because it’s always working people who pay for it.
