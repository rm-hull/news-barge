A top North Yorkshire school has said banning phones during the teaching day has resulted in students having “no pressure to check a screen and no digital world competing for their attention”.

Cundall Manor, a private school near Easingwold, said its pupils have “always handed their phones in at the door”.

It comes after the government announced a smartphone ban in English schools from June.

Most schools in York had already banned phones during the school day prior to the government announcement, with education leaders saying the measures brought positive benefits for staff and students.

A Cundall Manor spokesperson said banning mobile phones has improved pupils’ focus and meant “break times really are a break”.

“Pupils talk, play sport, head outside, find friends from other year groups or simply have a moment to reset before their next lesson,” they said.

“There is no pressure to check a screen and no digital world competing for their attention.

“Perhaps most importantly, it gives genuine relationships room to develop.”

They added: “While the world beyond our gates becomes increasingly digital, we think there is something rather valuable about giving children the school day to concentrate on being exactly that: children.”

Meanwhile, Fulford School was among the schools in York to have banned phones during the day before the government made it law.

  Russell Harris, head teacher at Fulford School 

*(Image: Supplied)*

Fulford School’s headteacher, Russell Harris, told The Press more students were enjoying being young since the school banned mobile phones.

“We’ve seen some more people kicking a ball about at break time, throwing a frisbee, just enjoying themselves and being young – being young people without the hassle of worrying about a mobile phone,” Mr Harris said.

Mr Harris also welcomed then prime minister, Sir Keir Starmer, announcing a ban on under-16s using social media.

Sir Keir said the ban, due to be implemented next Spring, was made in response to concerns social media is unsafe, making children miserable and could cause lasting damage to their mental health.

He acknowledged some youngsters would try to find their way around a ban but said he would not compromise on the “safety and happiness of our children”.

Mr Harris said the under-16s social media ban represented a “significant – and in many ways necessary – step towards protecting young people in a digital world that has evolved faster than safeguarding measures have kept pace with”.

He added that the social media ban should come as part of a broader strategy to reduce “unnecessary digital pressures” facing young people.

“By reducing unnecessary digital pressures, we can give students the time, space, and clarity to build healthy habits and strong relationships – both online and offline,” Mr Harris said.

“We fully support measures that help create calm, purposeful environments in which all students can learn, feel safe, and thrive.”
