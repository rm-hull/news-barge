Hastie ramps up fight against One Nation, labels Hanson ‘weak and glass jawed’

Andrew Hastie has continued to step up the fight against One Nation, after one of its cartoon Please Explain videos referred to the Liberal MP and SAS veteran as a “traitor” after he gave evidence against Ben Roberts-Smith in his failed defamation claim.

Hastie tells RN Breakfast the cartoon effectively attacked not just him but all veterans.

Yesterday he told News24 Hanson should “own” the cartoon because Hanson said she hadn’t called Hastie a “traitor”, or take it down. Hanson then refused to take down the video and accused Hastie of making a “mountain out of molehill”.

Hastie this morning says he won’t take legal action against Hanson, because she’s backed by mining billionaire Gina Rinehart, but promises to make it a costly battle politically.

She’s not fit to govern this country. And she’s also cast a massive slur on veterans around the country who think that Australian soldiers should hold high standards of conduct on the battlefield.

It’s actually an attack on veterans, not just an attack on me, because you’ve got to remember more than 20 plus SAS combat veterans were called to give evidence in the federal court. Are they traitors too? This question she won’t answer and I think it shows weakness from her.

I’m not after legal action, particularly when she’s financed by Australia’s richest person. Why would I walk into a fight that I can’t win financially? But yes, I’m going to impose costs politically.

Israeli government’s policies and actions ‘unconscionable’

Julian Hill says he’s been “extremely outspoken” about the increasing settler violence against Palestinians in the West Bank, but won’t say how far the government will go with new sanctions.

The foreign minister, Penny Wong, this week announced the government would take more action against the settlements, but wouldn’t follow the UK in implementing trade bans.

Hill tells RN Breakfast:

This is against international law, and the current Israeli government’s policies and actions are unconscionable. The government has already taken significant steps. We’ve sanctioned Israeli ministers, we’ve sanctioned individuals, we’ve sanctioned entities.

We never speculate on specific measures before we take them. Sanctions are ultimately an expression, a serious powerful legal tool, an expression of our national interest looking at the international circumstance. And when the Foreign Minister signs off on new measures, she’ll announce them at that time.

Labor introducing new laws to crack down on illegal tobacco

Labor will introduce new laws to make it easier to prosecute criminals involved in the illicit tobacco trade, as the government fights an uphill battle against the exploding black market.

The new laws will target organised crime, distributors and retailers selling illegal cigarettes.

The assistant customs minister, Julian Hill, is this morning announcing legislation that he says will target organised crime, distributors and retailers selling black market ciggies.

He tells RN Breakfast the customs rules were designed for small-scale “revenue fraud, you know, under-declaring, smuggling a little bit of stuff in” and need to be completely overhauled.

The new laws think about it as a list of observable indicators, very simple, fact-based things like: is the tobacco appropriately packaged? Is it a menthol cigarette even hiding in plain packaging? Is it being concealed? Does it have paperwork at the border? Or my personal favourite: is the retail price of this packet of cigarettes less than the amount of excise and GST combined? If any of these facts are proven, just one of them, that establishes the reasonable suspicion of the criminal offence of importing, possession, or conveying. It’ll give powerful new tools to Border Force but every state and territory police force to go after the crooks.

‘We need to explain Australia doesn’t have a migration crisis’: Malinauskas

Peter Malinaukas says he understands the concerns of Australians worried about migration and the housing shortage in Australia, but that the answer is politicians need to explain there is a housing crisis not a migration crisis happening.

He tells the ABC’s AM program that Australia will soon have more people over 65 than under 15 and migration will be essential to replace the ageing workforce.

We need to explain that Australia doesn’t really have a migration crisis so much as a housing crisis. If we have a housing crisis, we would do well to focus our skills policy on making sure that we’re bringing in the very workers that we require in the housing construction industry. You know, Australia’s got a massive shortage of almost every trade.

Everyone, I think, accepts that it needs to be a well-calibrated, balanced migration program. My point is that we haven’t seen much evidence in the political discourse in this country in the last few years making the case for migration. Just to be seeing be a lot of culture wars being executed against migration. And I think that’s self-defeating, and we’ve got a responsibility to stand up against it.

But when asked what the magic number is for a well-balanced migration program, Malinauskas won’t say.

Instead of coming up with an arbitrary number and then cutting different parts of the migration program, Malinaukas says governments should work from the ground up, determining the skills shortages, and growth in regional areas, and then coming up with a target.

Don’t appease One Nation on migration, Malinauskas warns

The South Australian premier, Peter Malinauskas, has warned the Albanese government not to join the “madness” of cutting migration to appease One Nation.

In a speech in Sydney last night, Malinauskas – whose grandparents migrated to Australia after the second world war – said the wave of populist politics has “more than arrived in Australia”, but the government should push back.

Above all else, [we must] preserve our strong migration program … The correct political response to anti-immigration politics and populists is not appeasement.

More troubling is the prospect of mainstream politics entering a political race to the bottom for who can cut migration numbers the most. This is not unfortunate – this is madness.

There could be no greater act of national economic self harm at this time than to slash migration numbers for political purposes.

He also warns against cutting international student numbers, calling it “crazy”.

Imagine if we did this to the mining industry, there world be an ad on every television station and mobile phone about how we’re killing the goose that lays the golden egg. It is crazy.

In Victoria a car is stolen or broken into every 43 minutes: data

Melbourne recorded more insurance claims for stolen and broken-into cars than all other Australian capital cities combined, according to the Insurance Council of Australia.

Property crime data released by the ICA showed Victoria had the highest rates of car theft and home burglary claims in the country in the last financial year. Analysis of Insurance Statistics Australia (ISA) data showed that there were more than 12,300 claims – costing insurers $244m – lodged in the state for cars stolen or broken into.

In the same time period, every other state and territory recorded a decline.

The ICA’s chief executive, Andrew Hall, said in Victoria a car was stolen or broken into every 43 minutes while a home is broken into every two hours.

The insurance data shows Victoria has a crime problem, and it is being felt directly by households, businesses and communities.

The ICA has called for a statewide strategy and better intelligence sharing, the establishment of centrally coordinated clean-up arrangements, and the abolishment of stamp duty on business, home and contents insurance products.

It also called for improving insurance affordability for healthcare professionals, community organisations and live music venues by “modernising civil liability laws”.

Hopes Australia would join UK trade ban on Israeli settlements in West Bank

Speaking to the ABC’s 7.30, Emily Thornberry, the chair of the UK’s foreign affairs select committee, was asked whether she hoped Australia would join the UK’s trade ban decision:

I think it was hoped that they might but, you know, all countries have their own decisions to make. We all want the same thing and nobody says this is easy.

On Tuesday, the UK government announced it would ban imported goods from illegal settlements in the occupied territories. Australia is preparing another round of sanctions against Israel but has stopped short of joining the UK’s trade ban.

Thornberry said she had a “a lot of time” for the foreign affairs minister, Penny Wong:

I don’t know the basis upon which she’s made a decision not to be involved with us, but I’m sure that you know she believes that what she’s doing is in the interests of Palestinians and in the interests of justice.

She later added:

I’m always happy to talk to my Australian friends and maybe even persuade them.

Australia should ‘put shoulder to the wheel’ on multilateral diplomacy: Rudd

Tom McIlroy

Former prime minister Kevin Rudd is in Canberra for a round of high-profile speeches this week, marking his return to Australia after serving as ambassador to Washington DC.

Rudd spoke at the Australian Institute of International Affairs on Wednesday night and will be at the National Press Club today.

He said Australia must continue to push multilateral diplomacy, even as Donald Trump withdraws the US from a range of important global institutions. Australia, as a leading middle power, should promote the United Nations system, which has support across Asia and Europe, according to Rudd.

There remains an opportunity for Australia, in partnership with other middle powers around the world, to put our shoulders to the wheel in supporting those UN multilateral institutions which now find themselves under financial and institutional pressure as a result of the US withdrawing.

Australia remains active in the UN, the IMF and the World Bank. We remain active in the G20 and in APEC.

We remain active in major regional institutions, such as the Pacific Islands Forum, where the government has once again registered a remarkable series of foreign policy achievements.

Rudd said this kind of serious engagement would “put flesh on the bones” of the plan outlined by the Canadian prime minister, Mark Carney, at Davos earlier this year.

And it would mark a deeply practical contribution to sustaining the multilateral rules-based system as America resolves its internal domestic debate on its future posture within it.
