FOR a post-war generation of Bradfordians, a night at the theatre was woven into the fabric of local life. Long before television had found its way into every household, people could see a different show each week, performed by a cast of regular players – from comedy and thrillers to farces and North Country family dramas. It was called Rep – short for repertory – and the Prince’s Theatre on Little Horton Lane could boast one of the most successful companies in a nationwide network run by a larger-than-life impresario named Harry Hanson. They were called the Court Players.

Today, Rep has vanished in all but a handful of towns and cities – in most places, it’s something that belonged to a Britain of street-corner newspaper sellers and rattling electric trams; of smog in winter, and works outings to the seaside in summer. But the memories live on…

Jean Kitson was a mainstay of the Court Players’ company in Bradford during the fifties, and after leaving the stage to start a family loved to remember her days in Rep – days of a new show every week, an extraordinary range of parts, frequent mishaps and working with many actors who went on to national fame.

  Jean Kitson was a stage stalwart in Bradford.


Before her death in 2020, aged 91, Jean committed those memories to paper in a booklet intended purely as a family memento. But now her story has been published for a wider audience – whether people with their own memories of those long-ago days or anyone interested in an important chapter in British theatre history.

The book is called Jean Steps Out, echoing the title of the play that provided her favourite role. That play was a comedy, Jane Steps Out, and in a bonus for readers of the book, an original 1948 sound recording of part of a Court Players’ production starring Jean has been recovered and restored from a privately-made 78 rpm record. Readers can access the recording via a QR code in the book.

Jean’s story is told with warmth, humour and affection, with Bradford one of the main backdrops, not least for her friendship with an actress who later became one of Britain’s best-known TV stars – Pat Phoenix, known to millions as Elsie Tanner in Coronation Street.

Back at the Prince’s in 1953 she was known as Patricia Pilkington and shared a dressing room with Jean, who was by her side as bridesmaid for her first wedding, to fellow Court Player Peter Marsh at Bradford Cathedral.

  Jean (seated, left) on one of the regular public appearances made by the Court Players around Bradford


The theatre was finally demolished in 1964 as part of Bradford’s post-war redevelopment. Where once it stood, just in front of the National Science and Media Museum, thousands of people pass by every day, while the echoes of yesterday’s laughter and applause inevitably fade deeper into memory.

As the publisher of Jean Steps Out, keeping some of those memories alive has been a labour of love; Jean was my mum, and it is a privilege to share her story with people in Bradford and beyond.

In this extract from the book, Jean remembers one of her earliest starring roles at the Prince’s Theatre, in February 195:

“After a season away in Chester, I was looking forward to a short break before rejoining the cast at the Theatre Royal in Leeds. It was Monday morning, and I was just finishing my breakfast at home in Morley when there was an urgent rapping at the front door. I opened it to find the director from the Prince’s on the doorstep and a taxi waiting outside the front gate.

In a few brief sentences he informed me that one of the actresses in Bradford who was due to appear that same night had been taken ill and… would I take her place? It wasn’t until he’d got me safely in the taxi and we were hurtling towards Bradford that I discovered the part I was expected to perform that same evening was that of Aunt Anna Rose, the leading role in Treasure Hunt – a part made famous in the West End by Dame Sybil Thorndike.

It turned out to be quite a day. Apart from the fact that I knew nothing at all about the play and there was a discrepancy of almost sixty years between my own age and that of Aunt Anna Rose, the costumes and shoes didn’t fit, and the wig was far too tight. In between trying to digest chunks of dialogue my stage outfits had to be let out here, pinned up there, my hair was somehow crammed into a very small wig and my size 6 feet wedged into a pair of size 5 shoes.

I suppose a small consolation was that being so physically uncomfortable helped to take my mind off the main problem, which was how to get through the night’s performance without making a complete fool of myself. There was just one supporting factor. According to the script, Aunt Anna Rose, having supposedly spent much of her life abroad and being a very eccentric old lady, spent most of her time sitting inside a sedan chair. This had a little window at the side, and it was through this that most of her lines were spoken.

  Alan's new book, Jean Steps Out


This was an enormous help of course, as I was able to keep the script on my knee and steal surreptitious glances at it.

Well! We got through the performance somehow and the next night the theatre was invaded by reporters from local newspapers, anxious to get some quotes and take photos. I still have a few pages in an old scrapbook which show me going through the painful procedure of being prised into Aunt Anna Rose’s costume yet again, assuring the assembled reporters that “Yes, I will be playing the part for the rest of the week and hope to be word perfect very shortly.” So that was that.

Next day the headlines were all along the same lines: “SOS FOR JEAN AND THE SHOW GOES ON!” Which thankfully it always did.”
