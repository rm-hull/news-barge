Questions are being asked over why a man was taken to court for moving a dead deer off the A66 amid “scare and stretched” police and court resources.

  Church magazine editor Guy Carpenter was prosecuted by Durham Police after scooping up the carcass off the __A66 at Greta Bridge, near Barnard Castle__, on June 7. 

The 42-year-old had moved the animal from the central reservation with the aim of taking it home to eat - but was stopped by police officers and told he was committing a crime.

The Dalton man found himself in front of magistrates in Peterlee on Monday (September 7) where an out-of-court resolution was put forward by lawyers.

But Durham Council deputy leader Darren Grimes has today questioned why it was taken so far, saying there is a “public interest test in this country for a reason” and that it exists “precisely so that cases like this one do not end up in front of magistrates”.

Benjamin Elks, grassroots development manager at the TaxPayers' Alliance, also said that taxpayers will “struggle to understand why scarce police and court resources are being spent pursuing a case like this.”

“With forces under pressure to tackle serious crime, officials should be able to show that prosecutions are proportionate, necessary and genuinely in the public interest,” Mr Elks said.

Cllr Grimes said he does not believe the public interest test has been “properly applied” in this case and “does not believe any reasonable person in County Durham would think it had”.

He said: “People in this county are told regularly that resources are stretched.

“They are told there isn't the capacity to attend a burglary, or to do much about the quad bikes going missing off farms, the illegal bikes in our residential areas or the shoplifting that has become effectively decriminalised on our high streets.

“Those same people are now watching officers, lawyers and a magistrates' court spend months on a dead deer that nobody wanted, that nobody owned in any meaningful sense.

“Had this been found on Durham County Council's highway and not National Highways, we certainly would not have viewed it as a good use of public resources to pursue this through the courts.”

Mr Carpenter, a self-confessed nature lover, said it did “not feel as if it is in the public interest” following the hearing in Peterlee on Monday.

He said the incident, and subsequent looming court date, had “taken a toll” on both his physical and mental health, questioning who benefits from taking the matter to court.

Mr Carpenter, who was also a volunteer at the now closed Wetheriggs Animal Sanctuary, said he has, for many years, picked up roadkill to clear the road or make use of the meat.

  Mr Carpenter, who edits the magazine for churches in the Holmedale Benefice north of Richmond, was prosecuted by __Durham Police__ under The Deer Act 1991.

This states that it is an offence to take or move the carcass of a deer without the consent of the owner or land occupier.

It is understood that, under UK law, deer are considered ownerless until they are killed, after which it becomes the property of the land owner where it was found.

As the carcass was found on the A66, the owner deer would technically be National Highways.

Following his appearance in court, Mr Carpenter was granted conditional bail and the hearing was adjourned until October 26, unless a resolution is found outside of court.
