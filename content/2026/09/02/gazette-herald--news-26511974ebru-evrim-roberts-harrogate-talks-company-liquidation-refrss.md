The entrepreneur behind a highly regarded North Yorkshire Pilates company has recalled how she fought to save the business and deal with many other challenges.

Ebru Evrim Roberts has spoken to The Press about the voluntary liquidation of Ebru Evrim Ltd, a limited company based in Harrogate.

A liquidator’s report known as a statement of affairs says the company had an estimated deficiency or debts of more than £1 million, including £6,696.81 the company owed Ebru as an employee.

The 46-year-old, who formed the company in 2019, says a company going into liquidation “is rarely explained by one decision or moment.”

Ebru had a background in pathology and biological science, followed by professional experience in marketing, and many years of studying, teaching and building a career in Pilates and Yoga.

She established the limited company after building up a successful career as a sole trader and developed a strong following around her teaching.

Ebru explained: “I believed deeply that what I was teaching could benefit more people, and I wanted to turn that passion into a sustainable business. I had professional advisers around me and, like any entrepreneur, I believed in what we were building.”

However, several months after establishing the business, the pandemic began, making the following two years “extraordinarily difficult.”

Ebru continued: “Like thousands of businesses, we were trying to survive circumstances none of us had planned for. My family and investors put significant money into supporting the company because we believed it could survive and ultimately become successful.”

Coming out of the pandemic, the business was able to move from Skipton to larger premises in Harrogate, where Ebru worked hard to make it succeed.

At the time the company had two directors, with Ebru very much its public face.

Ebru says she became increasingly concerned about the company’s financial position and took on a more active role, bringing in extra financial expertise to advise her.

She told The Press: “We made difficult changes. We reviewed the operation, reduced costs, closed areas that weren't performing and changed our pricing, which initially meant losing some members. We then rebuilt.

“The results of that work were significant. Over approximately 18 months, the company's turnover increased by around 100 per cent.

“That didn't happen accidentally. It came through an enormous amount of work, commitment and determination from me, alongside the support and guidance of my business advisers and investors, to turn the business around.”

However, the business faced rising costs like wages, tax, VAT, utilities, pensions and other obligations. The business also faced “historic liabilities from incredibly difficult trading periods.”

Ebru continued: “Then the economic environment changed dramatically. Energy and operating costs increased, wages increased, the cost-of-living crisis affected customers and businesses alike, and the impact of the war in Ukraine contributed to inflationary pressures across the economy.”

She continued fighting for the business, with small improvements in profitability, giving her hope for success until a rent review happened. This rent was “significantly higher” than previously and combined with increases in business rates and other operating costs “fundamentally changed the calculation.”

Ebru spoke with her advisors and those financially supporting the company and they concluded the business was no longer sustainable, and they made the difficult decision to close the business and enter voluntary liquidation.

Voluntary liquidation is a standard, legally advised process for insolvent companies, and not a choice to simply walk away from debt.

Ebru could have kept trying to keep the company alive, she says, but this could have allowed the liabilities to become larger. Instead, she focussed on closing as responsibly as possible, not shutting the doors overnight.

Ebru explained: “We gave our clients and teachers time, and I personally took on financial commitments because I wanted to do everything reasonably possible for the people who had worked for us.”

Not only did the company take up years of Ebru’s life but also significant family and investor support.

She said: “There were many nights when I didn't sleep because I was carrying the responsibility of keeping the business, its employees and its community going.”

Ebru seeks not to escape responsibility, but says knowing what she does know, there are decisions that would have been made differently. She also believes a businessperson’s ability should not be judged from a single figure in a liquidation document, figures which are currently estimates and may change.

Ebru said: “I took a business through a pandemic, rebuilt it, substantially increased its turnover, created employment and opportunities for teachers, brought in professional advice when I recognised that it was needed, invested personally in trying to save the company and ultimately made the decision to stop when continuing would have risked creating greater losses.”

Ebru believes losses are part of entrepreneurship, and she says she did all she reasonably could to protect people around the business and bring it to an orderly conclusion.

Undeterred, she has now launched a separate business in Leeds called Evrimotion, with a different business model and a primary focus on Pilates education and teacher training.

Ebru added: “I remain incredibly grateful to everyone who taught with us, worked with us, invested in us and, most importantly, trusted us as clients. The experience has taught me lessons about business that no textbook could have taught me, and those lessons will stay with me for the rest of my professional life.”
