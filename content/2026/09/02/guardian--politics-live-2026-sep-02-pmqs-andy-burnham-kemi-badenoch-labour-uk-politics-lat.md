Burnham offers to work with Tories on getting welfare spending down

Kemi Badenoch starts by congratulating the PM “on his stamina – three hours”. (That generates some laughter.) She asks if Burnham can name a single spending request to which he said no.

Burnham says he was clear about the need to get welfare spending down.

It is not right to have so many young people on benefits. He wants to invest in their success.

This is an area where both parties can engage “in problem solving, not point scoring’.

So much for the talk of a new-look PMQs. (See 11.12am.) Although Andy Burnham and his team have spoken about adopting a new approach to the traditional Commons joust with the leader of the opposition, in the end this felt much like business as usual. Burnham was more willing to engage with Kemi Badenoch’s questions than Keir Starmer was (certainly in the late Starmer phase), and he did make a point of stressing several times his desire to work with opposition parties. But, although he has made “problem-solving, not point-scoring” his mantra, Burnham was quite happy to resort to point-scoring. His main blast was directed at Andrew Griffith, the new shadow chancellor, and his role in the Liz Truss mini-budget.

Burnham may not have been particularly innovative, but he was good; confident, plausible, quite funny. In the exchanges with Badenoch, there was no obvious winner, but he had the best single put-down. That came when Badenoch asked:

The prime minister told the house that his summer of promises would be paid for by reprioritize within government. So what exactly has he cut?

Burnham replied: “In terms of what has he cut? Tax is the simple answer.” This was a reference to the point he made yesterday about how his early announcements included two tax cuts in his first week.

While Burnham and Badenoch will both be fairly happy with how the exchanges went, one person less pleased will be the PM’s diary secretary. Burnham did seem to agree to a lot of meetings with MPs wanting to discuss this, that or the other. (Starmer would have fobbed them off with a meeting with a minister, which is what Burnham himself will probably be doing before too long.) And this did seem to confirm Badenoch’s point about Burnham being a people pleaser. (See 12.09pm.) She claimed that, in response to 125 questions yesterday, Burnham did not once say no to a spending request. It is a fair line of attack, although probably not one that will be available for long because Burnham will soon discover that he cannot do this job without having to disappoint.

Burnham suggests he has committed to getting defence spending to 3% of GDP by 2030

During PMQs Tan Dhesi, the Labour chair of the Commons defence committee, asked Burnham if he would commit to raising defence spending to 3% of GDP by 2030.

He said:

It is imperative that we invest in our armed forces in the here and now, rather than be sorry later. Indeed, defence is definitely one area where vibes alone just isn’t going to be enough.

So rather than kick the can down the road waiting for a fiscal event in the future, will the prime minister urgently commit to charting a pathway to reach 3% of GDP spend on defence by 2030, which, as his chancellor rightly noted, is critical to keep our country safe.

John Healey resigned as defence secretary when Keir Starmer was PM because Starmer would not commit to raising defence spending to 3% of GDP by 2030. He is now chancellor, but has not publicly committed to reaching the 3% target he insisted was essential by 2030.

In reply, Burnham said:

I’ve made that commitment, made it very clear, and I have done for a considerable period of time. And I was in Ukraine last week for a very specific reason to repeat it on the world stage. There [Dhesi] can be absolutely sure of that.

This sounded like a clear commitment to 3% by 2030.

But there was some doubt about his intention because earlier during the exchanges, when Kemi Badenoch said that the armed forces needed a plan to hit 3% now, Burnham in reply just repeated the commitment to get defence spending up to 3.5% by 2035. (See 12.18pm.)

Samantha Niblett (Lab) tells Burnham that this is her first question at a PMQs too. Niblett is the MP who made headlines with her “summer of sex” campaign, and she says he has had a tremendous response to it. She says:

I have discovered that shame, guilt and embarrassment around talking about sex and body parts like penis and vagina is even leading to late diagnosis of health conditions and illnesses such as cancer.

She asks if Burnham will meet her to discuss her campaign.

Burnham says he will. He says Niblett has been raising important issues, and he jokes that her summer sounds as if it was more exciting than his.

Jack Rankin, who has just been appointed as Kemi Badenoch’s PPS, says Burnham did not answer any of Badenoch’s questions. He asks if Burnham can say what the fiscal rules are, and categorically say he will stick to them.

Burnham replies:

No borrowing to fund day to day spending, debt falling as a percentage of GDP by the end of the parliament. And yes, we will.

Helena Dollimore, the Labour MP for Hastings and Rye, welcomes a scheme that will ensure that young people from her constituency get access to the Bayeaux Tapestry exhibition.

Burnham praises Dollimore for her campaigning on this. He says he would like all young people to be able to see the tapestry that they learn about at school.

Andrew George (Lib Dem) asks if Burnham will back his private members’ bill to give local communities more power over housing. He says this is a particularly issue in Cornwall.

Kim Leadbeater (Lab) asks what Burnham will do to help people suffering from loneliness, or anti-social behaviour.

Burnham thanks Leadbeater for raising these issues. He raised them in the Makerfield byelection, he says. On antisocial behaviour, he says he accepts the broken windows theory – that small acts of criminality should not be ignored.

Bradley Thomas (Con) asks if Burnham will meet him to discuss a new approach to protecting the green belt.

Burnham says as mayor of Greater Manchester he adopted a brownfield first approach. But there is a balance to be struck, he says. He says the Tories should not turn their back on the need for more homes.

Sorcha Eastwood (Alliance) says the Northern Ireland executive cannot function properly under the rules it has to follow (for cross-community government, giving nationalists and unionists in effect a veto over any decisions). She asks if Burnham will reform the situation.

Burnham says he will address this.

Our first responsibility is to get Stormont government up and running and functioning properly with a budget. I’ve empowered the Northern Ireland secretary to go into talks to, to resolve that issue and the broader issues about reform.

Lindsay Hoyle, the Speaker, accepts Burnham is trying to answer questions, but says he is taking too long. At this rate, they won’t get through all the questions, he says,

Burnham says he wants closer relationship with EU - but ducks Lib Dem challenge to back joining single market

Ed Davey, the Lib Dem leader, says Burnham was right yesterday to blame Brexit for the UK’s lack of growth. The answer to the problem is staring us in the face, he says – join the single market.

Burnham says he is glad Davey accepts his analysis. He says he wants a closer relationship with the EU, building on the work of Keir Starmer. The EU’s “Made in Europe” plan could pose real problems for British steelmakers.

Davey says he hopes Burnham will rejoin the single market.

He says in North Devon a maternity unit has been closed, meaning women have to travel two hours to give birth. They are being taught how to give birth in cars. This is “unthinktable”, he says. Will he commit to reopening the maternity unit within days?

Burnham says he will commit to reopening it – but not within days.

He says there are structural problems with maternity units in the country.
