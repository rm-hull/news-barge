The same week union members at the University of Sydney agreed to a 24-hour strike, staff received the results of an internal survey.

In the faculty of arts and social sciences, 0% agreed with the statement: “I trust the University of Sydney to act in the best interests of staff, students and the broader community.”

It was the issue of trust, alongside longstanding concerns over staff protections from artificial intelligence, workload fairness and job security, that saw about 2,000 members to walk off the job on Wednesday.

From 7am, about 500 people were stationed at picket lines at each of the inner Sydney campus’s entrances, armed with flags, banners and megaphones.

The University of Sydney advised students “almost all” scheduled classes would move online as a result of the strike, while others were rescheduled or cancelled.

Peter Chen, the branch president of the National Tertiary Education Union (NTEU) at the University of Sydney, said management had rebuffed the union “at every opportunity” to enforce appropriate protections around the use of AI.

“Higher education is the canary in the coalmine,” he said. “Where we go on AI … will travel through the rest of the economy.”

![Students and staff gather at the University of Sydney as staff take strike action](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/f1657803bdaaed002f9e1b773114894f611c7c0f/0_0_6278_4187/master/6278.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

The New South Wales division secretary of the NTEU, Vince Caughley, said the University of Sydney’s own green paper advised AI was the most “fundamental transformation” education had seen, pointing to AI chatbots that were already being used to help teach online-only classes at other universities.

“We are asking for best practice, we are asking for due diligence,” he said. “Not their own commercialisation aims and certainly not the interest of venture capital.”

The union was confident it would reach an agreement with management within the year but had concerns over the lack of senior leadership at the table, including the provost, Prof Annamarie Jagose. The last bargaining process represented the longest running industrial action at an Australian university, and the prospect of future strikes remained active.

![Students and staff gather at the University of Sydney as staff take strike action](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/064253aa7bd881fcfb6c6d9162f809c1406f77c1/0_0_8192_5464/master/8192.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

A spokesperson for the University of Sydney said it was “continuing to engage” with the union and maintained industrial action was “premature”.

“We agree using AI ethically requires a human-centred approach, and our … principles and guardrails that govern use have been developed in consultation with our community,” they said.

“We support many of the objectives of the unions’ claims, but believe it’s more appropriate to implement many of these through relevant policies, instead of in the Enterprise Agreement.”

There are broader concerns, some interlinked and widespread in the sector. Growing class sizes and reduced student satisfaction. A new proposal for teaching-only roles with no research component or scope for future growth.

Prof Laura Shepherd in the school of social and political sciences is part of the bargaining team and said students often sacrificed a “huge amount” to come to the university, including spending $50,000 on degrees.

“They come here … for quality and access to research leaders,” she said. “I think we should be very ashamed if we allow anything in the enterprise agreement that threatens that.”

The University of Sydney’s NTEU branch is the largest in Australia, and the university has long been a hotbed for activism. As management approaches its fifth month of bargaining, it is also grappling with continued opposition to its response to Gaza and restrictions on protest.

New restrictions were brought in as a result of the university’s pro-Palestine encampment in 2024, which received intense scrutiny during this year’s royal commission. On Wednesday, keffiyehs and pro-Palestine banners stood out among purple NTEU flags and signage.

![Students and staff gather at the University of Sydney as staff take strike action](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/b7cf0234a36f44f4a5225bd94fd12efe5274550e/0_0_8192_5464/master/8192.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

The Student Representative Council was also striking in solidarity with staff and picketing online classes. Its education officer, Dana Kafina, said the university’s “draconian” protest restrictions were a motivation.

“In the wake of … suppressions of free speech and activism, and some of the lowest rates of student and graduate satisfaction in the country, we have had enough,” she said.

Despite the presence of campus security and police, the picket lines had a jovial atmosphere. Some blasted music on speakers, others banged drums and chanted with abandon.

![Students and staff gather at the University of Sydney as staff take strike action](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/b2bb44d2c023566b569466082d373ce7a4a99a51/0_0_8192_5464/master/8192.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

Kurt Iveson, a professor of geography, was among them, running a quiz on his megaphone as, behind him, students sold baked goods to raise funds for lost income of strikers.

Iveson has been with the university for 20 years, and wants “meaningful protections” that do not outlaw AI but ensure “the tech is serving our purpose, rather than us serving the tech”.

“All of us on the picket line today are here because we care so much about the education and research we do,” he said.
