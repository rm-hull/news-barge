Whoever picked up the phone at Bradford’s Nelson Street Fire Station at 2.33pm on Monday August 21st, 1916, would never have imagined the scenes the city’s firemen were about to witness.

The call was logged in the Alarm Room at Central Station, leading to engines being mobilised from there, as well as Odsal.

Initially 18 firemen were despatched to Low Moor Munitions Factory and the sight that met them must have shaken them to the core.

That fine, sunny day started as many other for the factory workers, but that afternoon everything changed.

Employees James Broughton and Sam Wade were involved in a routine transfer of loaded drums of picric acid - used as an explosive in the First World War - to the packing shed. Suddenly a drum burst into flames, knocking James to the ground. Within seconds the fire had spread to the inside storage shed and remaining drums.

The fire spread, triggering multiple explosions and by the time the two engines arrived it was out of control. One of the engines, Hayhurst, was caught in a blast and blown into the air.

At some point a nearby gasometer was ruptured sending a huge fireball skywards, its heat being felt a mile away. Soon, fire appliances from elsewhere came to help, the scene being one of devastation, with many casualties.

Nearby New Works School acted as a mortuary and schools in Wyke served as first aid stations. The explosions killed 40 people, including on-site workers, a railwayman and six firemen.

The tragic events are the subject of a new book Low Moor Explosion, A disaster waiting to happen. Compiled by Mary and Geoff Twentyman, Barbara Reardon and Chris Smith, it has been published by Bradford Historical and Antiquarian Society.

The 55-page book contains a list of those who lost their lives - 39 men, with jobs ranging from yardman to dyeworks labourer, blacksmith’s striker and sheet metal worker. John Majerus, the works manager, also died, a day after the explosion, from inhaling fumes while bravely assisting fireman. Six firemen died.

A woman, Martha Briggs, aged 59, also died, from apoplexy brought on by shock.

A recurring sentence: ‘Body recovered from the debris’ accompanies a list of the dead. Many more suffered severe injuries.

The books pays tribute to the bravery of many that day, some of whom later received national awards. As well as firemen, police officers and St John’s Ambulance and Red Cross members, they included telephone operators on duty at a nearby exchange.

Eyewitness reports also feature. Ida Clarkson had left Wesley Place Methodist Church on the arm of her husband Frank, who she had married just moments earlier, when the works blew up.

“The horses waiting for us bolted and the reception was ruined. Only the wedding cake was saved because someone was quick enough to cover it,” she later recalled in a 1976 interview with the Telegraph & Argus. The newlyweds home had its roof blown off, but they went on to enjoy many years of happiness – they are pictured on their Golden Wedding anniversary.

This well-illustrated book contains many photographs, many of the site after the explosion and others of the people who died and survived. The final image in the book shows the funeral procession, with thousands lining the streets as the sombre cortege made its way through Bradford city centre.
