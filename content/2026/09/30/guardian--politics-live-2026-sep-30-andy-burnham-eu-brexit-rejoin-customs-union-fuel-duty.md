Burnham says Britain needs to resolve EU relationship because it can't afford another decade of post-Brexit drift

In his Today interview, Andy Burnham also said that he thought Britain needed to resolve what its relationship with the EU should be because it could not afford another decade of drift.

He explained:

What I am saying is it’s not good enough to leave that question just hanging.

I am saying, let’s be clear about where we want Britain to go in the next decade. And I’ve asked people to join with me on that.

The country cannot have a decade, another one like the one we’ve just had, where we’ve had too much drift, too much division.

I am very clear, and I will say this bluntly, Brexit has caused more harm than good and I think around 60% of the British public agree with that statement.

So the question is what do we do about that?

And I’m not doing the usual politician thing. I’m being honest about facing up to that, and give people options that they can look at and judge.

Tories accuse Burnham of going back on Makerfield byelection pledge to respect outcome of Brexit referendum

The Conservatives have accused Andy Burnham of going back on a byelection pledge not to consider rejoining the EU.

During the byelection in Makerfield, a constituency that strongly voted leave in 2016, Burnham said that the Brexit vote should be respected and that, if he was elected, he would not propose rejoining the EU.

But Burnham has now said this option should be considered. (See 7.49am.)

In response to Burnham’s Today interview, Alex Burghart said:

You cannot trust a word Andy Burnham says. Just a few weeks ago he said he wanted to move on from the Brexit arguments and now he is dragging Britain back into them by floating the prospect of rejoining the EU.

Labour cannot blame a democratic vote from over a decade ago for the consequences of their own economic decisions. While they look backwards, the Conservatives will look forwards.

Burnham says he has spent 'a lot of time' working out how to fill £4.7bn funding gap in defence investment plan

Andy Burnham has said that he has spent “a lot of time” working out how to fill the £4.7bn gap in the plans to fund the defence investment plan.

When Keir Starmer unveiled the defence investment plan in June, after he had announced he was standing down to make way for Burnham, he said the increased investment would cost £15bn. While explaining where most of the money would come from, he said the remaining funds – worth £4.7bn over four years – would have to come from savings to be announced in the budget.

In an interview on Times Radio, Burnham said that finding this money had been a “major priority”.

He said:

I said as soon as I came in that we would fully fund the defence investment plan. I’ve spent a lot of time on that and it will be fully funded.

There was a shortfall - there won’t be [at the budget].

Burnham suggests it would not be legitimate for any party to form majority government with less than 30% of vote

Andy Burnham has suggested that it would not be legitimate for a party to form a majority government with less than 30% of the vote.

In his Today interview, asked why he wanted electoral reform and a move towards proportional representation, Burnham replied:

I found it better being elected under a different system as mayor of Greater Manchester, where people had one or two, they could put that on the ballot paper.

Because you had a reason to stop on every doorstep and chat to people who might not always support your party and try and get their second preference.

It just led to a better politics.

And I also feel that in an era of five party politics, can we really watch a party with less than 30% just walk into Downing Street? Is that something we should feel comfortable about?

As mayor of Greater Manchester, Burnham was elected under the supplementary vote system. This means, if no candidate gets more than 50% when the votes are counted, the top two go into a run-off and then second preferences are taken into account.

When it was put to Burnham that he was just saying that because he wanted to stop Nigel Farage becoming prime minister, Burnham replied:

No, it could happen with any political party. It’s whether that would be a legitimate government.

In the 20th century, when two-party politics was the norm, parties that went on to form a government normally had at least 40% of the national vote – and sometimes a lot more.

But, with the revival of the Liberals, and then the Lib Dems, the success of nationalist parties in Scotland and Wales, and now the emergence of the Greens and Reform UK as significant parties, it would now be quite possible for a party to win a Westminster majority with under 30% of the vote.

At the last election, Labour won a landslide victory with just 33.7% of the vote.

I think you’ve got it completely wrong really, if you don’t mind me saying, to put it like that, because I have put out … a vision for a national care service.

There are pensioners out there who watch your programme who are paying for care costs from their basic state pension. Now, is that right or fair?

Burnham says he wants to rejoin EU in his lifetime, but not necessarily 'now'

Here is more detail on what Andy Burnham said about the possibility of rejoining the EU when he was being interviewed by Nick Robinson on the Today programme.

When Robinson put it to Burnham that ideally he wanted to rejoin, Burnham paused before saying: “I want to look at the options.”

Robinson said that was a long pause, and it sounded as if he meant yes, but did not want to say so.

Burnham replied:

I have said it, there’s no point me denying it, I’ve said in the book I would like to see Britain rejoin in my lifetime. So there was no point saying otherwise.

But it’s not necessarily the case that is now. Because we need to understand what would be the nature of how we would look at the different options.

Burnham then set out four options; the status quo, joining a customs union, joining a customs union and the single market, or rejoining. (See 7.49am.)

Burnham seemed to be referring to his book, Head North, which he co-wrote with Steve Rotheram. In that, they set out an extensive plan for political reform. But they did not say much in it about Brexit.

However, Burnham did declare last year that he wanted the UK to rejoin the EU in his lifetime.

Burnham says he's not getting rid of pensions triple lock, but just adjusting how it works

Andy Burnham has said that it is not accurate to say that he is getting rid of the pensions triple lock.

In interviews this morning, he challenged this description because the plan he announced yesterday means that the value of the state pension will continue to rise in line with prices, earnings or 2.5%.

But under the current system, pensions rise every year either by 2.5%, or by inflation, or by the annual rise in earnings – which is highest.

After 2030, normally they will rise by 2.5% or by inflation. But if that is not enough to ensure that pensions have risen in line with the average increase in earnings since the policy was introduced, they will rise by that amount.

The pensions triple lock is not a benefit; it is an uprating mechanism, and what Burnham announced means that the earnings element of that mechanism will apply according to a new formula.

The change will save money because, under the current system, if the rise in earnings is particularly big one year, as sometimes happens, there is a rachet effect, and the increase gets baked in for future years.

Burnham told Times Radio:

The decision on the triple lock, the adjustment – and it is an adjustment – it’s not getting rid of the whole concept of the triple lock.

Burnham says he doesn't have 'huge room for manoeuvre' to help people with cost of living

Andy Burnham has said that the government does not have “huge room for manoeuvre” as it looks at how it can help people with the cost of living.

In his interview with BBC Breakfast, Burnham said:

We’re thinking all of the time, ‘how do we take a bit of pressure off people, give them some breathing space?’

It’s why one of the first decisions I took as prime minister was to remove VAT off electricity bills, and that will happen on Thursday …

We’ll do something to help people with bus fares next year, the £2 cap for the whole of the year, and then in the budget we will consider further measures.

It’s important for me to say that we don’t have huge room for manoeuvre.

I think people can see that when they look at the financial picture, but within that we will do our best, and we’ll also try to support people who are struggling with the cost of doing business ’cause it’s hard to run a business right now as well.

Burnham says he's looking at 'every possible measure' to help people with energy costs, but stresses funding not easy

Andy Burnham has said that he accepts there will could be an energy crisis this winter.

In his interview with the Today programme, Burnham was asked if he agreed with Simone Rossi, CEO of the energy company EDF, who told the programme in an interview that Britain was “walking into a second significant energy crisis after the one we experienced just four years ago”.

Asked about the comment, Burnham said:

I don’t think that is an overstatement to be honest.

The situation was “very challenging” for people buying petrol, or heating their homes, he said.

I’m doing what I can, removing say VAT off bills. But it is very difficult indeed, and it’s why the Middle East situation needs to command a lot of our attention.

Asked what he might be able to do to help people, Burnham told BBC Breakfast:

I’m considering everything that might help people in very difficult times and I will be honest and say every possible measure is being looked at.

The question I’ve got is, how many can I afford, what can we do given the state of the public finances?

And people wouldn’t thank me for making unfunded payments that then cause pressure on mortgages – and we’ve seen that in the past and we’re not going back there.

Burnham said, when he talked about considering every possible measure to help with energy costs, that included looking at whether to scrap a fuel duty increase planned for next year.

We could stay as we are. That’s definitely an option, if people think this is the right place to stay.
