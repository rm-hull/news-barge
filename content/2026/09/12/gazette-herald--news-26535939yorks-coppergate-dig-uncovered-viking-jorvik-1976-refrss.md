Fifty years ago this autumn, York stood on the brink of a discovery that would rewrite its own story.

In 1976, as the old Craven factory in Coppergate and many surrounding buildings were demolished to make way for redevelopment, archaeologists moved in expecting routine finds. Instead, they uncovered Jorvik, a thriving Viking city frozen in waterlogged earth.

  Archaeologist Richard Hall, left, and Peter Addyman, who was director of the York Archaeological Trust for almost 30 years, pictured on the site of the old Craven factory in Petergate in 1976, before they began the Viking dig which led to the founding of Jorvik Viking Centre.


The so-called Coppergate Dig, which ran until 1981, cut through nine metres of spongy, oxygen-starved soil that had done what few environments can: preserved the perishable.

  50 years since York's Coppergate Dig uncovered Viking Jorvik - pictured, Richard Hall, of York Archaeological Trust, on the Coppergate site in May 1978. Photo: The Press


Timber buildings, workshops, fences and streets emerged almost intact. More than 40,000 artefacts were recovered, including leather shoes, wooden objects, combs, tools, textiles and food remains, turning a city centre plot into a time capsule of 10th-century life.

The discoveries transformed our understanding of Viking life. Rather than fierce raiders alone, residents of Jorvik were shown to be skilled craftspeople, traders and manufacturers, connected to a vast network stretching across Britain and beyond.

Coppergate itself takes its name from the Old Norse Kopparigata, meaning ‘street of the cup-makers’. Fittingly, evidence emerged of large-scale wood-turning and other craft industries operating from workshops fronting the street.

  The moment the York Helmet, bottom centre of picture, was discovered during the Coppergate dig. Picture York Archaeological Trust


Among the most celebrated finds was a helmet, now known as the York Helmet, unearthed in 1982 just outside the main excavation in a wood-lined pit. It was regarded as one of the finest surviving examples of Anglo-Saxon metalwork ever discovered in Britain. Though Anglo-Saxon in origin (c. AD 750–775), it had been buried during Viking rule.

  Queues to get into Jorvik Viking Centre in 1984 shortly after it opened. Picture: York Archaeological Trust


The extraordinary discoveries inspired the JORVIK Viking Centre, which opened on the excavation site in 1984 and remains one of York’s most visited attractions. Millions have since walked its reconstructed streets, complete with the sights, sounds and smells of Viking-age York.

  Prince Charles taking a closer look at the Coppergate dig. Picture: York Archaeological Trust


The centre turned archaeology into a civic attraction. High-profile visitors, including Queen Margrethe of Denmark and a young Prince Charles, came to see the trenches. Locals watched as their city went ‘Viking-mad’, and a new identity took root.

  Coppergate dig director Richard Hall meets Queen Margrethe of Denmark. Photo: York Archaeological Trust.


What began as a response to demolition became the birth of York’s ‘archaeological industrial revolution’.

Half a century on, the story is still unfolding. Researchers continue to study the finds, and advances in technology allow fresh analysis of artefacts examined for decades.

Gareth Henry, of York Archaeology, highlights that filling in the trenches was only the start. “Many people may assume that archaeology ends when trenches and pits are filled in, but really that is just the start of the story. What follows is an extensive programme of research and exploration, piecing together clues in a 1000 year old jigsaw to help us understand what life was like so many generations ago.

“As technology develops, even items that we have looked at dozens of times before can reveal new secrets and insights into the past, and that is exactly what has happened with the Coppergate Dig.

“We’re delighted to be bringing together many of the people who have worked on this project to share their stories and discoveries at the Richard Hall Symposium.”
