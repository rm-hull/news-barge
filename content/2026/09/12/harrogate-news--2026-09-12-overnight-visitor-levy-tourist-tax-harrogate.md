**HARROGATE**— Plans to introduce a regional “Tourist Tax” on overnight accommodation across North Yorkshire have advanced this week, sparking intense debate between local government leaders and the district’s hospitality operators.A new economic impact report has recommended the implementation of a flat**£1-per-night Overnight Visitor Levy.**

According to data models, the charge would apply to tourists and business travelers staying in hotels, hostels, and short-term holiday rentals, potentially raising up to **£26 million each year** for regional investment.

The proposal follows an official government announcement confirming that elected regional mayors in England will be granted devolved powers later this year to introduce municipal visitor levies through the incoming English Devolution and Community Empowerment Bill.

## Infrastructure Reinvestment vs. Business Pressures

The Combined Authority has indicated that revenue generated from the levy would be directed toward public transport connections, high street maintenance, and cultural events, alongside supporting the county’s broader target to achieve a carbon-negative regional economy by 2040.

The report prefers a flat £1 fee over a percentage-based model due to its administrative simplicity, recommending the deployment of a low-cost, region-wide digital platform to manage collections. To shield budget operators, the framework proposes specific exemptions for low-cost accommodation variants and traditional campsites.


**David Skaith, Metro Mayor of York and North Yorkshire:**

It’s good to have more clarity on what an Overnight Visitor Levy would look like and how it might be applied. No decision will be made without a full public consultation and more engagement with the hospitality and tourism sector.

I know that people want to see improvements to our town and city centres, investment in our public services, and us showing off just how great our county is.

That’s what money raised from a visitor levy will help us deliver.



## Harrogate Hoteliers Express Severe Caution Over Spending and Scale

Despite assurances from the mayoral office that any funding would be spent region-wide to boost the visitor economy, the plan has encountered immediate resistance from regional hospitality operators.

Industry figures have raised concerns regarding how the funds will be allocated and managed behind closed doors.

Critics argue that unlike European destinations, which operate under lighter core business taxes, UK hospitality firms are already absorbing intense financial pressures from high VAT rates, business rate valuations, and staffing costs.

Industry representatives warn that adding an extra administrative charge could compromise the competitive positioning of domestic tourist towns like Harrogate, particularly within price-sensitive visitor markets.


**Simon Cotton, Managing Director of HRH Group (including The Yorkshire Hotel):**

My position hasn’t really changed in that I’m not against the levy but remain very concerned about how it will be collected and more importantly spent.

I strongly believe that a full strategic business plan should be presented to the local hospitality industry and that guarantees need to be given that any monies raised will be strictly ring fenced to be put back into supporting hospitality and tourism in North Yorkshire and not on any ‘general projects’.

They also need to limit admin costs rather than spending a chuck on office staff and rents etc.



Further local skepticism stems from previous localized attempts to launch matching infrastructure funding initiatives within the town boundaries, highlighting the distinct geographical challenges of a sprawling mayoral authority.


**Dan Siddle, General Manager of The Crown Hotel, Harrogate:**

Having co-chaired the ABID steering group (Accommodation Business Improvement District) which looked to introduce similar at a more local Harrogate only level, and ultimately failed by one vote, I think it shows the complexity of the matter.

The mayoral region that Harrogate would be part of for this decision is vast and therefore it would be hard to appease all parties and could potentially be hard to see tangible positives.

Hospitality as a whole needs support from central government and initiatives such as reducing VAT, as supported by local MP, might be a better outcome nationally, rather than mayoral led decisions.



## No Immediate Implementation Before Consultations

In response to growing business anxieties, Mayoral leaders from a group of ten combined authorities have signed a collective declaration pledging to cap any future tourism levies at a maximum of 5% of the total accommodation cost to protect consumer choice.

The Combined Authority has confirmed that no final decisions regarding charging models or implementation timelines have been adopted. If the policy receives final statutory approval under the upcoming parliamentary bill, the Mayor has committed to launching a full, formal region-wide public consultation before any local ordinances are enforced across the district.

**ABID stands for Accommodation Business Improvement District**.

It is a legally defined zone where local hotels and guest houses collectively vote to pay an extra mandatory fee (a levy) on top of their standard business rates.This pooled money is then ring-fenced and managed by an independent board to be spent strictly on local initiatives, such as tourism marketing, high street beautification, and attracting major events to the town.Unlike the sweeping, mayoral-led Overnight Visitor Levy currently being proposed for the whole of North Yorkshire, an ABID operates under very different mechanics:

- **A Democratic Vote:** An ABID can only be established if the local hoteliers themselves vote in favor of it through a formal ballot. As Dan Siddle noted in his statement, a previous attempt to set up an ABID specifically inside Harrogate town boundaries failed to launch after falling short by just a single vote.
- **Hyper-Local Control:** The money raised by a town-level ABID stays entirely within that specific town’s borders. The local operators who pay into it retain absolute control over the budget, ensuring the funds aren’t swallowed up by a larger regional council or spent on broad, general municipal projects outside their immediate area.
