FOR generations of Bradfordians and Aire Valley folk, no hiking route has captured the imagination quite like the traditional ramble from Dick Hudson’s to Ilkley.

Stretching for about half a dozen miles across the rugged expanse of moor immortalised in Yorkshire’s unofficial anthem, On Ilkla Moor Baht ’at, this was the walk that whole families took. Mine certainly did, year after year, back in the days before hiking meant expensive gear, GPS devices and technical clothing, when a sturdy pair of shoes, a sense of direction and a bit of common sense were all you needed, especially on this well-beaten track, walking in the footsteps of Victorian excursionists, mill-workers seeking a day’s escape, and countless earlier travellers who crossed these uplands long before modern roads threaded through the valleys below.

  Historic Dick Hudsons is still a popular pub


The walk begins on the southern edge of the moor at one of Yorkshire’s most celebrated landmarks, Dick Hudson’s, an inn steeped in local legend. Originally a 17th-century farmhouse, it became a public house under its namesake, Richard ‘Dick’ Hudson, during the 19th century.

Before the large-scale availability of the automobile, Dick Hudson’s represented both a physical destination and a symbol of freedom. Sundays offered the only respite from long hours in the mills, and thousands made the journey uphill to ‘Dick’s’, where a pint of ale or a pot of tea often marked the beginning of a day’s moorland trek to Ilkley.

The transition from enclosed farmland to open moorland is immediate. Beyond the first stiles, the landscape unfolds into a sweeping tapestry of heather, gritstone and vast northern skies. The route climbs steadily onto the plateau, traversing terrain that is quintessential Yorkshire moorland.

The landscape changes dramatically with the seasons. In late summer, the hills glow with purple heather. In winter, the moor becomes a stark world of dark peat, rust-coloured bracken and relentless winds. But in spring, the turf is springy and the air has a still quality, as I recollect. Whatever the season, the path alternates between sandy gritstone tracks, boggy hollows and stone flagging laid to protect the fragile peat from generations of walkers.

Rombald’s Moor, as we should properly call it, is a wildlife haven. You may well suddenly be aware of the haunting call of the curlew drifting across open ground, or see red grouse bursting from the heather with their distinctive cries, and skylarks filling the air above with song, depending on the time of year.

*(Image: Mik Bullock)*

Actually, however, the moor is one of the most significant archaeological landscapes in Britain. Near the highest point of the route, a worthwhile detour leads to the Twelve Apostles stone circle. Standing around 1,250 feet above sea level, this Bronze Age monument consists of 12 weathered stones that have endured more than three millennia of exposure to the elements. Its precise purpose remains uncertain, though archaeologists believe it may have served ritual or ceremonial functions - but it is a reminder that people have sought meaning in these upland landscapes for thousands of years.

The surrounding moor also contains numerous cup-and-ring-marked stones, enigmatic examples of prehistoric rock art whose significance again remains another of archaeology’s enduring mysteries.

Crossing the ridge brings the walk’s most rewarding and dramatic moment. The southern views towards Bradford, smoky back in the day, give way to a magnificent panorama across Wharfedale. Below lies Ilkley, framed by the rolling countryside of the Yorkshire Dales and distant landmarks such as Simon’s Seat. On days of exceptional clarity, the horizon seems almost limitless, drawing the eye deep into the Dales and emphasising the remarkable scale of the landscape.

From here, the route can take you down to the moor’s most famous geological feature: the Cow and Calf Rocks. Known collectively as Ilkley Crag, these imposing gritstone formations dominate the northern escarpment. The Cow forms a towering cliff face popular with climbers, while the Calf stands apart as a detached boulder below. Local folklore attributes their separation to the giant Rombald, who supposedly leapt across the valley while fleeing his furious wife, breaking the rock beneath his feet. Geology offers a less dramatic explanation, pointing instead to millennia of erosion and glacial action, but the legend remains an enduring part of the moor’s character. Alternatively you can stay longer on the uplands, to and descend into the leafy outskirts of Ilkley.

The contrast between wild moorland and elegant Victorian townscape is striking. During the 19th century, Ilkley earned a national reputation as a hydropathic resort. Visitors arrived from across Britain to benefit from the town’s fresh air and spring water, among them - famously - Charles Darwin, who spent time here while working on the later stages of On the Origin of Species.

A fitting conclusion to the walk begins at White Wells, the historic spa cottage on the hillside, where the original plunge pool survives as a reminder of Ilkley’s therapeutic past. From there, most walkers continue into the town centre for tea and refreshments. Few rewards feel more appropriate after a moorland crossing than a hot drink and a Fat Rascal at Bettys.
