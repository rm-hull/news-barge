The family of a six-year-old boy who was left "breathing through an airway the size of a straw" after chemotherapy for a brain tumour affected his vocal cords are calling for change.

Six-year-old Seth Heighway, from County Durham, underwent an emergency tracheotomy after chemotherapy for a brain tumour left him struggling to breathe.

The chemotherapy affected his vocal cords, leaving his airway dangerously restricted.

  Seth during treatment. 

*(Image: HEIGHWAY FAMILY)*

His family is now urging the Government to increase funding for research into childhood brain tumours.

His father, Chris Heighway, said: "A day or two after his chemotherapy, Seth would start really struggling with his breathing.

  Seth Heighway. 

*(Image: HEIGHWAY FAMILY)*

"Leanne, my wife, kept a journal of when he was having treatment and when he was ending up back in hospital, and we could see there was a pattern.

"When they eventually put a camera down, they found his vocal cords had closed and he was basically breathing through an airway the size of a straw.

  Seth in hospital. 

*(Image: HEIGHWAY FAMILY)*

"He needed an emergency tracheotomy, and the situation was terrifying. You put your trust in the treatment to make your child better and suddenly we were dealing with something we never could have imagined."

Seth’s health problems began in 2022 when he started dragging his right foot.

Concerns grew after his school noticed he was having trouble using his dominant hand while writing.

An MRI scan revealed a mass in his head, and further tests confirmed he had a brain tumour growing on his brainstem, later diagnosed as a slow-growing pilocytic astrocytoma, a type of brain tumour common in children and young adults.

Because of the tumour’s location, surgery was not an option, and Seth began chemotherapy to shrink it.

Mr Heighway said: "A brain tumour was the last thing on my mind.

"Seth was always falling over, so when he started dragging his foot I thought he had probably just hurt himself.

"The phone call from school was when things became more worrying.

"He is right-handed, but he was using his left hand to physically move his right hand while he was trying to write.

"Looking back, there were the headaches too, but at the time we never imagined all of these things could mean something so serious.

"When we were told they suspected a brain tumour, our lives crumbled.

"I knew what a brain tumour was, but you never think it is going to happen to your own family."

After his treatment was adjusted, Seth’s vocal cords began working again and the tracheotomy was reversed.

His MRI scans showed the tumour was shrinking, and he has since regained movement in his right foot, leg, and arm.

  Seth ringing the end of treatment bell. 

*(Image: HEIGHWAY FAMILY)*

Now aged 10, he continues to be monitored with annual MRI scans.

Mr Heighway said: "I’ve never known such a brave little lad.

"What he went through at that age, with the operations, chemotherapy, intensive care and tracheotomy, I don’t know how I would have coped with it myself."

Mr Heighway is raising awareness of the urgent need for more effective and less harmful treatments for childhood brain tumours.

He is also preparing to take on the Great North Run tomorrow (Sunday, September 13) to raise funds for Brain Tumour Research.

Chris said: “Whenever it gets hard, I think that if Seth can go through everything he has been through, I can get through this.

"I don’t really care what time I finish in.

"Nothing is going to stop me getting to the end and seeing Seth and Leanne waiting there.

  Seth Heighway with his dad Chris and mum Leanne on his wish trip to The Titanic Exhibition 1. 

*(Image: HEIGHWAY FAMILY)*

He has already raised more than £1,000 for Brain Tumour Research.

Despite a foot injury interrupting his training, he remains determined to complete the 13.1-mile challenge.

To support Mr Heighway’s Great North Run fundraising, visit www.justgiving.com/page/chris-heighway-1.

Funds raised support research at the charity’s Centre of Excellence at the Institute of Cancer Research, where scientists work on developing better treatments for children and young people with brain tumours.

Mr Heighway said: "Seth follows the fundraising and wants the money to help other people who have what he has.

"That means a lot to me.

"I want more research to mean better treatments and a better future for children like him."

Katrina Jones, head of community and digital fundraising at Brain Tumour Research, said: "Seth’s story is a powerful reminder of the devastating impact a brain tumour can have, not only on the person diagnosed but on everyone around them.

"It’s thanks to supporters like Chris, who raise vital funds and awareness, that we can continue to invest in the research needed to improve treatment options, campaign for change and, ultimately, find a cure."
