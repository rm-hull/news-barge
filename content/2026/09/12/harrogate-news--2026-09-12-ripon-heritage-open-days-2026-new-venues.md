**RIPON**— A series of historically significant properties and hidden architectural features are set to open to the public this week as Ripon launches its local contribution to the national Heritage Open Days initiative.Operating under the central theme of* Everyday Histories*, the ten-day festival runs from

**Friday 11 September to Sunday 20 September 2026**. The event allows visitors to explore landmarks that are routinely closed to the public, highlighting the historical preservation efforts coordinate across the city environment.

## The Rescued Queen Street Victorian Mural

Among the key additions to the urban lineup is the history behind 6 Queen Street, a property previously occupied by Handley’s and Dewhurst butchers and currently home to the Big Bites sandwich shop. The building historically housed a massive, hand-painted Victorian tile mural that became a distinct regional tourist attraction.

The artwork was saved from destruction in 1983 following a swift intervention by the Ripon Civic Society. The building’s then-owners, Dewhurst Butchers, funded a specialist team from the Jackfield Tile Museum in Shropshire to carefully extract and reassemble the delicate individual tiles. While the original physical mural remains permanently housed at the Ironbridge museum facility, a custom commemorative banner has been installed in the Queen Street seating area to mark its local heritage.


![Mural Ripon](https://www.harrogate-news.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Mural.webp)


**Richard Taylor, Chair of Ripon Civic Society:**

The two properties are additions to this year’s programme and it is particularly pleasing to be able to reflect on the role that we have played over the past 58 years in protecting the city’s heritage assets. There was, however, one downside of the rescue operation as the society was unable to find a new location in Ripon for the mural, but it can be seen in its full glory at the world-famous Ironbridge museum.



**Peter Hartley, Former Dewhurst Shop Manager:**

At the time Dewhurst had two shops in Ripon and the one that I ran single handed was so quiet in terms of customers through its doors, that I often nodded off in the afternoons, only to be awakened by visitors who hadn’t come to buy any meat but wondered if they could have a look at the mural and take photographs of it. It’s only since I received a recent phone call from Richard Taylor that I discovered what had happened to the mural and seeing this image of it brings back memories of the days that I spent in the shop.

## From Police Cells to Print Rooms at Kirkgate

A second prominent addition centers on the multi-layered history of the Kirkgate premises currently occupied by The Portly Pig alehouse. The property served as the Ripon Council offices between 1835 and 1851 before being converted into a Borough Police Station complete with custom cell extensions. Following a later stint as a local florist, the site became widely recognized as the active editorial offices for the Ripon Gazette newspaper.

As part of the public schedule, the venue will host dedicated retrospective sessions focusing on mechanical print production methods used during the pre-internet era, tracking the transition from traditional newsroom typewriters to digital desktop publishing frameworks.

## A Broad Regional Heritage Programme

The two new locations join an expansive city-wide itinerary that includes access to Ripon Cathedral, which traces its foundational roots back to the seventh century. The schedule also opens doors at the nearby Georgian Courthouse, the 18th-century Town Hall, and a series of structured guided historical walks across the district.

The annual event is delivered via support from the National Trust, utilizing funding collected through the People’s Postcode Lottery.


**Lilla Bathurst, Manager of Ripon BID:**

This year’s national theme is Everyday Histories and we have many fine examples of buildings that have been refurbished and remain part of our ancient city’s built environment. What makes Heritage Open Days particularly special is the opportunity to discover the stories behind buildings that people pass every day without necessarily knowing what happened there in the past.
