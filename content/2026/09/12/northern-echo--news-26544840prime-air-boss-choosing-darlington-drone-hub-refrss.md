On a small concrete slab, beside a mammoth Amazon warehouse in Darlington, lies the dawn of a new era of drone deliveries for the whole of Europe.

You would probably drive past it without giving it a second glance - but this fenced off car park is where the retail giant’s state-of-the-art drone fleet is taking to the skies.

With a maximum take off weight of 37.5 kilos, each of the the firm’s five ‘hexacopter’ MK30 drones are loaded with parcels for customers and fly up to 70mph within 7.5 miles to drop them off.

It’s a technology previously unseen in the country - and the Darlington fulfilment centre is now being used as a model “not just for the UK but for Europe”.

Prime Air Vice President David Carbon hopes that, within six weeks, the drone’s year-long trial will be green-lit to become a full commercial service.

  Prime Air Vice President David Carbon at Amazon Darlington 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

And speaking to The Northern Echo at the Darlington drone ‘paddock’, he said one of the reasons the town was perfect to launch this technology was its history of innovation, being the birthplace of the modern railway and a continuing hub for sciences and engineering.

“The feedback we got from the Amazonians here was that this is a really good place to come - and they have a history of innovation,” he explained.

“We have a customer base here and there is a strong aerospace legacy. They got us and we got them, but we knew it wasn't going to be easy.

“We had to work with the Civil Aviation Authority, work with NATS (National Air Traffic Services), come up with a regulatory framework, so we want to really do it with people that understand [it].”

  Prime Air Vice President David Carbon at Amazon Darlington 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

Mr Carbon said the Symmetry Park site is “on the cusp” of getting approval for its full service, saying the trial has been “great” and that he can see drone deliveries scaling in the future.

“Most of our customers are now repeat customers, so that's always an indication that things are heading in the right direction,” he explained.

But, with every trial, issues arise.

  In recent months, there have been two notable incidents involving drones, the first being when the aircraft __mistakenly dropped a parcel into a Darlington grandad's garden.__ 

  Amazon drones in Darlington 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

  Weeks later, a drone then crashed into a garden while out on deliveries, when bosses said it believed the drone __had “touched an object extending above the roof of a moving vehicle”.__

But Mr Carbon said that while the trial has seen the team “learn as they’re doing”, he said that they had not encountered many issues.

He said: “For instance, some of the property lines in the UK are really old on maps. So therefore, the property is not actually where a property line is.

“We pull that data to set up our algorithms to work out where we're going to deliver.

“And so sometimes you deliver to the adjoining property and it's because of property lines in the wrong spot. They always find that stuff in the US as well.

  Amazon drones in Darlington 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

“Then we correct it - our drone delivery points can be adjusted, which is awesome, so you're not stuck delivering to one place.

“They're the sorts of situations that we have and we deal with.”

He even said that it “wouldn’t be unheard of” for Amazon to amend a flight route if, for example, there was a wedding happening at a Church that didn’t want drones flying above.

"Where we can, it makes no sense for us to alienate the community,” he said. “We want to be part of the community.”

## 
  **But who are these drones for?**

Amazon Prime customers are already able to get parcels delivered within hours of pressing the order button on the same day. So why is this new technology needed?

Mr Carbon said it’s about cutting down time even further and making it easier for customers to get products that might otherwise be difficult to get their hands on.

  Prime Air Vice President David Carbon at Amazon Darlington 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

“Sixty eight per cent or 70 per cent of what people order in a day, we can deliver,” he said.

“Six hours is great, but it's not as good as five hours. And five hours is not as good as four hours. Four hours is not as good as an average of 34 minutes.

“When we do end up getting to a scaled service here, the promise will be around 120 minutes.

“In the facilities that we operate of this size in the US, our promise to deliver is two hours and our average is 90 minutes.


“There's all sorts of really cool examples of people ordering stuff they need.

“I was at home and one of the kids was going out, they are 21, and he wanted some hair gel.

“We didn't have that and it was Saturday night, everything was shut. In 34 minutes, you get your hair gel and you're up and running.

“The real serious stuff is where you've got a sick child who needs medication or stuff like that and five hours is too late and driving to the store is a pain.”

  Amazon drones in Darlington 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

At the moment, Darlington, which was selected due to its mix of residential areas, major roads and proximity to Teesside Airport, can deliver as far as 12km away.

It is only able to operate within a ‘slice’ of the radius from the warehouse at the moment, but there are hopes this will be expanded in the future.

Hundreds of shipments have been delivered across the last six months, with a team of 13 currently managing the flights, which lasts around 13 to 14 minutes.

  Amazon drones in Darlington 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

The firm’s commitment in Darlington is to deliver within two hours, but averages at 90 minutes so far. Its quickest flight so far is just 39 minutes from purchase to home.

Mr Carbon said the response so far had been great: “If it had no utility, people wouldn't be using it. [In the US] we're struggling to keep up with the demand, frankly.

“So that's one of my biggest challenges.

“Obviously, first and foremost, maintain safety requirements, service the customer needs, but then expand our capacity, because we can't keep up with what we've got.

“It tells you people want their stuff faster. They expect it to be safe, they expect it to not be a nuisance.”
