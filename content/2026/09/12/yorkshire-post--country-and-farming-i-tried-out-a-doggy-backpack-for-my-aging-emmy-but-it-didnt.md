# I tried out a doggy backpack for my aging Emmy but it didn't go to plan - Julian Norton

## It turned out that Emmy’s new backpack was not quite as successful as we had hoped.

For the first few days in the high alps, she (we) had not needed to use it, as we adjusted our activities in line with her limitations.

A twenty or thirty-minute morning walk, with something similar later in the evening seemed to be fine.

For much of the day – like she does at home – she was happy to relax in her bed, occasionally getting up to sniff the alpine air, but mostly sleeping, as old dogs do.

![Vet Julian Norton.](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOjQ0ZWQ4ZTdkLWY0NDItNGEyYi1iNzkyLTYxYzNlNmMyYzhkMDoxZDQzNmU3Yi05NjNmLTQzMzAtOWM5Yi1kMmI0NWNiY2U1Yjk=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)



Her favourite walk around a nearby lake was ideal too, because it was, by definition, quite flat. If it was warm she could cool off in the water. And swimming is her other passion in life, so all was good.

The first longer route was with Anne, walking with friends, while I took to the steeper slopes on my mountain bike.

They had decided to take the telecabine up and walk down. Anne carried the backpack in case it was required for the later stages of the route.

It turned out that the eight-kilometre circuit was perfectly within Emmy’s capability, since it was all downhill and cool under the trees.

“How was the back pack?” I enquired by text message. “Not needed” came the succinct reply.

But on another walk, which was a couple of hours along a river path up to an alpine restaurant, we took no chances.

Her canine friends, Ginny and Seamus, trotted along with enthusiasm. Emmy kept up and, for much of the time, even led the way.

On the return journey however, we noticed a distinct change in pace. It was time for assistance.

Inserting her into the backpack was just as challenging as it had been the first time, especially without the instructions.

As before, she immediately escaped, but eventually we got her strapped in and the backpack hoisted onto my back.

I strode onwards but there was incessant wriggling from behind; Emmy was not impressed nor amused.

At one point, she wiggled so much that she almost toppled out. I had to shout for assistance from Anne to keep her in place. There were a few minutes of calm.

The babbling of the river Dranse (which, incidentally, supplies the Evian plant with its water source), the dappled shade from the trees and memories of a lovely lunch made everything seem perfect.

But, from her high vantage point, Em could only watch the younger dogs having an exciting time amongst the trees and on the riverbank and she didn’t like her constraint.

After some frustrated Jack Russell squeaking and a brief struggle, we admitted defeat and decided it was time to release her.

The rest of the walk was trouble-free, but we’d learnt an important lesson. Emmy was not yet ready to have her aging process managed by means of a doggy backpack.

I’m sure it will help at some point in the future – surely, she would rather accompany us on a lovely outdoor adventure than stay at home – but at present, adapting the journey is the key.

Back at the car, the three dogs piled into the back. Ginny and Seamus, Em’s juniors by six and thirteen years respectively, fell into a deep sleep as soon as their furry heads touched the back-seat blanket.

Em, despite everything, sat on my knee and looked out of the window, eagerly anticipating another adventure.

This came along a couple of days later, as we took to the lake, Anne in her kayak and Em and me on a stand-up-paddleboard.

We pondered trying to put her in a buoyancy aid but, given the backpack experience and her swimming prowess decided against it.

I would paddle and Emmy would relax and watch the world go by. What could possibly go wrong?
