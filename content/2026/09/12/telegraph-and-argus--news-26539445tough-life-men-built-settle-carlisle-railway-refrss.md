THIS year is the 150th anniversary of the opening to passenger traffic of the Settle-Carlisle Railway.

The Midland Railway Company needed its own independent route to Scotland after disputes over access rights with rival companies, and there was only one route - straight through the Yorkshire Pennines.

There has been much written and commemorations throughout this year. But I wanted to find out what it was like for those who actually built the line - the navvies.

So I took a trip over its 72 mile tracks. The day of my journey was wet and on the high points there was fog and swirling mists rolling down though the Dales. I got off the train at Ribblehead, a very remote spot in the middle of nowhere. I was greeted with a 40 mph howling wind and lashing rain - so I was getting a real feeling as to what it must have been like for the navvies working on the railway.

The scenery is amongst the finest in the country - when it can be seen!

I made my way to Settle to meet historian Sarah Lister at Ascension Church graveyard. Sarah has written an excellent book, The Railway Navvies of Settle: The End of the Line, detailing her research into the railway.

Sarah told of 6,000 navvies working to build the Settle-Carlisle Railway between 1869 and 1878 under harsh weather and remote conditions with freezing winds, deep snowdrifts and heavy rain.

Sarah said there were graves of navvies in several locations along the line.

In the Holy Ascension Church churchyard in Settle are the graves of 20 people workers and their families. They include William Peacock who died on April 23, 1873 when a crane fell on him.

Also the grave of Thomas Cooper of Newry County Down who died on February 22, 1875, aged 23 from pneumonia. Thomas was buried in the same grave with Thomas Ferris, also of Newry, who died on April 30, 1873 aged 23, along with Mary Jane Ferris who died aged 17 months.

The main thing which stuck me were the length of the tunnels, hacked out from solid Yorkshire millstone grit. There were 14 of them, the longest tunnel on the line is Blea Moor Tunnel, at 2,629 yards (2,404 metres / 1.5 miles) long, located between Ribblehead and Dent.

Ribblehead station’s waiting-room was used for church services during the construction of the Settle-Carlisle Railway.

Along its 72 miles, there are 14 tunnels and 22 viaducts - the largest is Ribblehead Viaduct which has 24 arches 104 feet (32m) from the ground.

One point worth mentioning is that this line was entirely hewn by hand, not by machine.The Victorian tunnels were known as ‘hell holes’. Dynamite was favoured to make places like the Blea Moor Tunnel because it was safer than gunpowder, the use of which killed over 100 workers in the tunnels.

By comparison, the Crossrail which heavy machinery used to dig the tunnels in London were the giant Tunnel Boring Machines (TBMs), which measured approximately 150 metres (492 feet) in length.

On the Settle-Carlisle line, navvies laboured with sledgehammers to drill holes deep into hard gritstone and limestone, pack them with dynamite, and clear away the rubble while breathing in thick, toxic smoke. No wonder there were constant fatal accidents, which were treated as necessary casualties.

For every three miles of track built, three men lost their lives. We may never know the total number of casualties.

Rock falls inside the tunnels being hewn out was another reason many didn’t survive. But good pay encouraged men to take a chance.

Sarah Lister says more died of diseases like smallpox and cholera than from industrial accidents.

There were large camps consisting of wooden buildings to house the workers. Of course there were disputes and fights between navvies in the camps. The Irish experienced hostility from non Irish navvies.

The railway company employed missionaries to provide education for the railway construction communities. A Mr Tiplady who was put in charge of a mission by the Midland Railway Company attempted to get men away from “pursuits and places of a corrupting character”. A reading room was opened “for mental improvement and social engagement”.

  Ribblehead station where there was a reading room for railway workers


A concert was held in December 1871 in this reading room at Denthead, which even to day is in a wild, bleak and remote spot, put on at the cost of the Midland Railway Company.

A reading room at Ribblehead station was provided, for the benefit of the men on the railway. It was fitted up with books and periodicals. Rev Pearson, vicar of Settle church, said the workman had “the privilege of reading and being instructed in writing arithmetic”. These efforts weren’t always appreciated by the navvies, said Sarah.

The camps lacked clean water and sanitation, causing deadly outbreaks of smallpox and other illnesses alongside dangerous tunnel and viaduct accidents.

A horse and cart transported bodies from Batty Green (which was the navvie camp alongside the Ribblehead Viaduct) constantly to Chapel-le-dale church where the dead were buried in unmarked graves, a distance of three miles.

Such was the loss of life that the railway paid for an expansion of the graveyard - the site can still be visited in this very remote spot.

Church records suggest that another 15 navvies may lie buried in unmarked graves somewhere in the churchyard at Chapel-le-dale and Dent where there are more. Further unmarked navvie graves are believed to be across the moor.

I do ponder if the families of those men ever receive news of their deaths, or were they just left wondering?
