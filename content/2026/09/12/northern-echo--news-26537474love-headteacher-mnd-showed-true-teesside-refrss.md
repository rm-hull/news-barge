*How the heartwarming response to a headteacher’s MND diagnosis showed the real community spirit of Teesside.**PETER BARRON** reports*

   THE image of Teesside has been sullied in recent weeks, with disturbing national headlines resulting from the deaths of two police officers – along with five young men who had been speeding the wrong way down the A66 at South Bank.

But the following story – the story of Mrs Park’s Park – shows the real side of Teesside: the caring, kind, positive side of Teesside, with good Teesside people coming together, and Teesside community spirit shining powerfully through the saddest of circumstances.

As someone who grew up on Teesside – in South Bank, in fact – it is the Teesside I know…

AT the beginning of 2025, Claire Park, the popular headteacher of Hartburn Primary School, just off the A66, near Stockton, was told she had Motor Neurone Disease.

Shortly before the MND diagnosis, Claire had a vision to create a new playground at her beloved school, and news of her illness inspired a fundraising appeal to turn her dream into a reality.

The close-knit school, the Friends of Hartburn Primary, Prince Regent Street Trust, and the wider community responded so magnificently that more than £100,000 was raised.

Around £89,000 was spent on creating the playground, with the rest donated to the Motor Neurone Disease Association, which is striving to find a cure.

Last week, Claire – now in a wheelchair, and robbed of much of her speech, but not her spirit – was able to return to the school to cut the ribbon and officially open the playground, which has been named Mrs Park’s Park in her honour, and as her legacy.

With tears in her eyes, and supported by her husband, Matthew, Claire was able to make a speech – with the help of artificial intelligence – in which she told an audience of family members, friends, colleagues, and supporters how much the success of the appeal meant to her.


“When I look round today, I don’t just see a playground, I see memories,” she said. “I see the children who will run, climb, laugh, and learn here. I see friendships being made, and I see a little piece of my vision continuing to live on at the school I love so much.”

In more than 40 years of writing about the people of the North East, it was the most extraordinary speech I’ve heard – not just for the power of the words used, but for the way technology helped Claire to deliver them.

It was made possible by Claire’s voice being recorded in the early stages of her illness. By feeding those recordings into AI, the audience could hear Claire's speech in her own voice.

We all had tears in our eyes, and lumps in our throats – here is a further extract:

   *“When I was headteacher of Hartburn Primary, I had a vision for this space. I wanted to create somewhere really special for our children, a place where they could play, explore, learn, be curious, make friends and, most importantly, have fun.*

  *“Although I had to take early retirement, that vision was something I cared deeply about, so to come back today to see it become a reality is incredibly special to me.* 

  *“I'm so proud that this playground will be part of Hartburn for generations of children to come. They deserve the very best and this beautiful space provides them with high quality resources and opportunities to learn and play in an environment they can be proud of.*

  *“And what makes today even more meaningful is that this is not simply a playground, it is a legacy. It represents a community coming together and believing that our children deserve something wonderful, and then making it happen.”*

Those watching included Claire's mum and dad, Martin and Julie Small. "We're very proud of Claire, and it's absolutely wonderful to see the playground being opened," said her mum. "Children will love it for many years to come and it's lovely to see something so positive come out of this."

"She was born to be a teacher," added her dad, going on to tell how, as a little girl, Claire had turned the spare bedroom at the family home into a classroom with invisible pupils.

Her sister Hayley, now a nurse, also remembered how her friends were recruited to fill in for the invisible pupils: "In the end, they stopped coming round because they didn't want to have to go to school, so we played at their houses instead," she smiled.

It is hard to imagine what Claire's family are going through as a result of this cruel disease. But they should be intensely proud that the love for her has inspired a special legacy, and helped raise awareness of MND.

So should everyone involved in the appeal: the Friends of Hartburn Primary School, the Prince Regent Street Trust, the business sponsors, and the army of individual fundraisers, who walked, ran, skydived, or whatever else.

There should also be an honourable mention for the members of neighbouring mental health charity, Stockton Men's Shed. They wanted to play their part by renovating Hartburn Primary School's nearby pond area, using fencing from the old playground, and aggregate recycled from the resurfacing of the A19.

It has all added up to a wonderfully positive community effort, leading up to the emotional opening of Mrs Park's Park.

The wooden roof above the entrance to the new playground is painted orange and blue. Balloons, tied to the gateposts to celebrate the opening, were also orange and blue, for they are the colours of the Motor Neurone Disease Association.

By rallying round Claire Park and her family, and making her vision a heartwarming reality, Teesside has shown its true colours too.

The Teesside I know is fiercely proud, and not defined by the tiny minority of anti-social cowards, hiding their faces behind balaclavas, setting off flares, burning cars, and spreading fear, in the mistaken belief they are beyond the law.

It is defined by the vast majority of kind, caring, hard-working people, who come together when one of their own needs love and support.
