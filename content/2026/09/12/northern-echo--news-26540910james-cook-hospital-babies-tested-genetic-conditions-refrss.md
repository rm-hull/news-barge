More than 1,000 babies have taken part in a pioneering genetic research study in Middlesbrough.

The Generation Study at The James Cook University Hospital is testing newborns for more than 200 rare genetic conditions, offering the chance of earlier diagnosis and life-changing treatment.

A newborn’s genome is sequenced using a small blood sample, typically taken from the umbilical cord, with parental consent.

Tracy Ruddick, an obstetrics and gynaecology research nurse, said: "Reaching such a significant recruitment milestone at James Cook is an incredible achievement and a testament to the dedication, teamwork and commitment of everyone involved.

"The Generation Study is incredibly important as it aims to build a better understanding of how genetics influence children's health and development.

"It can help to identify conditions sooner, potentially allowing children to receive support or treatment earlier."

The study has also seen strong uptake at the University Hospital of North Tees.

Zana Sellars from Middlesbrough enrolled her baby Remi in the study shortly after birth.

Her decision was motivated by the potential to help others.

She said: "It's important for future generations.

"Although it might not identify something for your own family, it has the potential to help someone you know."

Professor Rich Scott, chief executive officer of Genomics England, added: “We believe genomics can transform healthcare in this country and be used to get ahead of serious illness.

"It’s been incredibly moving to see the life-changing impact the Generation Study is having for families. Every year, thousands of babies are born in the UK with rare genetic conditions, but they can be hard to diagnose.

"On average, it takes around five years for a rare condition to be diagnosed, at which point it has often progressed to the point where treatment, if it exists, is far less effective."
