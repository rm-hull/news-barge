The fact that we all saw it coming somehow didn’t make it any less shocking. On Monday morning news magazine Der Spiegel described the state election results from Saxony-Anhalt as “a political earthquake of magnitude 9.0”.

If you’ve been following our coverage, you’ll be aware of the stakes.

From the changes the confirmed far-right extremist state chapter of the Alternative for Germany (AfD) had promised to bring in Saxony-Anhalt, to some of the limits on what they could do there, and the fact that regardless of how it all shakes out in the state parliament, this represents a scary moment for immigrants and people with foreign backgrounds both in Saxony-Anhalt and around Germany.

Just at this moment, I think it's important to keep in mind that two things can be true at the same time.

Yes, it’s true that this election was significant and represents a real and threatening development in German politics.

But it’s also true that only about two percent of the country’s total electorate was represented in the Saxony-Anhalt vote – and that across Germany the majority of voters are still opposed to the AfD, many of them vocally so.

We have to take the threat of fascism seriously. But there’s also a danger in making too much of the far-right’s victories. Flooding the zone with shocking headlines, grabbing national and international attention – this is exactly what the AfD wants and thrives on.

Meanwhile, people demonstrating against the far-right across Germany – and yes, even in Saxony-Anhalt – become a kind of silent majority because protests are rarely news. The same goes for people who speak kindly, and work quietly to keep communities together.

So with that in mind, and before the news cycle moves onto another state election that is sure to stir up another round of shock and awe, it’s worth taking a moment to reflect on some of the people and moments that remind us that most of Germany ain’t all that bad.

**Where is this?**

![A pro-democracy protest is seen in Magdeburg.](https://apiwp.thelocal.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/617017882.jpg)


On the day before the state election last Sunday, several thousand gathered at this Cathedral Square for the Democracy Festival.

With enough young people and pride flags to maybe pass for Berlin or Cologne, you might be surprised to hear this photo was taken in Magdeburg, the capital city of Saxony-Anhalt.

The districts of Magdeburg and Halle, which are the two biggest cities in the state, were the only places which did not elect an AfD candidate by direct mandate.

There was a modest bit of good news in those results for the Green party, which did better in the election than had been expected. The Local’s Rachel Loxton spoke to Josephin Winkler this week, a board member for the Greens in Magdeburg, who promised to “hinder right-wing extremism in any way we can".

One thing Winkler said that struck home for me was that she noticed AfD voters seemed to have a "completely different understanding of reality…which they get from alternative sources of media."

As an American, that sounds like most Trump voters I’ve talked to during his second term, and it also explains many of the comments I see on social media on the topic.

The dangers of social media for democracy really can't be overstated, and the AfD has proven incredibly adept at harnessing digital media for its own gain.

To give just one small example, journalists at Die Zeit were on site in Magdeburg this week when they saw a clip getting attention on TikTok that claimed 100,000 pro-AfD supporters were gathered there. The reality was a rather small and quiet crowd, which they showed in their own video clip.

Of course, on the flip side, social media can also be a place to find the people who are standing up for their neighbours and sharing ideas about how to organise.

If you're looking for more German content in your feed, you might consider Marcant, a German Youtuber and video journalist who has made a name for himself by talking politics on the streets of Germany – and occasionally taking the time to explain to AfD voters why their policies are harmful.
