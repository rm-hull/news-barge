From mysterious ruins and abandoned sites to fantastical follies, these hidden gems will leave you awestruck – or send a shiver down your spine

  Coldstone Cut 

*(Image: Paul Tranter)*

  **The Coldstones Cut, Pateley Bridge**

The Coldstones Cut, created by artist Andrew Sabin, is art on an enormous scale. Overlooking an active quarry that dwarfs the work, the experience of visiting this structure can feel somewhat disorientating. The stone walls give you the sense of walking around a castle or the grounds of a grand building, yet the yellow lines and bollards have a distinctly urban feel. The site is perfect for those with young children, who will love nothing more than running between the narrow stone walls and up the twin spirals to the viewing platforms. It is quite a hike to reach the top of the sculpture, but the views are certainly worth it.

Take the B6265 west from Pateley Bridge for 2 miles, then pull into the car park (or take the 820 bus from Pateley Bridge). HG3 5JQ

  Buttertubs 

*(Image: Supplied)*

  **Buttertubs, Thwaite**

Carboniferous limestone dominates the landscape here. Being a soft alkaline rock, over periods of thousands of years limestone can react with rainwater and slowly dissolve. The rainwater would have gathered in cracks in the rock which, through this process of weathering, got deeper and deeper, until, over time, the vertiginous fissures formed. There are a few stories about how Buttertubs got its name. The most entertaining is that in a time before refrigeration, farmers carrying their buttery wares to the markets in Hawes would have used them as makeshift fridges.

From the Green Dragon, Hardraw, take Bellow Hill East, then left, follow signs to Simonstone and Muker (via Buttertubs). There are ‘buttertubs’ on both sides of the road. DL11 6DR

   

*(Image: Supplied)*

  **Forbidden Corner, Middleham**

Alice in Wonderland-like passageways twist, turn, shrink and grow, generally disorienting and baffling you in equal measure. It’s impossible to describe and even harder to find your way around. There are doors that may lead nowhere or continue on and on up into towers, through a rabbit warren of rooms, down yellow brick roads or around Romanesque gardens. It is very much a tourist attraction, but the ordinariness of the snack outlets and gift shop only serves to reaffirm the oddness of this place.

From the A6108 at Middleham, take Coverham Lane. Keep on this road until you see the iron railings, stone wall and brown sign directing you to Forbidden Corner. DL8 4T

   

*(Image: Supplied)*

  **Oliver Duckett Folly, Richmond**

The little sham castle on top of a small hill to the north of Richmond was most likely designed by Daniel Garrett for Sir Conyers D’Arcy sometime during the mid-18th century, when folly building was nearing its zenith. Conyers would have had the Duckett built as an ‘eye catcher’ or a picnic site. It is likely it would have been a three-storey building and used during hunts across the estate. There is a small hamlet nearby called Oliver, and ducket is an old word meaning ‘dovecote’. In June 1927, Britain was poised to observe its first total solar eclipse in almost two centuries and it was calculated that the centre of totality was to pass directly over

Oliver Duckett Folly. L10 5QA

   

*(Image: Supplied)*

  **Arnagill Sighting Tower, Ilton-cum-pot**

This tower was constructed, along with two others in the area, to help align the three-mile-long Carlesmoor Aqueduct tunnel. The walk up to the tower is challenging, to say the least.

From Masham, just off the A6108, follow the Fearby Road west through Fearby, Healey and Leighton until you reach the reservoir. There are a number of pull-ins on the west side of Roundhill Reservoir. From here, follow the lane to the dam, cross the dam and take the diagonal footpath uphill. Head south to stay on this path to the brow of the hill, then just past the outcrop, look out for the path heading northeast to the tower. Do not take a shortcut through the bracken; there are trip hazards and ground-nesting birds. HG4 4LU

  **The Wainstones, Cleveland Way**

A sandstone outcrop, on the North York Moors, just off the Cleveland Way footpath. There are conflicting stories about how they got their name. The stones may have once resembled a wagon, with the word ‘wain’ being the Middle English term for a four-wheeled vehicle drawn by a horse. A more fanciful tale tells of a Danish chieftain who was killed at the stones, with ‘wain’ being derived from the Saxon word wanian, meaning to grieve.

On the B1257 from Great Broughton continue past the Clay Bank Forestry England car park to laybys on either side of the road; aim for the one on the right-hand side and look for the steps leading upwards at the far end on the right on to Cleveland Way; remain on this footpath for 1.6km (or a mile) until you reach the stones. TS9 7JA

  **The Rocket Ship Folly, Aysgarth**

A stone’s throw from the famous Aysgarth Falls, The Rocket Ship is one of three follies built sometime around 1860 in the grounds of Sorrelsykes Park Hall. Resembling something from a comic book of the 1950s, the folly gained its modern name much later. The large buttresses that give the folly its rocket-like shape were added by the builder, who was worried the whole structure would tumble

down without them. The other two follies on the land are slightly less impressive. One, the Pepper Pot was used for curing bacon and therefore was not built solely as a folly.

The Rocket Ship is on private land, but it can be seen quite clearly from the road and the nearby footpath.

DL8 4UW

   

*(Image: Supplied)*

  **Marton Moai, Middlesbrough**

Marton Moai stands outside the Captain Cook Museum in Middlesbrough. The statue is the creation of Maori artist George Nuku along with carver David Gross. It took the pair two years to make the large statue. Although the carving at first appears to be wooden, it is made from Aislaby sandstone, quarried down the road in Whitby. The figure is a modern interpretation of the heads found on Rapa Nui, or Easter Island as it came to be known.

Take the A172 south from the centre of Middlesbrough. Park at Stewart Park car park. Follow the path up past the playground, then turn left to the museum. Marton station is nearby. TS7 8AR

  **Grassington Mine, Grassington**

There are around 10 separate sites of scattered mining remains over Grassington Moor, which once made up a vast network of leadworks on this desolate site. Perhaps the most iconic is the large lonely chimney standing against the backdrop of the moorland.

Underneath the ground there are 1.7km of ventilation tunnels, which would have taken noxious gases from the leadworks. There are old shafts and tunnels throughout the area, and the Grassington Mines Appreciation Society runs tours of them for members.

Head north from Grassington on Moor Lane to the car park at the end of the lane. Take the gate to the right and follow the footpath through the mining ruins; there is a marked footpath with 10 boards highlighting aspects of the mine. BD23 5EQ

  **Norber Erratics, Yorkshire Dales**

Littering the entire hillside in upland Lancashire, Norber certainly takes the crown for one of the most impressive collections of erratic boulders in the country. Not only are there hundreds of rocks strewn across the hillside, but there is also huge diversity in

their arrangement, with rocks positioned on top of rocks looking like enormous tables, smaller stones scattered in piles, massive boulders on their sides, and clusters of rock as far as the eye can see.

The darker stones are greywacke, a stone prized by the Egyptians because of its resistance to fracture. This ancient civilisation used it to make vessels, along with impressive dark statues of their pharaohs. LA2 8BS

  **Druid’s Temple, Masham**

Despite appearances, the Druid’s Temple doesn’t date back to the Bronze Age but only as far back as the early 19th century. It was built during a time of high unemployment, when it was seen as philanthropic to give soldiers returning from the Napoleonic Wars a means of employment. William Danby, the local landowner, gave his workers the princely sum of a shilling a day, which in today’s money would be a little under £3. As Danby loved to entertain, it is likely it was built as a picnic table. If you want to have the place to yourself, it’s best to visit outside the school holidays or come early in the morning, before 10am.

West from Masham follow signs to Ilton again, first left, first right; follow the lane to the car park. Follow the main path into the woods, then take a right and then a left to the Temple. HG4 4JZ

  **Redcar beach, Redcar**

During the Mesolithic period, around 7,000 years ago, a large landmass we now call Doggerland stretched from Britain across the North Sea and into the Netherlands and Denmark. Before trawling was prohibited, fishermen brought up mammoth tusks, tools and human bones from this submerged prehistoric world. In Redcar, a forest dating from this period can be seen on the seafront after stormy weather. Trees were first documented in 1871 and have only been uncovered a handful of times. For the best chance of seeing the petrified forest, visit during low tide not long after winter storms.

Head to the end of the Esplanade, into Granville Terrace, then out to Redcar Rocks. TS10 3A
