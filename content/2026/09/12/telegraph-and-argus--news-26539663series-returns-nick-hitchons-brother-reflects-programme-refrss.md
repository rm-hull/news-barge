A TELEVISION era with a strong link to West Yorkshire comes to a fitting end next week.

Back in the 1960s, before Emmerdale Farm or All Creatures Great and Small used the Dales as a scenic backdrop, a corner of rural Craven was captured by a TV documentary team.

They were making a film for Granada Television’s current-affairs programme World in Action. The 30-minute documentary, entitled Seven Up!, was intended as one-off project, looking at the lives, and views on life, of 14 seven-year-olds.

Spanning different parts of Britain and contrasting backgrounds, the programme would, it was hoped, provide a snapshot of our social structure as it was in 1964.

And that was supposed to be that, but events took a different turn and television history was made.

The children were revisited every seven years (or thereabouts) in what has become known as the Up Series, chronicling their lives and, along the way, winning awards and accolades, prompting similar programmes, and even being parodied in The Simpsons (Springfield Up, 2007).

Now the series is being drawn to its conclusion with 70 Up, to be shown on ITV1 in two parts, on September 15 and 22.

One of the voices that is due to be heard is that of my late brother, Nick Hitchon.

When he appeared in 63 Up in 2019 Nick revealed he had cancer and was seriously ill.

Mike Apted, who had directed all but one instalment of the Up Series, died in 2021. There was little news about what might happen next. Some thought 63 Up might be the finale.

Nick disagreed, and pressed the production team to film an interview with him while there was still time.

So in 2023 long-time series producer Claire Lewis flew to Nick’s home in America to interview him. A few days later he died.

Nick was a determined character, and it seems likely to me that he wanted to finish what he had started.

The story told in my family was that in 1964 a researcher set out from the Granada studios in Manchester with the task of finding a child from the farming community to add to the Seven Up! mix.

The researcher scoured rural Lancashire but was struggling to find a child who would talk to the camera. The story goes that a schools inspector told him to head to the former Arncliffe CE Primary School, as the inspector was confident the head teacher would provide a child who would talk.

The head said she did indeed have a farmer’s son who would talk, but Nick was only six. Never mind, the researcher was under deadline pressure and Nick would do.

Consequently he was at least a year younger than he was supposed to be for all the future editions of the series, but he turned out to be rather precocious so this never mattered much.

Nick aged six talked about wanting to learn about the Moon and refused to talk about girls. The film suggested he walked to school, which he didn’t, and his route to school passed Kilnsey Crag, which it didn’t, but it made a good atmospheric black-and-white shot.

  Nick as a child at Kilnsey Crag in the original 7 Up programme


Several years later Apted, an up-and-coming director who had been a researcher on the 1964 film, was told by a senior figure at Granada that he should see how the Up children were getting on.

Thus was born 7 Plus Seven, which featured the children at 14 (or 13 in one case). After that the cameras would return to record their subjects every seven years.

By Apted’s own admission, the Up Series set off with a socio-political purpose, to suggest that British people stay in the class they are born into. Somewhere along the line the focus switched to telling the stories of the people involved.

This may be partly because most of the participants’ lives did not fit the anticipated pattern. This was certainly true of Nick who, far from staying on the farm, went to Ermysted’s Grammar School in Skipton, where his love of physics was firmly established, and then to Merton College, Oxford. In the early 1980s he moved to the University of Wisconsin in the USA to continue his research into nuclear fusion.

  Nick teaching at the University of Wisconsin


The Up Series teams crossed the Atlantic to catch up with Nick, occasionally bringing him back to the Dales, which sometimes seemed to be mainly about getting him to walk down country lanes and past Kilnsey Crag.

Unlike some of the participants, Nick stuck with the programme through every episode.

The Up subjects all have fascinating stories to tell, and sadly space does not allow me to relate them here. They will be addressed in the final instalment of the series, which I had not seen at the time of writing.

The new director is renowned documentary maker Asif Kapadia, who has gone painstakingly through the archives of previous episodes. I understand that 70 Up will include past footage that has never been broadcast before.

One thing that is clear to me is that, far from being some sort of “fly-on-the-wall” observation, the Up Series significantly changed the lives of those who featured in it. It became part of their lives. Will they miss it when it’s over, or accept its passing as a natural process?

I hope that 70 Up will bring them proper closure and allow them to let go of a phenomenon they have co-existed with for more than six decades.

Most of all, I hope filming that last interview did the same for Nick.

* 70 Up airs at 9pm on Tuesday, September 15 on ITV1 and ITVX. All previous episodes in the Up series are streaming on ITVX in the UK.
