## Why getting a permanent place at Spark York is proving difficult

Spark York in Piccadilly, York *(Image: Gabriel Macarthur)*

  *Journalist Gabriel Macarthur has taken a look at the challenges facing the business incubator Spark York in helping traders take the next step towards independent high street success.*

Spark York, a hub for start-ups in the hospitality and retail industries, was launched in 2018 on Piccadilly as a space for entrepreneurs to test their ideas in affordable, short-term units made from shipping containers.

The concept, supported by City of York Council and run as a community interest company (CIC), was to help new businesses transition into permanent premises and contribute to the growth of York’s independent economy.

Sam Leach, co-founder of Spark, said: "The goal was always to offer space where people from all walks of life could test, build and grow their ideas.

"We also realised we could subsidise community groups who couldn't afford central locations to meet.

"It was all about letting people have a go."

  Spark York in Piccadilly, York 

*(Image: Gabriel Macarthur)*

Since opening, 25 businesses have made the leap into bricks-and-mortar locations, with two earning places in the Michelin Guide.

But that momentum has stalled, with no new graduates since 2024 and tenants staying long term.

Malkani Wijesinghe, co-owner of Elephant Feast, a Sri Lankan restaurant in Huntington, said she has been on the waiting list for a permanent spot at Spark for more than a year.

Ms Wijesinghe said: "I love Spark.

"I think it’s a great place for propping up businesses. We’ve had a pop-up event there before and found it to be very successful.

"However, getting a permanent place is difficult because many restaurants stay for years after securing a placement, which makes the waiting list grind to a halt."

Some believe the slowdown is due to wider economic pressures.

Mike Brand, founder of Clucking Oinks, started his business at Spark in 2018 before moving into a city centre restaurant, which later closed due to financial pressures.

Mr Brand said: "It's not just Spark.

"We had Covid, then the Ukraine war which shot up food prices, then the energy crisis, and instead of supporting us they raised minimum wage, taxes and National Insurance.

"The biggest problem is nobody's making a big enough profit to grow. We're doing stuff to survive. There’s no growth."

Others see things differently.

Pablo, who runs Tricolour with his family, graduated from Spark four years ago.

  Pablo, who runs Tricolour with his family, graduated from Spark four years ago 

*(Image: Contributed)*

He said the move was tough, but not impossible.

Pablo said: "We thought all the customers would come to us because they knew us from Spark, but people go to Spark, they don't automatically come to Tricolour."

Despite the challenges, Tricolour is now a well-established restaurant.

Spark’s original model relied on regular turnover to give new entrepreneurs a chance, but that cycle has slowed as existing traders remain in place.

Tom Kerrige, founder and co-director of Spark, said: "When we opened, the hope was that one or two businesses a year would graduate and move on, but originally, up until around 2024, there was a lot more than that.

"Now we’ve noticed there’s a lot more trepidation in terms of graduating on to a permanent premises."

  Spark York in Piccadilly, York 

*(Image: Gabriel Macarthur)*

Spark has never set a time limit for tenants, and recently began working with traders on personal development plans to support growth.

Mr Kerrige said: "We can’t tell people to leave with nowhere else to go just for the sake of them leaving because we want to support them to exist and do well.

"Variety and change is what makes us interesting as a venue to visit, but it also definitely brings risk, and things have certainly started slowing down in terms of businesses naturally choosing to move on the high street."
