‘We shouldn’t have to model’ super changes, Joyce says

Barnaby Joyce is using every which way he can to say that One Nation shouldn’t have to model the impact of One Nation’s policy that would allow an individual to divert 3% of their superannuation to pay their rent or mortgage.

After a pretty heated exchange on 7.30 last night, Joyce is back on the ABC speaking to RN Breakfast, and there’s once again a lot of back and forth.

He says having to model what happens to someone getting access to their own money is “conceptualised by socialism”.

Is there an implication beyond it being their money? Do you model the money you get paid at the end of the week? Do you have to model that money before you get paid? Or do you believe you have an entitlement to your own money? … We shouldn’t have to model you getting your own money.

When host Sally Sara then asks whether economists have backed the policy, Joyce ridicules the suggestion.

Now we have to get economists to decide whether you get access to your own money … now we’ve got another group of people that apparently have to give approval for you to get your money. I mean, who else do you want to go to? Your local priest, Buddhist temple, before you get your money? It’s your money, for goodness sake.

‘Australians are fed up’: Haines blasts community fund

Independent MP Helen Haines has added her voice to the list of MPs angry over reports of Labor using a community infrastructure fund to pork-barrel money into target seats at the last election.

The ABC has reported analysis by the Centre for Public Integrity that found the majority of money from an “invite-only” grants program was given to safe, target and marginal Labor seats.

In a statement, Haines said:

Australians are increasingly fed up with self-serving political establishment, and pork barrelling only further undermines trust in government and our democracy. Political parties should not be able to use taxpayers’ money to buy votes.

Community need should come before political need.

Haines was a leading figure behind the push to set up a national anti-corruption commission.

One Nation super plan ‘not our policy’, Liberal senator says

The shadow housing minister, Andrew Bragg, says One Nation’s plan to allow access to superannuation for mortgage and rental payments isn’t Liberal policy but doesn’t directly knock it on its head.

He tells RN Breakfast the idea doesn’t address long-term structural issues around housing ownership in retirement.

It’s a short-term factor that would help with cost of living relief, I imagine, but I don’t think it addresses that long-term structural challenge. So 30 years ago, you had about 20% of retirees renting. Now you’ve got about 30%, and in 20 years it’ll be about 40%. That is going to change the character of retirement.

Bragg dismisses reports that the Coalition was cooking up a similar policy offering, and tries to put the pressure on to the government. He also won’t say whether the Coalition has modelled anything.

I think it’s very telling that the treasurer, having destroyed the budget and destroyed the housing system, can’t even bring himself to have this debate. Instead, what he wants to do is to go and raise donations for the Labor party on the back of a public debate.

Bragg is referring to a text blast that was sent by Jim Chalmers last month to supporters, claiming that the Coalition and One Nation were coming for superannuation and calling for donations.

Joyce responds to reports of Hanson loan: ‘I think we just get used to these stories’

Barnaby Joyce has tried to deflect reporting in Nine Newspapers this morning that Pauline Hanson has offered loans to her party since her campaign to re-enter parliament in 2016. One Nation has publicly reported it owes $400,000 in debts to Hanson.

Hanson has refused to say whether she has charged interest on those loans.

Joyce tells RN Breakfast we can probably expect more of these stories from those who are “scared” of his party.

Joyce adds that he’s not an accountant for Hanson so he doesn’t have details of her financial affairs:

I know that people have a pathological dislike of One Nation if they’re a socialist. I’m completely aware of that. And I imagine we’re going to get lots and lots of these stories, not only from the left, but from those who are scared of us, who are supposed to be on the conservative side of politics. So I think we just get used to these stories. They’re going to roll out. I mean, we’re under the microscope. I accept that.

Daniel Mulino is facing the wrath of RN Breakfast host Sally Sara to explain exactly how the “invite-only” grant scheme is legit and above board and not pork barrelling.

He says repeatedly that the scheme is “longstanding practice” that allows a government to deliver its election promises.

(ie Labor promised money for parks, pools and sports ovals to seats that it either already held or really wanted to win and then this was the program to deliver that once they were re-elected.)

Sara asks which other grants programs have been invitation only, but Mulino doesn’t know, replying, “I don’t have the background on how frequently it’s used.”

He says:

It is a specific kind of grants program which is designed to deliver election commitments and that’s something which is specifically set up after an election in order to allow governments to basically follow through on what they’ve promised to do.

One Nation policy will leave people $25k worse off at retirement, Mulino says

Superannuation is kicking off again today with One Nation pushing to allow people to divert 3% of their superannuation to their rent or mortgage.

Cue the government cracking their knuckles ready to lash out against the suggestion that super should be messed with.

The assistant treasurer, Daniel Mulino, continued the fight on News Breakfast earlier:

It took us decades to get to 12% superannuation guarantee and that’s a level that will provide people with a high level of dignity and security in retirement.

To do what the One Nation party is suggesting will see somebody who is 30 years old, $25,000 worse off at retirement and a couple around $50,000 worse off.

To unwind that is dangerous and undermines the whole superannuation system. What we’ve suggested over recent years is that the far better approach is to support people getting pay rises. We’ve supported increases in award wages and the minimum wage.

Matt Canavan ‘deeply sceptical’ over social media changes

The government will later today reveal draft legislation for new digital duty of care rules.

But the Nationals leader, Matt Canavan says he doesn’t “think the government’s got clean hands” on the issue, and says the government shouldn’t be able to censor a person’s social media.

The government argues that it is not censorship to ask digital platforms to remove harmful content, and allow users to choose if they want algorithms on their feed.

Canavan told News24 this morning:

I’m deeply sceptical of giving any government, whether I was in charge, or Anika Wells is in charge, I’m deeply sceptical of giving any government the power to decide what you read on your phone.

‘Invite-only’ grants program ‘standard practice’ says Labor minister

The grants program is all above board and there’s nothing to see here, says the assistant treasurer, Daniel Mulino, defending the government’s use of the Major and Local Community Infrastructure Program.

As mentioned below, that program committed tens of thousands of dollars to particular seats that were “invited” to join the program, but left out almost a third of seats across the country. Analysis by the Centre for Public Integrity showed $493m of the $560m fund went to safe, fairly safe or competitive seats for Labor.

Mulino told ABC News Breakfast:

It’s standard practice and longstanding practice for governments to set up these kinds of grants programs in order to deliver those election commitments. They’re different to the kinds of grants programs which are run in the ordinary course of government. And this program, as Catherine King has explained, is part of long standing practice and there are checks and balances in there.

Ministers won’t approve programs in their own electorate and it’s an important way for governments to be able to deliver commitments.

Independent MP Kate Chaney says reporting of an “invite-only” community infrastructure grants program by Labor shows “governments from both sides use taxpayer-funded grants to buy votes”.

The ABC has reported the Major and Local Community Infrastructure Program committed tens of thousands of dollars to key seats, but 45 seats around the county (almost a third) were not invited to apply for this fund.

Kate Chaney. Photograph: Mick Tsikas/AAP

Chaney says:

This is pork barrelling, and we’ve seen it before. The Coalition had Sports Rorts, and now Labor is ‘park’ barrelling – money for parks and playgrounds, pools and sports ovals all promised during the election campaign.

The Coalition’s colour-coded spreadsheet was Sports Rorts’ smoking gun, and this report shows that Labor’s pork barrelling is no different – same spreadsheet, different colour.

Chaney says the Commonwealth Grants Rules and Principles has recommended the use of a competitive, merit-based selection process for grants – but so far those rules have been ignored.

Good morning, Krishani Dhanji back in the hot seat here with you for another busy sitting day.

Howard claims US is Australia’s ‘ultimate security guarantor’

John Howard says the US remains Australia’s “ultimate security guarantor” despite its “unorthodox” president in Donald Trump.

At ANU last night, the former prime minister said:

Although there may be occasions for superficial provocation … [we shouldn’t]lose sight of the fact that our ultimate security guarantor is the United States.

Howard said while on a flight from Hawaii back to Australia after the September 11 attacks in 2001, the then foreign affairs minister, Alexander Downer, urged him to invoke the Anzus treaty to show US solidarity.

John Howard in October 2001. Photograph: Reuters

Howard said he spoke to then US ambassador to Australia, Tom Schieffer, about his decision during the flight, who he said became overcome with emotion.

Howard believed it was this decision that would pave the way for the Aukus treaty becoming possible two decades later.

The 87-year-old was in Canberra last night for an hour-long chat with Australian National University national security professor Rory Medcalf before the 25th anniversary of the September 11 terrorist attacks in the US.

The discussion began with last year’s alleged terrorist attack in Bondi, before turning to whether immigration settings can prevent potential terrorists from entering the country.

Howard said the “law of averages” meant some people with nefarious intentions could enter the country but he did not believe the “evidence is overwhelming”.

He continued:

But I think the mood is overwhelming that we have to try very hard to stop terrorism creeping in under the cover of immigration, and you can’t do it by just stopping immigration, and it’s very hard to do it by stopping categories of immigration.

When some people say you stop it by saying ‘we won’t take migrants from any country that has got a reputation for terrorism’, well, it might be a lot more limiting than those people imagine.

Neither major political party has proposed a country-wide ban on specific countries for permanent migration. One Nation has said it would like to see country-wide migration bans on people coming from countries the foreign affairs department has listed as “do not travel”.

Joyce says super plan has 'minute' inflationary effect

Adeshola Ore

Joyce has also defended his party’s new policy allowing renters and mortgage holders to dip into their superannuation to boost their pay, saying it will have a “minute” impact on inflation.

The superannuation industry has warned the policy – which Labor and the Coalition have said they do not support – would stoke inflation. The Association of Superannuation Funds of Australia chief executive, MaryDelahunty, said the proposal would be “economically disastrous” and make people poorer in retirement.

Barnaby Joyce and Pauline Hanson. Photograph: Mick Tsikas/AAP

Speaking to the ABC’s 7.30, Joyce – the party’s Treasury spokesperson – said withdrawing your superannuation from your fund due to financial hardship was currently a “protracted” system.

Asked if the party had modelled the inflationary impact of the policy, Joyce said it would have a minimal impact but did not point to any modelling:

Now are you saying that it has no effect whatsoever? No, I wouldn’t say that but I’d say it is almost minute.

One Nation’s deputy leader, BarnabyJoyce, has been asked about the party’s Victorian leader, WarrenPickering, who continues to attract scrutiny over allegations made against him by an ex-partner about alleged drug use.

Pickering said his ex-partner’s claims were false. “They are extremely hurtful and I just hope the public can see through them,” he said in a statement, according to The Australian.

If he’s good enough to serve our nation in the defence force, as determined by our nation, then I presume he’s good enough to serve the people of Victoria.

If we’re saying that any person will be held to account for any action they ever did, no matter how far in the past it was, I would suggest there are lot of people in this parliament who will no longer have a job Monday morning.
