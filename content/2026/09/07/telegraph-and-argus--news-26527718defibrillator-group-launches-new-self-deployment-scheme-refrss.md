A YORKSHIRE-based defibrillator charity has launched a new self-deployment scheme after raising concerns about changes to the way public access defibrillators are deployed by Yorkshire Ambulance Service.

Public Access Defibrillators UK says the Yorkshire Ambulance Service (YAS) no longer routinely directs callers to nearby defibrillators when a patient is reported as having a heart attack, instead waiting until cardiac arrest has been confirmed under the NHS Pathways call handling system.

The organisation claims the change could reduce the chances of survival for some patients who suffer a cardiac arrest following a heart attack.

According to Public Access Defibrillators UK, around 20 per cent of heart attacks can lead to cardiac arrest. The charity says early access to a defibrillator is critical, with survival rates significantly improved when a shock is delivered alongside CPR within the first few minutes of a cardiac arrest.

The charity claims that since the introduction of the latest NHS Pathways system in 2025, defibrillators are only deployed once a caller confirms a patient has gone into cardiac arrest.

It says this could lead to delays where callers mistake agonal breathing, which can occur during cardiac arrest, for laboured breathing associated with a heart attack.

In response, the charity has launched its Self Deploy Public Access Defibrillator (SDPAD) scheme, allowing members of the public to access nearby defibrillators as a precaution while waiting for emergency crews to arrive.

The scheme has been developed by award-winning Guiseley resident Brian Firth, a former community first responder, who founded Public Access Defibrillators UK. The volunteer-run, non-profit organisation installs and maintains community public access defibrillators across Yorkshire.

Under the scheme, people can locate its defibrillators through an online map and obtain an access code to unlock the cabinet if they believe a patient may be at risk of cardiac arrest.

The charity stresses that anyone faced with a suspected heart attack or cardiac arrest should call 999 immediately before attempting to retrieve a defibrillator and should inform the ambulance operator if a device is being collected.

Public Access Defibrillators UK is encouraging residents to familiarise themselves with the locations of their nearest defibrillators at home and work and to learn how the new system operates before an emergency arises.

Mr Firth said: "All our defibrillators will still be deployed by the Yorkshire Ambulance Service to anyone in cardiac arrest, but the new scheme allows you to Self Deploy it if someone is having a heart attack and you feel more reassured having the defibrillator on scene as a precaution.

"The format is very simply and quick. You can access the SDPAD locations map by creating a link on your laptop or phone to www.sdpads.uk. Apparently, this link does not work with all servers, in which case you can create a link directly to www.publicaccessdefibrillators.uk/locations."

A spokesperson for Yorkshire Ambulance Service said: "NHS Pathways is a telephone triage system used by more than half of the UK’s ambulance services to assess patients needing urgent and emergency medical help and it was first introduced to assess 999 calls at Yorkshire Ambulance Service from May 2025 in a phased transition from the previous system.

"The NHS Pathways system asks more detailed medical questions, which means the advice given is typically more accurate and better matches the patient’s condition.

"Since switching to NHS Pathways, the way 999 calls are handled and coded has changed. As a result, patients may receive different advice depending on the information gathered during the assessment.

"One key change is how defibrillators are allocated to emergencies. Now, a defibrillator is only advised when it’s clear someone is having a cardiac arrest and CPR instructions are being given over the phone. The previous system used to prompt the allocation of a defibrillator more often and this was done as a precautionary measure when, in many cases, they weren’t needed. Our deployment rates are now at similar levels to most of the other ambulance services who use NHS Pathways as their telephone triage system.

"Defibrillators are still extremely important when someone’s heart has stopped, and they should be used alongside CPR wherever possible.

"We continue to monitor the quality of 999 calls made to the service and address any issues that may arise from the use of NHS Pathways in our Emergency Operations Centre."
