## From a bullet train to a murder trial relic: 6 objects you can adopt at York's NRM

JR West 'Shinkasen' or Bullet Train - 2002. Photo - Trustees of the Science Museum Group.

  *York National Railway Museum launches adopt-an-object scheme*

FROM a Japanese bullet train that transformed global travel to a model rail carriage linked to a sensational murder trial, York's National Railway Museum is giving people the rare chance to adopt one of six remarkable objects in its collection.

The six objects available for adoption span almost two centuries of innovation, royal travel, engineering achievement and even crime.

Among them are the last steam locomotive built by British Railways, Britain's oldest surviving royal railway carriage, and one of the world's most influential trains: the Shinkansen Bullet Train.

Standing proudly in the museum's Great Hall, the sleek white train remains one of the most recognisable rail vehicles ever created.

  Shinkansen bullet train NRM credit Science Museum Group. © Trustees of the Science Museum Group


When Japan launched the Shinkansen in 1964, it revolutionised long-distance travel.

At a time when railways around the world were struggling to compete with growing road and air travel, the Japanese high-speed rail network demonstrated what rail could achieve when reliability, speed and passenger comfort were prioritised.

The original trains could travel at speeds of up to 130mph and reduced journey times between Tokyo and Osaka dramatically.

More than six decades later, the influence of the Shinkansen can still be seen in high-speed rail networks across Europe and Asia.

Rob Scargill, lead curator at the National Railway Museum, said: “Our bullet train at the museum is particularly special because it is one of only two examples outside Japan, as well as being the first rail vehicle built and operated outside Britain to enter the National Collection.

"For anyone fascinated by world-leading Japanese technology, innovation and design, this beautiful power car offers a tangible link to one of the 20th century's greatest engineering success stories.”

For those who prefer their history with an element of mystery, another adoption option tells a far darker tale.

Hidden away in North Shed, the museum's open store, is a model of a third-class railway carriage that played a pivotal role in one of Edwardian Britain's most sensational criminal cases.

Built in 1901, the model was created to help a jury understand the layout of a railway compartment in which a fatal shooting had taken place on a train travelling between Surbiton and Vauxhall.

The detailed model shows the compartment where farmer William Pearson was murdered during an attempted robbery by George Parker.

  One of the objects you can adopt at the NRM – a model of a third-class railway carriage that played a pivotal role in one of Edwardian Britain's most sensational criminal cases. Photo - Trustees of the Science Museum Group


A fellow passenger, Mrs King, witnessed the incident and famously persuaded the attacker to throw his revolver from the train before alerting railway staff and helping lead to his capture.

Today, the model remains a fascinating reminder that railway history is about much more than locomotives and engineering. It offers a glimpse into social history, crime, justice and everyday life on Britain's railways at the turn of the 20th century.

  One of the objects you can adopt at the NRM. Photo - Trustees of the Science Museum Group.


Another standout object, this time combining railway history with wartime survival, is the Furness Railway locomotive, known as "Coppernob".

Coppernob is one of the oldest locomotives available for adoption and one of the museum's most distinctive exhibits. Built in 1846, during the formative years of Britain's railway network, the locomotive earned its nickname from its striking copper-clad firebox.

  'Coppernob' – one of the objects you can adopt at the NRM. Photo - Trustees of the Science Museum Group


"Coppernob" worked for more than 50 years before being preserved, a remarkable achievement in itself.

But what makes it truly unique is what happened after retirement. In 1941, whilst displayed at Barrow Station, the locomotive was caught in a German air raid. Shrapnel damaged the engine and, remarkably, the scars can still be seen on the copper casing today.

  'Coppernob' – one of the objects you can adopt at the NRM. Photo - Trustees of the Science Museum Group


Andrew McLean, head curator at the National Railway Museum, is a great admirer of Coppernob and always includes its remarkable story on his tours of Great Hall.

He said: “It is rare to encounter an object that tells so many overlapping stories: the birth of Britain's railways, Victorian engineering, preservation history and the impact of the Second World War. More than 180 years after it was built, Coppernob continues to capture visitors' imaginations.”

The remaining adoption choices are equally varied. Supporters can adopt Evening Star, the final steam locomotive built by British Railways and a symbol of the end of Britain's steam age; Queen Adelaide's Saloon, the oldest surviving royal railway carriage in Britain; or Diesel Railcar No. 4, an innovative streamlined vehicle that helped shape the future of rail travel in the 1930s.

  Evening Star at the NRM - Photo - Trustees of the Science Museum Group


While each object tells a different story, every adoption helps support the museum's work as a charity. Donations contribute towards conservation, learning programmes and the ongoing care of a collection that attracts visitors from around the world.

Find out more at railwaymuseum.org.uk/adopt-an-object.

For as little as £3 a month, unique-gift-hunters can adopt one of these six objects from the museum's collection for a year.

Adopters will receive a digital adoption certificate, a personalised thank you from the museum’s senior curator of rail transport and technology, Anthony Coulls, and regular updates about the object.

Anthony said: “This exciting scheme is a first for the museum, and a great opportunity to be one of the first adopters of some of our most treasured railway objects.”
