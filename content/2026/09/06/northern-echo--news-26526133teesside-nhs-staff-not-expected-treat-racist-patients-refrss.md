Health workers have been told they are not expected to carry on treating patients who are persistently racist.

NHS bosses on Teesside are taking a zero-tolerance approach to violence and aggression against healthcare staff following “constant” attacks in emergency departments.

A&E staff at James Cook University Hospital have started wearing body cameras and other action, like prosecuting violent offenders and refusing treatment to abusive patients, is being taken. It’s after Teesside hospitals recorded more than 1,400 incidents of violence and aggression in 2025.

The issue was raised at board meetings of University Hospitals Tees NHS group, which covers both North and South Tees NHS Foundation Trusts, as leaders strive to get the message out that abusive or threatening behaviour towards staff is unacceptable. Last September members heard the trusts were “not moving the needle” in the victimisation and harassment of staff from Black, Asian or ethnic minority (BAME) backgrounds.

That meeting was told of a lack of improvement in the treatment of staff in global majority staff – from African, Asian, dual heritage or previously referred to as ethnic minorities – who experience discrimination, bullying and harassment. In the latest board meeting yesterday (Thursday, September 3), non-executive director Mark Dias said: “We’re now seeing some positive action, certainly the emergency department and the wearing of body cameras.”

He referred to the latest workforce race equality report, saying: “Our global majority staff are facing discrimination and harassment from patients on a regular basis. That’s consistent across the trusts. I just ask that we keep a vigilant eye on this.”

Group chief executive Stacey Hunter said: “This is an all-day, every-day effort. We are trying to make sure that when staff are unfortunate enough to be on the receiving end of racist or other attacks, they feel able to talk to us and raise that. That’s the first really important step.”

She said they were treating such attacks as “never events” – serious and preventable incidents – and they had good support from Cleveland Police to make staff feel safe when they are not at work: “Some of those incidents have been when staff are travelling into work.

“Definitely always more for us to do but I think we are gaining more confidence that our staff feel able to raise these issues with us. Without that, it’s very difficult to make any kind of response.”

She said they wanted to send messages and appeal to people: “Without the staff from a global majority that work in our teams, that make up quite a significant part of our capacity and capabilities, we would not be able to look after our local community. If you want to get care when you are in need of care, then we really need you to live within our values when you are in our hospital.

“We are being very clear with our staff that, unless somebody’s got an emergency life-or-limb problem, if they are facing persistent racism from the patient, then we do not expect them to look after that patient because that’s not right.

“We will keep having to reiterate so that staff feel confident because people often worry that they’ll get into trouble. There are certain situations where it would be wholly unacceptable to walk away from patients.

“But there are some times where, if you’ve got persistent behaviour that is outwith our values and what we expect to see, then actually we cannot and will not expect our staff to continue to treat that patient in that moment.”

Her separate chief executive report says emergency department teams on Teesside “have continued to raise awareness of the increasing incidence and impact of violence, aggression and abusive behaviour directed towards NHS staff”, with protecting staff from harm a key priority. Body-worn cameras in the James Cook emergency department have been described as a safeguard for staff to aggression and help investigate incidents.

“Early feedback from clinical teams has been positive, with staff reporting increased confidence and reassurance when managing challenging situations,” says the report. “The trust remains committed to working with staff, partner organisations and local communities to reduce incidents of violence and aggression, support staff wellbeing, and ensure that all colleagues can work in an environment that is safe, secure and respectful.”

The race equality report highlighted rising numbers of global majority staff experiencing bullying, harassment and abuse from staff in both trusts, from patients, relatives or the public in the South Tees trust which includes James Cook, or discrimination from a manager or team leader in the North Tees trust. Such staff make up 15% to 16.5% of the trusts’ workforce.

Chief people officer Rachael Metcalf said: “Over the last couple of years we have really tried to increase engagement with our staff and our networks. And staff are coming forward and they are telling us.

“Our global minority staff do have a negative experience of work in comparison to white colleagues. That gap remains, we’re reducing the gap but I think the effort and work we’re putting in to reduce that gap and lived experience isn’t filtering through at that same pace.

“We will continue doing what we are going. We are seeing more than ever colleagues coming forward.”
