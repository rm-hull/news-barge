Up to 6,000 tradies will get free qualifications under a $78m federal scheme designed to tackle construction skills shortages.

Workers with at least three years’ experience will have their prior learning recognised, and they will then be given “gap training” to reach their qualification, fast-tracking the process.

The skills and training minister, Andrew Giles, said the program would “back in workers who have the right skills for the job, but need that extra help to get the formal qualification they need”:

This program recognises the expertise they’ve already developed and helps them gain nationally recognised qualifications faster.

For a long time industry and skills stakeholders have called for greater recognition of existing skills and alternative pathways to formal trade qualifications – our government has listened and is taking action.

Queensland triple zero glitch fixed, minister says

Queensland’s health minister said a computer bug in the state’s triple zero system was just a “minor hiccup”.

The service was forced to switch to a manual backup last week after a bug emerged during a system update to a new computer aided dispatch system. Staff were required to radio crews directly and write down information manually, rather than using the system.

Health minister Tim Nicholls said that caused “some slowness in the system”, but that a patient would not have noticed any difference as a result. All low-acuity patients made to wait were monitored by the services’ clinical services hub. He said:

The alternative system kicked in. No triple zero calls were missed. There were no adverse patient events.

The care that’s being provided by the (clinical services) hub and by the [Queensland Ambulance Service] was unchanged.

He said a patch had been implemented on Friday and fixed the problem.

Queensland government announces 2,200 homes for Brisbane site

Queensland’s government has signed off on a plan for 2,200 homes in Brisbane’s north, on a site that had been earmarked for an Olympics athletes’ village.

The deputy premier, Jarrod Bleijie, announced five companies had won bids to build the riverfront development, at Northshore Hamilton.

The area is listed as having a high likelihood of flooding and high likelihood of storm tide flooding according to Brisbane city council’s flood mapping.

Asked if the area had a risk of flooding, Bleijie said:

That all gets taken into consideration as it does with anyone building along the river.

He said that public transport access would be taken into consideration by Economic Development Queensland, which owns the site and will be the development authority.

Taxpayers will pay $154m to prepare the site for development. It will not include any dwellings designated as affordable housing; under Labor’s plan for the site, about 5% of dwellings were mandated to be let below market rate.

The development will also include about 21,600 sq m of shops, dining and services.

Plan to criminalise burning of Australian flag about ‘national pride’, says Taylor

Tom McIlroy

As touched on earlier, the opposition wants new laws to make it a criminal offence to burn the Australian flag or damage war memorials.

As the Coalition fights to regain standing against Pauline Hanson and One Nation, the opposition’s legislation would create a federal criminal offence for serious public desecration of the Australian flag, where a person is reckless or where their actions would incite hatred or violence, threaten public order or cause a public disturbance.

It would also create a federal criminal offence for deliberately damaging, defacing, desecrating or destroying war memorials and other significant memorials commemorating Australia’s history.

The opposition leader, Angus Taylor, says the plan is about “standing up for the national pride that we should all feel for our great country.”

The assistant foreign minister, Matt Thistlethwaite, says the plan is about politics:

I think that there’s a bit of an element of desperation there, trying to chase One Nation.

It’s been tried on several occasions over many, many years. The parliament has generally always rejected it because of the notion of upholding freedom of speech for Australians and that freedom of political expression.

Nepal situation becoming more difficult with time: assistant foreign minister

Tom McIlroy

Matt Thistlethwaite, the assistant foreign minister, has told News24 there is no change in the situation of missing Australians in Nepal overnight.

He says 36 Australians still remain unaccounted for as of Sunday morning. He said:

We haven’t got any confirmed deaths yet. We’re hopeful that we’ll be able to relocate and locate as many of those as we possibly can.

Obviously, the longer this goes on, the more difficult that that becomes. But there are glimmers of hope. We managed to locate seven Australians this week.

Damaged houses covered in sludge in the aftermath of flash floods at Devighat in Nepal’s Nuwakot district on 5 September. Photograph: Prakash Mathema/AFP/Getty Images

He said it was positive that two people had been rescued from hydroelectric tunnels in the disaster area in recent days:

We sent 47 Australian personnel to the region to assist with the rescue and recovery, and we’re sending a further seven this week, so we’re doing everything we can to try and locate those Australians that are missing.

There are now 47 Australians deployed to the crisis, including seven disaster victim identification specialists and a psychologist.

More than 5,000 people are still missing and there have been 1,332 confirmed deaths.

Natasha Stott Despoja to lead inquiry into sexual violence in military

Former Democrats senator Natasha Stott Despoja, who has done a wealth of work with women and domestic violence, will lead an independent inquiry into sexual violence in the military.

Stott Despoja tells the ABC the issue was both systemic and underreported, possibly by more than 60%. She said the inquiry would likely report by the end of next year.

She said:

I’ll make sure that we do this in a way that is trauma informed, victim-centric, and people will have the wellbeing and the legal supports that they need.

But I do acknowledge some people have told and retold their stories, and I do not underestimate the level of trauma involved in that.

So I’m very conscious of making sure that victim-survivors are at the heart and the core of this inquiry and that they’re treated with the dignity and the respect that they deserve.

Coalition supports compulsory superannuation: Taylor

Angus Taylor has been asked about Pauline Hanson’s calls for people facing tough financial pressures to be able to access their retirement savings.

Hanson argued earlier this month that in a cost-of-living crisis, it’s not right to force people to save for the future when they can’t make ends meet today.

Taylor has told News24 the Coalition supports the superannuation guarantee rate of 12% and notes there are already rules allowing people in financial distress to access some of their savings.

He said the Coalition had an “an open mind” on revisiting its policy to allow first homebuyers to access more of their retirement savings for a home deposit. That policy was put to voters at the 2025 election and is being considered again by the Coalition ahead of the next poll. Taylor said:

The important point I would make at this point is that we need to cap immigration based on housing supply, get more houses built, slash the red tape in the construction industry.

Taylor unconcerned about tobacco donations to Nationals

Angus Taylor has told News24 the decision by the Nationals to take political donations from tobacco companies is a decision for the party.

The Nationals are the only major party in Australia to still accept tobacco money. The Liberals stopped accepting tobacco donations in 2013, when Tony Abbott was opposition leader.

Labor stopped taking tobacco donations in 2004.

In 2024-25, the Nationals received $225,500 from two large manufacturers. British American Tobacco donated $55,000 to the Nationals in 2022-23 for the first time in more than a decade. A year later, it sent the party $88,000.

Taylor said he wouldn’t discuss donations to the junior Coalition partner:

That’s a matter for the National party. I’m not going to get into that,” he said.

We’ve made this decision. What this is about is doing what’s right for Australia and Australians.

Excise cut plan won’t see smoking rates rise: Taylor

Tom McIlroy

The opposition leader, Angus Taylor, says the Coalition’s plan for an 80% cut to tobacco excise won’t cause an increase in smoking rates, telling News 24 the new policy idea will undermine the business model of organised crime.

Asked if cheaper cigarettes will see more people take up smoking, Taylor said “the horse has already bolted on this”:

The price is already around $15 or less for most smokers, and anyone can choose to buy that. You can walk into shops all over the country and and pay $10 or $15 to buy a pack of cigarettes now.

You’ve got to recognise the reality here. This is funding organised crime.

It will disrupt the business model. It will take the profits and the customers away from organised crime in this country.

[Legislation] could also include misinformation and disinformation. This would risk the usual culture war debate about free speech, while we blithely accept the changes to our culture and society that are being freely imposed by Mark Zuckerberg, Elon Musk and others.

What we should be worried about is the sovereignty of the information landscape and the fact that we’re being controlled by the systems – not the other way around.

Hefty fines for social media companies under government plan

The onus will be on social media companies to identify and mitigate risks on their platforms under the government’s proposed legislation, Insiders host David Speers says, and that legislation will be unveiled this week.

He says it will cover illegal content including illegal pornography being made available to adults, and for kids there would be “around half a dozen additional areas of psychosocial harm and that includes content around body image and bullying”.

He says:

The companies would face hefty fines of more than $100m if they fail to restrict this content and as for algorithms – regular pop-up messages would be required, asking if users want to stick with the algorithm or only see people they’re following and see what they’re posting. The user would have the choice.

Researchers and the eSafety commissioner would also be empowered to police the social media platforms by using what are called ‘sock puppets’ posing as children to monitor what is being fed to kids online.
