## 127 years and still standing: the remarkable story of York's 'Morrisons chimney'

Foss Islands Road in the early 1920s.Photo: City Engineers/ Explore York Libraries and Archives

  *How York's Foss Islands chimney survived for 127 years by DAVID WILSON*

KNOWN to present-day York residents as the ‘Morrison’s chimney’, the chimney was for many decades part of what was officially known as The Destructor.

Plagued by the menace of growing heaps of household waste and their associated dangers to health in the 19th century, the City Fathers decided to incinerate York’s waste.

In 1899, they commissioned the local firm of Parker & Sharp to build a towering chimney half-way down Foss Islands Road, and it’s still there today.

  The Destructor today in Foss Islands Road - aka the Morrisons chimney. Photo supplied


The chimney is a tapering, 55-metre (180 feet) octagonal red-brick shaft with cream brick necking. Early on, the chimney ran into problems when settlement was detected on the site.

John Ormerod of Stockton Lane wrote to this newspaper in 2010 stating that the chimney was built on a wooden foundation and calculated to weigh 2,120 tons.

He explained that the settlement of the foundations had caused some of the brickwork to crack, so in 1904 several iron bands were fitted as reinforcement.

York Corporation minutes from the decade of the 1890s through to the first decade of the 20th century note concerns about delays in the construction of the chimney. Another concern raised was the build-up of refuse on the platform.

  Foss Islands Road in the early 1920s.Photo: City Engineers/ Explore York Libraries and Archives


Originally, the Destructor was constructed to process 40-60 tons of waste every 24 hours. In 1896, household waste had been calculated at 118 loads per week.

The official 1891 census figures for the municipal borough of York had recorded a population of 51,105.

By 1896, the population of the city was estimated to be around 72,000.

Five years later, the official census of 1901 gave the population of York parliamentary borough as 75,521.

The city was growing fast and producing more refuse.

By 1902, the figure for household waste had increased to 180 loads with a corresponding increase in the weight of the loads from 14 cwt to 1 ton. That same year, the City Engineer noted that the use of the Destructor would need to be extended.

  This image shows the rear of Navigation Road and Constitution Place, in Walmgate, in about 1933. The photograph shows people standing in the doorways of most of the houses, looking directly at the camera. There is also a pram in the yard. The chimney


Adjacent to The Destructor was a coal-fired power station which powered the lights of the city and this facility initially shared the chimney with The Destructor incinerator.

Surplus steam produced by incinerated refuse was used by the electricity generating station. The power station later built its own chimneys nearby, and stopped using the original chimney from the 1920s onwards.

During the Second World War, the power-station and the chimney were camouflaged to prevent bombing by enemy aircraft. Both installations survived the war, and a subsequent explosion at the power-station on 22 October, 1949.

The blast threw 20-25-ton chunks of machinery over 150 feet away. Six of the nine staff members were injured and there was one fatality.

Repairs were undertaken and the facility continued to supply power to the city.

The 40 MW power-station was officially closed on October 25,1976, although it was in use as a stand-by station until 1977.

  Foss Island chimney. Photo by.Barbara Hudson


The Destructor facility and its chimney were decommissioned in the 1970s and left redundant for several years.

Between the 1970s and 2018, most of York’s non-recyclable waste was sent to the Harewood Whin landfill site near Rufforth and managed in partnership with Yorwaste.

Morrisons first opened its supermarket on Foss Islands Road in 2007, and as owners of the surrounding land, the company remains legally responsible for the upkeep of the chimney.

It initially wanted to use it for marketing purposes by painting a huge sign MORRISON’S in large letters down the side of the chimney. The planning application met with considerable opposition from local heritage groups, and York City Council rejected the application out of hand. Any moves to have the chimney demolished were met with equal refusals since the chimney had been accorded Grade II listed status some years previously on June 24,1983.

Last year, Morrison’s agreed to undertake repair work to the chimney. Repointing is required as well as the replacement of the metal rings. Morrison shoppers will have noticed that the path and metal benches around the chimney have been fenced off as a security measure.

Longstanding York resident Sheila Dickson recalls seeing smoke billowing out of the Destructor chimney in the early 1960s.

  2015: Steeplejacks pictured on the chimney in the Morrisons car park. Pic: Anthony Chappel-Ross


She also remembers watching TV documentaries featuring Fred Dibnah. Dibnah was a famous steeplejack who dismantled numerous industrial chimneys. There is, however, no record of Dibnah attempting to demolish The York Destructor chimney.

He is said to have consulted his close associate about it, York-based demolition expert Lawrence ‘Lol’ Ryan. Dibnah was, in fact, a passionate conservationist who deeply respected Victorian craftmanship.

hundred years later, it was decided to incinerate household waste once again. A £1.4 billion incinerator at Allerton Waste Recovery Park was opened in February 2018 on a quarry site next to the A1(M) between York and Harrogate.

It was intended to be a modern, cleaner version of the York Destructor in the wake of stricter legislation surrounding emissions. The Allerton Park facility can process around 320,000 tonnes of household waste as well as generate about 219 gigawatt hours (GWh) to 220,000 megawatt hours (MWh) of electricity.

It’s a far cry from The Foss Islands chimney belching out smoke from incinerated waste. But quantities of household refuse continue to grow and challenge our capacity to deal with them satisfactorily. We urgently need to find solutions to the perennial problem of waste management.

  *David Wilson is a community writer with The Press*
