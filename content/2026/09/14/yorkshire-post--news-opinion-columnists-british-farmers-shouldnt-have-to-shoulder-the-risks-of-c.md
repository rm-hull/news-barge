# British farmers shouldn’t have to shoulder the risks of changing climate - Angela Eagle

## I know that the past year has been incredibly difficult for many farmers. You’ve had rising input costs, water supplies drained by back-to-back heat waves and climate-driven outbreaks of disease, and most notably bluetongue, which we’re having to deal with at the moment.

I think when a third of farmers report that they make no profit, we’ve got to think about how to change that. If we’re relying on farmers to put food on our tables, farming has to put money in the pockets of those who farm. That is not a rocket science conclusion to come to.

So that’s why we’re taking action to help the sector respond to immediate pressures and build a more profitable, productive, and sustainable future.

And we do that on the cusp of another great agricultural revolution, I believe, with the technology that has increasingly become available.

![Farmers drive their tractors through a dry and dusty field. PIC: James Hardisty](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOmVmYzk4Y2Y3LTc4ZGMtNGY5OS1iNGI1LWNjMTExZWNkYzY3ZDo4ODEyYTYyNS1kNThmLTRiNDYtODE5NC0wZmM0NTUwNzMzNTM=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)



In his first month of office, the Prime Minister visited a farm to announce a £65m support package, including improved access to water and £15m for on-farm reservoirs.

I also know this is an incredibly difficult time for animal keepers dealing with the bluetongue outbreak. APHA has published a new heat map of suspected and confirmed cases. And from September 11, a revised reporting process will make it quicker and easier to confirm disease.

Please speak to your vet about vaccination. It significantly reduces the severity of the disease, and you can apply for a free annual vet visit under the Animal Health and Welfare Pathway.

But immediate support isn’t enough. For too long farmers have been left to get on with it with nobody thinking about the overall structure. That’s why I worked closely with the sector on the first ever strategic plan for agriculture, setting out how farming needs to evolve – combining nature-led solutions and farming together that will make us more resilient as we look to the future.

I’d like to reiterate my full-throated support for the Farming Roadmap. The Roadmap doesn’t see nature and farming pulling in opposite directions. Healthy soil holds water in a drought and it drains it in a deluge. Hedgerows shelter livestock in a heat wave, as do trees.

Our Land Use Framework says that 15 per cent of land use needs to change and that we can change it and produce the same levels of food. We want to help that transition come about.

Our Sustainable Farming Incentive encourages nature-based practices which we need to see adopted.

Our Farming Improvement Partnership Board is bringing industry leaders into the same room to set system-wide plans for growth, starting with our growth plan for horticulture, which I am extremely committed to making a success of. With poultry and other sectors to follow.

British farmers shouldn’t have to shoulder the risks of changing climate.

I believe we’re at a turning point, especially after the summer we have just had, which, let’s face it, is the third difficult summer in the last five years, and it’s probably going to be repeated in its difficulties perhaps next year, with the El Niño that we’ve got forming in the ocean on the other side of the world.

So, we’re at a turning point where the Farming Roadmap, the Farming and Food Partnership Board, the Land Use Framework, all need to come together to set a direction to make farming profitable and ensure that we can support a good and secure food supply.

An adapted version of a speech by Environment Secretary Angela Eagle at the National Farmers' Union’s Back British Farming Day 2026 reception.
