WHEN the village of Neerkant, Netherlands, gathered on Sunday, June 21, it marked the end of a long journey.

It began with a single question asked by resident Jürgen Beekers: How could a wartime tragedy so close to home be remembered when almost nothing about it was known?

Jürgen, born in Neerkant, now living in nearby Meijel, had contributed to 2019 project 75 Years Liberation of Neerkant and supported Neerkant Historical Heritage with research into the Second World War. His work documenting the occupation, liberation and civilians and soldiers killed in and around the village uncovered a forgotten story: the crash of a Vickers Wellington Mk X, serial number HE924, of No 166 Squadron, Royal Air Force, which came down just outside Neerkant during the night of June 21-22, 1943.

All five crew members were killed. Their names were known, but little else. No memorial marked the site. No detailed account in the village. Jürgen began to investigate.

His research soon expanded far beyond local archives. In 2021 he contacted Sandra Pickles, genealogist and an ex-Hanson School girl, while seeking information about Pilot Officer George Roland Wright, the aircraft’s navigator. Sandra obtained extensive material about Wright’s life and service, and even reached a relative who shared family photographs of George, his wife and parents.

Piece by piece, Jürgen reconstructed the story of the aircraft, its mission and crew. As the picture grew clearer, so did his conviction that the men deserved a permanent memorial at the crash site.

George, known to his family as Roland, was born in Bradford in 1908; second youngest of four children. His father was a bank clerk. Roland attended Hanson Boys School from 1919-1924.

In 1938 he married Mary Driver, a teacher at Tong Street Church Infant School; Mary had attended Hanson Girls School. Her brother, James, an ex-Hanson boy, was also killed in the war.

Roland joined the Auxiliary Air Force in 1938, and gained his ‘wings’ in 1942. He was employed as the Midlands Representative of Bradford Fabrics Limited. His probate lists his address as Undercliffe, Russell Bank Road, Sutton Coldfield, so he named his house after the area of Bradford he’d lived in.

In December 2022, Jürgen presented his idea at a village council meeting. Four residents volunteered, forming a five-person committee. What followed was three years of persistence, negotiation and problem solving. Land ownership, environmental restrictions, soil conditions, traffic safety and proximity to a protected nature reserve all posed obstacles. Each required formal approval and specialist advice.

Finally, in March 2026, permission was granted. The municipality allocated a generous area, around 250-300 m², for a memorial space. This allowed the project to grow beyond a single monument. The completed memorial area now contains distinct elements: A striking Corten steel sarcophagus bearing five steel plates, each engraved with the name, age, nationality and laser cut portrait of the crew. A tall pole carrying the silhouette of a Vickers Wellington, whose shadow falls across the concrete foundation when the sun is at its height.

  The monument at the going down of the sun 

*(Image: Sharon Knott)*

A rare surviving defensive trench built by the Dutch army in 1939-40. Discovered during excavation works, it is believed to be the only remaining concrete trench in the Netherlands.

Seven steel columns listing the names of 72 Allied soldiers who died during the liberation of Neerkant.

Set between meadows and a nature reserve, the memorial area offers a quiet, contemplative space where local history and international sacrifice meet.

The five men who died in the crash are now honoured at the site: Flight Sergeant William Francis Payne, Bomb Aimer, age 23; Flight Sergeant Arthur Burgess, Pilot, 23; Pilot Officer James Karl Somers, Wireless Operator, 29; Pilot Officer George Roland Wright, Navigator, 35; Sergeant Eric Jeffs, Rear Gunner, 21. Four of the men were British; Pilot Officer Somers was Australian.

The unveiling ceremony on June 21 drew a large crowd of villagers, guests, 22 relatives of the crew, and two representatives of the RAF. Although no members of Roland Wright’s family were able to attend, their earlier contributions to the research were acknowledged.

  A large gathering, including 22 relatives of the men, at Netherlands WW2 memorial 

*(Image: Sharon Knott)*

The ceremony included a reading from the family of James Karl Somers, delivered by Jürgen; a poem recited by local children; musical accompaniment from a pipe band; and performances of Vera Lynn’s The White Cliffs of Dover and We’ll Meet Again. Speeches were given by a municipal representative and a spokesperson for the families from England.

  Pipers playing at the moving ceremony. Images: Jürgen Beekers


For Jürgen, the moment was both the culmination of years of work and a reminder of why he began: to ensure that the men of Wellington HE924 would not be forgotten.

What started as a single research question has become a landmark of remembrance, connecting Neerkant’s wartime past with its present. Thanks to the dedication of Jürgen Beekers and his committee the story of the crash, the crew and the liberation of the village now has a permanent home.

* Sharon Knott is a genealogist and volunteer researcher at the Museum at Hanson Academy. She is also a public speaker and tour guide with the Commonwealth War Graves.

As part of Heritage Open Days, Sharon is giving a presentation on her research that culminated in the book The Long Shadow of War - Hanson Boys Remembered. The talk is at Hanson Academy on Saturday, September 19 at 1pm.
