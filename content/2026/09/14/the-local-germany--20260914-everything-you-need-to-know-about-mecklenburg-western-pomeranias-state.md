On Sunday September 20th, voters in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, Germany's north-eastern Baltic Sea state, will elect a new state parliament.

Although it’s one of Germany's smaller states, with around 1.6 million residents, the main election battle between the centre-left SPD and the far-right AfD is attracting attention far beyond its borders.

Known locally as "Meck-Pomm" or simply "MV", the state stretches from the Baltic Sea islands of Rügen and Usedom to the Mecklenburg Lake District.

The results of Sunday's election will help determine policies affecting schools, universities, policing, integration programmes, public services and the political climate in the state for the next five years.

**Who can vote in the election?**

Only German citizens can vote in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania's state election. Additionally, voters must be at least 16 years old and have had their primary residence registered in the state for at least 37 days before election day.

Voters cast two votes: one for a local constituency candidate and one for a party list. Parties generally need at least five percent of the vote to enter the Landtag, or state parliament, which has at least 71 members.

The race has effectively become a two-way contest between incumbent Minister-President Manuela Schwesig of the centre-left Social Democrats (SPD) and the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD).

According to the latest ZDF Politbarometer, the SPD is polling at 34 percent and the AfD at 37 percent, with the gap between the two parties having narrowed in recent weeks.

The SPD has made a number of election promises, including an "MV Investment Plan 2035", continued free childcare and major investment in schools. But much of their campaign actually centres on Schwesig herself – as well as constant reminders that a vote for the SPD is the best way of voting against the AfD.

Posters show Schwesig dressed in red in front of Schwerin Castle under the slogan "The Woman Against Blue" (*Die Frau Gegen Blau*).

ZDF reports that 61 percent of respondents want her to remain Minister-President, while only 28 percent back AfD candidate Leif-Erik Holm for the role.

The AfD is campaigning under the slogan "Ready for the Blue Turn". Its programme focuses heavily on tougher migration controls and major changes to policing and energy policy.

The Christian Democrats (CDU) are languishing on 7 percent in the latest polling, while the Greens are fighting to stay above the five-percent threshold needed to enter parliament.

The BSW is polling at around 4 percent, making it one of several smaller parties whose performance could prove decisive when coalition negotiations begin after the vote.

While migration often dominates headlines, polling suggests that voters in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania are actually more concerned about bread-and-butter issues – including the economy, jobs, schools, education and infrastructure.

Rising living costs are also a major concern among voters, along with pensions, high gas and energy prices, and Germany's support for Ukraine.

**What happens after polling day?**

Current polling suggests the existing SPD-Left coalition could struggle to retain a majority.

As in Saxony-Anhalt, smaller parties such as Sahra Wagenknecht's BSW are hovering around the five-percent threshold, meaning their performance could have an outsized effect on coalition mathematics.

The CDU is facing a particularly difficult election. If the polls are correct, the party is reportedly on course to record its worst result in a state election anywhere in Germany since 1949.

**What could the result mean for Germany?**

Much of the national attention surrounding the election is focused on issues such as migration, energy prices, support for Ukraine and dissatisfaction with Chancellor Friedrich Merz's federal coalition – despite the fact that the state government in Schwerin has limited power in any of these areas.

Nationally, politicians and media outlets have increasingly framed the election as a test of broader political trends beyond Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania – as a snapshot of public opinion towards the federal government's black-red coalition in Berlin and an indication of whether the AfD can build on its election victory in neighbouring Saxony-Anhalt.

Sahra Wagenknecht is among the politicians keen to push that interpretation. The founder of the BSW is openly arguing that a strong result for her party could topple Chancellor Friedrich Merz.

Wagenknecht is also one of the few German politicians to openly reject the conventional "firewall" against the AfD. While Germany’s mainstream parties generally refuse to cooperate with the far-right party, the BSW has agreed to talks following the AfD’s shock election victory in Saxony-Anhalt.

The talks have raised the prospect of the AfD entering state government for the first time - although the parties remain divided over what any cooperation would look like.
