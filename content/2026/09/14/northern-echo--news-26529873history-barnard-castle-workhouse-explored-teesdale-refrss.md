IN July, we told how the Ripon Workhouse Museum had reopened after a £2.8m refurbishment, led by the National Lottery funding, which has allowed the former council offices to be transformed into a thought-provoking visitor attraction.

It tells a story of poverty, welfare, justice and asks questions about fairness.

  Ripon Workhouse Museum


“My grandfather, James Arthur Wass, was born in the Ripon Workhouse on April 21, 1884,” writes Les Bland in response to the article. “His mother and his brothers and sisters – Lucy, seven, Sabina, six, William, four, and Henry, two – had to go there when their father deserted them, going off to Ireland where he started another family.

“My grandfather went on to have 10 children. He died in 1958, when I was 11 – I remember him well.”

  


After the refurbishment, Ripon boasts that it has probably the best preserved workhouse in the country, which is a big boast because nearly every town had one.

They were set up by the Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834. It ordered parishes, which had previously had their own spasmodic arrangements for dealing with the poor, to club together into unions to build a workhouse.

Each parish had to pay a poor rate to finance the workhouse.

The workhouse was for people who had no means of supporting themselves either due to illness, old age, young age, alcoholism, mental health issues or family breakdown, like desertion.

Its regime was designed to be tough and cheap so that no one would want to go there for a cushy life, but at least it prevented the poverty-stricken from clogging up the streets with their annoying begging.

  


In our “workhouse of the week” slot, we come to Barnard Castle, the home of the Teesdale Union workhouse – the union covered 175,000 hectares from Middleton and Bowes through Gainford and Barney and out to Hutton Magna on the edge of Richmondshire.

Barney’s workhouse was built in 1838 at the top of Galgate on the edge of the countryside.

It was designed by renowned Newcastle architect John Green along the lines of the model workhouse proposed at a national level by the Poor Law Commission.

  The infirmary wing of Barnard Castle workhouse has been converted to residential 

*(Image: Chris Lloyd)*

It could house 138 inmates in its two wings. One wing was for males, and the other for females, with rooms in each wing for the old, the young and the able. Each inmate was expected to work in tough, menial jobs like breaking stones and unwinding old rope, and to assist the running of the workhouse in the garden or kitchen or by working in the laundry or scrubbing floors.

Just like at Ripon, the Barney workhouse was dominated by the central master’s residence. The master, rather like Harry Secombe’s character in the 1968 film Oliver!, rules the workhouse, and was usually married to the matron.

With the fearsome master in charge and the sexes divided into separate wings, there was no room for hanky-panky, yet in 1841, two female inmates in the Barney workhouse gave birth to children conceived while inside.

How could this be? No one knew, but the master was strongly suspected of being the father.

Barney’s workhouse is typical as in the late 19th Century, an infirmary wing and an infectious disease ward were added – workhouses became the place where the poor could access crude healthcare.

As the 19th Century turned into the 20th, regimes softened. In 1922, Barnard Castle children were placed in foster homes and allowed to attend local schools rather than the harsh one run by the master.

  The infirmary wing of Barnard Castle workhouse has been converted to residential 

*(Image: Chris Lloyd)*

Workhouses were abolished in 1930 and instead became places where poor people could still access healthcare. It is no coincidence that the Richardson hospital – originally a convalescent home – grew up alongside the workhouse.

In 2005, the 1830s workhouse wings were demolished to make way for a carehome but the infirmary wing and infectious ward were converted into period properties.

SOME years ago, we were loaned an item by a reader whose details we have shamefully now lost. It is a filthy document, the shape of a cheque book, that they had found in an attic, the purpose of which made no sense to them or to us.

But now it does.

It is the paying-in book for the contributions of the township of Westwick to the Teesdale Union to finance the Barnard Castle workhouse, worked out based on population and rateable value.

Westwick, to the east of Barney, was in the ancient parish of Gainford which was divided into smaller townships, including Whorlton, Winston, Headlam and Westwick.

From the stubs left in the cheque book, it appears that between 1871 and 1885, Westwick pays its dues to the union every six months.

  A stub showing that Westwick's contribution has been paid in 1878 by John Bowes Esq 

*(Image: Chris Lloyd)*

The stubs also contain the name of the person paying into the union. Usually, it is the rector of Winston or the vicar of Barnard Castle, but sometimes it is listed as “John Bowes Esq”. This would be the great John Bowes, founder of the museum, whose home of Streatlam Castle was in the township of Westwick.

And twice “the master of Trinity College Cambridge” is listed as the payer.

When Henry VIII founded the college in 1546, he granted it the living of Gainford – it owned the rectory, the tithes and ecclesiastic revenues collected by the vicar, and the right to appoint the vicar.

So the book shows that the great players in this tiny township were making their contribution to the poorest of Teesdale. It doesn’t show if they were happy about it, though.
