**23 September 2026**— A new nationwide teaching resource launched by the** PSHE Association**and the** University of York** has entered secondary schools this term, triggering an immediate debate on whether the updated curriculum sufficiently addresses anti-male sentiment (misandry) and the potential alienation of young men.

The lesson pack, titled *‘Challenging the narrative: Thinking critically about misogyny’*, is designed for Key Stage 3 and 4 pupils. Backed by European Research Council funding, the initiative aims to challenge the influence of the online ‘manosphere’ and has already been downloaded over 1,000 times, with projections to reach 300,000 pupils in the coming months.

Proponents argue the lessons are a necessary response to the Department for Education’s (DfE) updated statutory guidance, which places a stronger emphasis on combating harmful online subcultures. Professor Harriet Over from the University of York defended the launch, stating the framework is rooted in decades of psychological research into social learning and prejudice reduction.

> **Professor Harriet Over, University of York Department of Psychology:**
>
> These lessons are based on decades of psychological research into how to encourage positive relations between children, as well as on extensive conversations with teachers and young people… it takes educators, families and whole communities to come together to support young people on how to develop healthy relationships and mutual respect.

## The Call for Neutrality: Addressing Misandry

However, critics and educational commentators argue that the singular focus of the press release and the lesson pack presents a one-sided view of gender-based hostility. Concerns have been raised that by heavily scrutinising male-centric online communities without an equivalent evaluation of anti-male prejudice, the curriculum risks creating a classroom environment that treats young men with inherent suspicion.

Detractors point out that young men face distinct vulnerabilities online, including extreme narratives that vilify or generalise boys. Critics argue that true educational balance requires schools to explicitly address misandry alongside misogyny to prevent male students from feeling isolated by the curriculum.

> **Educational Balance Review:**
>
> By focusing almost exclusively on the ‘manosphere’, modern lesson plans risk missing the broader picture of online hostility. If the curriculum does not overtly challenge anti-male sentiment and the negative stereotyping of young men with the same rigour, it ceases to be a neutral educational tool and risks alienating the very demographic it needs to engage.

While the DfE’s statutory guidance technically mandates that all forms of sex-based discrimination and prejudice are unacceptable, the practical implementation remains a battleground. The guidelines do instruct teachers to reinforce that the vast majority of boys treat others with respect, leaving individual schools with the complex task of ensuring these new media literacy modules are delivered with genuine, even-handed impartiality.
