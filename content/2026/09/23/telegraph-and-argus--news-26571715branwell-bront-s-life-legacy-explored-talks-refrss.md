Talks exploring the life and legacy of Branwell Brontë are taking place at the Brontë Parsonage and Brontë Birthplace.

The events aim to shed light on the often-overlooked brother of the famous Brontë sisters, who made his mark as both an artist and poet but whose life was ultimately overshadowed by personal struggles.

### Talks to explore Branwell's artistic legacy

On September 22, the Brontë Birthplace hosted an online talk titled Sibling Rivalry: Unpacking the Art of Branwell Brontë, led by guest speaker Dr Maebh O’Regan.

![]()The birthplace of the Brontë siblings. *(Image: Bronte Birthplace.)*

The talk explored Branwell’s artistic output, focusing on his creative process.

Branwell’s most well-known work is The Brontë Sisters Portrait, also known as The Pillar Portrait, painted in 1834.

Though the piece has received negative criticism, it remains one of the most popular works in the British National Portrait Gallery.

### How grief shaped Branwell's writing

Later in the week, the Brontë Parsonage will present an in-person and online talk on September 24, titled Grief and Loss in the Writing of Branwell Brontë.

Led by Brontë scholar Rose Gant, the talk will begin at 2pm at the Parsonage, with a Zoom session offered at 7.30pm.

![]()The Brontë Parsonage Museum. *(Image: submitted)*

A spokesman for the Brontë Parsonage said: "Like his siblings, Branwell suffered a series of losses throughout his lifetime, attending the deathbed of his mother, his aunt and his close friend William Weightman.

"Branwell drew on these losses as inspiration for his writing, capturing his experiences in both his letters and poetry.

"This talk will explore these writings, looking at both the inspirations and how Branwell captures his experiences of grief."

### The life and decline of the fourth Brontë sibling

Born in 1817, Branwell was the only Brontë sibling who received his entire education at home in Haworth.

He showed promise as a writer and painter, and his family hoped he would become the primary breadwinner.

His life took a downturn in 1843 while working as a tutor at Thorp Green Hall, where he reportedly began a relationship with Lydia Robinson.

After the relationship was discovered, Branwell lost his job and became increasingly dependent on alcohol and laudanum.

His health deteriorated rapidly, and he withdrew from creative pursuits, dying in September 1848 at the age of 31.

It is believed he died from tuberculosis, with addiction contributing to his decline.

Branwell’s death marked the start of a period of tragedy for the Brontë family, as both Emily and Anne died within the following year.
