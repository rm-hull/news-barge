A North Yorkshire nurse is helping families reconnect with loved ones with dementia through personalised books about their past.

Jude Rhodes, a registered nurse and professional genealogist based in the Yorkshire Dales, combines both skills to create conversation resources for families affected by dementia.

She uses family history to spark conversations and preserve memories.

Ms Rhodes said: "Nursing and genealogy probably sound like two completely separate jobs, but there is a point where they meet.

![]()Jude Rhodes, Jude Rhodes, a registered nurse and professional genealogist *(Image: Supplied)*

"When somebody is living with dementia, their own history can give families somewhere to begin.

"That might be a photograph, but it could just as easily be a village, a school, the job somebody did, or something that was part of everyday life when they were younger.

"The point isn’t to test somebody on what they remember.

"It’s about knowing enough about the person to find subjects that mean something to them."

Instead of a traditional family tree, she creates personalised books packed with photos and stories about the people, places and events that shaped someone’s life.

The books help relatives and carers start conversations based on familiar memories.

Ms Rhodes said: "That’s where family history can be useful.

"You’re not just collecting names and dates. You’re finding out what somebody’s life actually looked like."

She completed the Pharos and Society of Genealogists’ Advanced Skills and Strategies course.

She is an AGRA associate, chairs the British Association for Local History’s Outreach Team and is secretary of the Society for One-Place Studies.

Health professionals support using personal history in dementia care.

NHS guidance recommends photos, objects, videos and music for reminiscence therapy, while Dementia UK encourages families to create life stories based on a person’s background and interests.

Other Yorkshire organisations are taking a similar approach.

The Yorkshire Film Archive’s Memory Bank: Bradford project uses archive footage and local history materials to help older people, including those with dementia, reminisce.

Launched in 2025, the project gave every care home in the Bradford district free access to its films and an accompanying book, and was still being promoted in summer 2026.

The Memory Bank project is separate from Ms Rhodes’ work but shows how personal or community history can spark conversation and connection.

Ms Rhodes will explained her approach in a free Family Tree magazine online session on Tuesday, September 22.
