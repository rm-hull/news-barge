Latvia’s Edgars Rinkēvičs is up next.

He begins by repeating warning that “the world order is breaking down,” as “the rules-based order that brought stability is under growing strain.”

But he says the threats once far away from home are now coming increasingly close: whether the Russian aggression on Ukraine, or the worsening landscape in the Middle East.

He also warns about climate change threats:

“Nature respects no borders either. Heatwaves do not stop at checkpoints. Rising seas threaten to rise. … That threat means that those island nations may disappear from the face of the earth, and far from the ocean.

The danger is just as real in the mountains. A river can turn into a deadly flood. It may happen in seconds. These floods sweep away homes, roads and lives. We just witnessed a catastrophic flood wave in Nepal, where also 33 Latvian citizens disappeared.

Global climate change does not have borders or citizenship.”

Rinkēvičssays that the main lesson appears to be that “none of us can face [the threats] alone.”

“We are in the same boat. Perhaps Noah’s Ark is no longer just a biblical story.”

Drawing on Latvia’s own history, he pivots to Ukraine and Russian attempt to deny its sovereignty and territorial integrity.

“Russia’s war against Ukraine strikes at the very heart of the United Nations charter,” he says.

Rinkēvičs urges leaders “not to look away” from Russian actions in Ukraine.

“Russia is violating the very principles it is entrusted to uphold. Russia started this brutal war. Russia can end it. It can stop its attacks. It can withdraw its forces. It can leave Ukraines sovereign territory.”

A Russian helicopter violated Polish airspace for 42 seconds, Poland says

A Russian military helicopter briefly violated Polish airspace on Wednesday morning, Poland’s army said.

The Mi-8 helicopter of the Russian Armed Forces crossed 300 meters into Polish airspace from the Russian exclave of Kaliningrad and remained there for 42 seconds, the army’s statement said.

“The aircraft’s flight was monitored by the Polish Armed Forces’ radar systems. Fighter jets were scrambled, while ground forces and assets remained on standby.

The nature of the incident suggests that Russia is once again testing the readiness of our air defence.”

Trump’s threat to annihilate Iran to UN general assembly is stunning, even for him - analysis

Robert Tait

Donald Trump has a habit of discomfiting delegates to the United Nations general assembly. But this year, he may have upstaged the ghost of Nikita Khrushchev by openly contemplating the annihilation of a fellow UN member state.

Eleven minutes into a 40-minute address, the US president announced he had “a big decision to make” over Iran, which has staged a much more determined resistance than Trump anticipated when he decided to go to war against the country in February.

“Will a deal be made with Iran that lets them rebuild, and create a far greater country than it ever was before?” he asked. “Or do I annihilate the Islamic Republic and do it quickly, never giving them a chance to kill and destroy people and countries again? Do I drive them into hell with no chance of survival and no hope of future greatness or generations?”

Possibly not since Khrushchev, the late Soviet leader, brandished his shoe and allegedly banged it on a desk in 1960 in protest at a fellow delegate’s speech has the annual gathering witnessed such a display of bellicosity from a superpower leader.

But this seemed more shocking.

The world may have become inured to Trump’s dire threats to send Iran “to the stone age” or end its civilization – so often have they been issued, only to sometimes be followed by a ceasefire or negotiations. But hearing them voiced in a forum created in the aftermath of the second world war, with the explicit aim of preventing future conflicts, was disconcerting.

Iran was the focal point of much of Trump’s speech, which began with a catalogue of familiar boasts of his supposed achievements that seemed better fitted to a domestic audience.

Whether or not the latest threat should be taken literally, little of what he said about Iran seemed reassuring. He appeared to play fast and loose with the facts, consciously or otherwise.
