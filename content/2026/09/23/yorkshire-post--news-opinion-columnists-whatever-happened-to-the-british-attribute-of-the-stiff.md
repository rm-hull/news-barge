# Whatever happened to the British attribute of the stiff upper lip? - Sarah Todd

## While no fan of former top Tory Jacob Rees-Mogg he was right in his recent call for a return to the once typically British attribute of the stiff upper lip.

In a video message shared on his social media channels - that does seem very modern for such an old-fashioned sort of chap - he referenced the “misery memoir” that has been written by the brother of the late Diana, Princess of Wales. The author’s full name or the title of the book won’t be dignified with a mention in these column inches.

While Rees-Mogg could have referenced the band playing on as the Titanic sank, he chose instead to talk about the iconic comedy classic Carry On Up the Khyber.

In particular, he made mention of his admiration for the character played by Sid James, the unflappable Sir Sidney Ruff-Diamond, who maintained his stiff upper lip while the building tumbles down around him.

In his own roundabout way, the former Leader of the House of Commons is right to remind us of the notion of duty, of trying to do the right and decent thing although it might not be the easiest option.

Attributes such as service, thinking of others rather than of ourselves, personal responsibility, a sense of duty and resilience all seem to have gone up the Khyber in our woke world.

For the generations before us enduring a little hardship was just part of life’s rich tapestry. People didn’t expect to sail through life without there ever being bumps in the road.

Nowadays, the hand that is dealt is rarely endured without claiming compensation or at the very least some kind of outburst. Yes, the very unseemly habit of washing dirty linen in public.

The dictionary definition of this is ‘discussing or arguing about unpleasant or private things in front of other people’.

Thanks to social media and infectious influencer culture airing private struggles publicly has become a massive trend, with our over-sharing nation never seemingly shutting up about the likes of romantic breakups, family feuds, financial ruin and mental health crises. All regularly broadcasted for all and sundry to gawp over.

The Husband was a passenger in a car at the weekend - he had been given a ticket to see Leeds United which he doesn’t often get to watch - which was involved in a bump. We already have a side bet on as to when the calls will start coming asking if he is suffering from whiplash or any other physical or mental aftereffects. He was a bit stiff (the car was a write-off) but he ploughed on. These things happen. Well they used to. Now there always must be repercussions.

Such a cultural shift away from putting one’s best foot forward, keeping some elements of the life we are living private, has sent our country’s moral compass into a right old spin.

People’s relationships no longer seem to be able to fizzle out like they did back in the day. Now there always has to be some drama documented about the other person. They are always abusive, narcissistic or whatever the latest line is. Same for messing up at work. We used to get right roastings in the newsrooms of yesteryear and - as a result - never dared do whatever wrong again, but now it’s the norm to talk of toxic workplaces; bullying and discrimination. Nobody seems to hold their hands up and say ‘I messed up and got sacked’ there is always lingering litigation and mental health traumas to tiptoe around. Get a grip.

That’s not to say people should suffer in silence when there is a genuine grievance. But if the world’s workforce was a little more intelligent it would surely realise that the more unnecessary fuss and complaining it makes, the more the message is diluted when something seriously does go wrong. Remember the boy who cried wolf?

While we’re going over old sayings, such as stiff upper lip and the washing of dirty linen, it was interesting to learn that the famous line "If you can't say something nice, don't say nothing at all" is known from the 1942 Disney animated film Bambi.

It was a young Thumper the rabbit who was given this moral lesson by his mother, after making a rude remark about the newborn fawn being wobbly on his feet.

That’s a good one for any bitter brothers to remember. For our King, Winston Churchill’s "If you are going through hell, keep going" sounds sensible advice, along with the words of former president of the United States Franklin D. Roosevelt, who advised: "When you come to the end of your rope, tie a knot and hang on."
