The World Economic Forum expects 39% of workers’ existing skills to change or become outdated between 2025 and 2030. It also identifies AI and big data, creative thinking, resilience, and curiosity as increasingly important skills. Together, these changes offer a useful view of where **business education** is heading. Business theory will remain useful, but graduates will also need to know how to respond when familiar frameworks don’t work.

This is where the **future of undergraduate business** education begins to look different. Students will need to use technology with judgement, understand markets beyond the ones they know, and make sensible decisions even when they do not have the full picture. Among these shifts, the growing role of AI is likely to have the most immediate influence on how students learn to approach business decisions.

By 2030, AI is likely to sit behind many routine business decisions, from understanding customer behaviour to testing prices and forecasting demand. Access to these tools, however, will not guarantee a sound decision. Students must learn to question the information they receive, notice weak assumptions, and recognise when human judgement matters more than an automated recommendation.

This is why relevant **undergraduate programs** will connect technology with finance, marketing, operations, and strategy. Tetr’s**four-year Bachelor’s Program in Management & Technology**, for instance, follows this direction through coursework in artificial intelligence, machine learning, product, finance, and strategy.

However, sound judgement cannot develop from these learnings alone. It also requires the pressure and unpredictability that come with execution. Therefore, undergraduate business education must prepare students for these aspects as well.

The defining **business education trend in 2030** is a move from discussing businesses to creating them. A live venture exposes questions that a finished case study cannot:

* Will a customer pay?
* Can the team recover from a poor launch?
* What changes when the numbers challenge the original idea?

At Tetr, term projects place students inside that process. They may build a dropshipping business, develop a consumer brand, or work on a technology venture while learning the concepts behind each decision. Building across countries makes this learning even more demanding and relevant.

The **future of business schools** will depend partly on whether international exposure changes how students think. A short visit may broaden awareness; sustained work in a new market asks far more. Students must read unfamiliar customer habits, communicate across cultures, and adjust ideas that worked elsewhere.

**Tetr’s Bachelor’s Program in Management & Technology** is delivered across seven countries over four years. Each location becomes part of the curriculum, allowing students to encounter different markets while their understanding is still taking shape. Experiences like these ultimately point to a broader question: what should a business graduate be prepared to do by 2030?

The **future of business schools in 2030** will not be decided by technology alone. It will rest on how well institutions combine knowledge, action, and reflection. Graduates will need the confidence to begin without complete information, the curiosity to ask better questions, and the discipline to revise a decision when reality disagrees.

Tetr’s model offers one view of that future: learn across borders, build with real stakes, and use technology as a business tool. Students can explore **Tetr’s Bachelor’s Program in Management & Technology** and decide whether this kind of education fits the way they hope to enter the world of business.
