Thousands of people have visited historic Durham Miners’ Hall since its £14 million restoration, as Redhills prepares for its next chapter.

The Grade II\* listed Durham building reopened in June 2025 and has already welcomed more than 15,000 visitors.

Sell-out tours, performances and Redhills Sundays have brought fresh life to the former Durham Miners’ Association headquarters.

![]()The £14 million restoration has brought new life to Redhills. *(Image: Supplied)*

Helen Featherstone, the National Lottery Heritage Fund’s director for England, North, said: "It’s fantastic to see the transformation that has taken place at Redhills and that its doors are now open to the public.

"This impressive building can now continue to act as a space for the communities of Durham and beyond to celebrate and connect to the important mining heritage that is at the heart of the area.

"We are very proud to have supported its redevelopment, thanks to money raised by National Lottery players, and it’s wonderful to hear the praise from visitors who have stepped foot into the Miners Hall already.

![]()Redhills Sundays continue the hall’s tradition of community gatherings *(Image: Supplied)*

"We are excited to see how Redhills develops for the future following its phenomenal transformation."

The National Lottery Heritage Fund provided £9 million for the restoration, with trade unions and community fundraising supplying further support.

Redhills is now a centre for culture, education, community organising and events.

Redhills chief executive Andrew McIntyre said: "Restoring Redhills was never simply about saving a remarkable building.

![]()The new entrance to Redhills, 'Durham's other cathedral'. *(Image: THE NORTHERN ECHO)*

"The people who built this place believed that by organising together they could improve their lives, build institutions of their own and give their communities greater power over their future.

"Before the restoration began, people across the Coalfield told us they didn’t want Redhills to become a museum to a vanished industry. They wanted that inheritance put back to work."

Redhills Sundays, a weekly programme inspired by coalfield community gatherings, have attracted hundreds of visitors.

Visitors can explore the building, enjoy food, join creative activities and learn about mining history through talks and films.

One visitor said they "felt really connected to working-class history – my roots and that of others," while another called it a "true story told with passion and appropriate detail, bringing history to life."

Since reopening, the Miners’ Hall has hosted more than 300 events, including film screenings, conferences and performances.

Highlights include the Brass Bands England National Conference, Durham Brass Festival and a concert by folk artist Eliza Carthy.

Redhills will look to the future at the Redhills Assembly on October 2 and 3.

The event will bring together community organisations from across the coalfield and beyond to discuss how the hall can support collective action and community development.

Mr McIntyre said: "That is what this Assembly is about.

"We want to learn from what previous generations built, from the extraordinary work already happening in our communities today, and from people doing this work elsewhere.

"And together, we want to ask what we can build now – how communities can rebuild their Common Wealth, and what part Redhills can play in making that possible."

Redhills, also known as the Durham Miners’ Association New Hall and Offices, was built in the Edwardian Baroque style between 1913 and 1915.

It replaced the original Miners’ Hall on North Road and cost around £30,000 to construct.

At its heart is the "Pitman’s Parliament", a council chamber that once seated a representative from each of Durham’s 297 coal pits.

The hall is largely unchanged from its opening, apart from minor updates such as lifts and modern heating.

Redhills now hosts community events, conferences, concerts, plays, weddings and guided tours.

The Redhills charity is also working in former coalfield villages to help communities rebuild the "Coalfield Commonwealth".

It means the collectively owned, democratically controlled assets, institutions and networks that once gave coalfield communities prosperity, security and shared purpose.

Visitors can book tours through the Redhills website.
