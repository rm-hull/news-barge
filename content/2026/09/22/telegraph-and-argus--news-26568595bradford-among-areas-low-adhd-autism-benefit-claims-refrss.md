Bradford has been highlighted as one of several English local authorities with lower-than-expected rates of young people claiming disability benefits for ADHD and autism, according to a new report.

Analysis by health think tank the Nuffield Trust found that while the number of 16 and 17-year-olds receiving Personal Independence Payment (Pip) for ADHD and autism has risen sharply across England, uptake appears lower in some areas with more ethnically diverse populations.

The report found that the proportion of young people claiming Pip for ADHD and autism has increased nationally from 1.3 per cent in 2019 to 3.4 per cent in 2025.

However, researchers said places including Bradford, Newham, Brent, Leicester and Wolverhampton recorded "much lower-than-expected" rates when compared with other local authorities.

## Link to ethnicity and deprivation

The Nuffield Trust said its analysis, which incorporated data from the 2021 Census, identified a relationship between Pip claim rates, ethnicity and levels of deprivation.

Sally Gainsbury, senior policy analyst at the Nuffield Trust, said areas with a relatively high proportion of white residents tended to have higher rates of people receiving Pip for ADHD and autism.

She said: "Areas where there is a relatively high proportion of white people have a higher proportion of people receiving Pip for these disabilities."

Ms Gainsbury added that there appears to be "an association between being white and relatively prosperous with faster growing levels of Pip for ADHD and autism".

According to the report, this helps explain why local authorities such as Bradford recorded lower-than-expected claim rates, as they have comparatively smaller proportions of residents identifying as white.

The think tank report said: “This association goes some way to explaining why, at local authority level, we saw much lower-than-expected levels of ADHD and autism Pip rates in areas such as Newham, Brent, Leicester, Bradford and Wolverhampton, as these areas have relatively low proportions of the population who identify as having a white ethnicity.”

## **Sharp national increase**

Across England, the number of 16 and 17-year-olds receiving Pip for ADHD and autism rose from 16,045 in 2019 to 51,148 in 2025.

The Nuffield Trust estimated spending on Pip for young people in this age group with ADHD and autism reached £296 million last year.

The report also found rates of claims have increased fastest in the least-deprived areas of England. In the most affluent fifth of neighbourhoods, the proportion of 16 and 17-year-olds receiving Pip for ADHD and autism more than tripled from 0.8 per cent in 2019 to 2.5 per cent in 2025.

By comparison, rates in the most deprived areas rose from 2.1 per cent to 5.1 per cent over the same period.

## Questions over diagnosis and support

The researchers said the findings may reflect inequalities in access to diagnosis as well as wider questions about how autism and ADHD are assessed in both the welfare and healthcare systems.

Ms Gainsbury said: "Pip is meant to reflect how much a condition affects someone's day-to-day life, rather than purely whether they have a diagnosis, so it's intriguing to see that recipient rates have grown most in the areas where people seem to be doing relatively well in terms of employment and education.

"Our findings could reflect unequal access to diagnosis, which we know is substantial.

"But they could also point to inconsistencies and uncertainties, both in the welfare system and in wider society, about how disability is understood and assessed when it comes to autism and ADHD."

## Review expected soon

The report comes as a Government-commissioned review into ADHD, autism and mental health is expected to be published shortly.

Nuffield Trust chief executive Thea Stein said policymakers would need to ensure support is targeted "fairly and effectively" as diagnosis and recognition of neurodiversity continue to increase.

She said this could involve considering whether people are best supported through healthcare and benefits systems, or through wider changes in schools, workplaces and society.
