Woodlands Hospital Executive Director Debbie Dobbs tells Mike Hughes she wants to challenge the assumption that private healthcare is an option only for a few.

Debbie Dobbs is a passionate believer in helping people improve their lives.

With cutting edge technology and some of the North East’s most experienced surgeons, Woodlands Hospital in Darlington, part of the vast Circle Health Group offers private healthcare that is regularly setting new benchmarks.

But for Debbie and her team, that still isn’t enough. She is on a mission to explain the benefits of private healthcare to a much wider audience who might simply have thought it was not possible for them to walk through the doors at Woodlands.

As the very real struggles of the NHS continue, private healthcare offers an alternative that more people are turning to. But many more have not yet come to terms with paying for their health. Cars, yes. Homes, yes. Holidays, yes. But while there is a free version of healthcare available, making that big change is a challenge.

In one of the immaculate consulting rooms, Debbie tells me: ‘I think sometimes the myth about private healthcare is it’s unaffordable for most. But when we look at a lot of the procedures, the general feedback from patients is, “Oh, it’s actually a lot less than I thought it was”, and there are now more routes to paying for your treatment.’

The facility’s proposition is clear. For patients who can be treated there, a private route can mean selecting a consultant, choosing a date, accessing diagnostic services or treatment more quickly, and paying for all or part of the journey in a way that feels manageable.

For some, that will be insurance, but for a growing number, it is self-pay.

‘There are a lot of people now choosing self-pay, where they will take their own money out either from savings or a loan and cover the cost of what they need, for part of the journey or all of the journey,’ Debbie says.

It might be an MRI, a scan, a consultation or a procedure. It may be an ophthalmology patient facing a long wait; someone whose planned treatment has been cancelled; an individual who wants certainty before retirement; or a younger patient seeking a health check for peace of mind. The common thread is not money, but rather the ability to make a decision about their own future.

The Woodlands option is what is helping to redraw the picture of what private healthcare can be and opening those doors wide.

Debbie, executive director at the Morton Park site, has been at Woodlands for 19 years. In that time, she says, insured activity has remained broadly steady.

‘But I would say our self-pay, certainly since Covid, has probably quadrupled,‘ she says.

'I understand that instinct to go to the NHS because we work in a region where the NHS remains central to many people’s assumptions about how healthcare should work. I’ve been an NHS nurse myself, so I can appreciate both sides of the discussion.

‘I think in my generation and my parents’ generation, it was, ‘NHS, NHS, NHS,’ because it was set up to be free at the point of delivery and people valued that. But now they might choose to invest in a diagnostic appointment to make sure that they are as well as they can be.’

She is very measured when discussing the pressure on NHS services. Woodlands, she stresses, works collaboratively rather than competitively, and recognises the demands created by emergency care, beds and the wider realities of running a large acute hospital.

‘We are an elective surgery hospital, which means we haven’t got ambulances at the door,’ she says.

‘That’s not our priority. Most of the hospitals have got A&E and the new walk-in centres. They’ve got the ambulances there bringing in poorly patients as their priority. So if there’s a squeeze on beds, for example, the squeeze would target the elective surgery.

‘We all know that is how the NHS has to work, and we understand and appreciate what they have to go through every day and why cancellations and delays will happen because we sometimes have cancellations as well. But perhaps the difference here is that we have added flexibility because we are not an emergency hospital so if we do cancel, we can probably get our patient back in straight away, where sometimes the NHS does not have that advantage.

‘That capacity to respond can be important for some people who come here when they have already begun their journey elsewhere and realised that waiting is no longer possible for them. They may have a condition worsening, a job to return to, a family commitment or simply a desire to regain a better quality of life sooner.

‘When people are doing that, they come in, they choose their date, they choose their surgeon. They will be seen, they’ll be booked, and they’ll be offered a date. Those high standards of care and surroundings are what they’re paying for.’

Not every patient can, or should, pay a large bill at once – a headline figure can be enough to stop any enquiries. But the hospital’s Patient Liaison Officers can talk about that and give a ballpark indication of price.

Woodlands also says it price-matches where a patient presents a written quote from a competitor and for eligible patients seeking to spread the cost, it works with Chrysalis Finance, a provider of payment plans. ‘Through Chrysalis we have options we can offer to potential patients,’ Debbie says.

‘It is a third party that we deal with, so it is a relationship that exists with the patient and Chrysalis and they just apply. And then once that’s been approved, the money comes through and they have a payment plan.’

The analogy is deliberately simple, to bring the discussion into the financial choices people already make in other areas of their lives. Debbie is not offering a blanket promise about affordability.

Payment options depend on an application and approval and the right procedure and pathway differ patient to patient. But her message is that an initial conversation costs nothing – and assumptions about the price should not stop someone from at least exploring the options.

‘We certainly encourage that so we have an idea of what they need and make sure that we can do it. We don’t want to waste their time or their money,’ she says.

‘Once the patients are in our system, Patient Liaison Officers would tell them about the cost and how it breaks down, and then they have a plan that can be updated if the treatment continues.

‘After that, when a patient arrives with us, we want them to have complete confidence in clinical quality, so we have particularly high standards for our surgeons and staff. We don’t employ our consultants directly, they are self-employed practitioners with practice privileges that are part of a stringent and continuing process.

‘Practice privileges are literally what they says on the tin. It’s a privilege, not a right, which encourages surgeons to contact us and say, ‘I’d really like to come and do some work with you’, and then there’s an application process and there’s references, there’s indemnity, there are a lot of other things that go with it.

‘Applications are considered by a medical advisory committee, with representation from specialities. That is the beginning, rather than the end, of the oversight, so once that consultant has applied and have been through all of the rigour of making sure they are suitable, qualified, indemnified and reputationally okay, only then can they join us.’

The quality checks are ongoing, including whole-practice appraisal, information-sharing with the consultant’s NHS trust, training and data-protection registration. Once consultants come on board, the hospital supports them in setting up their private practice, building a profile, accessing theatre capacity and operating from Woodlands or outreach clinics.

The checks and appraisals are clearly the right way forward, as Woodlands has been expanding the range of expertise available to patients and has welcomed more than 20 new consultants over the past six months.

The hospital remains heavily focused on orthopaedics, including robotic surgery, while ophthalmology is another major area of demand among newer specialities such as gastroenterology and GP appointments which are readily available.

The recruitment process, she says, can be proactive. If Woodlands identifies a gap – perhaps in keyhole or laparoscopic surgery – it can look for the right specialist. That approach enables the hospital to give patients more choice, but Debbie also sees it as part of being responsive to the evolving needs of the people who come to them for help.

‘I think what I love about my job, and what I love about the private sector, is the agility,’ she says.

‘Somebody can come to Woodlands and say, “Deb, we’ve got this idea and we want to make it work”, and by the afternoon we can be progressing that. As a manager in the NHS, that can still be done, but it takes more time. I understand that, but the agility to be able to move and to be innovative and to set up services in a safe, quick way – that’s what I love.’

Robotic hip and knee surgery is one example. The hospital had recently completed its 2,000th Mako robotic procedure – a process that begins with a scan of the relevant hip or knee, which is then mapped to a robot which then helps the surgeon to offer reproducible precise surgery. Debbie describes the recovery after the robotic surgery as ‘phenomenal’.

‘For someone considering a self-funded procedure, that clinical confidence is inseparable from value. Affordability is not simply about price, it’s about knowing what sits behind it – the surgeon, the technology, the operating environment and the processes that support the whole pathway and merit the cost.

‘Patients tell us that cleanliness is an important factor in their decision making.’ Debbie tells me: ‘We obviously employ our staff, we look after them and train them and we also listen to feedback from them and from our patients, and that for me is really vital. The feedback is gathered through surveys and a patient voice forum, which can include people who have complained.

‘We also take part in PLACE (Patient-Led Assessments of the Care Environment), a national audit involving external visitors and patient representatives, where we consistently score highly, with visitors commenting that it is spotless, immaculate and free from unpleasant smells.

‘From that feedback, we make sure the team is congratulated regularly on being able to do that or challenged and supported on how to improve,’ she says.

‘The fundamental issue for me is that clinical care should never be a luxury product. It’s always a delicate subject because a good level of clinical care should be given wherever you go. I can only speak for what we deliver here, and I know that it is very high level clinical care, but I think anybody has the right to expect that, regardless of their ability to pay.’

‘My ethos as the executive director has always been “you treat everybody the way that you want your own parents or children or family to be treated”,’ she says.

‘And that includes how the hospital deals with any challenges that come its way. I won’t hide anything. We owe our patients that level of transparency and honesty.’

The expansion of self-pay is not solely about people seeking surgery after a diagnosis. Debbie is seeing more patients come to Woodlands earlier in their health journey, looking for a private GP appointment, a screening service, a diagnostic test or reassurance that an overlooked symptom is not a sign of something more serious.

The facility offers private GP appointments for a one-off consultation fee, as well as health screening options shaped around a patient’s age and sex. Depending on the outcome, people may be directed to the appropriate specialist, or may simply leave reassured and continue their care elsewhere.

‘We’re certainly seeing a lot of people in their 20s to 40s taking much more accountability for their own health,’ she says.

‘They’re wanting the health screening services. They want to be checked out, and they don’t mind paying just to make sure they are ok for the longer term. I think if we engage with them and get that relationship right in the beginning, we can become their provider of choice anywhere along their health journey.

‘I think of them as ‘the worried well’, people with no obvious major illness but who want informed reassurance and a clearer understanding of their own health and what they may be facing in the future

‘What they want is for an expert to tell them that everything is fine, as people are getting much more in tune with what is going on in their bodies.

‘Education is part of that strategy, so we offer consultant talks in person and online on subjects including cataracts, joint replacements and eye conditions, so that people can ask questions live, or revisit the sessions later.’

It is another small but revealing piece of the accessibility agenda. Information should not be shut away behind a door, reserved only for someone who has already committed to treatment. A patient who understands their options is better able to decide whether and when private care is right for them.

Away from the hospital, Woodlands is an active member of its community. The hospital sponsors grassroots sports organisations, supports local charities, works with businesses and colleges, and takes clinicians out into the community to offer advice. Not as a hard sell, but just talking informally to people experiencing shoulder, elbow or other injuries, offering guidance without charge.

The hospital has supported St Teresa’s Hospice, raising more than £25,000 with match-funding from Circle Health Group, and has worked with local education providers on basic life support training as it looks for longer-term partnerships rather than one-off sponsorship opportunities.

The ambition is not just to sign people up, it is much deeper that, as Debbie and her team help more people understand that they may have an answer for them.

For her, the change begins when people stop assuming that Woodlands is closed to them, financially or culturally, and begin with a simple enquiry.

They can ask what is possible, what it would involve, what it would cost and whether it is right for them. The answer may be a consultation, a diagnostic test, a screening appointment, a treatment plan, a flexible payment route – or a recommendation that another setting is more appropriate. But the first step is the same: replacing assumption with information.

At a time when patients are increasingly conscious of waiting, wellbeing and the need to take charge of their own health, the Woodlands door is always open.
