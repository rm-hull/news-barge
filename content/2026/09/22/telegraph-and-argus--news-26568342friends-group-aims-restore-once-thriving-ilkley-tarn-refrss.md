AN Ilkley group is planning to restore a once-thriving wildlife pond which is now polluted and struggling to support life.

Ilkley Tarn, once rich with amphibians and invertebrates, is polluted and largely devoid of other life owing to the large number of ducks, according to the Friends of Ilkley Moor (FoIM).

The group has launched a scientific restoration project to bring the tarn back to life, with support from leading academics and ecological consultants.

Owen Wells, chair of FoIM, said: "Ilkley Moor is now the largest part of the new Pennine Gateway National Nature Reserve and it is only right that Ilkley Tarn should be an example of how nature can be restored and take pride of place in the new nature reserve."

FoIM believes sticklebacks and other invertebrates flourished there 50 years ago.

Professor Rick Battarbee, an emeritus professor of environmental change at University College London, and Dr John Boyle of Liverpool University are guiding the project.

![]()Work is carried out at Ilkley Tarn *(Image: UGC)*

Goldsmith Ecology has completed a bathymetric survey to map water and sediment depths and estimate the tarn’s total volume.

The team is also monitoring inflows and outflows, analysing the water and surveying submerged vegetation.

Surface sediment samples are being analysed to determine the presence of invertebrate life, and sediment cores dating back 150 years have been collected to identify historical plant and animal populations before the rise in duck-related pollution.

Researchers are also surveying the higher Upper Tarn, where few or no ducks are present, as a potential control site.

FoIM said it is "excited to be able to commission a serious scientific evaluation of the tarn."

After analysing the data, the group hopes to draw up a detailed plan to restore the tarn as a “thriving wildlife pond”.

The project is part of wider conservation efforts on Ilkley Moor, the largest section of the new Pennine Gateway National Nature Reserve.

FoIM believes the tarn could become an example for ecological restoration within the reserve.

The group said it hopes Ilkley Tarn can once again become a haven for wildlife and a source of natural pride for the community.

Friends of Ilkley Moor is seeking to reduce the tarn's duck population from more than 50 to about 10 or 12, saying feeding has contributed to cloudy water and the loss of aquatic plants.

The Bradford Pennine Gateway National Nature Reserve covers more than 1,270 acres of peat bogs, heathlands and wetlands, habitats that Natural England says are important for carbon storage and biodiversity.
