Economists are warning Andy Burnham that tax rises will be almost certain in this month’s Budget – a worry for the prime minister just as he prepares for his first face-to-face meeting with the famously brusque Donald Trump.

The prime minister said he was hoping to find “common ground” with the US president when they hold talks in the margins of the United Nations General Assembly meeting in New York.

He said: "We've found a connection. So I will go with where I feel it's right, to meet him where he is at, and search for that common ground, which I'm sure we'll find.”

Mr Burnham also insisted he personally trusted Mr Trump, telling reporters: “When you come into this job, you realise how deep this relationship is.

“We trust our allies. Of course we do.”

The talks will involve discussions on wars in Ukraine and the Middle East and the rapid development of AI.

## Tax rises 'virtually inevitable' as government borrowing soars

Tax rises are “virtually inevitable”, economists say, after government borrowing rose in August to the second-highest level for the month on record.

Official figures also showed the Treasury had borrowed £8bn more than expected so far this year.

The Office for National Statistics (ONS) said the deficit between tax receipts and spending stood at £18.3bn last month, £3.5bn higher than expected.

It means chancellor John Healey has borrowed £77.3bn so far this financial year, which is £8.1bn more than forecast.

Emma Reynolds, the Chief Secretary to the Treasury, warned of “tough decisions” in Mr Healey’s Budget on 28 October.

Philip Shaw, an economist at Investec, told *The Telegraph*: “We suspect that some tax increases are virtually inevitable.”

Thomas Pugh, chief economist at RSM UK, said: “The jump in borrowing in August compared to last year sets the stage for what is likely to be a much trickier budget than Burnham or Healey anticipated when they came to power just a few months ago.

“Another round of tax rises in October now looks inevitable.”

## Entrepreneur gives Burnham socks emblazoned with his face

Andy Burnham was presented with a rather unusual gift of a pair of socks emblazoned with a cartoon of his face as he met an entrepreneur in New York’s Central Park.

The prime minister laughed and said “I don’t know what to make of that” as he was given the pair, which used a stylised cartoon likeness of him similar to the image used on his campaign material.

Mr Burnham left the meeting with several additions to his sock drawer as Josh Turner, the boss of Stand 4 Socks, also gave him pairs featuring the Manchester and New York skylines.

Manchester-born Mr Turner is now based in New York after expanding into the US market.

For every pair Stand 4 Socks sells, another pair is given to the homeless.

Mr Burnham praised the model of social entrepreneurship, saying: “You get on and you give back.”

## Burnham says Britain should be 'trusted partner' to EU schemes

Prime minister Andy Burnham said he would argue for Britain to be a "trusted partner" to the European Union's "Made in Europe" ⁠scheme, saying they should not be pitted as enemies on a policy that has the potential to hammer the UK's car industry.

The Made in Europe policy aims to curb reliance on Chinese components by prioritising European-made goods – a worry for the British auto industry which feeds parts into EU supply chains and sells cars across the bloc.

Travelling to New York for his ⁠first United Nations General Assembly as prime minister, Mr Burnham told ​reporters ⁠he believed there was support for Britain to have access to the scheme.

"Europe's argument is not with us. We both suffer the same effects as other countries, particularly steel," he said .

Britain has argued that the "Made in Europe" ‌plan could impact supply chains and create unnecessary trade barriers between it and some member countries, with Mr Burnham urging the two sides to avoid further "collateral damage."

## RAF’s Saudi mission could help keep fuel prices down, says defence minister

British military aid to Saudi Arabia will limit the risk of further fuel price increases, a defence minister has suggested.

Luke Pollard said the deployment of an RAF refuelling aircraft to the country was aimed at preventing “further escalation” in the Red Sea, where Iran-backed Houthis in Yemen threaten another key route for oil exports.

The war in Iran and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz has already seen fuel prices spike.

Closure of the Bab al-Mandab strait, the only southern approach to the Suez Canal, would add further pressure on global oil supplies.

![The UK has deployed the RAF Voyager to Saudi Arabia](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/22/8/57/PA-82608030-d6376b27.jpeg)

Speaking to the BBC, Mr Pollard said the Government had taken some action to ease the cost of living, but added that “ultimately” it was peace in the Middle East and Ukraine that would cut fuel prices.

He said: “We’re working with our friends to try and deliver that de-escalation, and part of the reason why we deployed the RAF Voyager to Saudi Arabia, for instance, is to ensure there’s not further escalation in the Red Sea, which could further risk our fuel prices.”

The deployment, announced by Andy Burnham on his way to the UN General Assembly in New York, will see the Voyager aircraft provide air-to-air refuelling for Saudi jets.

## Burnham to position UK as an 'honest broker' over AI issues

Andy Burnham, who is being accompanied on his transatlantic trip by wife Marie-France van Heel, will also use his first UN General Assembly speech to focus on AI’s risks and opportunities.

The development of frontier models is being dominated by firms in the US and China, with concerns raised by experts that the breakneck pace of progress is coming at the expense of safety.

Mr Burnham is seeking to position the UK as an “honest broker” in bringing countries together to address the issue, comparing his role to that played by Gordon Brown in the wake of the 2008 financial crisis.

He said recent warnings from AI experts about the risks it could pose to humanity “made me pause” but “I had already decided, though, to make a step change on AI when I came into the role”.

“I will be focusing on AI as a force for good, because it can be and it’s really important to keep that,” he said.

## Opinion: Our people-pleasing PM faces his ultimate challenge in Donald Trump

The least of Andy Burnham’s worries as he prepares for the UN General Assembly in New York, and his first meeting with Donald Trump, is what he has said about the US president in the past.

Before he became prime minister, Mr Burnham was certainly critical of the president, taking the view in 2021 for example that “any politician who gave Trump the time of day should be ashamed right now”.

Yet it is a curious feature of Mr Trump’s generally vengeful personality that he doesn’t allow things said in the past to get in the way of business. After all, and despite some mildly disobliging remarks made in the past, Sir Keir Starmer managed to forge a surprisingly warm relationship with his counterpart, at least in the earlier phase of his premiership. Until the Iran war and disagreements about “civilisational erasure” in Europe soured the bromance, Sir Keir, ably assisted by Buckingham Palace, enjoyed a brief career as world champion “Trump whisperer”. That ended with the president dismissing Sir Keir as “no Winston Churchill”.
