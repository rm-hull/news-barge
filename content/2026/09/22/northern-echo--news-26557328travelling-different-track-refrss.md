**BUSINESSiQ Editor Mike Hughes talks to Richard Salkeld, of Lumo, about how the open access rail operator is driven by affordable travel, supporting local businesses and the widest sense of regional purpose.**

From Lumo’s Newcastle headquarters, the view makes its own argument.

There are trains moving through the city, office staff and on-board crews sharing the same base, and the wider North East beyond them. For Richard Salkeld, it is a constant inspiration and a reminder – if one were needed – that the business he is such a key part of has a role far beyond getting passengers from A to B.

Lumo is a train operator, running services up and down the East Coast Main Line between London and Edinburgh, calling at Stevenage, Newcastle, Morpeth and Glasgow. Its London to Stirling route travels to Milton Keynes, Nuneaton, Crewe, Preston, Carlisle, Lockerbie, and Motherwell before reaching Whifflet, Greenfaulds and then Larbert in the Forth Valley above the River Carron.

The blue all-electric trains have become a familiar sight since the company entered the market with an uncomplicated proposition: one class, competitive fares and a service designed to make rail a realistic alternative to flying, driving or staying at home – and with Ringtons tea and coffee on board, of course.

Crucially, it is also a privately-run open access service, run independently of any Government contracts and outside the Government’s nationalisation plans. So they have the agility and spirit of an independent, able to set their own high standards for performance and service, but also shouldering all the responsibilities.

![]()Richard Salkeld at the Lumo office on Orcahrd Street in Newcastle *(Image: Chris BOOTH)*

An impact study commissioned from Arup showed Lumo was expected to deliver economic benefits of up to £375million up to 2033, in the form of things like time savings, fare savings, and the direct jobs that it supports. Environmentally, in the financial year 2022/23, Lumo helped customers to avoid 60,600 tonnes of CO2-equivalent emissions by using their services, with a greenhouse gas benefit from 2021 to 2033 of up to £365m.

Achieving, maintaining and telling people about such impressive stats is Richard’s job in his role as director of communications and partnerships. But it is the second part of that title where Lumo is setting a new benchmark.

Richard is a man who is up for a challenge and immensely proud of the journey his company is on. But the popularity of Lumo’s services, he says, is tied to its ability to represent the place from which it operates.

‘We support, celebrate and champion the North East in all that we do. We are genuinely vested in championing Newcastle and the whole region,’ he tells me from the company’s smart offices at Central Square South in the city centre.

Just a couple of months ago, the highly-regarded Rail Customer Experience Survey ranked Lumo as the UK’s top rail operator for value for money, as well as being one of the three highest-performing long-distance train operators.

Clearly, something good is happening here.

That award-winning approach runs through the company’s offer, its staffing and its partnerships. Local businesses like Ringtons are brought into conversations about what passengers might buy on the train. Charities, cultural venues, visitor attractions and community groups are treated less as sponsorship opportunities than as partners.

Richard tells me: ‘Our customers love what we have done so far, so we have got an ambition to effectively grow the trains and make them longer, with orders currently pending with Hitachi.

‘There are plenty of stats to back up that satisfaction survey, like our customers travelling in a way that has 95% less carbon emissions than flying but, for me, the social value benefit of what we do is just as important when you try to define the DNA of Lumo.

![]()Lumo and the partners they work with *(Image: Chris BOOTH)*

‘One of the great things about this job is who we can work with. We support art, culture and comedy, through to partnerships that celebrate and bring communities together, such as some of our Pride initiatives. We have partnerships where we’ve helped to promote attractions, support the region’s visitor economy and so much more by celebrating what’s great about this part of the world. It is one of the privileges of being a successful and influential company, that we are in a position to share the spotlight with companies who can really benefit from the profile we can add. Why wouldn’t we do that? It seems so obvious to help your neighbours.’

In an industry often defined by complexity, infrastructure rules and political argument, Lumo has built its identity around being locally rooted in an increasingly controlled national blueprint.

Unlike many train companies operating contracted services, open-access operators receive no government subsidy for their services. They take the full commercial risk. Passenger revenue must cover the cost of running trains, which is why surveys like RCXS are so important – the proposition has to be attractive enough for people to choose it.

Richard is an enthusiastic champion of the model Lumo is perfecting, passenger by passenger, carriage by carriage, route by route.

He says: ‘We represent around 1% of the UK rail network, which is a fraction of all the other operators. That’s why I think we do things differently, because we are agile.

‘We’ve got a workforce here in Newcastle of around 125, and yet we run a handful of services each day connecting Scotland with the North East and London, and we can do things much more quickly and responsively than perhaps some of the other companies out there because our teams are embedded in their region.

‘We’ve got a sense of what’s happening and how we can support some of the organisations we work with. We’re so close to it all and it matters so much to us because this is our patch and I think we can help it get a powerful message around the network.’

In that satisfaction survey, all the operators in the top four positions are on the East Coast Main Line. That may not be a coincidence.

Richard says: ‘Where you’ve got that choice of open access, I think it keeps the indutsry on its toes and certainly when I travel, I often chat with the person next to me and find out that they are people who would have previously flown, so it’s making a massive difference to the environment, which is great.

‘Or it could be people who otherwise would have driven. A lot of the time, particularly with Newcastle and Edinburgh, it’s people who would not have made the journey at all because the price would have put them off.’

That one per cent share of the network does not mean the ambitions are small. Lumo is pursuing longer trains through an order with Hitachi, subject to the necessary processes, to increase capacity where demand along their routes requires it.

‘We launched Lumo on the West Coast in May. We’re connecting Stirling in Scotland with London and destinations along the West Coast Main Line,’ Richard explains.

‘There’s also a longer-term plan to launch a Lumo service connecting South Wales and Carmarthen with London Paddington, because there will always be that ambition that we want to do more because what we’ve seen is that, where Lumo operates and we’re giving customers that choice, the customer experience is enhanced.

‘Last month we ran a ten-coach Lumo service between Edinburgh, Newcastle and London that sold out. That was 1,544 seats returned – the equivalent of around eight to 12 domestic aircraft doing Edinburgh to London. It shows that the value of open access is driving more people to use rail.’

As part of that sustainability argument, Richard believes one of the most compelling pieces of evidence is people deciding to leave the car or avoid a domestic flight altogether. But he is equally interested in the social consequences of making travel more accessible: trips for work, study, culture, family visits and leisure that become possible when the fare is within reach.

‘That all means that customers who travel with us know they’re doing so in a way that is supporting the environment. But there’s also this wider social value, which is a very easy term for people to throw around. That’s why we work with the Purpose Coalition, who can measure that and evaluate it as we help patients get to their cancer treatment appointments, help people feel more included in communities through some of the work that North East does, or help businesses to thrive.’

The ideal combination, which the business practises week after week, is that its own staff use that local connection they are so proud of to steer Lumo’s social value efforts. They know where the gaps are because they see them in their towns and on their streets, and they know they have an employer who will listen and care when they pass them on.

Richards speaks of those colleagues as a team, almost family, as they look after each other from first day starter to boardroom boss.

‘We have something like 77% or more of colleagues on apprenticeship schemes, which is amazing. There’s a real sense of investment in learning and development, and continual professional development, for colleagues who come in here.

‘We’ve also got colleagues who began as customer ambassadors on board who are now working as drivers. Likewise, we’ve got colleagues who are now working as crew managers who were previously on-board crew.

‘There’s a very flat structure in the sense that people aren’t afraid to get stuck in and help if needs be, so there is that investment in people and opportunities, but also that sense of people being able to speak up and volunteer ideas.

‘This is a small but typical example: I was on a call with Dennis, who is a manager of one of the local under-17 girls’ football teams who couldn’t afford away kits, so had the embarrassment of wearing bibs to distinguish themselves when they went to away matches.

‘So we were able to sort them out with some kit, which is currently being made. They’re absolutely thrilled because, in several years, they’ve not been able to wear a proper away kit – and that change came to us through a colleague.’

The story is small in financial terms but significant in tone. It reflects a company willing to respond to a local need without turning every decision into a major corporate campaign.

‘We’ve also given Tyneside Cinema some investment so colleagues can visit at a reduced rate through our partnership. There are things where the colleague benefits are really good, but they’re also based on insight from colleagues themselves as to what they’re most interested in from the point of view of health and wellbeing.

‘It also has a very direct benefit to some of these cultural attractions in the region, so we aren’t motivated by promoting ourselves. It’s about what we can do to support others.’

When Lumo works with a North East organisation in this way there is a mutual respect, which means they become advocates of each other, so there is a natural synergy that I can sense drifting across the region and becoming the only way to do business. Help each other out, we’re all on the same team anyway.

When Richard sets out this inspiring strategy, his conviction that it is the right thing to do and the passion with which he delivers it makes it completely logical. As we know already at BUSINESSiQ, hundreds of firms across the region give their time to help others grow – it is just a matter of how far you shift the balance.

He tells me: ‘The team are all from Newcastle and the wider region, and we want to do things that make it an even better place to live and work. It’s only achievable because people care. I see that with the people I work with every day.

‘Outside our own business it doesn’t feel like hard work because you’re working with people who have that same sense of ambition, passion and conscientiousness. Some of the things we do, on paper, I used to think, “How did we achieve that with the resource we’ve got behind us?” But now I know it’s because of the strength of working together with people who have a like-minded approach.

‘My inbox is full every day with people saying, “Can we work with you on this, that or the other?” It makes for a busy time, but it’s good fun and it is so nice to sit here and talk about what a great job I’ve got and how very humbling it is to hear people talk about what we’re doing and how it’s making such a positive difference to communities. It’s wonderful.

‘Some organisations just want to be able to hand over a cheque and put their logo on something, but that just isn’t Lumo’s way. Like our charity partnership with Daft as a Brush, for example, helping people get to their cancer appointments and making them our designated charity partner.

‘What an organisation that is, and I think they share a lot of the values and a shared passion for helping people. So many colleagues volunteer in different ways to support Daft as a Brush, whether that’s giving their professional knowledge and expertise, or whether it’s David Hughes, our head of security, sustainability and safety doing a parachute jump over County Durham to help them raise funds.

‘But we put a lot of time and effort into making it all work the right way. A Lumo approach to each relationship means we make sure our partnerships are really purposeful and not artificial, superficial or transactional. We try to understand what it is our partner organisations are trying to achieve and use our ability to connect different parts of the country in a way that we can give a platform to start-ups, charities, good causes or initiatives that are keen to get some advice and ideas from us.’

Within his own organisation, Richard talks readily about those customer satisfaction scores, growth plans and the business discipline required of an open-access operator. At the heart of his approach is location. The business is headquartered in the city rather than hidden away in a station. On-board crews start and finish shifts there, alongside office colleagues. The arrangement creates an informal feedback loop between those making decisions and those dealing with customers every day.

‘You’ll see office staff mixing with on-board crews, which is really nice because you have that synergy,’ he says. ‘If there’s an issue on the day, we hear about it very quickly because we’ve got that relationship with our frontline colleagues.’

The same principle applies to the growing list of commercial partnerships. Lumo stocks beer from Newcastle brewery Full Circle, rather than simply opting for a mainstream brand. It involved partners in selecting the product, which now has exposure to passengers travelling across the country, while giving Lumo a more distinctive onboard offer.

Earlier this year, Lumo helped take more than 25 North East food and drink producers and start-ups to Westminster for a Taste of the North East event. For small businesses, access to a London audience can be priceless. It seems that the North East is both home and product for Lumo. It wants people to travel through the region, but it also wants them to come to it. That is why it has developed those vital collaborations.

Two of the clearest examples came when more than 100 travel writers visited Newcastle as part of a media event. Lumo acted as travel partner, bringing writers to the city on its services before they visited places including St James’ Park, Kynren in Bishop Auckland and the coast.

‘For me, that was all about us being able to facilitate an opportunity for the region,’ says Richard. ‘They all went back to their respective parts of the country, some travelling from abroad, and wrote about what you should do when you visit Newcastle.’

Then a Scottish media team was invited to experience Newcastle, with a partner hotel providing a stay, Lumo handling the journey and the Centre for Life providing tickets.

The resulting coverage positioned Newcastle as a destination for Scottish visitors, including families interested in the venue’s exhibitions.

Lumo has also worked with accessibility partners, Going North East, Northern Stage and Eldon Square on a video presenting Newcastle as an accessible and welcoming city. For Richard, that was undertaken because it was the right thing to do, but it also carried a wider message about who the city welcomes and what it can offer.

Among more recent projects, helping Newcastle bring back a tradition of having its version of the Tower of London beefeaters, to do a greeting every day, is the kind of project that could seem peripheral to a rail operation, but in Richard’s view, it is part of the same mission.

**The depth and longevity of the relationship between Lumo and its partners puts them in a powerful position to enable change across the city. Mike Hughes talked to some of the businesses about the impact it is having on their work**

## **Keith Merrin, North East Museums**

![]()Keith Merrin, North East Museums *(Image: Chris BOOTH)*

We’ve been working with Lumo for about three years now. They support a lot of the work we’ve been doing at Newcastle’s Discovery Museum, which tells the story of the region’s inventiveness, industry and transport. There’s a very obvious alignment between the two.

It’s a strong, ongoing relationship because we are both very interested in the history of the North East, promoting the region and spotlighting its identity. It’s important to us that Lumo is a company headquartered in the North East and physically gets people here because it has that reach outside the region to bring people into the city, which in turn supports our venues.

There’s a big alignment in Lumo’s commitment to promoting businesses and suppliers, and the focus on sustainable transport and sustainability, which is one of the five pillars of our work.

## **Andrea Clover, Daft as a Brush Cancer Patient Care**

![]()Andrea Clover, Daft as a Brush Cancer Patient Care *(Image: Chris BOOTH)*

We provide free patient transport to and from the Freeman Hospital, at the moment, but we are looking to expand that service more broadly. I don’t think people fully appreciate the barriers in place when you’re undergoing cancer treatment.

You may be immunocompromised, you may not be able to make public transport work for you, and you might have appointments left, right and centre that you cannot get to in time.

We take all of that stress and worry away. You can have a friend or family member with you, or, if you’re feeling nervous, we’ll provide somebody to stay with you. We’ll pick you up from your door, take you to where your treatment is taking place, wait for you and take you back.

Lumo has been absolutely superb in offering so many opportunities to us. We are even able to put volunteers onto their trains to fundraise for us and raise awareness on board , which helps get the word out about what we do and introduce us to a new audience.

It’s far more than just funding. The engagement their staff have had, beyond what you would consider a run-of-the-mill partnership, has been unbelievable. They’ve given us so much support in terms of their expertise — even helping us set up an ESG policy. On another occasion, their head of finance was on the interview panel when we recruited for a new finance role and they then helped set the questions and shortlist candidates.

They’re genuinely committed to meeting their corporate social responsibility. They’re good people to talk to. You can pick up the phone and say, “What do you think about that idea?’ and you’ll get, “Yeah, we’ll see what we can do”.

I’ve never yet had Richard say no to me on anything, and it means a lot when you’re a small organisation.

## **Ben Cleary, Founder of Full Circle Brew Company**

![]()Ben Cleary, founder of Full Circle Brew Company *(Image: Chris BOOTH)*

We’ve focused a lot on community ourselves, and I think that’s how this has come about – we both want to give back.

The big thing for me is that we want to be on every touchpoint, and having the beer on the trains is such a win for us.

We are just starting our journey with Lumo, but so far it’s been excellent. Richard’s on the phone a lot, asking, “What can we do? How can we do this? We can get you in here, we can get you in there”. It’s a breath of fresh air.

The brewing industry is very collaborative and very creative. Everyone is there to help, and this very much feels like that for me.

## **Peter Darrant, Out North East**

![]()Peter Durrant, Out Northeast *(Image: Chris BOOTH)*

We’re an LGBTQ charity working throughout the whole of the North East, providing safe spaces for LGBTQ people, education, work and employability skills. Really, it’s about connecting other organisations.

We’ve also set up the One Centre, the region’s dedicated LGBTQ centre, based in Gateshead, and I own Pride Radio, a community radio station on FM and DAB in the region, which reaches 127 countries online. We get about 200,000 people a week engaging through the station.

At the moment, minority groups in general are punching bags for deflection politics. It feels like we’re going back to the 1980s with Section 28. But back then, they didn’t have organisations like ourselves and the many other great organisations in the North East and further afield. We’re able to provide support, guidance and a bit of a fightback.

Working with Lumo is about education, outreach, community and making sure we’ve got a strong, positive voice. In 2007, we were number one on the LGBTQ Index in Europe, but now we’re number 22, which shows how far we’ve gone back.

The most valuable thing they give us is its partnership, friendship and visibility. We have one train that I call the Pride train. It has Out North East, Pride Radio, Glasgow Pride and Edinburgh Pride on it, and it travels from Edinburgh to London once a day.

For a young queer person who feels a bit lonely, seeing a large organisation visibly support them can create a sense of pride. To see somebody like Lumo say, “This is who we are. We’re an inclusive organisation. We welcome all passengers, regardless of how you identify or who you love”, is incredibly powerful.

They don’t just write a cheque. They want to come in, help you, introduce you and give you opportunities that we wouldn’t otherwise have. But we give back too. Wherever I can help Lumo with opportunities to get its brand across and talk to my community about them, we will. The relationship is reciprocal.
