# Social prescribing reminds us that health is not merely the absence of illness - Matt Bromley

## About 20 years ago, I sat next to the actor and singer Jerome Flynn on a long-haul flight to Singapore. Flynn was enroute to a mindfulness retreat; I was hopping on a connecting flight to New Zealand for a three-month sabbatical. Flynn was polite and friendly, introducing himself and his co-traveller.

Despite the fact he’d just had a string of number one hits with Robson Green, he had no airs and graces. We got talking about the retreat he was headed to, and he explained that, once a year, to alleviate stress and anxiety, he took himself off grid for a week to reconnect with his inner self. It was a detox of both mind and body.

That travelling tete-a-tete with a Soldier Soldier star came to mind recently when I was talking to a friend about his daughter’s charity, Dramatic Recovery, which promotes positive mental health and well-being through drama and the arts. What Dramatic Recovery offer is a form of social prescribing, an approach which recognises many of our health problems are rooted not solely in biology but in our lives, our relationships, and our communities. There was a time when almost every problem seemed to have the same answer: a prescription pad. Feeling low? Pills. Struggling to sleep? Pills. Lonely, anxious, lacking purpose? More pills. Medication undoubtedly transforms lives and, for many people, me included, remains essential. But what if, for some, the best medicine isn't found at the pharmacy at all? Imagine leaving the doctors not with a blister pack but with an invitation to join a walking group, a community am-dram company, or a creative writing workshop. It may sound unconventional, yet it’s precisely the thinking behind social prescribing: rather than treating symptoms alone, it connects people with activities, groups, and services that improve wellbeing.

None of this is about pretending illnesses such as depression can simply be walked away or that anxiety disappears after a pottery class. Serious mental illness requires proper clinical treatment, and social prescribing is not a substitute for medicine. Instead, it acknowledges the importance of connection as well as chemicals.

We work longer hours, spend more time online, and often know fewer of our neighbours than previous generations did. Many people, particularly older adults, carers, those living with chronic illness, and people recovering from bereavement, can go days without meaningful conversation. Others feel disconnected despite being surrounded by people. Loneliness is not always synonymous with being alone.

That isolation has consequences. It affects physical health, mental wellbeing, and even life expectancy. Humans are social creatures, and our health depends upon belonging as much as biology.

This is where social prescribing comes into its own. A weekly drama rehearsal offers not only creativity but friendship. A hiking group provides not only cardiovascular exercise but routine and mutual encouragement. A creative writing class gives people not only the chance to tell their stories but also the reassurance that someone is willing to listen.

Schools, too, have something to learn from this philosophy.

Social prescribing reminds us that health is not merely the absence of illness but the presence of purpose.
