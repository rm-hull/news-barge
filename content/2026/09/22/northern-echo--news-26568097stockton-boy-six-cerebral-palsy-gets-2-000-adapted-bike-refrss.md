A six-year-old boy from Stockton who was told he might never walk or talk is enjoying a new sense of freedom after a charity helped fund a specialist adapted tricycle worth more than £2,000.

Max Burton, who has cerebral palsy, can now ride his own bike independently after receiving a grant from Cash for Kids.

Max was born 11 weeks prematurely, at 29 weeks, and suffered brain bleeds that caused hydrocephalus; he had a shunt fitted at five months and was later diagnosed with cerebral palsy.

His mum, Viki, said doctors warned soon after his birth that he might never walk or talk.

Max cannot yet walk independently but has recently started taking steps with a walker at school; however, his mum says fears about his ability to communicate were completely misplaced.

Viki said: "He does not stop chatting from the second he wakes up to the second he falls asleep.

"He's got a really big personality. He's funny, he knows what he wants and he's headstrong. Despite all the challenges he faces every day, he's always laughing and smiling."

Viki applied to Cash for Kids for help paying for the specialist bike.

She said: "Max was able to get out and ride his bike and have that feeling of being able to do what other children his age do.

"If we hadn't had the grant, it would have been impossible. Like most families, we can't just pull £2,000 out the bag for a bike that might fit him for three or four years and then have to do it again."

Viki said many people underestimate the cost of raising a disabled child, with specialist equipment often far more expensive than standard alternatives.

The family recently spent £800 on an adapted potty because Max cannot safely balance on a standard one.

Viki and her husband Chris both work and receive help from their parents, but she knows many families are not so fortunate.

She said: "Charities like Cash for Kids are so important because without them, children like Max wouldn't get this equipment – parents just can't afford it.

"We're quite fortunate that we both work and have parents who help, so we can afford some things, but we would never have been able to afford things like the bike if it wasn't for the help from the charity."

Viki said raising a child with additional needs had taught her resilience and changed her outlook on life.

She said: "When they're very little, you think about all the things they can't do, or might never do, and you worry. But then you get there and think, 'We'll just manage.'

"You develop a level of resilience that you never knew possible.
