Man linked to suspicious death charged with bribing public officials in Bangladesh

A man has been charged with bribing public officials in Bangladesh to stop police linking him to a suspicious death, in a first in Australia’s courts, AAP reports.

An Australian-Bangladeshi woman’s death was deemed suspicious by local authorities when her body was found in Bangladesh in September 2024.

The Australian federal police began investigating the death due to the woman’s dual citizenship status, seizing international transaction records from banks in Sydney and Brisbane.

Police allege a 51-year-old man known to the woman conspired with people in Bangladesh in the two years after her death, transferring about $90,000 to bribe public officials to remove his name from police records.

Raids were carried out on Monday at homes in Goodna and Redbank Plains, in Ipswich, south-west of Brisbane, where authorities seized an electronic device and arrested the 51-year-old.

He was charged with bribing a foreign public official, an offence which carries a maximum sentence of 10 years in jail.

Any attempt to corrupt investigative processes, whether in Australia or overseas, was a serious criminal matter, AFP Cmdr Stephen Jay said.

“We will continue to work closely with our domestic and international partners regarding this investigation,” he said.

The Redbank Plains man is scheduled to appear before Brisbane magistrates court later on Tuesday.

It is the first time the AFP has put an allegation of foreign bribery of a public official for personal advantage before the court.

Bangladeshi police are conducting their own investigation into the woman’s death.

Solar and wind generation meet record 80% of power demand on Australia’s national grid

Sunshine and wind met 80.4% of demand on Australia’s main power grid on Saturday, setting a new high bar for renewable electricity.

Solar panels on rooftops provided more than half of the power used in the national electricity market, the grid that supplies the eastern states, South Australia and the Australian Capital Territory, for 30 minutes around lunchtime. This was complemented by solar and windfarms, and a small amount of hydro (1%). Fossil fuels, coal and gas, contributed 20%.

The energy minister, Chris Bowen, said the milestone meant there was “more clean, cheap, sovereign energy powering our homes and businesses than ever before”.

Ingredient used in cold and flu supplements to be removed over allergy link

A medicinal herb found in cold and flu supplements will be phased off Australian shelves over links to severe allergic reactions, AAP reports.

Andrographis paniculata (andrographis), also known as green chiretta, has been barred from new medicines entering the Australian market, the Therapeutic Goods Administration announced on Monday.

Existing medicines containing andrographis will not be removed from the market immediately, but the TGA has encouraged suppliers to recall products containing the herb.

Native to India and Sri Lanka, andrographis is commonly used in Ayurvedic and Chinese traditional medicine to support immunity and combat cold and flu symptoms. Around 80 products containing andrographis are listed on the Australian market, some of which are available from supermarkets, health food stores and pharmacies.

The ingredient has attracted growing industry concern in recent years after reports of anaphylactic reactions and one fatality in June 2024.

More on today’s changes to the Criminal Procedure Act in NSW …

The NSW Women’s Safety Commissioner, Hannah Tonkin, says:

Allowing complainants’ pre-recorded evidence in chiefto be used during sexual assault trials will enable them to give their account much earlier, in a safe setting, and to start rebuilding their lives sooner.

The government is also amending the law around the handling sexual assault complainants’ counselling communications so that courts can only consider them when it is necessary, rather than automatically or out of an abundance of caution.

University of Sydney-led research, due to report back in mid-2027, is considering alternative models for reform. Recent reporting has revealed how complainants’ counselling records, including audio recordings, have been used by alleged perpetrators’ legal teams.

NSW to pilot ‘ground rules’ hearings in sexual assault trials

Penry Buckley

A pilot of “ground rules” hearings will reduce distress and trauma for complainants in sexual assault trials alongside other reforms being introduced to NSW parliament today, the state government says.

Ground rules hearings, which are already used in child sexual assault proceedings in NSW, will be piloted in sexual assault trials at Parramatta, Wollongong and Dubbo district courts from March next year until 2030.

They will provide directions about how complainants will be questioned, including the duration and manner, topics put to them and aides to support their evidence. The government says hearings will require pre-trial discussion about jury directions regarding consent and differences in accounts “without compromising the accused’s right to test the prosecution case”.

The NSW attorney general, Michael Daley, says:

Sexual assault complainants and witnesses are especially vulnerable in our justice system. We want to help them give their best evidence by ensuring they do not need to tell their story multiple times and deterring inappropriate and irrelevant questioning in court.

Greens call for triple zero network to be brought back under independent control

Greens senator Sarah Hanson-Young spoke this morning about the idea for a national authority to oversee the triple zero network, which is responsible for emergency call operations.

A Senate committee said yesterday in a report that triple zero should be taken out of private hands.

The plan would move control of Australia’s emergency call system away from commercial entities like Telstra to a dedicated public authority.

Hanson-Young told RN Breakfast this morning:

At the moment, Telstra is contracted to do this work. The taxpayers pay it. That’s your money, my money, every Australian taxpayer’s money to fund the operation of triple zero … We think it’s time to really consider bringing that back under independent control so that the customer, the public, the safety is put first – it’s not seen as some kind of chore or add-on for a privately run company …

There needs to be an overhaul of the telecommunications laws. Standards and reliability guarantees must be in the law. Compensation must be available for people. And triple zero must be run for the safety of people, not the profits of corporations.
