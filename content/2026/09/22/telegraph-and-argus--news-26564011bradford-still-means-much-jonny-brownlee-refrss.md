Olympic triathlete Jonny Brownlee has recalled cycling to school along Bradford's canal paths, running through the district's parks and training with local clubs as he reflected on a career that helped transform the sport.

In an exclusive interview with the Telegraph & Argus, the 36-year-old opened up on retirement, fatherhood and his plans for life after professional triathlon.

The Yorkshire sporting icon announced his retirement this month, bringing the curtain down on a career that included three Olympic medals, multiple world titles and a lasting legacy through the Brownlee Foundation.

Now, after a career that helped turn triathlon into a mainstream sport in Britain, Brownlee is back in Yorkshire reflecting on a journey that began in Bradford.

## Bradford roots

"Bradford was a massive part of my life," he said.

"I used to cycle along the canal from Horsforth to Bradford Grammar School most days of my life.

![]()Jonny with brother Alistair Brownlee

"I could pretty much tell you every single footpath in Bradford.

"I absolutely loved my time running and swimming and biking around Bradford."

Brownlee's sporting journey in the district started at an even younger age.

"One of the reasons I started running is we were actually in a McDonald's in Bradford and my dad saw an advert for the McDonald's League," he recalled.

"That was a tester event for Bingley Harriers."

Brownlee would go on to learn his trade in athletics with the Harriers.

He believes Bradford and Yorkshire provided the perfect environment to develop as an athlete.

He said: "I don't think I would have had a career in sport without Bradford Grammar School and the teachers there that allowed us to run at lunchtime.

"I was very fortunate that the cities of Leeds and Bradford had such great swimming set-ups.

"It was the little things along the way, the fact that I could, at the end of my road, ride down and meet a group of 20 people cycling to the Yorkshire Dales."

## On retirement

Although Brownlee's retirement announcement came earlier this month, the decision itself had been building for years.

He said: "There wasn't one thing.

"It's been a slow burner over a few months and even a few years.

"I've always prided myself on being 100 per cent committed on the start line.

"I think that's been really strong in my career, to be on Olympic start lines knowing I've done everything I possibly can to be there in the best possible shape.

![]()Alistair Brownlee (left) and brother Jonny embrace after winning Gold and Silver in the Men's Triathlon at Fort Copacabana. *(Image: Mike Egerton)*

"I wasn't able to do that anymore."

A combination of injuries and the arrival of son Freddie 11 months ago gradually changed his outlook.

"My body was starting to let me down more," he said.

"Secondly, having my baby son Freddie, that changed my life completely.

"My commitments changed and I couldn't really have that professional lifestyle anymore of snoozing during the day, getting full nights of sleep and looking after yourself perfectly.

"With my body how it was and my age, I wasn't quite good enough to do it half-heartedly. And I didn't want to do it half-heartedly."

Brownlee admitted he became significantly more conscious of risk after learning Freddie was on the way.

"I never, ever used to think about crashing," he said.

"When it rained I used to think, brilliant, I'm from Yorkshire, everyone else hates the rain.

"Now I'm the opposite.

"I raced in Toulouse last year and I was really scared around the corners because I'd got it in my head that I didn't want to break my wrist before having Freddie and not be able to hold him.

"I'd never, ever thought that before."

## Brownlee Foundation 'changes children's lives'

Among his proudest achievements are his bronze medal at London 2012.

![]()Jonny Brownlee with his Silver medal for the Men's Triathlon. *(Image: Mike Egerton)*

But some of the achievements he values most did not necessarily result in medals.

Brownlee said finishing fifth at the Tokyo Olympics remains one of the performances he is proudest of because of the sacrifices he made in preparation.

"I was so proud of that race because I 100 per cent committed to that.

"I knew I wasn't very good in the heat so I turned my conservatory into a heat chamber, spent hours and hours training in there.

"I went to Toyko and delivered a performance I was really proud of," he said.

"Although it wasn't a medal, I knew I'd given everything and that's all I could ask."

Brownlee also spoke passionately about the Brownlee Foundation, which he and brother Alistair launched in 2014 to give children opportunities through sport.

At some point next year, likely during an event in Bradford, the charity expects to welcome its 100,000th child participant.

"We are changing children's lives," Brownlee said.

"The stuff that I read afterwards from teachers. They say this child was really under-confident, he wanted to do the event so he went and learned how to swim. Since then he's been great at school and way more confident.

![]()More than a thousand children took part in an event hosted by YPO and the Brownlee Foundation *(Image: Supplied)*

"It's just amazing."

He added: "It's about telling children they can do something when they don't believe they can do it.

"To have 100,000 children is pretty incredible."

## Future plans

Retirement, however, does come with uncertainties.

In one of the interview's most candid moments, Brownlee admitted he is concerned about replacing the sense of purpose elite sport has provided throughout his life.

"I'm very aware that a lot of my self worth comes from competing or achieving things day in, day out," he said.

"I'm very aware you're never going to be able to fill that gap completely.

I'm never going to have as good a feeling of when you've built towards an Olympic Games, you've thrown everything at it, and you deliver that performance under that pressure."

Despite those concerns, Brownlee is embracing the future.

He plans to continue working with the Brownlee Foundation and Brownlee Fitness and take on new endurance challenges.

Most of all, he is looking forward to having freedom after decades of sporting commitments.

"I'm looking forward to exercising rather than training," he said.

"I've spent my life saying no to things.

"I had that period in my last years of school where friends stopped asking you to do stuff because it's just, no I can't do that.

"So I guess (I'm looking forward to) finally being able to say yes to things."

And when asked how he hopes people will remember his career in years to come, Brownlee said:

"I'd like them to say I'm someone who gave it my all, did it with a smile on my face, and left the sport in a far better place than when I found it."
