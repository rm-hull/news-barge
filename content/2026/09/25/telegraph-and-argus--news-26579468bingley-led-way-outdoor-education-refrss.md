USING rare photographs and detailed research, I have explored four quirky events which happened in Bingley in 1916/1917.

Whilst they may appear to have been separate events, it seems they were part of a combined experiential outdoor learning and education strategy run by Bingley Teacher Training College. This would sow the seeds of approaches to outdoor education reintroduced by the College in the 1960s.

Masque of the Wool

In 1916 a Masque of the Wool was organised by the staff and students at Bingley College and the Workers Education Association in the bottom pasture of Myrtle Park to raise funds for the YMCA and the Friends War Victims Relief Committee. Hundreds of Bingley elementary schoolchildren took part, and the event attracted a local audience of 4,000 people.

![]()Hundreds of schoolchildren took part in Bingley Masque of the Wool.

The Masque consisted of stories, rhymes and songs presented by a range of characters representing the history, present and future of the town. The history was represented by Binghelia, a sleeping character who was awakened by a hero on horseback.

The development of local industry was shown through characters dressed in white representing farming and hand weaving and those with dark conical hats and dressed in black as the later black industrial smoke hanging over the town. Others wearing gold chimneys on their heads highlighted the quest for money which had caused the industrialisation of the town.

The Cooperative Society, Independent Labour Party and Workers Education Association were also depicted amongst Masque characters,

The Masque had been written primarily by Miss Morton,’ Mistress of Method’ at the Training College, as a fundraising opportunity and practical learning vehicle for local schoolchildren. She was responsible for creating different /inclusive methods of education/learning for trainee teachers from the inception of the College in 1911.

Masque of Education

In 1916 a Masque of Education was performed at the College featuring the Principal, Helen Wodehouse, as Binghelia, again written by Miss Morton as a student learning opportunity.

In 1912, Patrick Geddes had published The Masque of Learning and its Many Meanings: A Pageant of Education through the Ages. This was actively promoted through teacher-training colleges and schools at the time and was an available text enabling Miss Morton to identify and pursue new teaching ideas.

Camp Schools

In 1917 Margaret McMillan published The Camp School, a book based on her experiences in Bradford and London, which suggested bringing together fresh air, exercise, good food and education to improve children’s health.

![]()Margaret McMillan's Camp School book. *(Image: Image submitted)*

In the same year Bingley College ran their first Camp School on their sports field, prompted again by Miss Morton’s ideas and possibly McMillan’s book.

Performance of ‘The Birds’ Pageant

In 1917 College students ran a pageant in which they dressed as birds and performed for children from local schools. Photographs in this feature show them dressed as such in front of the Halls of Residence on the same sports field as the Camp School.

![]()The Birds Pageant with hostel buildings in the background, 1917.

The allegory/idea behind the performance may well have been on the theme of changing times for students and schoolchildren - Young birds leaving the nest, learning to fly or older and wiser birds preparing younger birds to face a changing world. The students possibly used the Masque structure and their experience to develop this theme.

![]()Camp school with the main building in background.

Experiential Learning 1917

All the events described suggest an early focus on the philosophy of learning from experience as part of college activity for staff, students and schoolchildren. This involved progressive education through environment, nature, activity and experience which then became part of the College tradition and philosophy of experiential education.

Experiential Learning 1962

Forty-five years later Wally Keay was appointed as Senior Lecturer at Bingley, still an all-women’s College (until 1963/4). Wally’s teaching experience had begun at Linton Camp School near Grassington in 1945.

At Bingley he initially introduced a Foundation Course involving student teachers in outdoor pursuits whilst also aiming to produce teachers who could use outdoor activities to enhance their teaching of more conventional subjects.

In 1968 West Riding County Council bought Buckden House Dales Centre, giving the College some preference in its use. Students enjoyed canoeing, caving, orienteering, and climbing and could additionally take weekend courses for qualifications such as the Mountain Leadership Certificate.

Importantly, Buckden was also used as a centre for environmental education, with the College organising camps for schoolchildren.

A College 10-year plan of the time suggested that this was a return to the camp schools and experiential learning organised by the college beginning in 1917!
