# Droughts and wildfires now seen as 'most pressing' climate impact by public

## Drought, heatwaves and wildfires have overtaken floods and storms in people’s minds as the “most pressing” climate impacts for the UK, polling shows.

A second extreme hot, dry summer in a row, with widespread drought, multiple heatwaves, blazes across the country, and hosepipe bans for millions, has thrust those issues high in the public consciousness.

Yorkshire is one of only two areas of England not in drought, however Yorkshire Water has already urged customers to reduce their usage as much as they can.

Even though there is no hosepipes ban in place in the region - unlike much of the rest of the country - the embattled firm has asked people to use watering cans instead of hoses

Many parts of the county saw no rain for the whole of July, turning Yorkshire into a tinderbox for wildfires.

Four fifths supported investment in parks, ponds and trees to provide shade and cooling in hot weather, according to the polling by Savanta for Climate Barometer – which tracks public opinion on climate change, while just 3 per cent disagreed with such a move.

But while nearly three quarters said they had experienced extreme heat, just one in seven said they had noticed measures to tackle heatwaves such as planting more trees in cities, shaded areas in public places or shutters being installed on buildings.

A similar proportion said they had seen new or upgraded parks and ponds.

The polling of more than 2,000 people shows that 62 per cent now name heatwaves and extreme heat as one of the most pressing impacts of climate change for the UK to deal with, while drought and water scarcity was named by 48 per cent and almost as many said wildfires.

They were all ahead of flooding, at 40 per cent, severe storms at 25 per cent and sea level rise at 23 per cent.

It is a marked switch from two years ago when, following the wettest September on record for some counties in England, flooding dominated the impacts people thought were most pressing, at 68 per cent of those polled.

By contrast, just 36 per cent thought heatwaves, 26 per cent named drought and only 13 per cent flagged wildfires as one of the most pressing climate impacts facing the UK.

Dr Niall McLoughlin, impacts and adaptation lead at Climate Barometer, said: “Summer heat used to be something we looked forward to in the UK – now it’s something many of us dread.

“With extreme heat, drought and wildfires now seen as the ‘most pressing’ climate impacts, it’s clear that this summer has had a profound effect on the British public’s concerns.

He added: “There are lots of ways to beat the heat, but there’s one clear route forward that has little friction: nature-based shading and cooling.”

Analysis by the Energy and Climate Intelligence Unit (ECIU) suggests the exceptionally hot, dry spring and summer could lead to the worst harvest on record.

A shortfall in cereals and oilseed could be costing UK farmers as much as £390m, and that is just one sector.

It is likely to have a knock on effect, in sectors such as dairy, over the coming months.

Martin Lines, CEO of the Nature Friendly Farming Network, said: "Farmers will unquestionably be among those alarmed about droughts and heatwaves following this summer.

“These hot, dry conditions have caused crop yields to plummet, vegetables to struggle for lack of water and grass for livestock to burn off. All of us will feel the impact of this over the coming months, as poor harvests inevitably make food more expensive.

"However, we need to move beyond worrying about heatwaves and flooding in the seasons when we directly experience them. We cannot deal with these events in isolation. We need to treat water as one of our most important resources and manage it all year round. For farmers, that means support to build healthy soil full of organic matter that can absorb downpours and then hold onto the water for use in long, dry spells.

"Farmers' fears for their businesses and public concerns about spiralling food bills are two sides of the same coin. Both can be alleviated by supporting nature-friendly farming which makes food production more resilient and by reducing carbon emissions, without which conditions will become too hostile for reliable farming."
