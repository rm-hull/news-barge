Hundreds of residents from villages on both sides of the River Tees turned out to witness the reopening of Whorlton Bridge on Friday, bringing an end to almost seven years of separation for the communities it links.

Crowds gathered as the ribbon was cut on the historic crossing, which has been closed since 2019 after safety inspections found it could no longer safely carry pedestrians or vehicles.

The Grade II\* listed suspension bridge first opened in 1831, one of the oldest of its kind in the country, has undergone a restoration programme costing more than £12m, with engineers rebuilding much of the structure after discovering parts were beyond repair.

![]()The historic Whorlton Bridge reopened after seven years of complex repairs. *(Image: SARAH CALDECOTT)*

The reopening ceremony featured a folk song written by villagers about the bridge's importance, while Barnard Castle Band performed during the celebrations - echoing the opening ceremony 195 years ago, at which the brass band also played.

![]()Barnard Castle Band also played at the grand opening of the bridge in 1831. *(Image: NORTHERN ECHO)*

George Stastny, from Whorlton, and Jo Harper, from Ovington officially became the first to take the journey across the restored bridge.

Mr Stastny said: "We were totally separated for seven years.

![]()George Stastny and Jo Harper were the first people to walk the rennovated crossing. *(Image: SARAH CALDECOTT)*

"I was 77 in 2019, and I was hoping I'd live long enough to actually see it open. So here we are.

"Being the first to walk over the bridge, it's a great honour.

"You don't realise how important it is until it's gone. You take it for granted but suddenly it's back."

Ms Harper said generations of friendships, family links and walking routes had been disrupted by the closure.

She said: "We've missed our local walks, our local circuits, our running, our cycling.

![]()The historic Whorlton Bridge reopened after seven years of complex repairs. *(Image: SARAH CALDECOTT)*

"We were very proud of having one of the oldest bridges. It's always been something that we've been proud of and to have it replaced and put back piece by piece is just so exciting.

"It's spectacular. It was the end and then the beginning again. So it all restarts our relationships with the community again, our family can meet each other, we can walk here again."

The 19th century crossing originally carried horse-drawn carts transporting coal across the Tees, but the restoration and repair project was driven by a desire to reconnect communities and secure the future of the crossing.

![]()Crowds gathered for an official ceremony including a brass band and folk singers. *(Image: SARAH CALDECOTT)*

![]()The historic Whorlton Bridge reopened after seven years of complex repairs. *(Image: SARAH CALDECOTT)*

![]()Villagers from both sides of the Tees gathered to celebrate the crossing. *(Image: SARAH CALDECOTT)*

Paul Anderson, Durham County Council's strategic highways manager, said: "It's a privilege for us to be here today, to reopen and reconnect this crossing across the River Tees in this beautiful part of Durham County.

"The big driving force is to reconnect the communities, not just for now, but future-proof it for many years to come."

Mr Anderson said the restoration had involved dismantling every part of the bridge individually before testing each component to determine whether it could be reused.

"It wasn't just putting a cosmetic bridge back here for cyclists and horse riders and pedestrians, it had to be a usable bridge for traffic," he said.

![]()Paul Anderson from Durham County Council said it was a 'privilege' to be at the reopening. *(Image: NORTHERN ECHO)*

The restoration proved far more complex than first expected.

As engineers dismantled the structure piece by piece, extensive hidden deterioration was uncovered, requiring the vast majority of the bridge's components to be replaced with newly manufactured parts designed to match the original appearance while meeting modern safety standards.

New foundations were also built at both ends of the crossing before the bridge was reassembled.

Councillor Tim McGuinness, Durham County Council's cabinet member for rural, farming and transport, said the authority was "extremely pleased and proud" to have completed the work.

He said: "This has been a complex and extensive programme of works to preserve an iconic landmark, which can now be used by generations to come."
