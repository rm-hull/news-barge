North East businesses have been given a first-hand look at the opportunities and challenges presented by artificial intelligence during a conference dedicated to the fast-moving technology.

The event, held at Darlington Arena, was organised by Stockton-based telecoms and connectivity firm Lucid, a long-time sponsor of Darlington Mowden Park Rugby Club.

It brought together delegates from a wide range of sectors, including law, concrete providers, and charities.

![]()VP of customer experience European unified communications as a service provider Wildix Stewart Donnor *(Image: Supplied)*

Paul Alsop, managing director at Lucid, said: "This is massively topical at every level.

"Jobs are going to change over the next two to five years.

"It’s about doing more with less effort.

"But the governance side is important to try and control AI.

"Many organisations and companies have no idea what is going on.

"AI can do really clever things.

"At this club it has been fielding calls about the link-up with Newcastle Red Bulls, for instance, without any of the Mowden Park staff having to be involved.

"But a lot of people, from manufacturing to management, lawyers to dentists, are getting things wrong.

"They are using it like Google when it has to be done right."

He stressed that misusing AI could expose businesses to serious legal risks.

Delegates heard that case law had been written making organisations responsible for their AI and that firms could be liable for fines of between £3m and £30m if things went wrong.

Mr Alsop said: "But I am incredibly enthusiastic about the technology.

"I have worked for 32 years in the industry and the past 10 months have been so exciting.

"But we need to move forward with a degree of caution and must constantly assess the risk.

"If you let AI loose it can take over the internet which would be no good as humans would no longer be making the key decisions."

![]()Digital strategist and innovator with Axonyx and Enginuity Matt Atkinson *(Image: Supplied)*

Matt Atkinson, a digital strategist and innovator with Axonyx and Enginuity, gave a historical overview of AI’s evolution.

He began with wartime codebreaker Alan Turing and traced the technology’s development from the 1956 Dartmouth Workshop through decades of slow progress and eventual breakthroughs.

Mr Atkinson said: "By 2025 AI was available in the web browser and without any training or warnings everyone just got stuck in.

"Now we have huge business adoption but in terms of control, enforcement and governance we are playing catch-up.

"We are now worried about it taking over the world and destroying humanity so we need to slow down and take control.

"AI cannot lie but it does hallucinate and invent things.

"Unless you tell it, it can share company secrets globally for life.

"With control you can accelerate confidently but you need policies and people in place and then you can look at the business process."

He compared AI to a powerful drug once users realise its productivity potential.

Mr Atkinson shared a case study where a task that took eight people 22 days to complete was processed by AI in just 90 seconds—1,500 transactions in total.

He urged businesses to audit their AI use, set clear boundaries, ensure human oversight, and keep records for compliance.

Delegates also heard from Stewart Donnor, VP of customer experience, and Trevor Creek, sales engineer, at European unified communications as a service provider Wildix.

Mr Donnor said: "If you are not using it someone else is which gives them competitive advantage.

"Once you see what it can do you will want it and it becomes addictive.

"It will be your best employee as it never rings in sick and can work 24/7.

"But it doesn’t replace staff it augments them and allows them to perform higher tasks.

"It will do the things the staff don’t like doing like chasing overdue payments.

"It is multi-lingual and saved one client £12,000 a month in translation fees."

Attendees welcomed the practical insights shared throughout the day.

Christopher Kewin, IT manager at Newcastle law firm Mincoffs, said: "This has been really interesting in helping us understand AI.
