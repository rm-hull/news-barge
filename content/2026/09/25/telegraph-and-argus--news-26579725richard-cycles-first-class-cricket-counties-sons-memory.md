RICHARD Clough, of Low Moor Holy Trinity Cricket Club, cycled to all 18 first-class cricket counties in England and Wales to raise funds for Marie Curie Hospice.

It was there that his son Mikey, 28, who had esophageal cancer, was cared for in the last 10 days of his life.

Having completed his challenge, which I have covered in previous articles, Richard reflects on the experience. In order, the counties he visited were Durham, Yorkshire, Lancashire, Derbyshire, Nottinghamshire, Leicestershire, Northamptonshire, Warwickshire, Worcestershire, Glamorgan, Gloucestershire, Somerset, Hampshire, Sussex, Kent, Surrey, Middlesex and finally Essex, covering 960 miles in all. He raised a total of £5,319.

He says everything he had control over on the ride went like clockwork, and even things he couldn’t control, like the weather, were fantastic. “I had one day of heavy rain and gales, I was quite glad of that because it made me realise how lucky I was to get all those days cycling in dry conditions,” he says. “As for punctures and cycling over the Peak District with one brake, you just find a way to overcome such setbacks.

“I didn’t carry much stuff with me as Ruth (his wife) was meeting me in Nottingham with a bag of clothing. Due to the mechanical condition of the bike, she came out a day early and brought my spare bike to Matlock in case I needed it. Within a mile of where we stayed was a good old-fashioned bike shop. We told the man I had a problem with the brakes. He took one look at the bike and said ‘We can fix it’. I said ‘How long is it going to take?’ He replied: ‘Have you had your breakfast yet’, I said ‘No’ and he said ‘There’s a cafe down the road, have your breakfast, come back in half an hour and it will be done’. And it was.”

This enabled Richard to get back on the road to Derby where, arriving at the ground, he found a meeting of a dementia group in progress. They offered some very welcome tea and biscuits. This was one example of some very nice people he met on the way: “A farmer in Somerset was on a quad bike herding cattle down a narrow country lane. I hadn’t seen anyone for over an hour before encountering these cows. I was forced to stop down a track at the side of the road. The farmer asked if I was going far, I explained my ride, he admitted he knew nothing about cricket, but reached into his back pocket, pulling out his wallet and handing me a £20 note.”

Richard had some company him on a few of the rides: “My friend Derek rode from Darlington to Boroughbridge, then came back next day with another of our cycling friends, Margaret, to accompany me through Wetherby, Collingham and into North Leeds. Our old neighbour’s son, Alex, who was a good friend to Mikey, endured the route over to Manchester, and finally I was joined by my niece on the leg from Portsmouth to Worthing. All of this made those days go faster and be more enjoyable.”

Going from Wyke to Cheadle to visit Old Trafford over the Pennines was a hilly ride, but the toughest was when he left Worcester to cycle through Malvern then straight over the Malvern Hills, through Symonds Yat and on to Monmouth, and in Somerset over the Mendip Hills, his longest day in the saddle, cycling 55 miles to Taunton. “I like a view,” he reflects. “There’s nothing worse than a flat route as you’re pedalling all of the time. At least going up hills enables you to get a break on the way back down. The physical side, once you get going each day, wasn’t a problem, but mentally if I hadn’t been visiting grounds or had a target that day, getting up in the morning, especially after 12 days with another 12 to go, would have been very challenging. But once I had the gear on and got on the road, it was fine.”

It wasn’t all country lanes. Most of it was on designated cycle paths or cycle lanes alongside main roads. Even when his app took him away from main roads it was often round the back of an industrial estate or through some housing estates.

“Coming down a path out of a wooded area to find you are riding against the traffic at the side of the M25 was not the most enjoyable experience,” he says.

As for the favourite ground, Richard says: “The Utilita Bowl at Southampton. As the name suggests, it’s a big bowl with wonderful viewing opportunities.

It was the fourth day of a game against Sussex, I told the steward on the gate what I was doing. He said ‘I don’t know if I can let you in, there’s a game in progress’. Sussex were just about to start their second innings and the Hampshire supporters coming out said ‘Come on. Let him in. It’s nearly over’.

He rang his superior and a lady asked me to meet her on the concourse. I walked round and saw a picture of Shane Warne on the side of the Shane Warne Stand listing his achievements, with his motto, which resonated with me, ‘Just Keep On Going’. On my way out I was so pleased to have seen the ground I forgot to stop and take a picture of the sign.”

![]()Richard at his favourite ground, The Utilita Bowl at Southampton, home of Hampshire CCC.

Richard adds: “Physically I was strong and in a good state of mind when I finished. But it was a good three weeks before I realised the enormity of what I’d done. People were forever telling me what an achievement it was, but as I’d said leading up to the challenge, in my own mind I was just going for a bike ride in memory of my son.”

![]()On home turf: outside Headingley Stadium.

As the ride progressed, it also became as much about doing it to challenge himself and to prove that even at his age, 71, there are ways of improving your wellbeing.

So what’s next? “I have a couple of things in mind. I was going to do the Open Golf Championship courses, but there are long distances between those in the South East, North West and in Scotland and Northern Ireland. I’d like to compete the circle by riding along Route One, a national cycle route, from Chelmsford through Suffolk, Norfolk, Lincolnshire, up to Scarborough, Saltburn, Northumberland, then finish by returning to Chester-le Street. If I can raise a bit of money so be it. I’m hoping to help a couple of local children’s cancer charities.”
