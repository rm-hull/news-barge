The Metropolitan Police should investigate itself over the arrest of Nicholas Brandram, who the force suspected of being the so-called “Putney pusher”, the Independent Office for Police Conduct says.

Mr Brandram, 44, was found dead at his home earlier this month.

Police said at the time that the death was “unexpected”, and was not believed to be suspicious.

The banker had been under investigation for months over the crime, in which a jogger shoved a woman into the path of an oncoming bus on London’s Putney bridge in 2017.

He was arrested on suspicion of attempted grievous bodily harm in connection with the case in June this year and released under investigation in early September.

Mr Brandram’s family has insisted that he was innocent, and believe the former army captain took his own life after the Metropolitan Police failed to formally clear his name.

They said that he was living with “shame, anxiety and humiliation” as a result of the investigation.

“There was no forensic evidence linking Nick to it, no evidence placing him on Putney Bridge at that time, and no credible evidence that he was the man shown in the footage. In fact, the evidence proved the opposite,” they said.

“Despite that, the police chose to arrest Nick rather than invite him to attend for interview. They searched his home and the Met Police press team then released information which inevitably led to speculation and Nick being identified in subsequent reporting.”

They said that the police were repeatedly asked to end their investigation and were warned about the impact the investigation was having on Mr Brandram’s mental health.

Chief Superintendent Dan Knowles said earlier: “Our thoughts are with Mr Brandram’s family and friends at this deeply distressing time. We recognise the significant impact his death will have on those who knew and loved him. We also understand the strain that being subject to a police investigation can place on individuals and their families.”

The force said that after Mr Brandram was released under investigation, officers’ enquiries into the offence continued.

Chf Supt Knowles said: “This remains the position, and further investigative work is ongoing. Investigations of this nature, particularly those involving complex evidential and forensic work, can take a number of months to complete as officers gather and assess all available evidence.”
