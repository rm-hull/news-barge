In Wooler, the countryside begins where the town ends.

This stone-built Northumberland market town sits at the Cheviot Hills’ foot, with some of the North East’s wildest landscapes beyond its streets.

Known as the Cheviots’ gateway, Wooler offers remote countryside without sacrificing a proper town centre.

Its High Street has traditional shops, pubs and cafes, while the surrounding hills offer walks, cycling, wildlife and sweeping Northumberland views.

Visit Northumberland calls Wooler a middle-of-nowhere escape with a market-town buzz, making it a useful base for exploring the county.

## A town at the foot of the Cheviots

Wooler's setting is difficult to ignore.

Sitting on the edge of Northumberland National Park, the town provides access to the valleys, hills and remote walking country of the Cheviots without requiring visitors to stay miles from shops and places to eat.

Northumberland National Park will mark its 70th anniversary in 2026 with walks, events and exhibitions throughout the year.

For something closer to town, Wooler Common provides another opportunity to get outdoors.

The peaceful landscape has a turbulent past: Wooler and the surrounding countryside suffered repeated fighting and raids from across the Scottish border, later including attacks by the Border Reivers.

Today, the area is considerably calmer.

Walking trails quickly take you from Wooler’s shops and houses into woodland and open countryside.

## Anglo-Saxon kings and Northumberland whisky

Wooler has also gained a major attraction of its own in recent years.

Ad Gefrin, on South Road, brings together an Anglo-Saxon museum and a working whisky distillery in one striking modern building.

The attraction tells the story of the seventh-century royal palace discovered at nearby Yeavering, around four miles from Wooler, which was once associated with the kings and queens of Anglo-Saxon Northumbria.

Alongside the history is a modern distillery producing Northumbrian English single malt whisky, while the building also contains a bistro and shop showcasing local produce and makers.

The unusual combination gives Wooler ancient royal history and locally distilled whisky under one roof.

## A proper market town

Wooler’s appeal is that it does not feel built for tourists.

Independent businesses, cafes, pubs and shops give the centre the feel of a working rural community.

The weekly market runs on Mondays, while the annual Glendale Show has featured in the area’s calendar for more than a century.

Traditional pubs and cafes serve food, while Ad Gefrin’s Bodern restaurant focuses on seasonal Northumberland produce.


You can spend a slow morning browsing Wooler before swapping coffee and shops for walking boots.

## Coast and countryside

Its location also provides another advantage.

Bamburgh is around 16 miles away, a 30-minute drive, so visitors can combine the hills with Northumberland’s famous coastline.

Alnwick, Berwick and the Scottish border are all within easy reach.

Yet the landscape west of Wooler can feel a world away from Northumberland's busier coastal destinations.

At night, that becomes even more apparent.

Northumberland International Dark Sky Park covers a huge area of the surrounding countryside, while Kirknewton, beyond Wooler, has a designated Dark Sky Discovery Site.

Visit in autumn or winter, and the experience continues long after the shops shut.

Wooler lacks Bamburgh’s castle skyline and Alnwick’s global fame, but that is part of its appeal.
