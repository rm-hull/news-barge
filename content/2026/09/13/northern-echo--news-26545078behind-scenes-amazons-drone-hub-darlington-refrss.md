Can it fly in the rain? What packages can it deliver? Will it drop a box on my dog?

It’s not just planes and helicopters taking to the skies above Darlington these days - the future has arrived in the town with the launch of Amazon’s state-of-the-art drone fleet.

Residents can now look up to the sky and see ‘hexacopters’ hovering and unloading parcels into gardens in scenes that, just years ago, would have felt at home in a Black Mirror episode.

But the tech giant has made drone deliveries a reality after selecting the 465,000-square-foot fulfilment centre at Symmetry Park as its first drone hub across the whole of Europe.

Since February, hundreds of packages have been delivered to residents by air, and, after months of trials, the firm is now awaiting the green-light to launch its full commercial service.

  How Amazon is operating its drone fleet from Darlington 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

But what actually goes on behind the drone ‘paddock’ doors - and how does this astonishing new era of technology actually work?

When Amazon first announced Darlington as the location it would be launching a drone delivery service early last year, it naturally sparked a lot of intrigue.

To many, the concept of same day delivery still impresses, never mind being able to order an item and have it in your hands in as quickly as 39 minutes.

It all starts on an inconspicuous large concrete slab outside of Amazon’s Darlington building.

The paddock, as it’s called by 'Amazonians', is where the packages are loaded into the MK30 drones before taking to the skies.

  Inside the drone paddock 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

The drones, of which there are five, are fitted with six rotors and take off vertically before switching to a horizontal flight.

But before that, the team, made up of 13 people, ensures the drone is safe to fly, carrying out a pre-flight inspection and installing a charged battery.

  The drone batteries 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

The batteries on the drones are always sent out with a 100 per cent charge, and the drone will calculate that it has enough juice to get to the customer and back to base.

If it is below 97 per cent, it does not take off.

Once the propellers, struts, and motors are checked and a 360 degree run of the aircraft is carried out, the package is scanned and the delivery address transferred to the drone.

  A package being loaded into a drone 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

The shoebox-sized package, which must weigh less than five pounds, is then loaded into the underside of the aircraft and the door is closed manually.

It can carry most items within the size limit, excluding breakables.

The drone, which has a maximum take off weight of 37.5 kilos, is then wheeled out to the launch pad and takes flight.

  The launch pad 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

It flies autonomously to the customer's property at speeds of up to 70mph - with each flight normally lasting around 13 minutes - and is quieter than an average delivery van.

Once the drone has arrived at the customer's property, it begins its descent while identifying and steering clear of obstacles with its detect-and-avoid technology.


When checking out a product, customers will see a satellite map of their home, which is pre-identified using Amazon’s digital mapping and AI systems.

The customer can then select one of these drop-off zones and will be reminded to keep the area clear.

Cameras are also monitoring the surrounding airspace and can navigate around unexpected aircraft. The drone then drops the parcel from a safe height.

Prime Air boxes also have"customized cushioning technology" at the bottom to protect the item during delivery.

Once the parcel is dropped, it then returns to the Darlington warehouse.

## 
  **Come rain or shine?** 

It is fair to say that British weather is hugely unpredictable.

But what does that mean for Amazon drones? Would gusty winds or heavy rain stop it from flying, how does it know, and what happens if the weather changes after you order?

The MK30 can fly safely in light rain and a range of temperatures and wind speeds, but, as you can imagine, it does have a limit.

  How Amazon is operating its drone fleet from Darlington 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

15.5 knots wind speed and 2.5mm of rain per hour is the cut off point for the drones, with teams regularly monitoring this in advance before giving the green light.

If conditions are too unsafe, Prime Air won't be available as a shipping option.

But what happens if you are able to order an item via Prime Air but the weather suddenly changes. Well, Amazon has a backup plan.

A van is on standby if the drone can’t fly, so the package is simply loaded into that.

In Darlington, the firm’s commitment is to deliver within two hours, but currently averages at 90 minutes. It’s quickest flight so far is just 39 minutes from purchase to home.

  How Amazon is operating its drone fleet from Darlington 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

And if your parcel is loaded into the contingency plan van, the two-hour delivery promise is kept.

There can be issues beyond weather, as residents may have already seen during the trial period.

  In recent months, a drone __mistakenly dropped a parcel into a Darlington grandad's garden__. Weeks later, another drone crashed into a garden while out on deliveries. 

Prime Air Vice President David Carbon told the Echo that this can happen due to out of date property lines on maps, and can be corrected and adjusted if identified.

  How Amazon is operating its drone fleet from Darlington 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

That said, no single point of failure should ever cause a drone to lose control, with a network of cameras and sensors allowing it to detect objects.

And what about the cost?

At the moment, Prime Air delivery is included as part of an Amazon Prime membership with no additional delivery fee for members.

But as it is rolled out to more areas, Amazon says it will “evaluate delivery fees consistent with other Amazon premium speed services”.
