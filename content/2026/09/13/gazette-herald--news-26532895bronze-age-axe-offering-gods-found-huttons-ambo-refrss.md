An ancient Bronze Age axe believed to have been offered to the gods has been unearthed.

It was found during an archaeological dig in Huttons Ambo, a village in North Yorkshire, as part of a broader excavation that also revealed medieval buildings and evidence of Second World War military activity.

Ethos Heritage CIC, working alongside the Huttons Ambo Local History Group, led the excavation.

The dig attracted more than 70 participants from across the UK, Europe, the Americas, Australia, and the local community.

The group included military veterans, members of the emergency services, and prison officers.

One of the standout finds was a large medieval stone building measuring about four metres by three metres.

The structure contained floor surfaces, walls, and related features that suggest it was of high status and potentially used for agricultural or administrative purposes.

Excavators recovered more than five kilograms of 12th to 13th-century pottery and butchered animal bones from the site, indicating long-term use.

Nearby, a second trench revealed 12th-century pits containing locally made pottery.

While their purpose remains unclear, possibilities include storage, waste disposal, or small-scale industrial use.

The discoveries reinforce the idea of a well-organised and active medieval community.

The dig also uncovered a new layer of the site’s history—Second World War artefacts dating from 1943 to 1944.

These items suggest Huttons Ambo served as a base for Allied troops, including Free French, Polish, American, and British forces, as they prepared for the invasion of France.

Recovered items include belt buckles, cap badges, uniform buttons, and equipment fittings, all pointing to the village’s temporary military occupation.

The Bronze Age axe, dating from 1500 to 1000 BC, is believed to have been used for ritual purposes rather than practical tasks.

Its damaged condition and placement indicate it was a deliberate offering to the gods, part of a broader tradition of placing valuable objects in the landscape for spiritual reasons.

Such offerings often took place at meaningful natural sites like river crossings, boundaries, or prominent features in the landscape.

The discovery suggests Huttons Ambo may have held ritual significance long before it became a medieval settlement.

Other finds include a Viking-era weight, Roman pottery, and various 20th-century artefacts.

The findings highlight Huttons Ambo as a landscape shaped by thousands of years of human activity.

The project is rooted in community engagement, with international volunteers, students, and archaeologists working together on site.

A participant named Alyson from West Virginia said the experience was deeply meaningful.

Alyson said: "Taking part in the archaeology at Huttons Ambo was an amazing experience.

"From the moment I arrived, the villagers made me feel completely at home, welcoming me into their community, sharing their stories, and showing such genuine interest in the excavation.

"Working alongside them while uncovering the village’s past was something truly special, and it’s an experience I’ll never forget."

Site director Rianca Vogles said the project is a model for community-led archaeology.

Ms Vogles said: "As site director for the excavations, I cannot emphasise enough how important it is for villages like Huttons Ambo to have the chance to explore their own archaeology and heritage.

"This work doesn’t just uncover the past, it strengthens community identity, pride, and understanding.

"What has made this season truly exceptional is the way the villagers have embraced the project and welcomed more than 70 participants from around the world into their community.

"Their enthusiasm, hospitality, and curiosity have created a unique atmosphere where global expertise meets local heritage, and the village benefits from both.

"It’s exactly the kind of partnership that brings archaeology to life."

Post-excavation analysis is now underway, with a focus on medieval pits, pottery, animal bone, the stone structure, and the Second World War artefacts.

Findings will be shared through public talks, digital updates, and community events.

Planning for the next season of excavation in 2027 is already underway.
