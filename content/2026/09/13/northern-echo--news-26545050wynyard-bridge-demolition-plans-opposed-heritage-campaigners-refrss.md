Campaigners have accused Stockton Council of “incompetence or scaremongering” and wasting money by planning to demolish two landmark bridges.

The council plans to demolish the Crawford’s and Black Squares bridges over the Castle Eden Walkway in Wynyard this autumn.

The council says the bridges are “redundant”, “no longer serve any functional purpose”, could shed masonry and risk collapse because of worsening cracks and movement.

  Cracks in the Black Squares bridge in Wynyard, across the Castle Eden Walkway. Picture: The HRE Group.


It argues demolition is “the most practical and responsible course of action” due to public safety concerns.

But the HRE (Historical Railways Estate) Group disagrees and wants the council to rethink the plan.

The group, which includes civil engineers, greenway developers and heritage campaigners, has accused the council of “exaggerating and misrepresenting” the risks associated with the two historic railway bridges, wasting public funds and closing a popular greenway for two weeks.

It says: “The intervention is unjustified from either a safety or engineering perspective, and represents an appalling waste of money.”

The group insists the defects pose no meaningful risk to public safety and says most of Crawford’s Bridge’s unsightly damage dates from 1963 or earlier.

  Crawford’s Bridge, a landmark on the Castle Eden Walkway in Wynyard. Picture: The HRE Group.


The group describes the bridges as prominent landmarks on the well-used Castle Eden Walkway. The North Eastern Railway built the 13-mile line through County Durham, which opened in 1878 to carry mineral traffic to Teesside.

Group member Graeme Bickerdyke said: “The council is guilty of exaggerating and misrepresenting the threat presented by the bridges. Masonry structures are extremely durable and surprisingly flexible.

“The cracks affect the wingwalls rather than the span over the walkway. There is no mechanism that could trigger a collapse and no loose brickwork that could fall onto the path.

“Anecdotal evidence indicates that increasingly wet winters and dry summers are resulting in more structures suffering defects associated with ground movement, as the material beneath their foundations shrinks and swells in response to the changing conditions. But that doesn’t justify demolition.

“At worst, these bridges need routine repairs. Neither of them carries any traffic so they don’t require significant load-bearing capacity. The council’s case smacks of incompetence or scaremongering.”

  Cracks in the Crawford's Bridge in Wynyard, on the Castle Eden Walkway. Picture: The HRE Group.


The group says social media users reacted to the demolition plan with sadness, frustration and anger, accusing the council of neglecting the bridges.

It says both structures are recorded as assets on the local Historic Environment Record which imposes “important material considerations” on decision-makers.

It has urged the council to review its decision to demolish the structures and publish evidence of surveys to show no bat roosts in the bridges. It says unauthorised destruction of these would be a criminal offence.

The group also says it has applied to Historic England to have the two bridges listed, protecting them from imminent harmful development.

It says it recently inspected the bridges and wrote to Cllr Nigel Cooke, Stockton Council’s cabinet member for environment, leisure and culture, explaining their concerns about demolition.

Demolition is expected to take up to two weeks, during which the Castle Eden Walkway will be closed to the public.

  Councillor Nigel Cooke, cabinet member for environment, leisure and culture at Stockton Council. Picture: LDR/suitable for all partners.


Cllr Cooke replied: “While we appreciate these two bridges are familiar landmarks for many people who enjoy the Castle Eden Walkway, we have to put the public’s safety first. These structures are not listed and are not historical railways estate bridges.

“The bridges are now old and have long outlived their original purpose. Due to the nature and severity of the failures we know that a repair option would effectively require a full rebuild of the defective areas to make them safe again.

“The cost to demolish the bridges was significantly lower than the estimated cost of repairing and maintaining the structures.

"This would far exceed the available budget, and does not include any additional costs associated with repeat failures and any further call‑outs for repair and maintenance.”
