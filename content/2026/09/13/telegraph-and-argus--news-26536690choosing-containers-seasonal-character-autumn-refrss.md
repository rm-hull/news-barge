After another record-breaking summer, gardeners across the UK are becoming all too familiar with the pattern: wet winters, followed by increasingly warm, dry spells through summer. By the time September arrives, the cooler nights feel like a kindness, offering both plants and gardeners a welcome pause. It is also the moment when the garden begins to change character. The light softens, warm colours glow through the borders and autumn starts to bring its own quieter beauty.

  **Bringing autumn closer to home**

If I am honest, autumn has often been neglected in my own garden. Having spent 22 years of my working life in RHS gardens,

I was fortunate enough to enjoy the season on a grand, panoramic scale. This year, however, I am looking closer to home and turning to containers as a way of bringing that fading, mellow beauty into everyday view.

  **Choosing containers with seasonal character**

The container itself is an important part of the display, especially at this time of year. Four materials work particularly well with autumn colour: corten steel or rusty metal, zinc, woven basket-ware and, of course, terracotta. In the height of summer, I tend to avoid planting directly into metal containers, as they can heat up fiercely around the roots. In autumn and winter, however, zinc and corten steel come into their own, lending a crisp, contemporary edge to seasonal planting.

  **Easy seasonal swaps**

One lesson I have learned is that container planting can be surprisingly labour-intensive. My preferred shortcut is to plant into plastic pots first, then sink these into larger planters and pack around them with composted bark. It is a simple trick, but it makes seasonal changeovers far easier – allowing your displays to be refreshed without disturbing the whole arrangement.

  **Planting for metal, basket-ware and terracotta**

For metal containers, contemporary planting feels especially effective. Grasses, coleus, dark-foliage dahlias and trailing creeping Jenny can be combined to create a display that is dramatic, textural and long-lasting. The contrast between strong foliage and cool metal gives the whole arrangement a confident, modern feel.

Woven basket-ware sits beautifully between contemporary and traditional styles. I like to use white bedding cyclamen, corokia, variegated ivy and saffron crocus bulbs, along with the vibrant varieties of leucothoe that appear in garden centres at this time of year. Basket-ware is also excellent for window troughs, being strong, lightweight and naturally suited to informal seasonal displays.

Terracotta pots, meanwhile, bring a softer, more nostalgic quality. Bedding chrysanthemums, golden grasses and copper-coloured heucheras all sit happily in clay pots, offering a gentle nod to bygone days and a slower, less hectic pace of life.

  **A display to carry the garden into winter**

Whether you lean towards the clean lines of metal, the relaxed charm of basket-ware or the timeless warmth of terracotta, autumn containers are a simple way to keep the garden feeling alive as the season turns.

With the right planting, using containers will bring colour, texture, and style well beyond September and on into the depths of winter.

  **PLANTS OF THE MONTHPerennial Asters**

Michaelmas daisies evoke in me a feeling of nostalgia. Every autumn, my grandmother’s garden was abuzz with bees visiting these September staples of the great British gardens. Avoided by some in the past, for their tendency to get mildew, but newer varieties are much more resistant. Look out for the frikartii varieties.

  **QUESTION TIMEWhen is it the best time to plant apple and other fruit trees?**

November through to the end of February is the traditional time to plant trees. I favour November when the soil is still warm. For expert advice for growing fruit in Yorkshire check out the northern fruit group website, thenorthernfruitgroup.com

  **I’m finding peat-free compost doesn’t perform as well as the composts that I used to buy. Are there any suggestions to improve this?**

Peat-free composts are now the norm, as our depletion of peat bogs was not sustainable. Peat-free compost has not got the water or feed retention that compost used to have. So, my suggestion is for every three 50-litre bags you buy, treat yourself to a bag of topsoil and divide this into the three to give your compost ‘guts’. It makes a huge difference to how well your compost will support your plants.
