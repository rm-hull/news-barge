AS the long summer holidays draw to a close, families across the region are turning their attention to going back to school. Whether your children are moving up to big school or just changing classes, it can sometimes be a tricky time for young people to handle.

Whilst for many the start of a new term can be exciting - with buying new pencil cases and bags or getting used to new uniforms, it can also be a time of uncertainty and anxiety as children head into uncharted territory.

The NSPCC is very aware of how difficult this period can be for young people, thanks to our dedicated Childline teams across the UK who support young people with this topic around the clock.

Last year, more than 1,500 Childline counselling sessions with young people across the country included mentions of returning to school or college and, in 35per cent of those sessions, children’s emotional wellbeing was the primary concern, making it the most common issue discussed.

One girl, aged 13, who contacted Childline, told counsellors: “I’m worried about going back to school. I’ve been bullied over the summer, like being sent mean messages online. I have been finding it hard to sleep as I have anxiety about the bullying.

“My parents know what is happening and are supportive. I feel a bit better for talking it through. I’m going to try to get some sleep now.”

Our Childline practitioners and website are available around the clock to offer support and advice to young people, no matter what they’re going through.

But there are also some good things parents and carers can do to help children starting or returning to school.

We have put together some advice which can help children, parents and carers to feel more at ease when returning to school or even going to school for the first time.

The first day of term or starting at a new school can be daunting for children so it’s a good idea to talk to children about the school day and what they can expect.

Try to get them used to the new routine, including when they need to be up and dressed, when to set off to school, and when they will get playtime and lunchtime breaks at school.

Also, make sure they know who is collecting them from school and from where.

Encouraging them to write down their feelings can also be helpful - whether children are excited or worried about anything, writing down their feelings and chatting through their concerns can help to ease their worries and gives you a chance to remind them about the positive aspects of their new term.

Try setting a good bedtime routine, keep any devices out of bedrooms so they can get a good night’s sleep and help them pack their school bag ready for the next day. If they have uniform, preparing it the night before can help them feel more at ease.

Once children have returned to school it’s also important to make time each day for things that they enjoy as this can help with any anxiety.

Playing outside or joining in with sport are great ways to support their wellbeing and keep a healthy routine, while going for a walk with them offers a great opportunity to find out more about how their day went.

Remember, it can take time to settle into a new school or new routine.

If you notice any changes in your child’s behaviour or you’re feeling worried, then speak to their teacher and find ways in which you can support them.

The NSPCC has teamed up with Matalan for its Back to School campaign which aims to help families feel ready, supported and confident as children and young people head back to the classroom.

Recently, one of our Childline practitioners was a guest on the latest episode of the Happy Mum podcast as part of the partnership where they chatted with presenter Giovanna Fletcher about children’s confidence, emotional readiness and practical ways to navigate the back to school transition.
