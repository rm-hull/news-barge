A new season of storms is on the horizon, and this year’s names include tributes to pets, meteorological pioneers and even a five-year-old weather enthusiast.

The UK’s Met Office has revealed the storm names for 2026/27, selected from more than 40,000 public suggestions.

The scheme is run in partnership with Met Eireann in Ireland and KNMI in the Netherlands, and the list starts each year in September to reflect the beginning of the autumn storm season.

This storm season includes names such as Austen, Boelo and Chloe and each has a story behind its nomination.

## Staying safe during a thunderstorm


Names were submitted in honour of children, pets and even characters like Shaun the Sheep, as well as in memory of loved ones and meteorological trailblazers.

Under naming conventions used for storms in the North Atlantic, the list runs alphabetically, missing out Q, U, X, Y and Z.

Chloe, the third name on the list this year, was nominated more than 120 times.

One suggestion came from the mother of a five-year-old described as being “fascinated by the weather” who can also “create a storm by herself” when tired.

Other names have similarly personal backstories.

Finn was put forward for a two-year-old described as “wild and a force to be reckoned with”, while Ivy was nominated in honour of a nine-month-old Labrador puppy affectionately called “the absolute destructor”.

Nicola was suggested as a tribute to a mother who has “weathered many a storm and only come out stronger”.

Some names reflect a wider cultural reach.

Shaun, selected following a public vote, was included as part of a collaboration with Aardman, the studio behind Wallace and Gromit, to help raise awareness of storm preparedness.

The list also honours figures in meteorological history.

Met Eireann chose Austen in tribute to Austen Nagle, the first director of the Irish Meteorological Service as it marks its 90th anniversary.

Evelyn was selected in honour of Evelyn Murphy, the first woman to present weather forecasts on Ireland’s national broadcaster, RTE.

## Why are storms named?

The scheme began in 2015 and aims to improve public awareness and response in the face of severe weather.

Last year, four names were used from the list, reaching Storm Dave in April.

The UK was also affected by three additional storms named by other European meteorological services: Benjamin, Claudia and Goretti.

The Met Office said names are reviewed for pronunciation, potential meanings across different languages, connections to public figures and any risk of controversy.


## When is a storm named?

A storm is named when it's expected to cause medium or high impacts in the UK, Ireland or the Netherlands.

Wind is the primary consideration, but significant effects from rain or snow are also taken into account.

Holly Clements, Met Office head of warnings and guidance, said: “For more than a decade, storm naming has helped us communicate the risks from severe weather more effectively.

“A named storm provides a clear focus for forecasts and warnings, helping more people understand the potential impacts and take action to protect themselves, their families and their property.”

Eoin Sherlock, Met Eireann head of forecasting division, said: “We know that naming storms makes it easier for people to track important and up-to-date forecast and safety-related information as we can see from the levels of traffic and activity through our website and across social media platforms during storm events.
