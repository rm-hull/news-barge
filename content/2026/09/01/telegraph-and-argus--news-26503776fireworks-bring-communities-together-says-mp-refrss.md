**This week's MP column is written by Bradford South MP Judith Cummins, who feels fireworks should bring communities together - not make people feel under siege.**

There is something wonderful about fireworks.

For many of us they evoke happy memories: Bonfire Night, New Year, Diwali, Eid and other celebrations.

I have my own memories of fireworks from childhood, and I understand why people enjoy them.

But there is a simple point we should not lose sight of: enjoying fireworks should not mean inflicting misery on other people.

Fireworks are no longer limited to a few predictable evenings each year.

They are increasingly being set off late at night, early in the morning and at all times of the year.

During the recent period of good weather, people’s peace and enjoyment of the outdoors has been disrupted by the inconsiderate actions of a few.

Families are being woken from their sleep, children frightened, older people distressed and pets sent into a panic.

For people with sensory sensitivities, sudden and explosive noises can be overwhelming. For some veterans, fireworks can also trigger deeply traumatic memories.

At a recent local community event, I spoke to a senior firefighter who told me that crews had just attended two fires in Bradford South — both of which had been started by fireworks.

This was a timely reminder that this is not simply an issue of noise or inconvenience.

Fireworks can have serious consequences for our communities, our emergency services and our environment.

  Bradford South MP Judith Cummins (right) with Halifax MP Kate Dearden 

*(Image: Judith Cummins)*

Many of you have told me that we need sensible, proportionate reform to tackle the misuse of fireworks while allowing people to continue enjoying legitimate celebrations. I agree.

In April 2024, I introduced my Fireworks (Noise Limits) Bill in Parliament, seeking to reduce the maximum noise level of fireworks sold to the public.

The principle was straightforward: people should still be able to enjoy fireworks, but the products available to consumers should not be so loud that they cause unnecessary distress and disruption.

The Bill sought to reduce the current legal sound limit for fireworks for general use from 120 decibels — comparable to the noise of a pneumatic drill or chainsaw — to 90 decibels, roughly the level of a ringing telephone.

That would still allow people to enjoy the colour, spectacle and excitement of fireworks, including in their own gardens, while tackling excessive noise that can cause so much distress.

Government has now launched a consultation on reforms to fireworks legislation, focused on noise and their contribution to antisocial behaviour. This consultation could make a real difference.

We should be mindful also that fireworks can be and have been misused as weapons.

Emergency workers have been targeted with them while doing their jobs.

On Bonfire Night a few years ago, I saw first-hand the risks they can face when we came under attack while out on a patrol with firefighters and police officers.

Emergency services should never have to worry about being attacked with fireworks while responding to incidents or protecting our communities.

There have also been tragic cases where fireworks have caused devastating injuries and loss of life.

None of this means that everyone who buys a box of fireworks is behaving irresponsibly. The vast majority are not.

That is precisely why I believe we should focus on tackling the minority whose behaviour causes harm, while making sensible changes to the products available to the public.

I have also presented a petition to the Government on fireworks and have met with Ministers to discuss the concerns raised by my constituents.

I will again be meeting the Minister in Bradford to discuss the Government's current consultation and ensure that the serious concerns I have heard locally are properly represented.

The new consultation is an important opportunity to look at whether the current rules are working and what further action may be needed.

I want to encourage everyone who has been affected by fireworks to make their views heard through the consultation.

The consultation can be found on the Government's website. [https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/fireworks-and-pyrotechnics-in-the-uk/fireworks-and-pyrotechnics-in-the-uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/fireworks-and-pyrotechnics-in-the-uk/fireworks-and-pyrotechnics-in-the-uk)

I will continue to press the Minister to look seriously at a package of measures: lowering noise limits for fireworks sold to the public; taking stronger action against illegal and antisocial use; and improving enforcement against rogue sellers

Let me be clear: I am not looking to take the fun out of celebrations. I am looking to make them fairer.

A professionally organised display can be spectacular. It can bring a community together, support local events and give people advance notice of when fireworks will begin.

For someone whose child struggles with sudden noises, whose dog becomes terrified, or whose memories are triggered by explosive sounds, that notice can make an enormous difference.

We should be able to celebrate without making our neighbours suffer.

Fireworks should light up the night sky — not leave families dreading the next bang.
