A hillside villa that served as a cultural hub for German writers and artists fleeing the Nazis and narrowly escaped destruction in last year’s Los Angeles wildfires reopened its doors to the public this weekend, marking the latest milestone in the city’s slow recovery from one of the darkest chapters in its history.

Hundreds of friends of the Villa Aurora, a 1920s Spanish Mediterranean house that has taken on a second life as a cultural center with a fierce commitment to artistic freedom, international dialogue and democracy, crowded into the house on Sunday to mark the resumption of speaker events and artistic residencies.

They applauded the return of several art works rescued while the hillsides were still ablaze in January 2025, including an 18th-century Hebrew scroll of the Book of Esther and several busts and paintings, and they applauded again for city firefighters who managed to stop the flames right at the property edge.

![people standing by a damaged house](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/4a3a6dee182d8dfdcffcef49ac1d95e244d34c9f/0_0_2432_1824/master/2432.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

“Villa Aurora is returning at a time when solidarity, dialogue and mutual support within the community are of particular importance,” the villa’s director, Claudia Gordon said. She recognized the thousands of neighbors in Pacific Palisades who lost their homes. And she acknowledged the continuing impact of the fires, as victims and city officials complain of inadequate insurance payouts and the Trump administration’s refusal to release about $15bn in federal aid.

To anti-Nazi German exiles including Thomas Mann, Bertolt Brecht and Lion Feuchtwanger, the novelist who purchased the Villa Aurora in 1943 and turned it into a cultural gathering point, Pacific Palisades became known as “Weimar-by-the-Sea”. The exiles were outspoken in their opposition to Hitler’s regime and, later, to the anti-communist witch-hunts in the United States that affected many of them directly.

Although nobody at the reopening mentioned Donald Trump’s name, both Gordon and the leadership of the nearby Mann house – which is also now a cultural center and also narrowly survived the fires – emphasized the importance of the villa as a symbol of refuge, freedom of the arts, freedom of speech and defense of democracy.

![outside of a building](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/ccb602a89813098679af87f4a0610c78d6d1afa6/0_0_4624_3472/master/4624.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

The burn scars are still plainly visible from Villa Aurora’s balcony. In the garden, now being rebuilt and replanted, the scorched stump of a eucalyptus tree indicates how close the flames came – and how unpredictable the pattern of fire damage proved to be. Several neighboring houses were destroyed outright.

Nineteen months on, the heart of Pacific Palisades is one large construction site, with some houses already reconstructed and reoccupied and others still being rebuilt. The Palisades Village, a shopping center belonging to former mayoral candidate Rick Caruso, reopened two weeks ago, with some tenants from before the fires returning and other local businesses whose buildings did not survive moving in. Other restaurants and businesses have announced that they too will reopen shortly – in many cases in different Palisades locations from the ones they occupied before.

![houses and trees](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/dd29f2a10a8c0e188dda05550460424b655552eb/0_0_5712_4284/master/5712.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

However, roughly three-quarters of Palisades fire survivors were still displaced as of late July, according to a report by the nonprofit Department of Angels, with many of them struggling with debt and inadequate insurance payouts even as sources of financial assistance have fallen away. In Altadena, where the Eaton fire ravaged a community in the inland foothills of the San Gabriel mountains, about two-thirds of fire survivors are yet to return home.

City officials and other experts expect full recovery to take as long as five years.

Villa Aurora’s own survival relied on support from many others including the German government and other cultural institutions in Los Angeles. In the days after the fire, staff of the Wende Museum, which curates artifacts from dissident movements including anti-communists in cold-war era Germany and Poland, won permission to cross police lines to rescue art work and verify whether the building was still intact.

![People hold up portrait](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/c6c05c7946e63789389a47e32d8321b407126e32/0_0_5100_3400/master/5100.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

Christine Rank, the Wende museum’s director of collections, described how she and a small team were given just an hour to gather what they could. “We brought boxes and blankets, but we also ended up using sheets and trash cans, whatever we could find to transport the material,” she said.

Among the artifacts was a portrait of Feuchtwanger’s wife Marta, whose unveiling was the centerpiece of Sunday’s reopening. Danijel Skrelja from the German consul general’s office in LA exclaimed: “This house is coming alive.”
