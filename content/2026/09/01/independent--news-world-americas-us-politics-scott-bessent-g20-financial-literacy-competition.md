Drawing on his upbringing in childhood poverty, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent is advocating for an international financial literacy campaign and encouraging other countries to embrace an investment initiative launched under President Donald Trump.

Speaking on Tuesday while hosting finance ministers from the G20 block, Bessent unveiled American backing for a contest named the "Financial Literacy Solutions Sprint." The challenge seeks to identify and highlight "innovative technology- and data-enabled solutions" created within the Group of 20 member states to boost financial understanding.

A group of four finalists will be narrowed down to one or two victors, who will be honored in Bangkok this October during the annual gatherings of the International Monetary Fund and World Bank.

Bessent also highlighted Trump Accounts as a template for other countries. The initiative deposits $1,000 into investment accounts for infants born throughout Trump's second term. According to the Treasury, about 7 million infants are signed up, with 86% coming from households bringing in under $200,000 annually.

The Treasury secretary frequently cites his modest youth in rural South Carolina, where he began working at nine years old clearing cafeteria tables and arranging beach equipment. His father, a property developer, depleted family assets through excessive financial leverage.

Early in his tenure as Treasury secretary, Bessent established Financial Literacy Month to educate the public on managing budgets, long-term saving, and avoiding scams and fraud. However, critics argue the core difficulty stems from a lack of disposable income rather than financial ignorance.

July survey data from *The Associated Press*-NORC Center for Public Affairs Research indicates around 7 in 10 American adults describe the nation's economic conditions as "poor," reflecting ongoing dissatisfaction with everyday expenses exacerbated by elevated fuel costs linked to the conflict in Iran.

"Americans can’t educate themselves out of their current financial crises, and it’s hypocritical for the Treasury Secretary to place the blame on ordinary Americans’ financial decisions when their struggles are the direct result of decisions that Scott Bessent himself has made," said Julie Morgan, chief executive of the left-leaning policy research group the Century Foundation.
