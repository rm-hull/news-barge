## Activity books for adults are booming, mixing puzzles, pop culture and mysteries. Their success, led by Murdoku, shows a hunger for screen-free fun and a playful workout for the mind.

**Adult activity books**, blending puzzles with pop culture and mysteries to solve, have become a publishing phenomenon. Murdoku, with more than 140,000 copies sold in Spain, leads a booming genre that many associate with digital detox.

**It used to be the newsstand crossword**, the one you took on the metro or into the dentist’s waiting room.** Now it is a purple-covered notebook** with the outline of a house drawn as a sudoku grid, or a hardback packed with questions about Rosalía and Netflix series.

Adult activity books, the latest mutation of the classic pastime, have become a publishing phenomenon **that is no longer confined to summer**, and within it there is a subgenre that is eating up all the rest:** solving a murder armed with a pencil, an eraser and a sudoku grid**.

The trend began a decade ago with **colouring books aimed at adults**, sold under labels such as art therapy or anti-stress, featuring mandalas, flowers and landscapes. What looked like a summer fad took root, and from there a more ambitious version emerged:** the adult activity book**, an update of the traditional newsstand puzzle book (crosswords, word searches, sudokus) with an extra ingredient that sets it apart: popular culture.

The arrival of the mobile phone did not kill off the analogue genre; **instead, the two now coexist**, just as YouTube cookery channels have not sunk recipe books, or television did not kill radio.

## Blackie Books and its distinctive activity books

Blackie Books was among the first to spot the potential. **Its adult activity books are already on their 14th volume**, one per summer, and have passed 250,000 copies sold, according to the publisher’s own figures.** The latest offers 60 exercises on music**, film, TV series, literature, science and sport, designed to "de-rust the mind".

Success has led the publisher to **branch out the concept**: the "Cuaderno Kids", for children from the age of six; the "Cuaderno Golden", aimed at older readers with memory challenges and questions about well-known faces from years gone by; and the "Quadern", its Catalan-language version, with local touchstones such as Mercè Rodoreda or the castellers.

**Other imprints have carved out their own niche** without resorting to crime: Debate has launched an activity book signed by "El Mundo Today" that tests the ability to distinguish reality from fiction, and GeoPlaneta is already on the fifth volume of a travel activity book,**complete with playlists and gastronomic guides.**

## Murdoku, the hit that has fuelled the craze for solving crimes

If one single book sums up the season, it is Murdoku. **The notebook**, with its unmistakable purple cover,** has already sold more than 140,000 copies in Spain**, and this year it has flown off tables at several spring book fairs. Its mechanics reinvent the classic sudoku: each puzzle places a group of suspects in the squares of a grid, without repeating any of them in a given row or column, and** the square that remains empty shows** where the victim is; the suspect whose position matches that square is the killer.

**The difficulty increases as the book progresses**, to the point that some bookshops are now organising live solving competitions. Behind it is Canadian Manuel Garand, a puzzle designer based in Montreal whose work has also been published in the "Financial Times".

**Such is the success the book has enjoyed**(and that of those trying to imitate it) that other people have even set up a website with the same concept where you can tackle Murdokus for free.** At the time of writing there are 35 puzzles** to complete, and that is with the site still in development.

## Murdle, Agatha Christie and the rest of the crime genre

**The other big name in the genre is Murdle**. "Resuelve el crimen" (published in Spain, like "Murdoku", by Temas de Hoy),** uses a different mechanism**: instead of a sudoku grid, it offers narrative clues about suspects, weapons and settings that you have to cross-reference to point to the culprit.

**It already runs to three volumes** and a children’s edition; its creator, the American G.T. Karber,**started by publishing a case a day online** before becoming a print bestseller. Espasa has followed suit with "Los casos de Agatha Christie: 100 acertijos y un misterio por resolver",**the first official activity book about the British writer**, which invites readers to step into the shoes of Poirot or Miss Marple.

**And there is more:**"El crimen del verano" takes the genre to the beach and poolside, with 121 suspects and clues scattered across sudokus, crosswords and number pyramids; "Crimigramas", meanwhile, hides the killer inside word searches.** None of these formats is new on its own**, but when combined with a mystery plot** they make sure the reader stops being a passive spectator** and becomes, puzzle by puzzle, a detective.

## Another way to switch off from your phone?

Behind this craze for paper and pencil there also lurks a weariness with screens. It is no coincidence that more and more of these books are being sold explicitly **as a way to disconnect from the digital world**, with no notifications and no rush, designed to reclaim that bit of leisure time that used to be swallowed by the mobile phone.

**On top of that comes a more practical argument**: those who follow the phenomenon closely point out that this kind of game works attention, memory and cognitive flexibility,** although there is still no study** to confirm exactly what lies behind their success.

**No one expects a paper pastime** to compete with the sophistication of a video game; in fact, that is not the aim.**It is more about a different niche**: a few minutes with the mind occupied and no screen involved, something that more and more people are seeking as part of the pastime itself.
