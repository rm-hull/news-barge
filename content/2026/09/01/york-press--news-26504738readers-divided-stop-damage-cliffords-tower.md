## Division grows over how to stop damage to historic city landmark

Readers are concerned about damage to the mound at Clifford's Tower

  *Readers divided over how to stop damage at Clifford's Tower, reports MAXINE GORDON*

READERS have shared mixed views after concerns were raised about damage being caused to the historic mound at Clifford’s Tower by people climbing and sliding down its slopes.

As reported by The Press, York resident Tim Mudd called on English Heritage to take stronger action after witnessing visible erosion on the mound and damage to areas planted with daffodils.

English Heritage said it "actively discourages" visitors from climbing the slopes and has already introduced measures including extra security patrols and signage. The charity said it was reviewing what further action could be taken.

The issue has sparked debate among readers, with some supporting greater protection for the landmark while others argued that public access to the area should not be further restricted.

One commenter, posting as Truetime, said the debate highlighted the challenge of balancing public enjoyment with preserving an important historic site.

"It’s a tricky balance, but surely we can find a middle ground rather than defaulting to polarised bans," they wrote.

The reader noted that some people viewed the mound as a place for children to play while others saw it as a location associated with a tragic chapter in York’s history.

  One of the signs warning people not to climb on the mound Image: Supplied


One of the worst anti-Semitic massacres of the Middle Ages took place at the site in 1190. The city’s entire Jewish community was trapped by an angry mob inside the tower. Many members of the community chose to commit suicide rather than be murdered or forcibly baptised by the attackers.

Truetime continued: "If it's handled sensitively, both sides can meet in the middle, protecting the fragile parts from erosion and respecting the site's history as a memorial, without completely cutting off public connection to the space."

Others suggested that concerns about damage were nothing new.

Guide73 said they had contacted the council around 20 months ago after noticing activity on the slopes.

The commenter claimed that roots of the specially planted daffodils were being pulled up and suggested larger and more prominent warning signs.

"I received an email saying it had been passed onto the appropriate department," they wrote. "Fourteen days later I received another email saying 'case closed'."

The reader questioned whether authorities were taking the issue seriously enough.

Some felt more signage could help, while others doubted signs alone would make a difference.

Beyond Infinity argued the problem appeared to be linked to late-night behaviour rather than daytime visitors.

"It's clearly become a tradition for young people on a night out to climb up and sledge down," the commenter wrote. "That's your root cause, deal with that."

Humorous suggestions also emerged, with one reader jokingly proposing: "Install a slide."

The site's historic significance was another theme running through the discussion.

Commenter gerry1962 argued that Clifford's Tower should be treated with greater respect, describing it as a memorial and comparing behaviour on the mound to climbing over gravestones.

However, another reader, Bloodaxe, challenged that description, saying the tower itself was not a memorial, although it stands on the site associated with the tragic massacre of York's Jewish community in 1190.

  Readers are concerned about damage to the mound at Clifford's Tower


Not everyone agreed that tighter controls were needed.

Subscriber Miss K Lola said climbing the mound had been taking place for decades and warned against what they saw as increasing regulation of public spaces.

"So much of York's spaces are commercialised and controlled by organisations," they wrote. "I'd be strongly opposed to any further regulation of this area."

The commenter also used the discussion to call for longer opening hours at the nearby Museum Gardens.

Meanwhile, one reader used the debate to highlight wider concerns about the state of York city centre, arguing that other issues deserved greater attention.

The discussion reflects the challenge facing English Heritage and local authorities as they seek to protect one of York's best-known landmarks while maintaining public access to the historic site.
