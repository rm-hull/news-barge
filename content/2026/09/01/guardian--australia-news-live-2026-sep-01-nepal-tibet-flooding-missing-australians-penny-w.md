Another bird story to follow on the good news below: A vaccination program to help protect a critically endangered parrot from deadly H5 bird flu has begun in earnest ahead of its summer breeding season.

AAP reports the migratory orange-bellied parrot, which has a wild population of 90-100, was considered a priority species by the federal government after the strain’s arrival in Australia.

Researchers have raised specific concerns about the risk of H5 to the parrot due to its loss of genetic diversity over the past century and its potentially weak immunity.

Orange-bellied parrot. Photograph: Minden Pictures/Alamy

Its wild population is propped up by a captive breeding program housed at Five Mile beach outside Hobart. Veterinarians at the facility have started vaccinating 350 of the captive birds, including juveniles who will later go free in Tasmania to join the wild population as part of annual releases.

Orange-bellied parrots, which are about the size of a budgie, only breed in Tasmania in summer and spend the winter in coastal Victoria and South Australia.

Their population dropped to just 17 wild individuals in 2016.

‘It’s fantastic’: 90 kākāpō chicks swell population of the world’s heaviest parrot

The population of the world’s heaviest parrot, the kākāpō, has surpassed 300 birds for the first time in 75 years, after a record breeding season triggered by the mass fruiting of a native New Zealand tree.

Ninety chicks were officially added to the population of the critically endangered birds on 1 September, pushing the total to 325 – a record high since recovery efforts began.

Ninety kākāpō chicks were officially added to the population of the critically endangered birds on 1 September, a record high. Photograph: Department of Conservation

“The last time the population is thought to have exceeded 300 birds may have been as long as 75 years ago,” said Deidre Vercoe, the conservation department’s kākāpō recovery operations manager.

We had a four-year gap between breeding seasons, so it’s fantastic to finally see the population increase after all that time.

Sarah Hanson-Young urges parliament to take up VAD reform bill

Greens senator Sarah Hanson-Young said calls from South Australia to reform the use of telehealth to access voluntary assisted dying (VAD) services amounted to a need for “crucial reform” that cannot wait.

Voluntary assisted dying is legal in every state and territory, yet too many Australians living with terminal illness are denied access to VAD advice and care because they are blocked from speaking to their doctor on the phone.

Health and advocacy groups call for community pill testing in NSW

Peak health and harm reduction groups including the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners, the Alcohol and Drug Foundation, and Unharm have written to the NSW premier, Chris Minns, and the NSW health minister, Ryan Park, calling for pill-testing services at music festivals to resume, and to be made permanent.

The groups also want testing to be offered in the community, and within the next six months.

A statewide pill testing trial at 12 NSW festivals ended six months ago, and there is currently no government-funded pill testing service in the state.

CEO of Unharm, Dr Will Tregoning, said:

This is as the NSW Government’s own alerts increasingly show dangerous opioids like nitazenes are turning up in the community, in counterfeit pharmaceuticals, ecstasy, heroin, cocaine and vapes.

On multiple occasions in the past two years, people in NSW overdosed on an opioid they never meant to take. These happened in the community, not at a festival. While we know that festival drug checking works and should resume in NSW, festivals cover only a narrow slice of drug use.

The ACT and Victoria have both moved beyond festivals to fund community drug checking. The letter called on the NSW government to do the same.

“Drug checking at festivals reaches people who can get to a festival. It cannot reach the people dying at home,” the letter says.

We ask you to commit to a next phase of drug checking in NSW that includes community-based services, designed for the communities they serve and independently evaluated from the start.

Beetaloo gas set to flow in NT, despite environmental concerns

The first gas from the controversial Beetaloo Basin fracking projects in Australia’s north is set to flow to homes and businesses in a territory touting its vast energy supply potential, AAP reports.

The basin is a major onshore shale gas resource in the Northern Territory’s Sturt Plateau, about 500km south-east of Darwin.

On Tuesday, first gas is set to flow, with more than 40 terajoules a day going into the territory’s gas grid. The territory uses about 60 terajoules a day of gas at peak levels for power generation and mines.

The NT chief minister, Lia Finocchiaro, said:

Every time Territorians flick on a light switch, or pull a cold beer from the fridge, they’ll see the benefits of the Beetaloo Basin at work.

Beetaloo fracking, construction of pipelines to get the gas to market, and using the gas to power AI datacentres have all been vigorously opposed by environmental groups. Fracking could see up to 6,000 wells drilled through underground water supplies, which supply 90% of the territory’s water needs, Environment Centre NT acting chief executive, Bree Ahrens, said:

Water is life in the territory and we are calling for a ban on fracking and AI datacentres before it’s too late.
