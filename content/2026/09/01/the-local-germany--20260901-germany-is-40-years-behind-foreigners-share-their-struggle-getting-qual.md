Last year German authorities set their personal record for approving the most applications for the recognition of foreign professional qualifications – throughout 2025 a total of approximately 86,600 applications were approved according to Germany’s statistics office (Destatis).

That’s 10 percent more applications than were approved a year prior in 2024. And it means that around 86,000 foreign skilled workers were thus able to go forth and do the jobs that they had trained for back home, and in many cases moved here specifically to do.

On paper that’s a resounding success for Germany, which has for years struggled to more efficiently bring skilled workers into its workforce.

But there is reason to be somewhat sceptical. Having qualifications from back home recognised – or not being able to do so – is a serious obstacle shared by foreign doctors, teachers and other professionals trying to settle into professional life in Germany.

So we asked our readers, who have applied to have their qualifications recognised, what the process was like for them.

We heard back from engineers, therapists, doctors and more – all of whom had worked in their professions back home and have since moved to Germany for work, with varying degrees of success.

Overall responses were pretty evenly split between people whose qualifications were recognised with relative ease versus those who faced long delays or other frustrating set-backs.

**Digital applications painless for some**

Of more than a dozen readers who had been through the process to have their foreign qualifications recognised, one trend stood out: engineers and people working in IT and HR reported relatively painless procedures, whereas people with jobs in medicine faced long delays and often multiple hurdles.

Kenneth, a British-German software developer, and Rahul, a payroll professional in finance, both had their qualifications recognised without issue.

Kenneth, who earned a postgraduate diploma in systems analysis and a masters of science in information systems in the UK said that his qualifications were "recognised without any problem".

In Rahul’s case, the process was handled by a "relocation provider". He added that it "was completed in two days".

Jenifer Stambaugh, an e-discovery analyst from the United States, called the process "surprisingly quick".

"I submitted my application online and received the recognition within just a few weeks," she said, adding, "It took more time to figure out and order all the transcripts than it did for everything to be recognised.”

Cansu, a teacher from Turkey, said the process was "pretty straightforward" and took about three months for two diplomas. "Of course I had to pay for it," she said.

It does seem that the application process for many has gotten a fair amount easier in recent years – especially since applications are now submitted digitally.

Lorie Whitaker, a user researcher from the US, noted that she went through the process before it was digitalised.

"I was successful but paid €200, and had to FedEx all the things…the ZAB database was only in German (that was fun)," Lorie said.

Because her university and degree were not already recognised in the system, she had to pay to file her credentials and have them recognised. "I don’t like that as I feel they should just recognise the degree without me having to pay," she added.

**It’s hard to be a doctor**

Largely on the other side of the spectrum were doctors and therapists, all of whom reported long waits and multiple road blocks.

"The process was extremely complicated," said Carolyn Wack, a clinical social worker who has worked in the field for 11 years.

“I have a bachelor’s degree in psychology from Bates College and a master’s degree from Smith College. Additionally, I did a year-long fellowship with intensive training at The Yale Psychiatric Institute," she said about her qualifications, adding, "Germany only recognised my social worker degree and required me to take a year long ‘*Brückenseminar*’ (bridge seminar) at the Katholische Universität in Munich."

For Carolyn, the process to have all of her qualifications recognised has dragged on for more than a decade.

"I would like to practice psychotherapy, as I did in Virginia," she said, but without an ‘*Approbation*’, or German medical licence, she has instead worked as a social worker since 2014.

"I have had a case pending with the *Anerkennung* authorities for over six years," she said, adding that she expects her case to be heard very soon. "I hope to be able to offer therapy to people with migration backgrounds through the Krankenkasse system. We’ll see what the court decides."

In medicine the stakes are higher, so it makes sense on some level that the process to have qualifications recognised would be more complicated. But it’s unfortunate that as the shortage of qualified medical professionals in Germany steadily grows, the country seems woefully unequipped to get foreign skilled doctors and professionals efficiently into its workforce.

"Germany is 40 years backwards in their standards," said Ruth, a psychotherapist and coach, from the US. "Despite 30 years experience I was forced to study again for three years to obtain their Mickey Mouse certificate to open my practice. It's ridiculous."

Leni, a doctor who said he worked several years before moving to Germany, noted that he received his *Approbation* two years after submitting his initial application.

"Like most doctors who trained outside the EU I had to sit two exams," Leni explained. "A German medical language test, after which I was allowed to work as a doctor on a limited work permit, and then secondly the *Kenntnisprüfung*, where our medical knowledge is examined to ensure it is on par with doctors who were trained in Germany.

"Most of the waiting time in the recognition process is for an exam slot to open up."

Leni said that he was in Bavaria "where the wait times are particularly long" and that he was "lucky" to pass both exams on the first try, otherwise he might’ve expected more years of waiting.

"It isn‘t always easy to get official information about the recognition process, as emails and phone calls go unanswered," he added.

"Most information comes from other doctors who have gone through the same process, and there are plenty of WhatsApp and Telegram groups to support each other."
