EATING out is about more than just food. So many places do decent sandwiches, homemade quiches, plus great cakes and, increasingly, good coffee.

  Inside the cellar at Moments, Barnard Castle


There needs to be more: the location, the view, the experience.

The 300-year-old cellars of Moments are one of the most remarkable experiences of Barney. It is musty and cool – even in the midst of the hottest summer – down there, footsteps creaking on the ancient beams overhead as you dine in a strange subterranean half-light.

  Inside the cellar at Moments, Barnard Castle


There are brick vaulted alcoves for adults to tuck themselves in and a secret half-height hidey-hole for children, in the old coal chute, to lose themselves in.

  Inside the children's hidey-hole in Moments, Barnard Castle


With fairylights around an old cast iron range, which is fired up in winter, the cellars are a magical experience, although if you allow your mind to wander, you can see centuries-old shapes moving in the shadows.

A dog under the table opposite whined, but not because its sixth sense had picked up something spooky. Instead, it was fed up and, I think, chilled through from lying on the stone flags, and as its owner ignored its snivelling, it stared at me with big, dark plaintiff eyes as I studied the menu.

I’d been expecting town centre café fare of ham and cheese sandwiches, and there are straightforward toasties and jackets plus soup on the menu, but the light lunches have a surprisingly Mexican flavour to them, with burritos, quesadillas and nachos jostling with a pizza naan, a loaded scone and a ploughman’s platter for attention.

We placed our order, and Theo pulled a blanket tightly around him, complaining he was cold after the blistering heat of the south Durham summer.

  The heraldic design on the range at Moments, Barnard Castle


The whining dog eventually got its way and trotted happily home, without even throwing a farewell glance back over its shoulder at me, which enabled me to study the 19th Century range fitted in the 17th Century fireplace. Plates on it show it was made by William Smith at Low Mill Foundry in the town – a company still going – and on its door is what the Historic England Grade II listing says is a “heraldic device of deer and tree”. This must relate to the family which, 150 years ago, had servants slaving away in the kitchens down here while they feasted up in the daylight.

Our food arrived promptly.

  The pizza naan at Moments, Barnard Castle


Theo had ordered a trans-continental margharitta pizza naan (£12.50), a mix between the doughy Indian bread, the Italian cheese and tomato topping and a little local spelling. He loved the classic tomatoey cheese taste – apparently named in honour of Queen Margherita of Savoy when she visited Naples in 1889. It was very large and came with a little salad so his mother, Petra, stuffed him with green leaves later that evening in the name of balance.

  The chickpea and red pepper quesadilla at Moments, Barnard Castle


Petra had chosen the chickpea and red pepper quesadilla (£9.50), a quesadilla being a soft tortilla wrap baked with the ingredients and cheese inside it.

It was served with tomato salsa, sour cream and guacamole, and was full of flavour with the chickpeas somehow refusing to be overwhelmed by the cheese.

  The nachos at Moments, Barnard Castle


I had ordered the loaded nachos (£9.50), which are apparently named after the chef, Ignacio Garcia, who in 1943 in Coahuila, near the border with Texas, invented these crispy slivers of tortilla chips and served them smothered in cheese and chilli – “nacho” is Ignacio shortened.

I was worried that I would miss the meat in this vegetarian version of “Nacho’s Specials”, but I didn’t.

It was a dish of rewarding contrasts: the crunch of the crisps, the deep mushiness of the guacamole, the long stretch of the melting cheese and the lovely smokiness of the bean chilli which had just a touch of heat to it. With sour cream and salsa, practically every mouthful had a different emphasis to it, and for £9.50, it was a good value dish.

We avoided the temptation of the traybakes, cakes, cannoli and scone selection, and, while Theo had a sweet hot chocolate, we had a very good coffee roasted at Lonton, just outside Middleton-in-Teesdale, with beans from Brazil and Indonesia.

For £45, the three of us had had a surprisingly cosmopolitan experience deep in the historic bowels of Barney.
