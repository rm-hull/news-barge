## Why your Uber fare could be taking money out of York

How does using services like Uber impact on our local economy? Fiona McCulloch of York Citizens Advice explains

THERE is a question we should be asking more often in York, where does our money go once we have spent it?

It is a surprisingly important question. We (especially me) talk endlessly about the cost of living crisis, low wages, expensive housing and people struggling to make ends meet.

At Citizens Advice York we see the consequences every day, helping local people dealing with debt, benefits, employment, housing and household bills. In 2025 to 2026 we helped York residents gain extra income, mainly in unclaimed benefits and write off debt to the tune of £5.5 million. But perhaps we should also look at the other side of the equation, why does so much money leave our communities in the first place?

Imagine you have £1 to spend. You could spend it with an independent York business. That business might pay a local employee, buy something from another local supplier, use a local accountant, pay rent to a local landlord, employ a local tradesperson or spend part of its profits in another York shop.

Your pound doesn't simply disappear. It circulates. Economists call this the local multiplier effect. The precise multiplier varies enormously according to the business and the place, so there is no magic figure that applies to every purchase.

But research into local procurement illustrates the principle very clearly. Evidence submitted to Parliament found that every £1 spent by participating local authorities with local small and medium sized businesses generated an additional 63p of local economic benefit through subsequent spending, compared with 40p from spending with larger local firms.

Another study using the Local Multiplier Three methodology found that £1 spent with a local supplier was worth £1.76 to the local economy, compared with just 36p when the money was spent outside the area. The point isn't that every York pound magically becomes £1.76. The point is that money has a geography.

Now consider the alternative. You spend your pound with a huge international company. Some of that money will still pay local workers and support local premises. Large companies can absolutely provide employment and investment in the places where they operate. But ownership matters. A greater proportion of the revenue can ultimately leave the city through payments to distant suppliers, shareholders, head offices and other parts of a multinational operation. The economic "leakage" is greater.

And then there is taxation. It is too simplistic to say that large companies "pay no tax". Companies operating in Britain are subject to a range of taxes and obligations, and corporation tax is payable on taxable profits.

But multinational companies can have complicated structures spanning several countries, meaning that the amount of corporation tax generated in Britain, or the taxable profit attributed to a particular part of their operation may be much smaller than the public might intuitively expect.

Take Uber, for example. Its business operates across jurisdictions and its UK tax strategy explicitly discusses the complexities of international taxation.

Uber says it complies with applicable tax law and does not engage in artificial tax planning designed solely to obtain tax savings.

The wider issue is not whether Uber is behaving legally. It is that the globalised economy makes it possible for economic activity, ownership, profits and taxation to exist in different places. For clarity, Uber takes a percentage of each fare, (around 25 per cent), and that's the money which disappears from the local economy.

That matters to York. If we want a stronger local economy, perhaps we should stop thinking about shopping locally as merely a sentimental choice. It is an economic decision.

When you buy from an independent café, use a local builder, employ a York based accountant, buy from a local shop or choose a locally owned service, you are potentially helping create another transaction, another wage, another job and another local purchase.

None of this means we should never use national chains or international companies. They are part of modern life and often provide useful, affordable services. But every pound we spend is also a vote about where economic value is retained.

And perhaps this is one reason the work of Citizens Advice York matters so much. Advising someone how to maximise their income or deal with debt is vital. But we should also ask the bigger question, what kind of local economy are we building? And what kind of local economy do we want?

A city like York can be full of economic activity and still have people struggling to make ends meet. Perhaps the real challenge isn't simply getting more money into York.

Perhaps it is making sure more of the money already passing through York stays here long enough to build prosperity.

That is an economic argument worth having.
