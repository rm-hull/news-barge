Newgate Street is supposed to be the heart of Bishop Auckland’s future.

A short walk from a gleaming new bus station, businesses are opening their doors and trying to make a living on a high street in a town which has been promised millions of pounds worth of renewal.

But pride in that future is being tested.

At the Brow lounge, a shopfront already scarred by vandalism was damaged again last month when, days later, a car smashed into it.

![]() *(Image: THE NORTHERN ECHO)*

Elsewhere, the high-pitched rumble of illegal bikes thundering through the town centre has become a familiar source of frustration, while topics of conversation in shops have turned to theft, intimidation and whether enough is being done to prevent it.

For Emily Blackmore, the contrast is difficult to ignore.

“You can’t have a pretty bus stop if the place that it leads to looks like it has been in a war," she warned.

“Many residents feel that our town centre has been allowed to deteriorate into a place where crime, vandalism and urban decay have become increasingly visible. Incidents like these contribute to the feeling that businesses are being left vulnerable and that our high street is becoming increasingly difficult to operate in.”

![]() *(Image: Sarah Caldecott)*

It is deeply troubling language from the founder of a new group set up to demand a safer, thriving Bishop Auckland, with greater police visibility and CCTV

Since the launch of the Bishop Auckland Community Action Group (BACAG) , more than 130 people have rallied behind the campaign, bringing together residents and businesses who say they are tired of feeling unsafe, unsupported and unheard.

Behind the shopfronts of Newgate Street, some of that frustration becomes more tangible.

Claire Stokeld, 52, works in a shop which was broken into less than two weeks ago.

She describes shoplifting and antisocial behaviour as persistent problems and says those dealing with them can be left feeling “ignored, abandoned and just let to deal with the crime.”

“We need foot patrols all the time, not just once a month or whenever – we need to see them every day, up and down the street," she said.

![]()A look at Newgate Street, Bishop Auckland, where a Community Action group fears antisocial behaviour is running riot. *(Image: Sarah Caldecott)*

Resident Lynn Morgan, 53, has her own worries about what she sees outside.

She described “idiotic boy racers” driving around the town and fears pedestrians will eventually get hurt.

“It’s a real problem – it shouldn’t take someone getting hurt for the police to step in.

“I fear that some car is going to hit one of these illegal bikes and then they have to live with that and it’s not fair.”

One shopkeeper, who has spent around 50 years in the town, has heard all these concerns before. He knows of customers who have felt intimidated, even pointing to one in the store at the time whose wife had been put off attending evening beautician appointments due to safety fears.

“I’ve been here 50 years and never really had any issues. Not to put the town down, but Bishop Auckland has kind of always been like this.”

At last week’s PACT (Police and Communities Together) meeting, officers said that recorded crime was down compared with last year.

It is a statistic which contradicts the picture being painted on the high street - and one that did little to silence the concerns in the room.

Families continued to raise the questions of antisocial behaviour, police visibility and how incidents are reported, while the conversation repeatedly returned to what could practically be done.

![]() *(Image: Stuart Boulton)*

Neighbourhood Inspector Mark Edwards said his team regularly carries out high-visibility patrols in the town centre and works proactively with residents, businesses and partner organisations.

“We encourage residents to attend local PACT meetings and to report antisocial behaviour to us or the council directly depending on the issue, rather than assume someone else has already done so.

“Make the call and help us tackle the issues where you live.”

The magnitude of the feeling means the issue needs to be looked at far beyond a local policing level.

Police and Crime Commissioner Joy Allen said: “People are rightly worried when they see anti-social behaviour, vandalism and crime affecting their town.

“Incidents like these have a real impact on community confidence.”

![]() *(Image: Supplied)*

She said additional money had been secured to tackle the antisocial behaviour in 2023, but argued the force was still operating with fewer officers than it had more than a decade ago.

It is against that backdrop that Ms Allen has commissioned a review of CCTV provision across the force area – one of the central demands to emerge from BACAG.

But cameras and officers are only part of her answer; the other, according to those in power, depends on residents themselves.

“I would encourage people to continue reporting concerns, even when they may seem minor,” she said.

“Individually, issues may appear isolated, but together they help build the intelligence picture that enables police to identify patterns, target resources effectively and take action.”

It is a request being made after weeks in which questions over crime, disorder and visibility have stretched far beyond Bishop Auckland.

![]() *(Image: SARAH CALDECOTT)*

Across Teesside, communities have been living through an extraordinary period of unease. The A66 crash last month which killed seven people including Cleveland Police officers Matthew Blades and Tom Clough, became the trigger point for a sprawling wave of serious and organised crime which risked getting out of control.

In the days that followed, the blue lights barely seemed to disappear.

More than 200 additional officers were brought in from forces around the country to support Cleveland Police, with detectives and armed response officers among those drafted into Teesside.

The disorder crossed the force boundary too. In Haswell, County Durham, five vehicles were set alight as a large crowd gathered for a memorial event connected to one of those killed in the A66 crash.

Durham Police condemned the behaviour and maintained visible patrols in the area afterwards.

By then, the conversation was no longer simply about individual incidents. It was about how safe people felt in their own streets.

Residents spoke publicly about keeping their heads down. Schools, GPs and businesses closed early. Political leaders called for greater resources. There was genuine fear.

The events in Bishop Auckland are different in both nature and scale. But the concerns are being voiced against that unsettled regional backdrop, and a fear that lawlessness is flourishing without consequences.

![]() *(Image: Sarah Caldecott)*

When residents here ask for more visible policing, they are doing so after weeks in which communities barely 30 miles away have been asking some of the same fundamental questions about safety, reassurance, and whether enough officers are on the streets.

Sam Rushworth, the town’s MP and resident of the place he represents, said: “I share residents’ concerns about crime, antisocial behaviour and the decline of the town centre.

“Victims of crime need more than a crime number; they deserve to know crimes will be investigated and perpetrators brought to justice.”

“We know that underreporting is a challenge.”

His answer is not confined to policing either.

He has called for CCTV and neighbourhood wardens and believes Durham County Council also “needs to step up and do more.”

![]() *(Image: Stuart Boulton)*

Graham Wood, Durham County Council’s economic development manager, said the authority understood the concerns being raised, but insisted work was already under way both to regenerate Bishop Auckland and address the problems affecting it.

“We appreciate the concerns of Bishop Auckland Community Action Group but stress that we are been working hard to regenerate the town and ensure it is a safe and attractive place to live and visit,” he said.

That work is visible across the town.

The new bus station – the same one at the heart of Ms Blackmore’s cutting description – was developed using £11.8m from the Future High Streets Fund, while another £2.2m has been committed through the Property Reuse Fund to help bring vacant units back into use, while construction of a new leisure centre continues.

Mr Wood added: “Our Neighbourhood Wardens patrol the town regularly, and our Clean and Green Team work hard to keep areas tidy.

“Meanwhile, partner organisations work together through a Multi-Agency Problem Solving group to address anti-social behaviour and other issues affecting community safety.”

For all the competing accounts of Bishop Auckland, that is perhaps where they meet.

Police say recorded crime is falling and the council points to millions of pounds of investment and work already being done. Residents can walk the same streets and tell very different stories about how safe they feel.

Ms Blackmore says BACAG was never intended to simply catalogue everything wrong with Bishop Auckland.

“We want to see an investment, accountability and meaningful action.”

“Community-led and driven people – together as a community trying to make our space better for everyone else.

“Even for people not involved to feel safe.”

Only months ago, Bishop Auckland was also bidding to become the UK’s first Town of Culture in 2028, an attempt to put its heritage, creativity and ambition on a national stage. It is still a place people want to rightly feel proud of.

Whether that is enough to change how people feel about the place they call home will take longer to answer, but Ms Blackmore is certain of one thing.

“Bishop Auckland needs to fight to be heard.”
