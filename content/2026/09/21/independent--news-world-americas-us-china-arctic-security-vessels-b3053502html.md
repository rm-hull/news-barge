American national security officials are closely watching growing Chinese activity in the Arctic region, according to a senior U.S. military officer.

Speaking in Oslo at an Arctic security conference, Rear Admiral Bob Little, Commander of the U.S. Coast Guard's Arctic District, said four Chinese research ships have operated off the U.S. Arctic coast so far in 2026, after five such vessels were recorded throughout 2025.

The Chinese vessels navigated through maritime zones off Alaska, traversing the Aleutian Islands, the Bering Strait, and pushing as far north as the North Pole. Although the vessels remained within international waters, Beijing failed to provide advance diplomatic notification, breaking standard protocol.

"Where I have responsibility, interest and concern is when they are operating in and around U.S. sovereign space," said Little. "So inside of our exclusive economic zones, or over our extended continental shelf, we certainly have a security concern. And so those are the types of activities that we see that we're watching very closely."

Little noted that when U.S. authorities make contact with the vessels to query their intent, "their response typically is either 'we are conducting operations in international waters' or 'we are conducting scientific research'."

The U.S. maintains a twofold concern regarding these operations. First, American officials worry Beijing could be using scientific research as a cover to target seabed mineral exploitation within U.S.-claimed maritime areas.

Second, there is apprehension that such missions might "provide them (the Chinese) with a military advantage or pose a security threat to the United States", according to Little, who oversees an area of operations nearly as vast as Europe.

China, which self-identifies as a "near-Arctic" nation with ambitions to establish a "Polar Silk Road", has increasingly expanded its footprint across the region through both independent operations and joint maneuvers with Russia.

During his visit to Norway, Little met with international colleagues to review similar patterns of behavior involving Russian research vessels in European waters. "Whether it's the Barents (Sea) or the Baltic (Sea), it's easy to find examples of hybrid threats and activities," he said.

"So I certainly watch that very carefully, because it's the same nation-state that's my neighbor on the western side of the Arctic," Little added, referring to Russia. Moscow denies that its research vessels engage in illegal operations or pose any threat to American interests.
