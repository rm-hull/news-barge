**HARROGATE**— A complete wall of silence has gone up across North Yorkshire’s law enforcement leadership today, with both North Yorkshire Police (NYP) and the regional mayoral team explicitly refusing to comment on the day-one national release of a highly critical independent watchdog assessment.The

**HMICFRS**, authored by His Majesty’s Chief Inspector of Constabulary Michelle Skeer and officially released from a strict media embargo at 00:01 on Tuesday 8 September 2026, delivers a stark whole-system critique of a policing model under “increasing strain.”Rather than engaging directly with press enquiries or answering specific local accountability questions, both

*State of Policing* report
**North Yorkshire Police headquarters and Labour Deputy Mayor for Policing, Fire and Crime, Jo Coles**, completely refused to issue standard media statements. Instead, Ms Coles circumvented direct journalistic questioning by releasing a pre-packaged op-ed to a regional print outlet, the

*Yorkshire Post*, leaving critical operational challenges facing local towns completely unaddressed.


**His Majesty’s Chief Inspector of Constabulary Michelle Skeer OBE QPM said:**

Policing has a once-in-a-generation opportunity to make changes that could improve how forces operate for many years to come.

The government’s reform programme should help address some of the long-standing problems that have held the police service back. While making changes, the government must take care to preserve our system of policing by consent and safeguard the operational independence of chief constables.

Forces must also make sure officers and staff are well supported, given the persistent problems of low morale, fatigue and poor well-being.

Our inspection findings show that policing is making progress in some areas, including neighbourhood policing. But there are still areas where performance is not good enough.

Too many victims are being let down by poor investigations and safeguarding needs to improve so that vulnerable people are identified and protected at the earliest opportunity.

This is a pivotal moment for policing. Policing in England and Wales has many strengths, but there remain areas where improvements are needed.

If managed correctly, I firmly believe that reform presents a significant opportunity to deliver a better service for both the public and those working within policing.



## Local Success Heavily Overshadowed by National Investigation Crises

The independent assessment presents a starkly divided picture for North Yorkshire. On one hand, the watchdog explicitly highlights **North Yorkshire Police (NYP)** as an organizational frontrunner, officially grading the force**“Good”** across three critical pillars:

- **Leadership and Force Management:** Praised for visible leaders with a strong, data-driven understanding of local demand and risk.
- **Workforce Retention and Inclusion:** Recognized for investing in professional talent development and supporting workforce well-being.
- **Managing Fraud:** Named as one of the few forces successfully pioneering localized ownership, triage, and preventative models for fraud offenses.

Despite this structural praise, North Yorkshire’s silent leadership faces the reality of a catastrophic nationwide crisis in criminal detection. In the PEEL inspection reports published so far in this cycle, the inspectorate named “investigating crime” as the poorest-performing area in the country, with **not a single force in England and Wales achieving a “Good” or “Outstanding” grade**.

According to the report, frontline investigators are being completely “overwhelmed by their workloads.” Forces are systematically failing to grasp basic characteristics of good detection, including gathering evidence during the critical “golden hour,” progressing data-driven lines of enquiry, and keeping victims updated. Neither NYP nor the Deputy Mayor would comment on local investigation standards.


**Police Federation of England and Wales**, Acting National Chair, Brian Booth, said:

The findings come as no surprise to police officers across England and Wales. The Inspectorate has recognised the evidence consistently presented by the Police Federation that officers are carrying ever-increasing workloads, morale remains low, fatigue is widespread and too many feel undervalued despite the vital role they play in keeping the public safe.

The report delivers a clear warning: if policing continues to lose experienced officers through burnout, fatigue and disillusionment, public services will suffer. Reform cannot come at the expense of the workforce.

HMICFRS acknowledges the risk that organisational change could create uncertainty and drive experienced officers away at precisely the time their skills and expertise are most needed. That warning must be heeded.



## Deputy Mayor Uses Column to Warn of Three Flaws in Yorkshire Merge

While acknowledging that policing must be reformed because crime constantly changes—noting that cybercrime now accounts for a staggering 48% of all offenses—Ms Coles used her print column to declare that “place has to come first.”

Examining the structural review currently being undertaken by former Metropolitan Police Commissioner Sir Bernard Hogan-Howe, the Deputy Mayor outlined three critical risks to taxpayers if North Yorkshire is swallowed by a regional consolidation:

1. **Public Safety & Service Disconnect:** A Yorkshire-wide force spanning four Mayoral areas would sever the link between the public and the police, putting neighborhood policing links and rural safety at risk. It would centralize authority at the exact moment other public services like health and housing are being decentralized to Mayors.
2. **Reorganization Cost & Bureaucracy:** A shift in focus toward centralized HR processes and reorganisation poses a severe risk to everyday, bread-and-butter policing. It could derail progress on the national mission to halve violence against women and girls. Furthermore, how council tax precepts would function under a merged force remains entirely unclear.
3. **Erosion of Public Confidence:** Diverting attention away from the delivery of increased neighborhood policing and the tackling of serious violence in favor of a massive nationwide administrative reorganisation risks undermining the UK’s bedrock model of policing by consent.

## “Policing York Isn’t Like Policing Bedale”

The Deputy Mayor highlighted the unique geographic challenges facing her office, stressing that a one-size-fits-all approach cannot protect North Yorkshire’s communities, which saw crime drop by 2.8 percent over the last year, resulting in 1,291 fewer victims.

Defending the region’s current model, Ms Coles wrote:

The suggestion that we could have one Yorkshire-wide police force is a real risk to our county. Housing, health, economic growth and resilience are all moving to Mayoral areas. It makes no sense to consider removing police from that mix.

Policing York isn’t like policing Bedale, which isn’t like Whitby… Our rural areas, coastal communities, cities and towns need different things, and they need them at different times of year too. Place-based decision making has to be the priority because people and the communities they live in really matter.


## Looming Tiered Accountability

The clash over local independence arrives at a pivotal turning point for political oversight under new Prime Minister Andy Burnham. Alongside structural shake-ups, the HMICFRS report detailed the active rollout of the new **Policing Performance System**. For the first time, watchdogs are assigning every single police force a definitive performance tier, ranging from Level One (the standard operational baseline) to Level Four (Special Measures).

This system reintroduces strict national targets for response metrics, such as 999 call answer times and neighborhood policing levels. While North Yorkshire Police’s organizational leadership has been recognized by the inspectorate, the escalating political tension between local mayoral teams and central government reformers over force autonomy looks set to dominate the regional landscape.

*Editorial Note: Both North Yorkshire Police headquarters and Labour Deputy Mayor Jo Coles explicitly refused to comment or engage with direct press questions following the release of the HMICFRS report. Ms Coles opted instead to address the structural elements of the reform white paper via a pre-coordinated column in the regional press.*




HMICFRS published 66 inspection reports relating to policing during the period covered by the annual assessment. Drawing on this work, the State of Policing report recognises:

- improvements in the police response to group-based child sexual exploitation;
- strengths in many regional organised crime units;
- that the firearms licensing system in England and Wales is not fit for purpose; and
- that the police need greater clarity about their role in safety advisory groups when planning major public events.
