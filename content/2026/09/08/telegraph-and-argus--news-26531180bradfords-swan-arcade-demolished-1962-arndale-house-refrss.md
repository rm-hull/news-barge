For more than 80 years, the Swan Arcade was one of Bradford city centre’s most distinctive shopping landmarks: a grand covered Victorian thoroughfare of shops and offices, running between Market Street and Broadway opposite the Wool Exchange.

It was demolished in 1962 and replaced by Arndale House, the eight-storey block that still stands in Charles Street.

   Swan Arcade 1954. 

*(Image: Staff)*

For more than 80 years, the arcade provided a covered route between Market Street and Broadway, opposite the Wool Exchange. Its shops and offices were used by generations of Bradford people, but no part of its stone frontage, glass roof or ornamental gates survives in the city today.

## Stunning architecture

The building was constructed between 1877 and 1880, during a period when Bradford was expanding as a result of the money being pumped into the local economy courtesy of the wool trade.

Architects Milnes and France designed it in the Italianate style. J and W Beanland built the arcade from Bolton Woods stone at a reported cost of about £160,000.

The development covered a 2,630-square-yard site. Bradford Beck ran beneath the building, while a west wing was added in 1881. Its name came from the White Swan Inn, which had previously stood on the land.

At its Market Street entrance, graceful swan motifs appeared in the stonework and ironwork, while handsome ornamental gates supplied by Hodkinson and Co and Taylor and Parsons became a much-admired feature.

The impressive new addition to the thriving city centre was opened by local MP and former mayor Angus Holden in 1880.

## JB Priestley connection

The four-storey building contained shops and offices. Several businesses were connected with Bradford’s wool industry, which dominated the city’s economy at the time. The arcade also had a literary connection through J B Priestley.

Priestley worked there as a clerk after leaving school. He did not enjoy the job, but admired the building and later became one of the people who wanted it protected.

## Changing times spell trouble

By the late 1950s, however, Bradford’s approach to its Victorian buildings had changed. Like many British cities, it was pursuing redevelopment based on wider roads, new offices and modern retail space.

The Arndale Property Trust, based in Wakefield, bought the Swan Arcade in 1954. The reported price was between £225,000 and £250,000. Its plan was to replace the Victorian building as part of a wider scheme for central Bradford.

The company had to wait for existing leases to end. In October 1960, it announced plans to demolish the arcade and create a development designed for the new city centre.

The last day of trading was Saturday, March 3, 1962. A report in the Telegraph and Argus said demolition was expected to take about four months. Workers soon moved in, and the arcade disappeared behind scaffolding and rubble.

  Swan Arcade is bulldozed 

*(Image: Staff)*

Its gates were sold to a wealthy businessman. Their fate remains a source of frustration for people who see photographs of the building and wonder where they are now.

Work on the replacement started the following year.

Arndale House was an eight-storey office block with shops around its base. It was completed in 1964, after Yorkshire and England fast bowler Fred Trueman ceremonially topped it out in December 1963.

The Swan Arcade’s disappearance reflected a period when much of Bradford’s Victorian architecture was considered expendable. Arndale House still stands in Charles Street, opposite the Wool Exchange.

It serves as a daily reminder of the arcade that once occupied the site. For many people, photographs remain the only way to imagine walking through its covered passage, past its shops, offices and ornamental entrance.
