Today's Object of the Week is a collection that traces one North East artist’s lifelong determination to record the world around her, from scenes and seafronts to the final abstract works she created.

A new Sunderland exhibition brings together previously unseen work by an artist who documented the world around her throughout her life.

Kitchen Sink Realism, the inaugural exhibition at Northern Gallery for Contemporary Art's new home at Culture House, focuses on the work of Sunderland-born artist Audrey Amiss, presenting paintings, drawings and archival material spanning decades of artistic practice.

Born in Fulwell in 1933, Audrey Amiss showed an early talent for art while attending Bede Grammar School for Girls.

Her studies continued at Sunderland College of Art, now part of the University of Sunderland's School of Media and Creative Industries, before she secured a scholarship to study painting at the Royal Academy School of Art in London between 1954 and 1958.

The exhibition follows that artistic journey from her formative years in Sunderland through to her final years in south London.

  Portrait of a sleeping man sat in chair by Sunderland-born artist Audrey Amiss on display in the Kitchen Sink Realism exhibition at Northern Gallery for Contemporary Art. 

*(Image: Wellcome Collection, London)*

Along the way, it reveals an artist committed to recording everyday life, despite personal and professional challenges.

Amiss left the Royal Academy in 1958 because of mental illness and later grew frustrated by the lack of opportunities to develop her career.

She worked as a typist for most of her adult life while spending periods in mental health hospitals and pursuing painting, drawing, journalling, collage and campaigning.

  Previously unseen artwork by Audrey Amiss featured in the Kitchen Sink Realism exhibition 

*(Image: Wellcome Collection, London)*

She died in 2013, leaving a vast archive of paintings, drawings, sketchbooks, scrapbooks, photograph albums and personal records. It was donated to Wellcome Collection in London in 2014.

What makes the exhibition particularly interesting is the way it charts recurring themes across Amiss's work.

From social realism and impressionism to abstraction, she remained fascinated by the world around her and determined to document it.

The opening section explores Amiss's working-class family life in Sunderland and its influence on her art.

  An untitled work by Audrey Amiss form part of the inaugural exhibition at Northern Gallery for Contemporary Art's new venue 

*(Image: Wellcome Collection, London)*

Her early paintings and drawings capture familiar North East scenes, from Roker and Seaburn beaches and Sunderland football practice to parks, buildings, tall ships, factory workers and everyday streets.

The exhibition also includes work from her school and college years, showing her efforts to master formal drawing and painting techniques.

Her time at the Royal Academy is represented through paintings that were originally entered into prize competitions, including submissions for the Landseer Prize and Arthur Hacker Prize.

  Beach Scene, one of the paintings featured in Kitchen Sink Realism, which charts Audrey Amiss's artistic journey 

*(Image: Wellcome Collection, London)*

Later works in oil and gouache turn their attention to the domestic interiors and green surroundings of the London neighbourhoods where she lived.

The exhibition ends with a striking grid of abstract flower and tree paintings—the final works she created.

Its title comes from one of Amiss's own observations about her changing style.

Reflecting on her work, she wrote: "I was once in the tradition of social realism, also called the kitchen sink school of painting, but I am now avant garde and misunderstood."

More than a decade after her death, the exhibition helps fulfil Amiss's ambition to be recognised as an artist in her own right.

Kitchen Sink Realism, developed with support from Wellcome Collection, runs until Saturday, January 17.
