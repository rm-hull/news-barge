We held a live Q&A on Tuesday on the aftermath of the Sunday's critical election in Saxony-Anhalt which saw the extremist, anti-immigration AfD party top the vote.

We know our readers were extremely concerned about the result and what will happen at future elections in Germany.

Below you'll find our answers to questions sent in by readers in a recent survey and in the comments section below we also answered more questions on the incessant rise of the far right in Germany.

Thanks to all who joined the conversation.

**Question: What would Afd do without all this immigrants that are doing the hard work? Where would the German economy be? Not to mention the German population is ageing and retiring, who would pay for their pensions? -* Borcea Catalin Ionut, 46, from Romania*.Tom Pugh, The Local:** Broadly, the AfD claims to think Germany can do without immigration if it boosts birth rates, mobilises domestic workers, encourages Germans abroad to return and doubles down on AI.

Most economists and business groups say that simply won’t be enough – Germany faces a huge demographic crunch, with hundreds of thousands of workers needed as baby boomers retire.

Ironically, the strongest AfD support is often found in eastern states facing the steepest labour shortages, raising fears of a vicious circle where anti-immigration politics make it even harder to attract the workers needed to sustain growth and pensions.

**READ ALSO: Can Germany still attract foreign workers as skilled labour shortages spike?Question: Why does Local Germany think Afd is winning so much? I find often that the answers people give to this question are lazy. It is more than just racism. -* Jeff, 60, USA*Rachel Loxton, The Local:** I think it comes down to lots of reasons. People are dissatisfied with the way things have been going in Germany, including modern developments like more immigration and the rising cost of living. 

In Saxony-Anhalt itself there is a feeling that the state is not doing well; the economy is struggling and there are not enough businesses or opportunities. The population there has been dwindling.

When I spoke to people who’d campaigned on the ground, they said that AfD supporters appeared to be living in a "different reality" – their perception of things was skewed due to the social media and messages they’d been consuming.

Other than that people do seem to be dissatisfied with what used to be the *Volkspartei* (people’s parties) like the CDU and SPD – this is a trend we’ve been seeing across Germany for a while which has resulted in a more fragmented political landscape. 

Historian Katja Hoyer, who grew up in East Germany, pointed out in an interview that there are socioeconomic and political differences in eastern regions which helps to explain the AfD's popularity here. She said people felt left behind and wanted change.

The AfD’s strong lead candidate Ulrich Siegmund helped. The AfD consistently pushed the message that it would make their lives better. I do wonder how many people read the government programme, though. Either they haven’t read all the details or they are okay with the extreme views.

**Question: What happens now that AFD has won the majority seats in Saxony-Anhalt? Will they force immigrant workers out of the state or country? Will their decisions be questioned during the state parliament sessions? Can the German government help?*- Anonymous, 35, from India*Paul Krantz, The Local:** Under Germany’s federalist system, state governments have a fair amount of authority, particularly over public education, policing and municipal law, for example. That said, laws or policies which are found to violate Germany’s Basic Law, or constitution, could be challenged and blocked by the courts.

So could the AfD force immigrant workers out of the state? Not directly just like that.

On the topic of the AfD’s big plans for ‘remigration’, Joschka Selingera, a lawyer from the Gesellschaft für Freiheitsrechte (GFF) told me that the party’s proposals could “hardly be implemented by a state government.” However, he added that a state government “can influence residency law through individual measures”.

This fits with comments on record from state AfD leader Ulrich Siegmund, who once said that the party’s goal should be to make living here “as uncomfortable as possible” for foreigners.

I do think any measures toward this end would be questioned by other parties in the state parliament, and many could be challenged in the German courts. The lawyer I spoke to noted that, “An administrative court can react very quickly in [matters of] urgent legal protection…sometimes within days to weeks.”

But he also noted that “the greater danger lies beneath openly unlawful administrative acts.

“A state government could undermine constitutional rights in the long term through its administrative practice and the allocation of resources.”

**Question: Why do the current parties unofficially 'blacklist' AFD by refusing to support motions by them? -* Archie, 36, from the UK and living in Berlin*Rachel Loxton, The Local:** In Germany, the strategy of keeping the far right out of government is referred to as the*Brandmauer,* or “firewall”. It is used explicitly against the AfD to prevent the normalisation of far-right extremism and to keep it from gaining political power through cooperation with mainstream parties.

That is why political parties in Germany do not form coalitions with the AfD. But recent electoral successes, particularly in eastern Germany, have put increasing pressure on the firewall, with some now arguing that the AfD will at least have to be tolerated as a political force.

In that respect, some argue the firewall has done the opposite of what it intended to do. By keeping the AfD out of governments, it means it has never had to take accountability. That allows it to keep its outsider status and potentially has made it more appealing to voters who are frustrated with the ‘mainstream’ political parties.

**Question: How likely are the AfD to take full control in a few years?*- Mathias, 28, from Denmark*Tom Pugh, The Local:** While the Saxony-Anhalt election result shows the AfD is getting close to single-party rule at state level, the party is still very unlikely to win an outright majority in Germany as a whole any time soon.

This is partly because the country’s electoral system makes outright majorities rare, and – for the time being at least – the political “firewall” remains in place. As mentioned above, this means that so far mainstream parties refuse to govern alongside the AfD.

It’s also because the AfD would still need to break through much more strongly in western Germany, where the vast majority of voters live.

Germany’s constitutional safeguards, including the possibility of a party ban, also remain in the background.

**Question: Have AfD any policies to protect legal immigration?*- Anonymous*Rachel Loxton, The Local:** In general, the AfD takes a tough approach on migration. The party in Saxony-Anhalt is calling for an end to asylum and wants to ramp up deportations of immigrants – among other measures. The AfD also speaks out regularly in support of toughening up the citizenship laws.

The party is also against the idea of internationals helping to solve Germany’s skilled worker shortage. The party programme states: “The problem of the skills shortage can only be resolved by a reversal in birth rates and an end to the German ‘brain drain’.”

Another part of the programme states that it’s “possible – without the slightest legal complication – to impose an immediate and total ban on immigration from selected non-EU countries that are responsible for problematic immigration. Entry from these countries will then, in principle, only be permitted with a visa for a temporary stay, such as for tourism.”

It is unclear which non-EU countries the AfD is talking about. However, this would suggest that the party does want restrictions on legal migration, and that legal migration is not protected in their view.

It’s worth pointing out again here that laws governing asylum, residency and citizenship are determined primarily at the federal and EU level.

**Question: How would a coalition of AfD and BSW work in practice? I don't think they'll agree on anything.*- Alex Lockwood, 41, from Adelaide, Australia*Tom Pugh, The Local:** The two parties share some positions, particularly on immigration, scepticism towards sanctions on Russia, criticism of the political establishment and opposition to parts of the green transition.

But you’re right that they differ sharply on economic and social policy, with the BSW favouring a much larger role for the state, and the AfD generally supporting lower taxes and a more market-oriented approach.

There's also a leadership problem: the BSW has floated the idea of a non-partisan minister-president, while the AfD insists that, as the largest party, its candidate should lead any government. That disagreement alone could prove difficult to overcome – although some analysts still see ad hoc cooperation or parliamentary deals as a possible option.
