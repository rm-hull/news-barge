Romanian prosecutors indict British man identified as Andrew Tate for number of alleged crimes

A British man – identified by local media as controversial manosphere influencer Andrew Tate – was formally indicted by Romanian authorities on Friday for a number of alleged crimes, including trafficking minors, money laundering, and sexual offences.

Tristan and Andrew Tate deliver a press statement in front of their house after landing back in Romania, near Bucharest, Romania in March last year. Photograph: Inquam Photos/Eduard Vinatoru/Reuters

His older brother – identified by media as Tristan Tate – was also indicted for alleged complicity and seeking to pervert the course of justice.

A press release issued by DIICOT, Romania’s Directorate for Investigating Organized Crime and Terrorism, outlines a number of alleged offences including alleged sexual exploitation of a minor who was moved to Luton, UK, for the purposes of online pornography.

The press release also includes allegations of attempts to pervert the course of justice by seeking to stop another victim from reporting it to the police.

The two men are not named in the press release, but the authorities said it was part of an investigation into two British citizens, aged 38 and 39 and members of the same family. National news agency Agerpress, Antena3 and Digi24 all identified them as the Tate brothers.

As part of the investigation, the prosecutors also requested that four of their luxury cars – worth €825,000 – are confiscated as part of the probe.

The Tate brothers also face separate proceedings in the US and in the UK.

Serbians targeted with spyware in country’s ‘largest documented wave of surveillance’

Aisha Down

Global technology reporter

Meanwhile, it emerged that at least 14 people from across Serbian civil society were targeted with advanced spyware earlier this year in what the digital rights group Share Foundation said was the largest documented wave of such infection in Serbia to date.

A student protest movement has been raging in opposition to Aleksandar Vučić’s government since 2024. Photograph: Darko Vojinović/AP

The wave of spyware infections was discovered in August after Apple notified people in 110 countries that they had probably been victims of mercenary spyware.

The Citizen Lab, which carries out forensic analyses of spyware attacks, released an analysis on Wednesday showing that at least one of the Serbians had been targeted with the NSO Group’s Pegasus spyware, which would have given the attacker “total access to the device”.

Share said those targeted included members of a Serbian student movement that has been a thorn in the side of the government of Aleksandar Vučić since 2024. Coalescing out of countrywide protests that followed the collapse of a train station that winter, it is one of the largest student movements in Europe.

Vučić has been in power for more than a decade, during which time critics have said he has overseen a hollowing-out of institutions while steering the country closer to Moscow.

There is no evidence that Vučić’s government was the entity that targeted the student with the Pegasus spyware. However, other spyware used against the students, including a software called NoviSpy, has been previously connected to Serbian authorities.

EU enlargement commissioner calls off Serbia visit over Mladić's 'glorification'

EU’s enlargement commissioner Marta Kos has called off her planned visit to Serbia in protest at what she called “the glorification surrounding the death” of Bosnian Serb war criminal Ratko Mladić.

A banner reading “Ratko Mladić, Serbian Hero” is displayed on a footbridge over a motorway as a convoy carrying the body of Bosnian Serb war criminal Ratko Mladić passes through Belgrade, Serbia. Photograph: Pedja Milosavljevic/AFP/Getty Images

Mladić, who died last week while serving a life sentence for genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity during the 1992-1995 Bosnian war, will be commemorated at a public event in Belgrade on Saturday afternoon before his burial on Monday.

Upon landing in Belgrade last night, Mladić’s coffin was wrapped in the national flag and Serbian army soldiers carried his body from the plane to a van for transport to the military hospital, AP reported.

In a strongly worded statement, Kos, a Slovenian diplomat and politician, said:

“Ratko Mladić was convicted of genocide, crimes against humanity and war crimes. The return of his remains to Belgrade reminds Europe of the darkest pages in our recent history.

The glorification surrounding his death is incompatible with the values on which the EU path is built. That path requires confronting the past, genuine reconciliation and respecting the victims of war crimes.

The values that underpin our common future are not negotiable. In this context, I have decided not to proceed with my planned visit to Serbia at this time.”

Volkswagen to cut further 50,000 jobs as it ends row with union over revival plan

Patrick Daly, Lisa O’CarrollandMark Sweney

The car company Volkswagen has approved controversial plans to shed 100,000 jobs in a battle for survival as it faces fierce competition from Chinese rivals.

A Volkswagen factory in Hanover, Germany. Photograph: Christopher Neundorf/EPA

After the latest round of talks this week, the company announced a further 50,000 job cuts and agreed a staggered end to current production at four German plants: Emden, Zwickau, Hanover and the Audi site in Neckarsulm between 2031 to 2034. There are no specific plans beyond that.

The agreement came as a surprise after a meeting of shareholders, unions, and state representatives on the supervisory board in what was seen as a considerable test for the German carmaker’s chief executive, Oliver Blume.

He had gone as far as to arrange an emergency meeting if the package was rejected in preparation for a potential showdown with unions.

“This is a strong signal for the future,” said Blume, who now has a mandate for what the company previously called the “most strategically profound transformation programme” in the history of the VW group.

He was booed last month by staff during a tour of the company’s headquarters in Wolfsburg, northern Germany, as part of a dialogue about the need to address the company’s financial challenges.

IG Metall, the company’s biggest staff union and representative on the supervisory board, said concessions had been made on both sides to avert “a dangerous escalation of the conflict”.

Chief among these was a stay of execution for four plants threatened with closure in exchange for an agreement on staff reductions. The deal includes a sweeping cost-cutting plan to cut a further 50,000 positions by 2030, bringing the total job losses in the pipeline to 100,000.

Meloni marks milestone as her government becomes longest-serving in history, surpassing Berlusconi's record

Angela Giuffrida

in Rome

Giorgia Meloni is celebrating her government becoming the longest-serving since the history of the Italian Republic, although she cautioned that “stability is not a record to flaunt”.

Italian prime minister Giorgia Meloni at Chigi Palace government office in Rome, Italy. Photograph: Anadolu/Getty Images

Meloni, leader of the far-right Brother of Italy, and her coalition allies will mark the milestone with a festival-style rally in the southern Italian city of Bari on Friday. About 10,000 people will be bussed in from across Italy and Meloni will deliver a speech, essentially launching her campaign ahead of general elections next year.

In a message on social media, Meloni said she welcomed the longevity record “with gratitude and a strong sense of responsibility”.

She added:

“Because stability is not a record to flaunt, but a condition that enables a nation to plan, make decisions, keep its commitments and command respect.”

Meloni came to power in October 2022, becoming Italy’s first female prime minister. Her government has so far lasted 1,413 days – beating by a day the late Silvio Berlusconi’s second administration between June 2021 and April 2005.

Her government has been buoyed by a strong parliamentary majoritywhile Meloni has earned respect overseas after jettisoning her previous hardline rhetoric in favour of cultivating a more moderate, pragmatic image. Still, some of her domestic policies, such as restricting LGBTQ+ rights and vigorously expanding law and order regulations, show her old instincts remain.

But she is now being challenged on the far-right by a former army general, Roberto Vannacci, who in the space of a few months has drawn defectors from all three of the main ruling coalition parties, as well a significant portion of their electorate.

Former Italian general Roberto Vannacci delivers his speech in Rome, Italy. Photograph: Remo Casilli/Reuters

Stability is a major achievement, especially in a country like Italy where governments easily collapse, but with internal infighting beneath the surface, Meloni’s passage towards a second mandate is much less guaranteed than it was a few months ago.
