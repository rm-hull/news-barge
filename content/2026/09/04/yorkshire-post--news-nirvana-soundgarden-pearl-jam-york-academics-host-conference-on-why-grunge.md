# Nirvana, Soundgarden, Pearl Jam: York academics host conference on why grunge still resonates today

## Twin sisters Kristīne and Sintija Brence are academics who are putting on an international conference about grunge. John Blow hears more.

Nirvana’s performances at the The Duchess of York in Leeds on October 25, 1989, or the Polytechnic’s students’ union exactly a year later, hold special interest for music fans in these parts – a local link to a band which would soon, after the release of the album Nevermind, be among the biggest in the world, whether they liked it or not.

In its wake, grunge – a category which homed various rock bands linked to Seattle, Washington, in the northerwestern USA such as Alice in Chains, Soundgarden, Pearl Jam and others – became one of the defining genres of the 1990s.

Years later, this sparked something in twin sisters Kristīne and Sintija Brence, who are now 26, both Humanities PhD students at York St John University and self-published authors on works about the social and political impact of alternative rock culture.

![Sintija and Kristīne Brence.](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/webimg/b25lY21zOmM0YmI0OGNjLWRmZjktNGQ1Ni04NjVjLWM5MTQxZmJiMDE2YzplODg1YzQzYS1jNzc3LTRlZjAtOTBiMi1iZjU2ZjM2NzNlOTc=.jpg?crop=3:2,smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65)



Raised in Cēsis, a medieval town in Latvia, the twins got into grunge and alternative music after coming to live in the UK in 2013.

Back home in the Baltics, it had been harder to access different genres of music when they were growing up. “It's pretty much really isolated,” says Sintija. “Back then, when it comes to music, you couldn't just buy a CD. You couldn't buy a vinyl or a cassette. You would need to go to the capital city in something equivalent to a supermarket and try to find a CD and even then, you couldn't find alternative music.”

They have now organised the UK’s first academic conference dedicated to grunge, which is due to take place at York St John University on Monday next week between 10am and 5pm.

Grunge Included 2026 – named after Sintija’s own blog – will take place in the Creative Centre at the Lord Mayor’s Walk campus in York.

The conference, bringing together academics and researchers from Poland, Latvia, the United States and from across the UK, will look at at how grunge developed, why it became such a powerful cultural movement and the ways its influence continues to shape music and popular culture today.

A highlight of the line-up is a Q&A with Gordon Raphael, who the record producer and musician best known producing albums by The Strokes. Now based in Hebden Bridge, he is from Seattle and worked with the band Green River, which were seen as the pioneers of grunge.

Meanwhile, when the sisters themselves came to England, the West Yorkshire-based pair went into academia, exploring the music they loved in a deeper way.

Sintija says: "For us, it is truly a political movement – not so much even a musical movement – that is highlighting the urgency to think critically and independently.”

And with each new generation, grunge still captures the imagination of young people – you don’t have to walk far in any of Yorkshire’s cities to see flannel shirts or Nirvana logos.

There are a few reasons for this, she says.

“When it comes to the sound itself, everything currently – especially when it comes to popular rock music, alternative music, that is being produced today – it's really polished and modern, and grunge is one of the last musical scenes that still maintained a really kind of distorted and truly unpolished sound, which showcases the human side of music, which AI is really challenging at the moment. So I think that's one aspect, but the other is, the political messages that grunge artists were trying to raise awareness for, they're still present in the modern day. This kind of self-exploration and trying to find oneself has always been prominent across youth. This is something that I always say: the grunge issues, as I like to call them, they're still prominent in the modern day and haven't been resolved.”

The ubiquity of social media technology is another reason – teenagers are tired of the pressure that comes with social media, she says.

“That's one reason why so many people look back at the 1990s. It was the last era where life wasn't built around smartphones and social media, making it feel more real, simple, and authentic.”

Audiences at the free event will hear an exploration of grunge as much more than a musical genre and can expect discussions about its influence on politics, culture, society, identity, design, fashion, art and alternative music more widely. Topics range from grunge political graphic design, “troubling feminism”, grunge protest in Africa, neurodivergence, philosophy of sound and more.

Speakers include the twins themselves, Klaudia Rachubińska, Michael Ayokunle Osagie, Amy McCarthy, John Moores, Grace Elizabeth Emerson, Joi Katskee and Zana Bayley.

Kristīne says: “Many times, young researchers and thinkers are left out of established academic networks and lose the opportunity to bring new ideas and develop the industry.

"This is the place where new ideas and a new generation are able to continue the conversation in a new light. Isn’t it interesting to see how a new generation views music and the grunge movement? We think it is!

"Sintija and I created this event to bring like-minded people together and build on the platform she has developed through her blog, Grunge Included. I really hope that, as we launch this international conference, researchers from around the world can build new networks and collaborations, and we can work towards more Grunge Included events.”
