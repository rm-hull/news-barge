What former CPS elections law chief says about legal implications of Reform UK investigation

Andrew Sparrow

Nick Vamos used to be head of special crime at the Crown Prosecution Service, which meant he was in charge of all prosecutions for electoral offences. He is now a partner at the law firm Peters & Peters. He has issued this statement about the legal implications of the undercover investigation into Reform UK.

UK electoral law can be fiendishly complicated but, on this issue at least, the rules are very clear. Only UK-registered voters, companies or other similar entities are permitted to make donations to political parties. A donation can include incurring expenses on behalf of a party, as is alleged to have happened here.

The criminal net is cast very wide: it is against the law for anyone knowingly to do any act in furtherance of an arrangement likely to facilitate an impermissible donation. This would include engaging in discussions to solicit or encourage an impermissible donation, provided the person knew that was what was being proposed.

It is also an offence to mislead a political party about the source of a donation, so the perpetrators of the sting, whatever their intentions, may themselves have broken the law. Each offence carries a maximum sentence of 12 months’ imprisonment.

It is very common for allegations of electoral offences to be reported to the police, especially by political rivals looking to score points. But the police should disregard the motives behind any report and focus on the conduct itself. They are not obliged to investigate every allegation, but will assess the evidence to determine whether a full investigation is justified.
