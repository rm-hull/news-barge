North East allotments could help protect communities from the effects of climate change, according to a leading plot holder.

Guy Herzmark, the National Allotment Society’s northern region spokesperson, says the region’s thriving growing community could help withstand future food shortages and rising prices.

Mr Herzmark said: “More droughts at home and abroad may well lead to the scarcity of some foods and rising prices but productive allotments are a guard against that, and we may even see more created as a result because they provide food when food prices are going up.”

Mr Herzmark said the abundance of fruit and vegetables grown across the region shows how valuable allotments can be.

  Produce harvested from Guy Herzmark's allotment, where he grows around 60 different crops 

*(Image: GSA MEDIA)*

He grows about 60 crops on his own plot, including potatoes, tomatoes, peppers and aubergines, with help from a greenhouse.

The North East has the highest proportion of allotments per person in the country, with County Durham and Redcar and Cleveland topping the list.

The region largely escaped the drought seen elsewhere during this summer’s heatwave, but Mr Herzmark said plots still needed far more watering than in previous years.

Durham County Council manages more than 1,200 allotments, with waiting lists in Durham City, Chester-le-Street and Bishop Auckland.

Demand is also rising in Stockton and Middlesbrough, where applications at some sites have doubled in five years.

Mr Herzmark, who chairs Newcastle’s West Jesmond Allotments Association and represents North East allotments for the National Allotment Society, said: “On an allotment you have many different plants growing, and these plants are providing a very helpful service, not only to the people on the allotment but to everybody else who goes anywhere near an allotment.

  Allotment holder Guy Herzmark says growing your own food can help communities adapt to climate change 

*(Image: GSA MEDIA)*

“They provide a green space which is actively used by people and which people can join, giving them an opportunity to be, in effect, in the countryside inside the city.”

Allotments are becoming more popular with women, despite traditionally having mostly male plot holders.

At West Jesmond allotments, which has a waiting list of about 20 people, the gender split among tenants is roughly equal.

Mr Herzmark, who says allotments can tackle “many of life’s problems – loneliness, stress, physical unfitness”, is calling for more plots to be created.

“Allotments are a good thing, and we need more,” he said.

“They’re good for people, good for towns, good for the planet.”
