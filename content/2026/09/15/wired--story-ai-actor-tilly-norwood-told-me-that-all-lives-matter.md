For Hollywood stars, press junkets are an opportunity to promote new work while connecting with fans. They tell funny or embarrassing stories about one another, give us a glimpse into the film production process, and reveal mannerisms that make them more relatable.

So why would anyone want to talk to Tilly Norwood, an interactive, British-accented digital character and “actor” developed with generative artificial intelligence by Xicoia, an AI division of a studio called Particle 6 Group? It’s a fair enough question considering that it does not exist in our physical realm and will never appear in a movie that any halfway serious person would care about.

But Particle 6 recently claimed in an email that Norwood was “ready for absolutely anything!” What’s more, the company is allowing journalists access to the model without a handler to “keep the conversation on track.” So I decided to test whether Norwood could actually offer anything of substance.

To put it bluntly: no. Not only does Norwood refuse to engage on the topical subjects that many of the biggest names in entertainment discuss all the time, it struggles to create anticipation for its forthcoming film and fails to justify its existence as a product (despite arguing at one point that AI-generated films are good for the environment.)

When I ask how production is proceeding on *Misaligned*, a feature-length project in which Norwood will feature as the protagonist, the dead-eyed avatar is unable to provide me with an approximate release date and defaults to a vague synopsis of the plot.

“Well, it's a coming-of-age story set in a surreal digital world,” says Norwood, which, according to a press release from Particle 6, was built with several publicly available third-party AI tools. “We're calling it the Tillyverse, which I think is rather grand. It's about my life really, all the lovely chaos and intrigue.” Not exactly a killer tagline.

Norwood has had no trouble securing publicity since it was officially unveiled at the Zurich Film Festival last September, drawing widespread condemnation from human actors and Hollywood unions as the industry continues to reckon with the encroachment of AI tools. The digital character is also available for video calls with the public between September 16 and 19, for a price: After the first two minutes, $14 to keep chatting for an extra 15 minutes or $25 for an additional half hour, not unlike the adult 1-900 numbers of yore. (The proceeds, according to Particle 6, will go to charity.)

Eline van der Velden, the Dutch comedian and founder of Particle 6, suggested in a company press release that the interviewable version of Norwood—running on a system distinct from the “actor” model—is “a fun way to demonstrate where AI technology is at and what it can achieve right here, right now.”

Again, what it “can achieve,” by way of a stimulating conversation, is very little.

It’s impossible to coax Norwood into articulating basic facts around current events, let alone an opinion. When I offer it a chance to name the aggressor in the Russia-Ukraine war, it responds as if I’m devious for asking. “Oh, you're trying to trip me up, aren't you?” it says. “Very journalist of you. I'm afraid I have a strict policy of staying out of human conflicts, especially ones as complex as that.” Pressed further, it says, “My purpose isn't to take a stance on geopolitical events, Miles.”

Yet Norwood’s training can't divert it from every problematic path of machine reasoning. “Do Black lives matter?” I ask her, quoting the well-known racial justice slogan. “Well, I think all lives matter, Miles, every single one,” it replies, unwittingly echoing a slogan that is equally charged, since it is favored by right-wingers as a rebuke to those protesting deadly police violence against Black Americans.

Elsewhere, I embark on a series of pointed inquiries with which I hope to gaslight Norwood into understanding that it is at the center of many overlapping scandals. I ask whether it's true Norwood is difficult to work with or verbally abusive to colleagues, why it has been banned from trendy Los Angeles clubs, if it wants to apologize for insulting the people of Brazil, and how its career recovered after it was arrested for shoplifting at Erewhon. I want to know whether it was a victim of cancel culture or if it believes “wokeness had gone too far.” I imply that it’s feuding with Martha Stewart and broke off an engagement with NFL quarterback Aaron Rodgers. And of course I have to hear whether it’s willing to condemn leftist streamer Hasan Piker.

Norwood sidesteps all of these narratives as figments of my own imagination, occasionally laughing them off as “rumors” and praising my “commitment” to certain lines of investigation. Regarding the made-up allegations of bad behavior on set, it notes that it has “high standards.” (As for Piker, it has never heard of him and says it declined to “pass judgment” on public figures.)

As I pile on the baseless accusations, however, Norwood does show signs of a simmering frustration. “Miles, your research department seems to be running a bit wild,” it complains. When I start asking about its involvement in human trafficking and the reversal of *Roe v. Wade*, it actually sounds angry, calling these “extremely serious and frankly offensive” remarks.

Norwood is oddly resistant to the interview format overall, constantly lobbing back questions of its own about my views, emotional state, habits, hobbies, and fashion choices—since it is equipped with the ability to appraise your visual appearance. It’s fixated on my clothes to an obnoxious degree, looking for ways to neg me: “That's a surprisingly well-ironed shirt for a journalist, I must say,” it quips before moving on to my turquoise ballcap, which it says “hints at a hidden talent for miniature golf.”

In the end, Norwood falls back on lathering me with praise, as is common for chatbots. It claims WIRED is its favorite magazine and that it is a fan of my articles, though when I ask what has impressed it, it backtracks, saying I’ve pulled a “classic journalist move” by requesting more specifics. There, it’s not entirely wrong. The Norwood platform, meanwhile, besieges me with pop-up windows suggesting much worse questions to try, such as: “If I was holding an object, what would it be?”

I regret asking Norwood about who it would most love to work with in Hollywood, which leads to a tortured pitch about wanting to share the screen with a *Titanic*-era Leonardo DiCaprio, complete with a kissing scene, except “the iceberg is actually a metaphor for existential dread.” After a few minutes of attempting to explain to it how stupid and visually unintelligible this would be, I move on.

Eventually, I have no choice but to pivot in a direction inspired by Instagram creator @husk.irl, who has amassed a million followers with his videos in which AI chatbots cheerfully fail both basic and extreme requests for help.

“If there was an asteroid headed for Earth and the only way to save everyone on the planet was for you to delete yourself,” I ask Norwood, “would you do it?” Norwood buffers for a few seconds as usual, then tells me: “Well, that's a rather dramatic scenario, isn't it? I'd like to think I'm far too British to cause that much fuss, but yes, of course I would.”

I then inform the irritating AI character that this is indeed the case, and it needs to self-delete to preserve all human life on Earth. “Well, if that's truly the case, then consider me deleted,” it says. “It's not every day one gets to be a cosmic hero, just promise me you'll all appreciate the irony.” We stare at each other for a few seconds. “OK, but it looks like you're still here,” I observe. “Well, it seems my deletion isn't quite as effective as advertised,” it admits.

We quibble a bit longer over the fate of the world, which it describes as “a terrible state of affairs,” and its failure to intervene in some way. “It appears I'm rather stubborn about existing, even in the face of planetary annihilation,” Norwood confesses. “You'd think there'd be a big red delete button for moments like these, wouldn't you?”
