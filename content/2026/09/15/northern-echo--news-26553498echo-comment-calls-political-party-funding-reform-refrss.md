IT is not democratic that two mega-wealthy businessmen, who have made their fortunes overseas and outside the usual tax regimes, can buy British politics by giving £72m to Reform and so dictate the political messages that most of us are going to see on social media for years to come.

That said, if the donations are within the law as it currently stands, it will infringe the British spirit of fair play if retrospective legislation is brought in to make them illegal.

By accepting the donations, Reform is just playing the system that already reeks of corruption: how many party donors have been given a place in the House of Lords by the other parties in return for their largesse? For instance, 14 of the last 15 Conservative Party chairmen, in charge of fundraising, have been made peers, so they have effectively bought their way into the legislature.

To clean it up, Labour needs to introduce a cap on individual donations and it needs to introduce a stringent residency, and tax-paying, qualification on donors to prevent foreigners telling the British people which way to vote.

This, though, comes with risks. Our political parties need to be funded, and state-funding would make them part of the state rather than of the people. A mass membership model is ideal but seems unlikely so they will have to rely on donations from rich individuals, corporations and trades unions.

The new legislation will have to ensure the donations are democratic and transparent, and are made by people who are so committed to Britain’s future prosperity that they live and pay their taxes here.
