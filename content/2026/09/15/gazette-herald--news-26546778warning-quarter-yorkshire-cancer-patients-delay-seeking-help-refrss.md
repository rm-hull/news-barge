People across Yorkshire are being urged to contact their GP without delay if they have concerns about their health after new figures revealed a quarter of cancer patients waited more than three months to seek medical advice after first noticing something might be wrong.

The call comes from Yorkshire Cancer Research, which highlighted that 25 per cent of people in the region diagnosed with cancer had delayed contacting their GP for more than three months after first suspecting a problem.

Early diagnosis can improve treatment options and survival rates, and cancers are often diagnosed through a route starting with a GP appointment.

  After her own breast cancer diagnosis, Dr Emily Lunn conducted research into the barriers many women face when it comes to attending healthcare appointments 

*(Image: Supplied)*

Dr Stuart Griffiths, director of research, policy and impact at Yorkshire Cancer Research, said: "Yorkshire Cancer Research understands there are many reasons why people may delay contacting their GP.

"People lead busy lives, symptoms can be easy to overlook and some may feel worried about what they might hear.

"However, if something doesn't feel right, it's crucial to seek medical advice.

"Most people who contact their GP with a health concern will not have cancer, but if cancer is present, detecting it at an earlier stage can make a life-saving difference.

"Early diagnosis can mean more treatment options are available, offering the best chance of cure.

"Yorkshire Cancer Research wants everyone in Yorkshire to feel confident contacting their GP if something isn’t right.

"Alongside raising awareness about the importance of early diagnosis, the charity funds vital research to find innovative ways to detect cancer earlier and better understand the barriers that can prevent people from seeking help, so that more people can receive a diagnosis as early as possible."

The charity’s analysis of the National Cancer Patient Experience Survey shows notable variation across the region.

In Wakefield and North Kirklees, 34 per cent of patients waited more than three months to contact their GP, compared to just 17 per cent in Harrogate.

Delays were more common in deprived areas and among some minority communities, including Gypsy, Traveller and Roma (GTR) groups.

Yorkshire Cancer Research is working with the University of York and community partners to better understand these barriers and improve access to early diagnosis.

This includes developing toolkits to support communication, informed decision-making and culturally appropriate care.

The charity also funds research into rural health inequalities.

The RURALLY study, based in North Yorkshire, found that long travel times, seasonal work, and financial concerns contributed to delays in seeking help for bowel cancer symptoms.

Dr Christina Dobson, lead researcher for the study, said: "Evidence suggests that people living in rural areas are less likely to survive cancer than those in urban areas.

"One of the reasons for this is that it takes longer for people in rural areas to be diagnosed with cancer."

Personal stories underline the importance of early action.

Jackie Buxton, a mother of two from Huby, saw her GP on the same day she discovered a lump in her breast.

She was diagnosed with a fast-growing type of breast cancer, but because it was caught early, she had effective treatment and is now cancer-free.

Ms Buxton said: "I was lucky to have my breast cancer diagnosed early, and I'm so grateful that I listened to my intuition and went to the doctor.

"You are never wasting a doctor's time when asking about changes in your body that worry you.

"The second you suspect something, make that appointment.

"It could save your life."

Dr Emily Lunn, a researcher from York, was diagnosed with stage 3 breast cancer after returning to her GP when she found a new lumpy area in her breast.

Dr Lunn said: "Everyday pressures, such as caring responsibilities, shift work and financial insecurity, often mean women feel they can’t take the time or risk losing income.

"By creating safe spaces and having open conversations about breast cancer, we can show why looking after your health and attending appointments matters – increasing early diagnosis and ultimately saving lives."

Yorkshire Cancer Research continues to raise awareness through its network of ambassadors and by funding research aimed at reducing health inequalities across the region.
