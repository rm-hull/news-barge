Irma Albrant is a job coach at the Institute for Vocational Education and Training (IBB) in Berlin, and is originally from Russia. She works with refugees, public authorities and employers. She has rarely experienced prejudice or rejection from Ukrainians because of her Russian roots. On the contrary, she says she is often met with gratitude. Her native language is Russian. One typical reaction was: “Finally, someone who speaks our language and can help us.” She points out that many Russians are married to Ukrainians, and her mother has a Ukrainian maiden name.

Albrant sees herself as a mediator and headed the IBB’s Ukraine project during its initial phase. Today, she works in the institute’s field team and is responsible for the northern region. She grew up in Lüneburg.

Refugees from Ukraine have a special status in Germany. They do not have to go through an asylum procedure, and their temporary protection has been extended until March 2027. In Berlin, once they have registered at the Reinickendorf arrival centre and applied for a residence permit, they immediately have the right to work without restrictions.

### In February, 22,697 Ukrainian Refugees Were Working in Berlin

According to the Central Register of Foreigners, 61,150 Ukrainian citizens of working age were living in Berlin in February. Of these, 27,432 were registered with Berlin’s job centres and employment agencies as able to work, including 11,670 who were registered as unemployed. The rest were taking part in integration and German courses, had low-paid jobs, attended school or were caring for children or relatives. According to the Federal Employment Agency, a total of 22,697 Ukrainian citizens were working in Berlin in February 2026. Of these, 20,225 held jobs subject to social security contributions and 2,472 were in marginal employment.

How well are Ukrainians integrating into Berlin’s labour market? Albrant says: “Some have found work, but mostly in jobs with low entry requirements and correspondingly low pay. Many have remained in this kind of work. There were manufacturing companies that hired large numbers of people, brought in someone to interpret and simply got started.”

### Many Work as Accountants or in Hotels

Ukrainian refugees have also found work in hotels and restaurants, including in housekeeping roles. Those who came to Berlin were almost exclusively women with children but no access to childcare, as well as older pensioners. “These people often attended language courses,” Albrant explains. The language barrier is the biggest obstacle to entering the labour market, according to Albrant.

Many women have found work as accountants. They had previously worked in this field in Ukraine and had the relevant qualifications. Many gained additional qualifications and learned German relatively quickly. There were also good opportunities to find related work in the IT sector.

### In Rural Areas, People Wait Up to Two Years for a Language Course

According to Albrant, language and integration courses are more readily available in large cities such as Berlin, with significantly fewer offered in rural areas. Waiting times there can be as long as two years. But even with a certificate, people’s language skills are often weaker in practice. Without these language skills, however, only poorly paid jobs are available.

Returning home is still a consideration for many Ukrainian refugees. But there are also mothers who want to stay here because they fear that their sons could be conscripted in Ukraine. For them, Germany is their new home. The majority of Ukrainians are now “completely settled here”, Albrant believes, have a flat and are in the process of integrating.
