A new 600-home estate in Yarm would cause “substantial and irreversible” harm and have “significant adverse impacts” on nearby roads, a council has argued.

Taylor Wimpey today (September 15) kicked off its appeal against the refusal of its contentious plan to build hundreds of new homes on fields south of Green Lane.

The developer said the “disappointing” decision to throw out the £133m plans was “striking”, arguing that there is an “acute and serious local need” for housing.

But Stockton Borough Council has argued that building on the “last significant tract of farmland” adjoining Yarm’s southern edge would be “unsustainable”.

The plans were initially rejected this year by councillors, who voted 13-1, following almost 600 objections and even a ‘Save Yarm’ petition.

Inspector George Underwood today heard opening remarks from legal representatives from both the developer and the council, as well as several residents.

Barrister John Hunter, for the council, said the farmland was an “important component of the town's setting and distinctness” and that it was “unsuitable” for so many new homes.

He told the inquiry, being held at the council’s headquarters at Dunedin House, in Thornaby, that the council agreed with Active Travel England (ATE) that issues with local highway networks have “still not been adequately assessed or mitigated”.

He added that concerns over the electricity network remain “unresolved”, given Northern Powergrid’s previous comment on how the area’s network has “limited capacity to support additional homes without upgrades”.

Mr Hunter also argued that the transport assessment was “not adequate to demonstrate that there will be no severe impacts", because it is "focussed entirely on journey times.”

It follows concerns over how the addition of two new junctions along Green Lane, featured in the plans, would impact traffic along Green Lane, Leven Bank, and the Crossways Roundabout.

   

*(Image: P+HS ARCHITECTS / TAYLOR WIMPEY)*

He said the council also agrees with ATE that the site “lacks proper integration with its surroundings” and that the hedgerow along Green Lane would “isolate” the development.

Mr Hunter also described the concerns surrounding electricity capacity as a “major capacity constraint” that “remains unresolved”.

“Approximately no more than 20 to 40 houses can be delivered without a new set of sub stations on the Mount Leven side,” he said.

“And that site does not have planning permission and the substation has not been subject of any previous scheme or on the site.”

   

*(Image: THE NORTHERN ECHO)*

The council's ecological concerns also remain unresolved.

Mr Hunter noted how increased footfall and dog activity would “bring risks of disturbance to wildlife” such as fire damage, unauthorised camping, and an improvised BMX track.

## 
  **Case in favour of appeal is ‘compelling’ - developer**

Sasha White KC, for Taylor Wimpey, cited a national housing crisis, the “most acute in living memory”, as a key reason as to why the application should be approved.

He told the inquiry that the council currently had "serious and significant shortfall" of new homes, at least 1,162 homes over a five-year period and 809 affordable homes since 2017/18.

He said this would require the release of sites outside the area's existing development limits, saying the council needs to green-lit seven to ten new sites of 120 or more home to enable delivery of a five-year supply.

Mr White also noted how “every statutory consultee with technical expertise supported the application” and that the case in favour of the appeal is "compelling".

   

*(Image: THE NORTHERN ECHO)*

He also acknowledged that residents “fear” the proposed estate - but that this happens in “every inquiry” he works on.

He added: “We also seek to give voice to those who do not currently have adequate housing.

“There's also other economic benefits, both direct and indirect, there will be an enhanced bus service with the extension of number seven, mobility hub, pedestrian, and active travel improvements, open space, and a kick about area.”

Addressing concerns over impact on roads, Mr White noted how National Highways did not object and that the council’s highways, transport, and design manager concluded residual impact on the network “would not be severe” following proposed mitigation measures.

He argued that the adverse effects “don’t come anywhere close” to the benefits.

“There will be contribution to the services and businesses in the vicinity,” he added.

Regarding the electricity capacity, Mr White said Northern Powergrid did not object but acknowledged the limited capacity of the network and said it would work with developers to facilitate a connection.

He said a short-term point of connection has been secured, which is sufficient for just 20 to 40 homes. Beyond that number, a new primary substation would be needed.

Mr White also thanked the audience, despite members “greatly disagreeing” on the proposals: “I thank them frankly for the immense respect that they've shown to put lots of things which they fundamentally disagree with.”

Stockton West MP Matt Vickers said on the day of the inquiry starting that the council was right to reject the plans, and that the town should not "swallow another 600 homes".

“I’ll keep standing up for Yarm and fighting these plans," he said.

"We need infrastructure before expansion - not hundreds more houses piled onto services that are already under strain."

The inquiry, which is due to last nine days, continues.
