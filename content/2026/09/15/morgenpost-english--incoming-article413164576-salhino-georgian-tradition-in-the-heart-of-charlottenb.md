Being awarded the Bib Gourmand feels like a birthday present, says Viktoria Shelia. This September marks ten years since her family began running the Georgian restaurant Salhino on Waitzstraße in Charlottenburg. When it opened, it was one of the first of its kind in Berlin. Today, it is the first restaurant serving Georgian regional cuisine to receive a Bib Gourmand from the Michelin Guide Germany for its high-quality ingredients and culinary craftsmanship, all at reasonable prices.

“Specialities such as khinkali […] may seem simple at first glance, but stand out with wonderful textures, balanced flavours and well-judged seasoning,” the inspectors’ verdict reads. “The good food is complemented by a pleasant atmosphere that is cosy yet slightly exotic, with attractive decorations such as nostalgic photographs and traditional accessories.”

Whether a restaurant like Salhino can still be considered “exotic” in Berlin today is certainly open to debate. One thing is certain: Salhino is a success story in Berlin’s often fast-moving restaurant scene. Viktoria Shelia cannot explain why the Michelin Guide has only now taken notice of the restaurant: “We have done everything just as we always have. We stand for consistently good quality.” Word of this has long since spread around the neighbourhood, and many regulars have been coming for years. The restaurant did, however, get off to a somewhat rocky start because of its location on a quiet side street with little passing trade. Since receiving the Bib Gourmand, its reservation book has been even fuller than usual. “We haven’t had a summer slump this year.”

### No One in the Family Has Formal Restaurant Training

Nearly the entire Shelia family moved to Berlin 22 years ago. They settled in Germany so they could reunite with Viktoria’s Ukrainian relatives, who were already living here at the time. Only the eldest daughter, Sophie, initially stayed behind. After a separation, she joined them in Berlin and, while initially helping out in the kitchen, discovered her keen sense of smell and her talent for working out combinations of flavours in her head. Today, she and her father Elguja jointly head the culinary side of Salhino.

No one in the Shelia family has formal training in the restaurant industry. Elguja originally trained as a lawyer, Viktoria as a computer scientist. Nevertheless, everyone now pitches in at the restaurant. Daughter Kristina handles marketing and communications, brother Boris works front of house, and mother Viktoria is responsible for the finances. “But actually, everyone here does everything,” says Kristina Shelia. “A family business only works if everyone pulls together. We are like an insurance policy for one another.”

> We Work Almost Like an Embassy.
>
> Kristina Shelia

Their shared motivation is a passion for food and a desire to bring some of their homeland’s traditions into the wider world. “We work almost like an embassy,” Kristina Shelia says with a laugh. When she started school after moving to Berlin, hardly anyone knew anything about Georgia, let alone the country’s cuisine. That is different today.

Writer and director Nino Haratischwili, who has received numerous awards for her work in Germany, has also contributed to this newfound popularity. But current trends are working in the restaurant’s favour too. “The concept of sharing plates is part of our culture; we have done it that way from the very beginning,” says Kristina Shelia. Georgian cuisine also naturally includes many vegetarian and vegan dishes.

### No Experiments with the Classics

The menu at Salhino is largely shaped by the cuisine of Mingrelia, the region in western Georgia where the family comes from. Classics found throughout Georgia include khachapuri, a flatbread filled with cheese before baking; khinkali, dumplings filled with minced meat or cheese; and mtsvadi, grilled skewers of chicken, lamb, pork or sturgeon. “We don’t experiment with these dishes,” says Sophie Shelia. That means no adapting them to trends or German tastes. “Anyone coming to us for the first time may be surprised by the intensity of the flavours. We don’t cook with just salt and pepper.” Typical spices such as coriander seeds, blue fenugreek, dill, marjoram, savory and saffron are therefore imported from Georgia. For all other products, Elguja and Sophie Shelia use what is available in Germany. “Especially when it comes to meat and vegetables, it’s a compromise. I often miss the intensity of the flavour,” says Sophie Shelia.

To ensure that the intended message, correct pronunciation and handling are conveyed to guests exactly as intended in the kitchen, all employees at the restaurant have at least some Georgian background. Despite the staff shortages in the restaurant industry, they have never had trouble recruiting staff under this policy, says Kristina Shelia. This is particularly notable given that Berlin’s Georgian community, with only around 5,000 Georgian nationals, is relatively small.

### Georgia Has an 8,000-Year-Old Winemaking Tradition

The small size of Berlin’s Georgian community is also reflected in the clientele at Salhino. “Eighty percent are Germans,” says Viktoria Shelia. Most of them are open-minded and curious about the flavours of another country. As a result, the service staff, who are explicitly praised by the Michelin Guide, often provide guests with inspiration and advice.

At Salhino, both the team and the wine list are exclusively Georgian. The country’s winemaking tradition stretches back as far as 8,000 years, making it one of the oldest in the world. The traditional method of fermenting wine in clay amphorae buried in the ground is still practised today, particularly in the Kakheti region. During skin-contact fermentation, the skins and seeds remain in contact with the wine for an extended period. This gives the wine a pronounced tannic structure. White wines also develop a distinctive amber to orange colour.

The name of the restaurant, incidentally, means “a place that brings joy”, Kristina Shelia explains. “One special feature of the Georgian language is that a great deal of information can be contained in just one word.” The fitting motto hangs on the wall of the dining room at Salhino in the form of a wood carving: “A well-set table is a joy” – written in the Georgian Mkhedruli script and therefore readable only to those familiar with it.

A short time later, Viktoria Shelia personally demonstrates Georgian hospitality when a neighbour enters the restaurant before it has opened. Can he still get a table on such short notice? He can – even though the evening is actually fully booked. “We don’t say ‘no’ to neighbours and friends,” says Shelia.
