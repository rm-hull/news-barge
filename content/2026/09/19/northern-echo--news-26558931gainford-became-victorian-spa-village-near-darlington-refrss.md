A County Durham village familiar to thousands of drivers travelling between Darlington and Barnard Castle once had ambitions to become something grander.

Gainford, on the banks of the River Tees, became a destination for Victorian visitors who travelled to the village to "take the waters" from its sulphur-rich spring.

Today the village is better known for its picturesque green, historic church, stone houses and location on the A67.

But tucked beside the River Tees is a surviving reminder of a period when Gainford joined Britain's Victorian enthusiasm for health resorts and mineral spas.

![]()Gainford *(Image: NORTHERN PERSPECTIVES/CAMERA CLUB)*

The story began when sulphurous water was discovered close to the river.

The water's distinctive smell may not have immediately suggested a luxury attraction, but mineral springs were highly fashionable during the 19th century.

Visitors were encouraged to drink spa water in the belief that it could help with a variety of illnesses.

Gainford and Langton Parish Council says the sulphur water was "claimed to assist conditions including indigestion, liver problems and chronic skin complaints."

The arrival of the railway helped turn the spring into a genuine visitor attraction.

Gainford railway station opened in 1856 on the line linking Darlington with Barnard Castle.

The parish council says visitors "subsequently travelled to the village, while substantial guesthouses were built on higher ground above the station to accommodate people who had come to take the waters."

Gainford never became another Harrogate or Bath.

But, for a period, its spa formed an important part of village life and the local tourist trade.

The final Spa Boarding House on the village green closed in 1910, according to the parish council, and the attraction faded further during the First World War.

The railway itself survived considerably longer, although Gainford station eventually closed to passengers in 1964.

The sulphur spring did not disappear with the tourists.

Parts of the old stonework survived into the 20th century, despite suffering vandalism and damage.

In 2000, some stonework was recovered from the River Tees by divers, and two years later villagers helped restore the spa spout.

It means traces of Gainford's days as a miniature spa destination can still be found today.

The village has history stretching back considerably further than the Victorians.

Its parish council records link with St Cuthbert and Anglo-Saxon Northumbria, while archaeological finds have included Viking sculpture from Gainford.

At the centre of the modern village is the large green with St Mary's Church nearby, a scene recorded in photographs and postcards for more than a century in Durham's historical collections.

Gainford sits around eight miles west of Darlington and remains a prominent settlement on the route towards Barnard Castle and Teesdale.

Thousands may pass through or close to it without realising that visitors once came for an altogether different reason.

For Victorian travellers stepping off the train, Gainford was not simply another Teesdale village. For a brief period, it was a spa destination.
