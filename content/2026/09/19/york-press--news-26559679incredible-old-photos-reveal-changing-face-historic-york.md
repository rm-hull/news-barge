## Incredible old photos from York’s past that you wouldn't see on a postcard

Parliament Street from a glass negative taken before the first world war. The men lining up could be war veterans from the Boer War or the South Africa War.

We’ve had a rummage through our archives and pulled out these fabulous old photos for our latest collection.

The common theme is that the images highlight the many sides to York, capturing moments in time that have shaped the city.

York has always been a city of layers with its Roman walls beneath medieval streets, gas lamps beside electric trams, chocolate factories within sight of the Minster.

The earliest image here is taken from a glass negative of Parliament Street, captured before the First World War - more than a century before people viewed everything from the ordinary to extraordinary through the likes of Instagram.

![]()Parliament Street from a glass negative taken before the first world war. The men lining up could be war veterans from the Boer War or the South Africa War.

Men line the pavement in orderly ranks, hats on, coats buttoned, postures stiff with formality. They may have been veterans of the Second Boer War (1899–1902), gathered for a parade or commemoration. If so, their presence on this bustling shopping street would have been a quiet reminder that the empire’s conflicts were never far from home. And many of their sons would have found themselves marching down the same street towards new uniforms, new trenches, and new losses not longer afterwards.

![]()September 10 1957, the scene at the junction of Parliament Street and Pavement as workmen carry out repairs to the roundabout.

By 1957, York was rebuilding itself for the motor age. On 10 September that year, workmen were photographed at the junction of Parliament Street and Pavement, repairing the roundabout that had become a pivot of city traffic. In the background, shopfronts are partly obscured by buses and lorries. For those who remembered horse-drawn carts on the same cobbles, the scene might have felt strangely futuristic.

![]()An unusual aerial view of Clifford's Tower and the city beyond, taken on March 17, 1983.

The aerial view of Clifford’s Tower from March 17, 1983, offers a perspective most York residents never saw in daily life. From above, the tower’s quatrefoil plan - overlapping circles, resembling a four leafed clover - is clear as the city spreads out around it in a patchwork of roofs, yards and chimneys. It is a view that emphasises how compact the historic core is and how surrounded it had become by postwar housing, industry and new roads.

Readers of the time might have felt a sense of pride at the panorama; others could have questioned how much more change could the centre could take.

![]()October 1975: Queues stretched outside York's newly-opened National Railway Museum as thousands flocked to the attraction. The Evening Press reported that the huge crowds brought traffic chaos to the city in the museum's first weeks.

Dialling back slightly to October 1975, York witnessed a different kind of landmark on its opening day. As the National Railway Museum welcomed its first visitors, queues stretched far beyond the entrance, snaking along the streets of Leeman Road and beyond. The Evening Press reported 'traffic chaos' - a common complaint to this day - as thousands flocked to see locomotives that had once defined national life. For a city already used to railway heritage, the scene confirmed that industrial history could draw crowds as reliably as any royal visit.

![]()1974: A bird's-eye view from the top of St Denys's Church captures a changing York, with industrial buildings and rooftops stretching across Walmgate towards the city centre.

The 1974 view from St Denys’s Church, Walmgate, captures a city mid-transition. From the tower, the eye travels over a landscape of industrial buildings, terraced housing and factory rooftops stretching towards the centre.

Clifford's Tower, factories and countless smaller workshops still dominated the skyline. Within a generation, many would be gone, replaced by housing, offices or open ground. The photograph, like so many, is a time capsule of a working city that has changed significantly over the decades.

![]()November 1983: Concerns had been raised about litter levels around York Market. *(Image: Newsquest)*

Taken together, these images paint a picture of a city honouring its veterans, adapting its streets for cars, celebrating its railway past, and watching its industrial present slowly recede.

None show the Minster or the Shambles, York’s usual postcard views.

Instead, they feature ordinary streets, working buildings, even the age-old problems of litter on the streets as the camera recorded moments and milestones in history.
