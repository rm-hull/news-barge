IN 1974, when the Keighley artist, Stan Boardman, exhibited his paintings of childhood memories at Cliffe Castle in Keighley, more than 7,000 visitors flocked to the museum and gallery to see them.

The exhibition later toured the district, visiting churches, the local technical college, Airedale Hospital, and Bradford Central Library.

It was estimated that 22,000 local people in total saw Stan’s artwork.

The exhibition later moved to a London gallery, attracting wider attention, and Stan was catapulted to national fame by television programmes about his life and work.

In September and October 1986, when the Shipley artist George Brumfitt displayed a series of nostalgia paintings based on memories of his local childhood, hundreds of people visited the Doncaster Museum and Art Gallery to see them.

Paintings that capture childhood memories are timelessly popular. For older people, they evoke a time when the present age of the viewer - perhaps now in his or her sixties, seventies or eighties - seemed a long way off.

They capture that sense of freedom and lack of inhibition - of running around a playground, splashing in the sea, or engaging in a snowball fight - that is, or should be, the essence of childhood.

The Bradford-born artist, Sue Atkinson was particularly adept at capturing the energy of childhood.

Her impressionistic painting, Snowy Playground, for example, is a blur of action as the children run, slide and pelt each other with snow, yet are cocooned against the cold in their red coats.

![]()Snowy Playground by Sue Atkinson.

Her paintings, by contrast, of summer by the seaside catch the unclothed freedom of the beach on a sunny day.

![]()Hot Day in Sandsend, by Sue Atkinson, captures the freedom of the beach.

Childhood paintings can also capture a sense of discovery about the world, including its darker edges.

![]()Oxo Kids by Stan Boardman.

Stan Boardman’s artwork, for example, was generally painted in a light-hearted style.

Yet there was also a sharp edge to a number of his paintings that depicted the harsher side of life in 1920s Keighley.

His paintings of limbless ex-servicemen, or shaven-headed orphans marching into their institution, display a recognition that fate can be hard and fickle; Stan was lucky, loved, and healthy, but others were not as fortunate.

![]()Orphans by Stan Boardman, whose paintings depicted the harsher side of life in the 1920s.

The restrictions and penalties imposed on children by adults can feature occasionally in paintings.

George Brumfitt’s Things Past series, all depicting his Aunt Hilda, includes the painting: Hilda’s Warning: ‘Mind you’re in by half-past seven; it’s bath night!’, and the follow-up: ‘You were having your way, but I was having mine - and I always won,’ that shows Hilda giving George a good hiding for defying her orders!

![]()George Brumfitt's Things Past series depict his Aunt Hilda. Image: Doncaster Museum Service. *(Image: Doncaster Museum Service)*

So nostalgia paintings of childhood give adults the chance and space to recall memories from their own past, both positive and negative.

The future may be uncertain, but the past is there, giving us a sense of identity and stability in the present.

We have been there, we remember, it is our history, our experience; we are alive, our memories have moulded us into who we are today.

Childhood nostalgia paintings can also give us an emotional connection with the children of today.

Although they may have modern gadgets to distract them, there is a timeless affinity between young and old to sharing a day on the beach, or playing outdoors in the snow, or at a bonfire.

For children, these nostalgia paintings are a form of social history, bringing the past to life in colour - and perhaps reminding them that the old weren’t always old!

And although Stan Boardman, George Brumfitt and Sue Atkinson have all now died, the tradition of childhood nostalgia painting carries on today in the work of contemporary professional local artists, such as Bob Barker and Danny Abrahams.
