IN olden times, on the road between Darlington and Haughton, there used to be a steep-sided, tree-lined dip with a streamlet running through it and a barquest haunting it.

The barquest was known locally as the "roaring ghost" and its howls at night kept anyone within earshot awake – and made them quake with fear, for its terrible noise was a harbinger of death.

According to a Victorian historian, the dip was "a most uncanny-looking little glen" near Throstle Nest, an old farm that’s site is now a little grassy area on the highest point of Haughton Road.

Here in the glen, he says, "the maiden, the cat and other shapes gathered around the luckless traveller". Because the barquest could take on many shapes.

![]()Is this the Barquest of Throstlenest? *(Image: Unknown)*

Usually it was a super-large black dog, known to some as "Padfoot" due to the quiet, stealthy manner in which it approached its victims. Other times it was a beautiful maiden or a friendly cat which would lead the unfortunate traveller into the glen – and to their deaths.

Then the Barquest of Throstlenest would roar its awful sound and next morning, when daylight broke, the people of the area would find someone dead within their midst.

The streamlet that ran through the glen never seems to have had a name, but it rose at the rear of St Aidan’s Academy on Hundens Lane, ran under the Stockton & Darlington Railway (now the Eastern Transport Corridor) and then became quite steep and deep as it ran down what is now Blackett Road until it crossed Haughton Road and then trickled its way through the plashy pastures down to the Skerne.

A good 200 years ago, the glen was filled in to stop Haughton Road from being regularly under water and also to get rid of the dreaded barquest. During the work, it is said that the remains of many human bodies were found in it.

They may have been the victims of the barquest but they were taken as proof of another old legend, this time that the district had once been the scene of a bloody skirmish between Scottish raiders and English troops.

In the centuries up to the Battle of Neville’s Cross in 1346, the Tees Valley often suffered the attentions of marauding Scots, brazenly and violently invading from the north.

They came again in the troubled times of the 1640s and during the Jacobite Rising of 1745.

In one of these actions, it is said that a young Scottish soldier – a Highland laddie – was injured and left behind by his fleeing compatriots who feared that they would end up in the uncanny glen with the barquest.

Rather than leave the young chap to bleed to death, the kind-hearted villagers of Haughton took him in – even though he was the enemy – and nursed him back to health. That’s why to this day the village pub is called the Highland Laddie.

* Throstle, of course, is an old word for a songthrush.

BLACKETTS the builders had the last working brickyard in Darlington. It was off Haughton Road, between Blackett Road and Throstlenest Avenue, and it employed 35 brickmakers up to its closure in 1958 (Memories 796).

Today, at the Blackett Road end, an industrial estate is on its site. At the Throstlenest Avenue end, housing has gradually encroached onto its land, with the last houses of Frosterley Drive and Rookhope Court going up in 2016.

"My son lives in one of a pair of semi-detached houses in Throstlenest Avenue and his garden backs onto Frosterley Drive," says David Pickup. "When he bought it, he was told the house had been converted from a former builder's office.

"I therefore assume this was the office for Blackett's brickyard."

BLACKETTS of Darlington began building in the 1830s but they weren’t the only building Blacketts in the area: John Askwith points us in the direction of W & R Blackett who were "experienced townsmen" of Bishop Auckland when they were given the contract to rebuild the town station in 1889.

This was a big contract – £13,000, which is £1.5m in today’s values – as befitted a station that had grown into a major railway hub.

It was such a big contract that when it was announced, The Northern Echo said that when it was complete it would be impossible "to persuade travellers and townsfolk that they are at Bishop Auckland".

Blacketts built the 22ft high clocktower and the 8ft wide footbridge over to the Durham platforms. They widened the Station Approach and built the central station buildings which included "a porter’s room, telegraph, left luggage and ticket offices, general waiting room, rooms for porters, lamps and foot warmers, and a parcel office".

The Echo said: "The buildings will be of brick, with stone dressings, the platforms will be relaid with cement, and the general waiting room will be lined with cream-coloured glazed bricks and chocolate bands. The platforms and approaches will be fitted with new lamps which, like every other appointment of the new buildings, will be of the best design on the most modern principles."

NER had adopted a two-tone colour scheme for its stations of this period with chocolate coloured glazed bricks usually on the lower half of waiting rooms and subways and cream ones above.

Glazed bricks resisted soot and smuts from the trains and could be easily cleaned.

From about 1879, NER used Burmantofts glazed bricks in its stations. Burmantofts in Leeds had started making ceramic pipes but had grown into making art pottery and decorative architectural ceramics.

And, yes, its bricks in Bishop Auckland did have "Burmantofts" stamped onto them.

You can still see the two-tone chocolate-and-cream NER colour scheme in Newcastle and York stations, but it would be fascinating to know if anyone had any salvaged NER Burmantofts bricks …

![]()Michael Stead's imprinting find from Shincliffe colliery *(Image: Michael Stead)*

"I HAVE had in my possession, for 45 years since I found it at Shincliffe Colliery pit pond, a curved brass plaque stating 'B. Clark Maker Durham' written in reverse, with a border," says Michael Stead, who was so proud of his find that he once made it into a belt buckle.

And you can see why. It is every bit as special as a NER Burmantoft brick.

It is clearly an imprinting device, but at 10cm by 7cm, it is too small to be for stamping the maker’s name into the frog of a brick – the frog is the indentation which reduced the amount of clay in the brick and provided a key for the mortar.

It’s also curved which doesn’t seem right for making something as flat and square as a brick.

So what is it? "I hope you may have some idea," says Michael.

We don’t. Can anyone help?

ON our recent guided walk of Haughton, which raised money for the village’s Christmas lights appeal, we were delighted to be told about Stanley House which is next to the post office.

It was infilled between two terraces in 1915 – on our postcard view with the cyclists heading towards the photographer, the gap between the terraces where it was built is clearly visible.

![]()Haughton Green today, with the post office on the right *(Image: Google StreetView)*

Around the time the house was built, we were told, the village schoolmaster and rector used a chain to bring to life the world’s most famous maritime disaster.

With their chain, they worked out that from the church gates right along the green to the gate of Stanley House was 882ft 9ins (they wouldn’t have understood that this is equivalent to 269.1 metres) and that is the length of the Titanic which struck an iceberg on April 14, 1912, and sank with the loss of about 1,500 people.

To intensify the interest of the children of Haughton who were presented with this golden nugget of information, large pieces of the White Star liner, including the rudder and the stern frame, were made just down the road on Albert Hill by Darlington Forge.

You’ll never be able to look at Haughton green in the same way now!

![]()The Titanic: nearly as long as Haughton Green *(Image: Unknown)*

IT is now believed that about 1,496 people died on the Titanic, including the Echo’s legendary former editor, WT Stead.

In terms of greatest disasters, the sinking has been overtaken by the Ferry Neptune which went down off Haiti with the loss of about 1,500 lives in 1993, the Salem Express ferry which went down on its voyage from Saudi Arabia to Egypt in 1991 with about 1,600 people on board, and the Dona Paz which was sailing between Philippine islands in 1987 when it collided with an oil tanker, and at least 4,386 people were lost.

These pale in comparison to the greatest military maritime disaster in January 1945 when a Soviet submarine sunk MV Wilhelm Gustloff, a German military transport ship, in the Baltic Sea. It was evacuating civilians and military personnel from places that the Russians were overrunning. It is now believed 9,343 people drowned.
