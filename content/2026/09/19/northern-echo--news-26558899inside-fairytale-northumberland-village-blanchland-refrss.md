Hidden among the hills on the Northumberland and County Durham border is a tiny village where modern Britain can seem a surprisingly long way away.

Blanchland is made up of honey-coloured stone cottages, cobbled corners and historic buildings surrounded by the dramatic countryside of the North Pennines.

There are no sprawling shopping streets or modern chain stores.

Instead, much of the village's character can be traced back hundreds of years to the religious community from which Blanchland grew.

The settlement developed around Blanchland Abbey, which was founded in 1165 by Walter de Bolbec.

Visit Northumberland says the name itself is believed to come from the white habits worn by the Premonstratensian canons who lived at the abbey, with “blanche” meaning white in French.

The abbey was dissolved during the reign of Henry VIII, but its story did not end there.

Stone from the former monastic buildings was reused in the village, helping to create the distinctive settlement that survives today.

That history gives Blanchland an appearance different from many modern villages.

Visit Northumberland describes its combination of stone, cobbles, narrow lanes and historic properties as one of the prettiest village settings in northern England.

At its heart stands Blanchland Abbey Church, while another of its best-known landmarks is the Lord Crewe Arms.

The historic inn occupies buildings connected with the former abbey and has its own colourful past.

One story links it with the Jacobite rising of 1715.

General Thomas Forster, a Northumberland landowner who became one of the English leaders of the rebellion, is traditionally said to have hidden behind the inn’s enormous fireplace.

The village has another royal connection dating from considerably earlier.

Edward III visited Blanchland in 1327 during a campaign against the Scots.

Despite those turbulent moments in its past, Blanchland today is better known for quiet walks, countryside and its unusually preserved appearance.

The village sits within the North Pennines National Landscape, close to Derwent Reservoir and surrounded by moorland, woodland and fell country.

Its relatively isolated position has helped it retain much of the atmosphere that gives it its reputation as a “storybook” settlement.

There are reminders, however, that the village is still very much alive rather than an outdoor museum.

Visitors can find the village shop and tearoom, church, accommodation and the Lord Crewe Arms among its historic buildings.

For people accustomed to the larger Northumberland attractions around Bamburgh, Alnwick and the coast, Blanchland offers a very different side to the county.
