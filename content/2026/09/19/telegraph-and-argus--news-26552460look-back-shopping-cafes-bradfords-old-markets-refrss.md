THERE was a time when going into Bradford city centre meant visiting the markets. That was the point of the journey.

For generations, the local markets were places to stock up on meat and veg, buy a whole range of cheap, colourful and useful things, perhaps banter over prices - and, above all else, sit down for a plate of pie and peas.

Bradford’s market tradition runs deep. A royal charter for a Thursday market was granted in 1251, while the modern era developed with the growth of open-air markets in the 19th century. The great Kirkgate Market, designed by Lockwood and Mawson, opened in 1872. With its striking architecture and labyrinth of stalls, it became one of the city’s best-loved landmarks.

The extraordinary range of everyday goods on sale had to be seen to be believed. I remember row upon row of stalls bursting with drapery, boots and shoes, confectionery, food and groceries, books and records. It was as much a general shopping centre as a food hall.

Rawson Place Market specialised in food. By the latter part of the last century, more than two dozen butchers competed alongside fishmongers whose counters offered everything from salmon to sand eels.

John Street Market, completed in 1930, was another bustling shopping place. It was originally an open-air market and for decades provided another busy focus for shoppers and traders. Over the years the site was redeveloped and the market changed with the times. A major refurbishment was completed in 2002, when it became the Oastler Shopping Centre, named after Richard Oastler, the 19th-century Bradford factory reformer and campaigner. It eventually housed more than just market stalls, with shops and cafés adding to its appeal.

These were the places to encounter traditional Yorkshire food, much of it now largely disappeared from everyday shopping. Hey’s Tripe Stall, at Rawson Place, was renowned for offal and prize-winning black puddings. Changing tastes eventually contributed to its demise.

Yet, for many shoppers, the ultimate reward for a morning’s errands was a hot lunch. Nothing defined the Bradford market experience quite like its pie shops. At the top of the list was Pie Tom’s in Kirkgate Market. Tiny, cramped and entirely unpretentious, it was legendary among Bradford shoppers as the place to stand or sit down, often squashed in with everyone else, and eat pie and peas. A plate of meat pie, mushy peas and mint sauce, washed down with a mug of tea, was a familiar ritual. The more adventurous could add black pudding.

Over on Ivegate, Philip Smith’s pie shop was equally well loved, particularly for its pork pies and dripping tea cakes. When I was an office boy in the Treasury Loans’ Office, one of my jobs was to go out and queue with all the other juniors to buy these lardy tea cakes for the blokes in the office. On the final shopping day before Christmas, the queues could stretch right up the street, as customers waited patiently to buy a large stand-pie to enjoy on Boxing Day.

Behind Kirkgate Market on Godwin Street stood Robert’s pie shop. Its reputation became linked with JB Priestley after the shop was damaged during a German air raid in 1940. He recounted the incident during one of his celebrated wartime BBC Postscripts broadcasts, describing the steaming meat-and-potato pie that had survived in the damaged shop window. He used the image as a symbol of northern resilience during the Blitz.

![]()Shopping at the old Kirkgate Market.

Unfortunately, to its eternal shame, Bradford lost the magnificent Victorian Kirkgate Market, demolished in 1973 to make way for a new shopping centre. To say the decision was controversial would be an understatement, and the gross act of vandalism went ahead despite large-scale public petitioning and protesting. What is particularly galling is that the council should have learned its lesson in the 1960s, when the first wave of vandalism took away such fine buildings as the Swan Arcade and the Mechanics’ Institute.

![]()The old Kirkgate Market is still much missed *(Image: Newsquest)*

Mercifully, some of the market’s original decorative ironwork survived. Gates and ornamental panels were retained by Bradford Council after the demolition and have since been restored. They now form part of the new Darley Street Market, providing a tangible link with the Victorian market that once stood nearby.

Rawson Market survived longer. By the 1990s, redevelopment and changing circumstances were putting increasing pressure on the old market and its traders. Shopping itself was changing too. Supermarkets offered convenience and longer opening hours, while changing tastes made specialist stalls increasingly difficult to sustain.

![]()Checking out the fruit and veg at Rawson Market, pictured here in 1972.

These markets were busy, noisy places at all times of day. You could hear stallholders calling out their prices, particularly strident near closing time. The smell of raw meat hung in the air, and you had to navigate aisles crowded with people bearing away brown-paper parcels, oozing blood.

Pie Tom’s, Philip Smith’s, Hey’s Tripe Stall, Rawson Market and Kirkgate Market were part of the everyday life of Bradford. They fed the city’s people, but they also created memories that have lasted long after the stalls themselves disappeared.
