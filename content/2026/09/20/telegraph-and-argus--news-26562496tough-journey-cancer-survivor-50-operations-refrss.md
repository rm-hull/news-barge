A CANCER survivor has had to learn to walk again after gruelling treatment and more than 50 operations.

Ryann Lambert-Tuthill, from Otley, was diagnosed with Ewing sarcoma, a rare bone cancer, at just 22 months old.

He has undergone more than 50 operations and is supporting Childhood Cancer Awareness Month to highlight childhood cancer’s impact and the need for research.

Mr Lambert-Tuthill, who turned 35 on Friday, September 18, said: "The biggest side effect of my treatment and the numerous operations has always been my mobility.

![]()Ryann Lambert-Tuthill as a baby when he had his first surgery

"Growing up it was tricky being excluded from sports at school and I wasn’t able to play anything competitively.

"The only sport I was able to do was swimming.

![]()Ryann Lambert-Tuthill as a young child

"I love swimming and I still do this now.

"As I have got older, I have had to adapt my life and accept that I’m not able to do certain things due to my mobility.

"I have always walked with a limp but that's just the ordinary for me.

![]()Ryann Lambert-Tuthill and his wife Emily *(Image: UGC)*

"It has been tough, but I've not known anything different throughout my life and my current health is great.

"I don't really see the negatives of the side effects.

"I try to look at the positives in life and live life to the full."

![]()Ryann Lambert-Tuthill in hospital after surgery on his leg

Now living in Leeds with his wife Emily, Mr Lambert-Tuthill is believed to be the youngest patient to have had his femur replaced with a titanium bone.

His cancer journey began when his parents, Nick and Wendy, noticed swelling in his left leg.

After an X-ray at a local hospital, he was admitted to Leeds General Infirmary and transferred to Birmingham’s Royal Orthopaedic Hospital, which became his second home.

The diagnosis turned family life upside down, forcing his older brother to move in with their grandmother as hospital appointments mounted.

He started chemotherapy soon after his diagnosis and had his first of many operations six months later.

Doctors at the time were not confident the procedure would be successful for such a young child.

By summer 1994, aged 3, he had completed his final round of chemotherapy.

He then began learning to walk again with extensive physiotherapy.

Due to the way the bone was set, he was unable to bend his leg and had to walk with it straight.

Twelve years ago, surgeons moved muscle from his back to his leg, allowing him to bend it slightly.

He had to learn to walk again.

Mr Lambert-Tuthill now uses a crutch because years of surgery left him with nerve damage and a dropped foot.

Since 2008, he has suffered three serious infections and still fears losing his leg.

Despite the challenges, he works in finance and refuses to let mobility problems define his life.

He and his wife Emily, who both love to travel, are working towards their "40 before 40" goal, aiming to visit 40 countries before they turn 40.

He has also launched an Instagram page, @thereislifeaftercancer, to encourage and support others living beyond cancer.

Mr Lambert-Tuthill and his family are urging people across Yorkshire to support Cancer Research UK for Children & Young People by donating or wearing a gold ribbon badge.

Jane Bullock, Cancer Research UK spokesperson for Yorkshire, said: "We’re grateful to Ryann for sharing his experience all these years later.

"Cancer is different in children and young people – from the types of cancer that affect this age group, to the long-term effects of treatment.

"So, it needs a different and dedicated approach.

"Thanks to our supporters, we’re driving discoveries, developing new drugs and transforming clinical trials to help accelerate a new generation of more effective and less toxic treatments.

"That’s why we’re urging people across the region to get behind our scientists’ efforts to ensure more children and young people like Ryann live longer, better lives free from the fear of cancer."

St James’s University Hospital in Leeds is among the centres taking part in trials run by Cancer Research UK’s dedicated children’s cancer clinical trials unit.

The trials give children with cancer across Leeds and Yorkshire access to innovative treatments.

Gold ribbon badges are available at Cancer Research UK shops, while donations can be made at cruk.org/childrenandyoungpeople.
