# More support is needed for police officers leaving the force - Dr Ria Vaportzis

## Who looks after police officers when they leave the service? When we talk about policing, the conversation usually centres on recruitment, retention and public confidence. How do we attract more officers? How do we keep them? How do we support them while they do one of the most demanding jobs in our society?

Yet there is another question we rarely ask. What happens when police officers leave? For many people, changing jobs is a significant life event. For police officers, it can be something much bigger. It is often the end of an identity, a community and a way of life built over decades of service.

Policing is unlike most professions. Officers are routinely exposed to trauma, conflict and human suffering. They make difficult decisions under pressure and carry responsibilities that extend far beyond the end of a normal working day. Over time, many develop a strong sense of purpose and belonging tied closely to their role.

When that role ends, whether through retirement or early departure, the transition can be profound.

This was the focus of our recent research, which explored the experiences of 325 current and former police officers across the UK. We wanted to understand how officers experienced leaving the service and what forms of support they felt were missing.

What we found should give policymakers and policing leaders pause for thought.

Again and again, participants described a sense of abrupt disconnection. Many felt supported and valued while serving, but far less so once they had decided to leave. Some described retirement or resignation not as a transition but as a sudden cliff edge.

Particularly striking were accounts from middle-ranking officers and those who left earlier than expected. Many spoke of feeling forgotten by the institutions to which they had dedicated years, sometimes decades, of service.

Several participants described spending years looking after the public, only to feel that nobody was looking after them once they left. Others spoke of losing not only a job but a sense of identity.

These experiences matter because leaving policing is rarely a purely administrative process. It involves practical, emotional and social adjustments that can affect wellbeing long after an officer hands in their warrant card.

Many participants told us they felt underprepared for life beyond policing. They wanted more support with career planning, financial decisions, mental health and maintaining social connections. Most importantly, they wanted organisations to recognise that transitioning out of policing can be just as significant as entering it. This is particularly relevant today.

Police forces across the country are grappling with recruitment and retention challenges. Public debate frequently focuses on attracting new officers and reducing resignations. Both are important. But focusing exclusively on getting people into policing risks overlooking those who are leaving.

The reality is that every officer will leave eventually. The question is whether we treat that moment as a simple HR process or as a major life transition deserving of support and attention.

Improving support for police leavers does not require a complete overhaul of the system. It means recognising transition as an important stage of the policing lifecycle. It means providing structured resettlement support, maintaining access to welfare services and helping officers prepare for the realities of post-service life.

Dr Ria Vaportzis is an associate professor in psychology at University of Bradford.
