County Durham and Darlington NHS Foundation Trust has acknowledged that there are likely to be further cases of harm among breast cancer patients outside the period currently being reviewed.

The admission appears in board papers ahead of the trust’s public meeting on September 24, when it will decide whether to examine older breast surgery cases.

The current review mainly covers patients treated between January 2023 and February 2025.

But the trust says its Clinical Reference Group has confirmed there are “likely more cases of harm” if the review stays within that timeframe.

It also admits that patients treated before 2023 have already been found to have suffered harm.

The warning appears in the trust’s assessment of Option One, which would keep the review between January 2023 and February 2025 with no extension.

Under the heading “Missed harm”, the papers say patients would not be identified unless they started their own review.

The revelation comes as the trust considers examining thousands of older breast surgery cases dating back to 2015.

At least 20 women have already been identified as having undergone mastectomies which were not necessary.

![]()Darlington Memorial Hospital *(Image: NORTHERN ECHO)*

Trust figures show that 315 of the 514 cases examined so far involved harm, including 77 cases of significant harm.

One patient is known to have died.

The cases include delayed or missed cancer diagnoses, unnecessarily extensive surgery and treatment that fell below best practice.

The board papers also reveal that patients outside the current review criteria have already suffered harm.

The trust’s current datasets and criteria would have missed 283 people identified for review because they were treated before 2023 or had surgery elsewhere.

![]()University Hospital of North Durham *(Image: CHRIS BOOTH)*

They were identified through the patient helpline, complaints, reports from other NHS trusts or concerns raised by the current breast service.

Of the 240 patients reviewed by September 2, 19 had suffered harm serious enough to trigger the statutory Duty of Candour process.

That finding is central to the debate over whether the Trust should review earlier years proactively.

One option would keep the review limited to 2023-2025, leaving patients outside that period to come forward themselves.

But the trust warns that approach could deepen healthcare inequalities because some vulnerable patients may never come forward.

Board papers call for “proactive, outbound patient engagement”, saying the trust should identify and contact affected people instead of waiting for them to come forward.

The document states: “Responsibility should not fall on patients.”

The trust’s preferred option is to finish the 2023-2025 review before working backwards one year at a time to 2015.

After each year, experts would assess the number and seriousness of the harm found before deciding whether to continue the review.

The North East and North Cumbria Integrated Care Board instead favours immediately extending the review all the way back to 2015.

The trust’s independent Clinical Reference Group says reviewing earlier years is "unlikely to identify large numbers of patients who now need further treatment or uncover substantial new clinical learning."

But it also accepts that records alone may miss some affected patients and that more cases of harm may emerge.

In papers prepared for the September 24 meeting, the Trust issued another apology to affected patients.

It said: “We acknowledge that, during the period under review, the standards our patients had a right to expect were not consistently met and that failures in care, governance and oversight caused distress, uncertainty and harm for some patients and their families.”

The Trust added that it remained “deeply sorry” and was committed to learning from the past and ensuring patients receive "safe, compassionate and high-quality care".

The board will decide the review’s scope and timeframe at its public meeting on September 24.
