Mozart's The Marriage of Figaro is being transported from 18th-century Spain to 1970s Bradford in a bold new production that blends Yorkshire dialect, Urdu and Punjabi with Indian classical music.

The ambitious adaptation, staged by Bradford Opera Festival, features a new translation by poet and broadcaster Ian McMillan and a reimagined score by Yorkshire composer and conductor Ben Crick.

And while purists may raise an eyebrow, Mr Crick insists the production remains true to the radical spirit that made Mozart's opera so revolutionary in the first place.

He said: "The Marriage of Figaro began life as a controversial play that challenged class privilege.  
"This production reclaims that original spirit, creating a new work that is socially relevant while remaining rooted in one of the greatest operas ever written,"

## A Yorkshire voice for a classic story

Set in Bradford during the early 1970s, the production follows the same characters and plot as Mozart's original, which revolves around servants Figaro and Susanna attempting to outsmart their employer, Count Almaviva.

But audiences can expect something very different from a traditional staging.

Mr Crick said: "If you want to hear the orchestration exactly as Mozart wrote it, that's already available and there are some fantastic recordings and productions out there. What we're doing is something different."

The adaptation follows the success of Bradford Opera Festival's acclaimed 2023 production of The Barber of Seville, which was also translated by Mr McMillan and relocated to Yorkshire.

According to artistic director Alex Chisholm, setting the opera in 1970s Bradford felt like a natural fit.

Ms Chisholm said: "Marriage of Figaro was written at a point just before huge social upheaval.  
Social hierarchies were starting to break down, there were enormous cultural changes taking place and there was a real sense that society was on the verge of transformation. Setting it in early-1970s Bradford captures a lot of that same energy and sense of possibility."

## Blending Mozart with Indian classical traditions

One of the most distinctive features of the production is its musical fusion.

Rather than using a full symphony orchestra, Mr Crick has reorchestrated the score for a chamber ensemble featuring both Western and Indian classical instruments.

Mr Crick said: "What I've tried to create is a genuine collaboration. It's not a bit of Indian music followed by a bit of Western music. There are moments where musicians are able to bring elements from their own traditions and place them within Mozart's harmonic world.  
That allows these different musical languages to interact and create something entirely new while still honouring the original score."

The production features tabla, Indian violin and Indian flute alongside more familiar orchestral instruments.

## **Urdu and Punjabi bring new audiences to opera**

Singer, poet and lyricist Kauser Mukhtar, who translated sections of the opera into Urdu and Punjabi, said the multilingual approach reflects modern Bradford and makes the production more accessible.

Ms Mukhtar said: "People naturally engage with stories when they can see themselves within them. For many South Asian communities, opera isn't traditionally viewed as something that belongs to them, even though music is absolutely central to our culture."

She said opera and South Asian culture share more similarities than some people realise.

"If anyone has watched a Bollywood film, they'll know that people break into song all the time.  
Musicality is already central to our culture, so bringing those traditions together feels like a very natural thing to do."

Ms Mukhtar added: "A lot of people in Bradford are multilingual and move between languages every day. Hearing those languages represented on stage creates a richer experience."

## Reflecting Bradford on stage

The production's diverse cast and creative team have also been designed to reflect the city in which it is set.

Mr Chisholm said: "The hope is that when people look at the stage, it looks a bit like Bradford.  
It's a genuine mix of people, backgrounds and experiences, which is exactly what you see in the city itself.  
We didn't want to create something that felt artificial or disconnected from the community."

He added: "Bradford is the best place to make any form of art because it's so rich, so diverse and has so many traditions you can draw from.  
That cultural mix produces exciting work and gives artists an incredible amount to explore."

The Marriage of Figaro will be performed at Bradford Arts Centre on November 17 and 18, before touring to Leeds, Skipton and London's Wilton's Music Hall.
