CARS of the future could soon recharge as they drive, thanks to a new breakthrough in wireless charging technology.

Researchers at the University of Bradford are developing 'WattCrete,' a type of smart concrete designed to improve the efficiency of power transfer to electric vehicles (EVs) as they move along the road.

The project is led by Professor Ashraf Ashour and Dr Boğaçhan Başaran from the university's Department of Engineering.

It has received £236,000 in funding through the Horizon Europe Marie Skłodowska-Curie Postdoctoral Fellowship.

Professor Ashour, professor of structural engineering at Bradford and the project’s supervisor, said: "The transition to electric vehicles is essential if we are to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and achieve net zero ambitions.

"However, challenges around charging infrastructure remain one of the biggest barriers to widespread adoption.

"This project explores whether the road itself can become part of the solution.

"By developing new forms of concrete panels that help improve wireless power transfer, we hope to contribute to technologies that could make charging systems more efficient, more sustainable and ultimately more practical for future transport networks.

"What makes this particularly exciting is that it brings together expertise from civil engineering, materials science and electrical engineering to address a real-world challenge with global relevance."

Wireless charging for EVs works by transmitting power from charging pads embedded beneath the road surface to receivers on the vehicles.

However, traditional road materials like concrete and asphalt block magnetic energy, resulting in significant power losses.

The Bradford team aims to solve this problem by developing high magnetic permeability concrete panels, which could allow more energy to reach the vehicle.

Even small improvements in transmission efficiency could reduce costs and make large-scale wireless charging infrastructure more viable, researchers say.

The project will test whether these specially engineered panels can deliver better performance without compromising practicality or affordability for widespread road use.

It will also include the development of predictive models to help engineers design roads that maximise charging efficiency.

Researchers believe the technology could support future generations of electrified transport infrastructure while helping reduce energy losses and improve the sustainability of electric vehicle networks.

The WattCrete project is part of the university’s broader research into sustainable infrastructure, advanced materials and low-carbon technologies.

If successful, the technology could play a key role in enabling continuous charging for EVs on the move, addressing one of the major barriers to mass adoption and paving the way for more sustainable transport networks.
