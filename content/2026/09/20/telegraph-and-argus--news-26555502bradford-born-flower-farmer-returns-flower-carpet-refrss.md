A Bradford-born flower farmer who built an award-winning horticultural business in Australia is travelling more than 10,000 miles back to his hometown to help create one of the city's most anticipated cultural events.

Jamie Adams, who grew up in Thornton, Denholme and Wrose before attending Salt Grammar School, has been invited to join the build team behind this year's Bradford Flower Carpet in Centenary Square.

The installation, which will use around 300,000 dahlia flowerheads, will transform the city centre into a vast temporary artwork inspired by Bradford's textile heritage.

For Mr Adams, the project is far more than just another horticultural challenge.

"I left Bradford at 18 looking for a different life and trying to work out who I was.

![]()Jamie Adams harvests dahlias at The Olde Creamery flower farm in regional Victoria, Australia, where he and his husband Gareth grow more than 7,000 plants across 550 cultivars. *(Image: Jamie Adams)*

"To return more than 20 years later, happily married, running an award-winning flower farm in Australia and coming home because of the dahlia is something I could never have imagined.

"It feels like two completely different chapters of my life suddenly meeting in the same place."

## A one-way trip that became a new life

Mr Adams moved to Australia as a teenager on what was intended to be a backpacking adventure.

However, the trip soon became something much more permanent.

"At that age I really needed to find myself and learn to stand on my own feet.

"Growing up gay during the Section 28 era hadn't always been a particularly positive experience, so Australia felt like a fork in the road, a chance to work out who I was without the expectations and safety net of home.

![]()Bradford-born flower farmer Jamie Adams at The Olde Creamery in regional Victoria, Australia, before returning to his hometown to help create the Bradford Flower Carpet. *(Image: Jamie Adams)*

"More than 20 years later, I'm still here."

Today, he and his husband Gareth run The Olde Creamery in regional Victoria, an award-winning flower farm and specialist dahlia nursery in regional Victoria.

## How one dahlia became 7,000 plants

The couple originally bought their property simply as a home, with dreams of one day opening it to the public.

"Dahlias definitely weren't in the business plan.

"I liked them, grew one, didn't kill it, and apparently that was enough.

"One plant became a few, a few became a collection, and suddenly customers were asking for them."

What started with a single plant has grown into a thriving business.

![]()The Olde Creamery's award-winning "Memoirs of the Dahlias" installation, which invited visitors to share memories and stories inspired by flowers and horticulture. *(Image: Jamie Adams)*

The Olde Creamery now grows around 550 dahlia cultivars and more than 7,000 plants operating an award-winning propagation nursery that sends plants and tubers across Australia.

"Even now, we don't always stop and think about how far it's gone.

"We have an incredible community of followers who have come along for the journey, and opportunities such as the Melbourne International Flower & Garden Show have introduced the business to audiences much further afield than we ever imagined."

## A full-circle moment

The opportunity to return to Bradford came after a family member spotted news about the Flower Carpet installation and shared it with him.

Unable to shake the feeling that the project represented a rare full-circle moment, Mr Adams decided to get in touch.

"I remember saying to Gareth, 'It's three weeks away. Shall we do this?'

"I sent what was probably a slightly cheeky email asking whether there might be a way to get involved and somehow it worked.

"Then it very quickly became real with flights, passports, farm arrangements and work commitments all needing to be reorganised."

His invitation has also allowed him to connect with artist Shiraaz Ali, with the pair already discussing ideas around the event.

## Transforming Centenary Square

Visitors to the Flower Carpet can expect an installation unlike anything normally seen in Bradford.

Around 300,000 dahlia flowerheads will be laid out across Centenary Square to create a large-scale artwork inspired by the district's textile design history.

"What I love about projects like this is that people don't need to know anything about art or horticulture beforehand.

"You simply walk into the space and experience it for yourself.

"That accessibility is part of the magic."

Having previously exhibited at the Melbourne International Flower & Garden Show, including the interactive Memoirs of the Dahlia installation, Mr Adams believes projects like Flower Carpet have the power to connect communities.

"Flowers have this incredible ability to connect people to a person, a place or a memory almost instantly.

"You don't need specialist knowledge to respond to them, and that's why they can be such a powerful way of bringing communities together.

"If somebody walks away inspired to visit a garden centre, support a local flower farm or grow their first dahlia, that's a wonderful outcome."

## Fish and chips first

Although he is excited to contribute to one of Bradford's flagship cultural events, there is one homecoming tradition that remains non-negotiable.

"Coming home is always slightly surreal because so much changes.

"But the first stop is invariably the local chippy.

"Proper soggy chips and haddock are probably the food I miss most."

When visitors finally see the completed Flower Carpet, Mr Adams hopes they leave with more than just a photograph.

"I'd love Bradfordians and visitors to experience the spectacle, but also to have that quieter personal connection.

"Dahlias have an extraordinary ability to transport people back to a grandparent's garden, an allotment, a childhood home or somebody they loved.

"And if somebody leaves thinking, 'I want to grow a dahlia,' even better."
