## Kangaroos, smallpox outbreak and finances explored in York rugby league book

The earliest known picture of York Football Club, from about 1875 *(Image: Ian Lockwood)*

Former sports journalist at The Press and author Ian Lockwood has released a book detailing the early history of rugby league in York which covers everything from an outbreak of smallpox, a touring Australian side complete with a real kangaroo and the founding figures of the sport in the city.

His book, The History of York RL Club 1868-1914 includes the story of when a touring Australian side came to York in 1908-09.

Mr Lockwood told The Press: "A kangaroo was meant to be leading out the Australian team. A lot of York people were hoping to see a live kangaroo for the first time but it did not appear.

"Sadly the kangaroo did not make it back home due to the conditions of a British winter."

The idea for the book came when Mr Lockwood, who worked for The Press from 1982 to 1992, noticed the lack of reporting on the club's formation and early years.

He said: "I do a bit of historical research, and one day I saw York were founded in 1868, so I decided to just have a look at those early days.

"And I couldn't find anything at all.

"So I decided to set about trying to put the pieces in the jigsaw."

![]()Former sports reporter at The Press, Ian Lockwood, author of The History of York RL Club, 1868-1914. *(Image: Ian Lockwood)*

Mr Lockwood said historical newspaper archives, including those of The Press's predecessors, the York Herald and Yorkshire Gazette, proved invaluable in piecing together the story.

However, gaps remain because many committee records have been lost and some newspaper archives were destroyed during the bombing of York in the Second World War which affected the archives for The Press.

One of the themes Mr Lockwood explored in his book was the social background of the club.

He explained that the earliest incarnation of York Rugby was associated with relatively affluent and well-connected members of society, including former pupils of St Peter's School.

He contrasted that with modern perceptions of the club as York Knights are now often viewed as underdogs competing against larger rivals.

The author, who was editor of the Craven Herald from 1993 to 2009, added: "My many years following the game show that every single time a club will vote in its own self-interest."

He cited an example from just before World War One, when clubs had to reapply for re-election.

Mr Lockwood also raised concerns about the financial sustainability of York today, given that they receive only half the funding of other clubs.

He questioned whether other clubs would be willing to vote for York to receive a bigger share of the funding, which would mean a smaller share for them.

The retired journalist said: "That's not sustainable long term, I suspect.

"So will the other clubs vote to give York a bigger share of their pie, which will automatically mean a smaller share for them?

"I don't know. I don't know the politics behind it.

"The moral case for York being in Super League is irrefutable.

"Clubs like coming here, key fans like coming to the city for a weekend. They make it a break.

"York's a big tourist place, and they've proven on the pitch and off it that they seem to be a well-run club, they can compete. But will it just be a matter of finances of saying how do we split the pie to allow York an equal share?"

The former sports reporter and editor believes the industrial history of towns and cities such as Wigan, Bradford and Leeds explains why these rugby league powerhouses have enjoyed comparative success to the Knights.

![]()A cartoon from the York Evening Press of March 31, 1906 which conveys a sense of the despair surrounding the club in 1906. This appears in Ian Lockwood's book. *(Image: York Evening Press)*

He told The Press: "Not only is York not as big as Leeds or Bradford, it tried to be more commercial rather than having big factories and lots of mills.

"There was Rowntrees and the railway carriage works but there were not the large mills you would get in Lancashire towns. They would have had 3,000 to 4,000 people working and going to the games, whereas there was more individual working in York.

"I am not qualified enough to comment too deeply on that but I have a theory that these mills and large industrial factories had a big impact on those clubs."

Mr Lockwood said one of the most unexpected discoveries when researching his book involved an outbreak of smallpox.

He found evidence that York proceeded with a fixture despite warnings from medical authorities about disease concerns. The story gained attention far beyond York and became a significant national talking point at the time.

For Mr Lockwood, this revealed how financial pressures and league regulations could influence decisions made by clubs even in the sport's earliest years.

![]()A cartoon from the York Evening Press summarising the 1911 match against Australia which appears in Ian Lockwood's book. *(Image: York Evening Press)*

Mr Lockwood said he chose the outbreak of the First World War as a natural endpoint for his new book because of his interest in the Victorian and Edwardian periods.

While there are currently no confirmed plans for a sequel, he said he may eventually continue the story beyond 1914, potentially taking it through to the Second World War and covering York's Challenge Cup final appearance.

Mr Lockwood has previously written biographies of England and Yorkshire cricketer Ted Peate, as well as French world champion boxer and First World War fighter pilot Georges Carpentier.

The History of York RL Club, 1868 - 1914 is 132 pages and available in paperback.
