# George Cayley: The Yorkshire engineer, inventor and aviator who was a pioneer of aeronautical engineering and dubbed ‘the father of aviation’

## George Cayley was an engineer, inventor and aviator from Scarborough who was a pioneer of aeronautical engineering - here is his background.

George Cayley is sometimes dubbed ‘the father of aviation’ and designed the first glider reported to carry a human aloft.

He is often credited with being the first person to understand the underlying principles and forces of heavier-than-air flight: weight, lift, drag and thrust.

George is also considered the inventor of the wire wheel, a circular power tool brush that is used for cleaning metal or a vehicle wheel with wire spokes.

![British scientist and engineer Sir George Cayley. (Pic credit: Hulton Archive / Getty Images)](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/heritage-and-retro/heritage/smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65.jpg)


## **Early life of George Cayley**

George was born in Brompton-by-Sawdon near Scarborough, on December 27, 1733, and later inherited Brompton Hall, Wydale Hall and other estates after the death of his father, the 5th Baronet.

His career was inspired by the optimism of the times, he engaged in a wide variety of engineering projects.

He developed many things including self-righting lifeboats, tension-spoke wheels, the ‘Universal Railway’ - a term he used for caterpillar tractors, automatic signals for railway crossings, seat belts, small scale helicopters, and a type of prototypical internal combustion engine fuelled by gunpowder.

![Wire being wound into springs for spring-locks in 1927. (Pic credit: The Print Collector / Getty Images)](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/heritage-and-retro/heritage/smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65.jpg)


A suggestion he made for a more practical engine using gaseous vapours rather than gunpowder was implemented, predicting the modern internal combustion engine.

George also contributed in the fields of prosthetics, air engines, electricity, theatre architecture, ballistics, optics and land reclamation, and held the belief that these advancements should be freely available.

His was the first successfully working hot air engine, in which much ingenuity was displayed in overcoming practical difficulties arising from the high working temperatures.

According to a 2007 discovery of sketches in George’s school notebooks - which are now in the archive of the Royal Aeronautical Society Library - unveiled that he was developing his ideas on the theories of flight since he was in school.

![Captain Ferdinand Ferber bringing a hang glider in to land in front of a local official in Nice in 1902. (Pic credit: Hulton Archive / Getty Images)](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/heritage-and-retro/heritage/smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65.jpg)


These sketches show that George had identified the principle of a lift-generating inclined plane as early as 1792. He also experimented with rotating wing sections of various forms in the stairwells at Brompton Hall.

## **George Cayley and his flying machines**

In 1799, George invented the concept of the modern aeroplane as a fixed-wing flying machine with separate systems for lift, propulsion and control.

The modern aeroplane design is based on his discoveries and cambered wings he suggested.

![Francis Chardon, a bank clerk from Bern, Switzerland, flying a hang-glider biplane at a glider plane event in France in August 1922. (Pic credit: Topical Press Agency / Getty Images)](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/heritage-and-retro/heritage/smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65.jpg)


He also constructed the first flying model aeroplane and also diagrammed the elements of vertical flight.

He also accurately foreshadowed that sustained flight would not take place until a lightweight engine was developed to provide adequate thrust and lift. The Wright brothers acknowledged his significant contributions to the development of aviation.

George is mainly remembered for his pioneering studies and experiments with flying machines, including the working, piloted glider that he designed and built.

A landmark three-part treatise titled ‘On Aerial Navigation’ (1809-1810) was also written by him and it was published in Nicholson’s Journal of Natural Philosophy, Chemistry and the Arts.

These scientific experiments led him to develop an efficient cambered airfoil and to identify the four vector forces that influence an aircraft: thrust, lift, drag and weight.

His discovery of the importance of the dihedral angle for lateral stability in flight helped influence the development of hang gliders. This resulted in him being acknowledged as the first aeronautical engineer.

George’s emphasis on lightness led him to invent a new method of constructing lightweight wheels which is commonly used today.

For landing wheels, he shifted the spoke’s forces from compression to tension by making them out of tightly-stretched string, essentially ‘reinventing the wheel’.

These wire wheels were soon used commonly on bicycles, cars, aeroplanes and many other vehicles.

The model glider was first successfully flown by George in 1804 and it had the layout of a modern aircraft, with a kite-shaped wing towards the front and an adjustable tailplane at the back consisting of horizontal stabilisers and a vertical fin.

He was the first to suggest the idea of a convertiplane in 1843 and he designed and built a biplane at some time before 1849, in which a 10-year-old boy flew.

His grandson George John Cayley and his resident engineer Thomas Vick later assisted him to develop a larger scale glider which flew across Brompton Dale in front of Wydale Hall in 1853.

A replica of the machine was flown at the original site in Brompton Dale by Derek Piggott in 1973 for TV and in the mid-1980s for the IMAX film On the Wing. The glider is currently on display at the Yorkshire Air Museum.

## **George Cayley’s political career**

George represented the Whig party as MP for Scarborough from 1832 to 1835, and in 1838 helped establish the UK’s first Polytechnic Institute, the Royal Polytechnic Institution (now University of Westminster) and served as its chairman for many years.

He was elected as a vice president of the Yorkshire Philosophical Society in 1824 and was a founding member of the British Association for the Advancement of Science and was a distant cousin of the mathematician Arthur Cayley.
