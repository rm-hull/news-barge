October is when Germany begins to change pace. Beer gardens start to empty, mornings become noticeably cooler and forests take on shades of gold and copper as autumn settles across the country.

It's also one of the year's most distinctive months for festivals and cultural events. Alongside harvest celebrations and wine festivals, you'll find some of Germany's biggest literary, artistic and historical events, as well as plenty of traditions that international visitors may never have encountered before.

Whether you're looking for onion markets, pumpkin sculptures, illuminated cityscapes or a castle full of Halloween monsters, here are 10 events worth seeking out across Germany this October.

**October 2nd to 4th: German Unity Day celebrations, Bremen**

Germany's national holiday falls on October 3rd each year, marking the reunification of East and West Germany in 1990. Unfortunately for workers hoping for an extra day off, the holiday lands on a Saturday in 2026, but that doesn't stop the celebrations.

Each year the official national festivities move to a different federal state. In 2026, Bremen takes on hosting duties, transforming its city centre and waterfront into a huge open-air festival stretching from the historic centre to the banks of the Weser.

Much of the programme is free and outdoors. Visitors can wander between stages hosting concerts, theatre performances, debates, readings and family activities, while all 16 German states showcase their cultures on the popular Ländermeile.

Big-name acts including Die Fantastischen Vier, MiA., ClockClock and Kamrad are scheduled to appear across the weekend, while a special evening production along the river promises one of the event's visual highlights.

The Frankfurt Book Fair is one of the most important publishing and media events in the world, bringing together everyone from global publishers and bestselling authors to comic artists and devoted readers.

While the first days are geared towards the publishing industry, the public programme opens up from Friday onwards. This year's guest of honour is Czechia, while the fair is also placing a particular emphasis on fan communities and popular genres, including romance, fantasy, crime fiction, manga and children's literature.

Cosplay has become one of the defining features of the event, with dedicated contests and performances taking place throughout the weekend.

Elsewhere, visitors can browse the huge comics and manga areas, attend author signings, meet literary stars, explore Japanese and Korean pop culture showcases or simply spend the day people-watching.

Few German festivals are as delightfully niche as the Weimar Onion Market. Dating back to 1653, it’s the oldest folk festival in Thuringia and has grown into one of the country's most distinctive autumn celebrations.

For one weekend each October, Weimar's historic centre fills with hundreds of traders selling intricately braided onion wreaths known as *Zwiebelzöpfe*. There are also stalls serving onion tart, regional specialities, local wine and beer, while multiple stages host live music and entertainment across the city.

This year's event will also see newly crowned Onion Queen Roswitha I officially open proceedings with the traditional cutting of the onion tart.

**October 9th to 18th: Festival of Lights, Berlin**

Berlin has no shortage of landmarks, and for 10 nights in October many become works of art in their own right.

During the Festival of Lights, major sights across the capital are transformed by large-scale projections, illuminations and video installations. The Brandenburg Gate, Berlin Cathedral and the *Fernsehturm* are typically among the best-known locations, though dozens of buildings participate each year.

One of the best things about the festival is that it requires relatively little planning. You can join guided tours by bus or boat if you wish, but many visitors simply walk through central Berlin after dark, discovering unexpected illuminations as they go.

Munich's Oktoberfest tends to dominate international attention every autumn, so it's only fair to spare a thought for its slightly less famous cousin in Stuttgart.

The Cannstatter Volksfest attracts around four million visitors each year and is officially described as the second-largest folk festival in the world.

Running until October 11th, it occupies a huge site on Stuttgart's Cannstatter Wasen and combines fairground rides, beer tents, food stalls and entertainment on an enormous scale.

One of the festival's most distinctive features is the historic Fruchtsäule, a decorated fruit column that serves as a symbol of the event. Visitors can also catch the large traditional parade, enjoy musical fireworks displays and explore the Agricultural Festival.

**Until October 12th: German Wine Harvest Festival, Neustadt an der Weinstraße**

Held in Neustadt an der Weinstraße, at the heart of the Palatinate wine region, the German Wine Harvest Festival combines wine, food, music and fairground attractions across several weeks.

The historic wine village known as the Haiselscher forms the heart of the event, while the w.i.n.e.FESTival programme adds tastings, music and themed experiences. Visitors can sample local wines, explore the old town and take part in guided tours through one of Germany's most important wine-producing regions.

The highlight arrives on October 11th with the Grand Wine Festival Parade, widely regarded as the largest wine parade in Germany, featuring decorated floats, winegrowers and marching bands.

Strictly speaking, this isn't solely an October event. But if there is a single attraction that captures the spirit of autumn in Germany, it might well be the Ludwigsburg Pumpkin Festival.

![Two visitors to the Blühendes Barock take photos of a pumpkin teddy bear by artist Pit Ruge before the opening of the “Let’s Play” pumpkin exhibition.](https://apiwp.thelocal.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/614061316.jpg)

Held in the gardens of Ludwigsburg Palace near Stuttgart, the event is billed as the world's largest pumpkin exhibition. More than 450,000 pumpkins are incorporated into giant displays and sculptures spread throughout the grounds.

The 2026 theme is Let's Play, with pumpkin creations inspired by toys, games and childhood favourites including Super Mario and Barbie.

Beyond the sculptures, visitors will find pumpkin-themed food ranging from soups to pumpkin ravioli, carving demonstrations, giant pumpkin competitions and even pumpkin boat races.

For one night each year, Munich encourages visitors to stay out late and spend the evening in museums rather than bars.

More than 100 museums, galleries, churches, archives and cultural institutions take part in the Long Night of the Munich Museums, remaining open from 6pm until 1am. A single ticket provides access to participating venues and shuttle buses that connect locations across the city.

Part of the appeal lies in the variety. Visitors might find themselves viewing Old Masters in the Alte Pinakothek, exploring science exhibits at the Deutsches Museum, attending lectures, listening to live music, joining guided tours or discovering specialist collections they might never otherwise visit.

**October 16th to November 1st: Frankenstein Halloween, Königstein Castle**

For decades, Halloween Frankenstein was held at Burg Frankenstein near Darmstadt, helping to establish its reputation as one of Europe's best-known Halloween attractions. After the original castle closed for extensive renovation work, organisers moved the event to Königstein Castle in the Taunus mountains, where it has continued since 2024.

![Horror clowns at Königstein Castle](https://apiwp.thelocal.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/487586087.jpg)

The 2026 edition, titled The Kingdom of Darkness, promises themed scare zones, costumed monsters, live shows and immersive horror experiences spread throughout the sprawling castle ruins.

This is one event where advance tickets are strongly recommended, particularly on weekends close to Halloween itself.

**October 31st: Reformation Day Festival, Wittenberg**

Reformation Day marks the anniversary of one of the most consequential moments in European history.

According to tradition, Martin Luther nailed his famous 95 Theses to the door of the Castle Church in Wittenberg on October 31st, 1517, helping to spark the Protestant Reformation and fundamentally reshape religion, politics and society across Europe.

To mark the event each year, historical markets fill the old town and artisans demonstrate traditional crafts.

One of the most striking events is the evening procession of lights, which begins at 5pm and winds through the historic centre with lanterns, candles, musicians and historical groups.

It also happens to fall on Halloween, creating an unusual overlap between one of Germany's most important religious anniversaries and one of its most colourful autumn celebrations.
