HOT and dry weather led to a surge in butterfly sightings across Yorkshire during this summer's Big Butterfly Count.

A total of 118,330 butterflies and day-flying moths were recorded during the three-week survey, with 8,309 people taking part across the region.

The most commonly spotted butterfly was the Small White, also known as the "Cabbage White," followed by the Large White and the Red Admiral.

Professor Richard Fox, head of science at Butterfly Conservation, said: "Adult butterflies love hot, sunny weather for flying, feeding and breeding, and that's exactly why people saw so many this summer.

"However, the very same weather that made it a good year for butterflies—lots of sunshine and very little rain—could mean we're actually on course for a population crash, which could take years to recover from."

The charity has warned that while 2026 was a strong year for sightings, the hot, dry conditions may have had a damaging effect on caterpillar food plants, many of which withered before larvae could fully feed.

A spokesperson for Butterfly Conservation said the true impact may not become clear until next year.

A notable highlight of the count was the Painted Lady butterfly, with 10,699 sightings in Yorkshire alone and a 335 per cent increase across the UK compared to the previous year.

Painted Ladies are known for their long-distance migration from Africa to the UK.

To help protect butterflies in the face of increasingly frequent droughts, Butterfly Conservation is encouraging the public to make their gardens more resilient.

The charity recommends planting trees and shrubs such as holly, ivy, and buckthorn, which provide essential food and shelter for caterpillars and adult butterflies.

Setting up water butts to collect rainwater over autumn and winter can help keep key plants hydrated during dry spells, and leaving areas of grass to grow long offers vital habitat for species like the Speckled Wood and Meadow Brown.

Longer grass also retains moisture better and provides cooler spaces for butterflies during extreme heat.

Gardens with areas of long grass have been shown to attract up to 93 per cent more butterflies and a greater diversity of species.

The Big Butterfly Count will return in 2027, running from Friday, July 9, to Sunday, August 1.

Butterfly Conservation says the results will be especially important next year to assess the lasting effects of 2026’s extreme weather.
