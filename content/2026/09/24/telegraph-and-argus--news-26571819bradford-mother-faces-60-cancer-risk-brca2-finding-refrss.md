A Bradford mother who faces a 60% lifetime risk of developing cancer has spoken of the difficult decisions she must make to protect her future health, saying it is something she thinks about "every day".

Beth Elias, 37, is helping shape a pioneering study that could lead to new ways of preventing inherited cancers after discovering she carries a BRCA2 gene variant linked to an increased risk of developing the disease.

Speaking about the choices she faces, Beth said: "If I developed cancer, I'd be facing treatment, being ill and my children having to experience that.

"I'm constantly weighing up both sides and trying to decide when the right time is to have surgery, knowing my cancer risk increases with age but not wanting it to affect my children while they're still young.

"It's a decision I think about every day."

## **Shock diagnosis after family testing**

Beth underwent genetic testing alongside her two sisters after concerns about her family's history of cancer.

The sisters agreed to wait until they were together before opening the results, but while her sisters tested negative, Beth learned she carried the BRCA2 gene variant.

"It was a huge shock," she said. "Suddenly it felt like it was just me."

Following assessments of her family history and lifestyle, Beth was told she has a 60% chance of developing cancer during her lifetime, with the risk increasing after the age of 40.

## **Living with an inherited cancer risk**

Now under the care of specialists in Bradford, Beth receives regular NHS screening while considering how best to reduce her risk.

She has explored several preventative options, including hormone therapy and surgery.

However, after being prescribed a hormone therapy drug, Beth experienced side effects including rashes and significant mood changes, leading her to stop taking the medication.

"It’s so important cancer research continues to explore more personalised approaches to treatment, taking into account the experiences and needs of different people," she said.

"Everyone is different, and a treatment that works well for one person may not work in the same way for someone else."

## **Weighing up preventative surgery**

Beth is now considering preventative surgery, including a double mastectomy to reduce her risk of breast cancer and, in the future, a hysterectomy to reduce the risk of ovarian cancer.

She described the decision as both physically and emotionally challenging.

"Surgery is a massive decision," she said.

"You're talking about weeks where you can't drive, can't work and can't pick up your children from school.

"Then there's the physical impact on your body, the scarring and the loss of sensation in the breast area.

"You also have to come to terms with the emotional impact and the changes to how you feel about yourself and your confidence.

"It's a hugely personal and complex decision, and not something to rush into."

## **Groundbreaking Yorkshire study**

Beth has become an ambassador for the PARCC (PARP Inhibitors for Cancer Risk Reduction in Carriers) study, which has received £346,531 in funding from Yorkshire Cancer Research.

Led by Dr Stephanie Archer at the University of Cambridge, the project will explore whether people with inherited genetic variants linked to cancer would consider taking PARP inhibitors, drugs already used to treat certain cancers, to reduce their risk of developing the disease.

More than 1,300 people from Yorkshire are expected to take part as part of a wider UK study involving 5,500 participants.

Researchers hope the findings will help determine whether future clinical trials investigating PARP inhibitors as a form of cancer prevention should be developed.

## **'This research gives me hope'**

Beth said she believes the study could help provide more options for people living with inherited cancer risk in the future.

"I’m hugely supportive of this study because it could be the first step towards creating new options for people like me," she said.

"Using PARP inhibitors to prevent cancer is an exciting prospect.

"Carrying a BRCA gene variant can feel isolating, but this research gives me hope more options for cancer prevention could be available in the future."
