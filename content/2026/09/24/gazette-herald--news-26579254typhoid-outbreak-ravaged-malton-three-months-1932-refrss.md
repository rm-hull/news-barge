On September 23, 1932, a typhoid outbreak commenced in Malton.

Malton Museum has posted this information about the 'pandemic' which lasted nearly three months.

Unbeknownst at the time, to be typhoid, a young harvester demonstrated persistent symptoms of enteric fever and was taken to the Institution, later named Spring Hall, after seeing a doctor.

No precautions were taken, however, and on 26th September ‘treat as typhoid’ was written on the patient’s record card.

From there, 270 people contracted the disease, approximately 5% of Malton’s population, and 23 died, including Dr George Colley Parkin, who worked tirelessly throughout the outbreak.

Malton and Norton were sealed off for nearly three months, and the Minister of Health confirmed the outbreak was due to pollution of the public water supply; the district council was blamed for ignoring warnings that the local water supply had been unsatisfactory since the late nineteenth century.

Norton was largely unaffected due to the installation of an entirely new, distinct water supply, with residents only affected if they were infected in Malton.

On the map, the blue lines indicate the water mains, the red lines represent drains and sewers, while the X symbolises affected households. The triangle locates Spring Hall, where patients were taken.
