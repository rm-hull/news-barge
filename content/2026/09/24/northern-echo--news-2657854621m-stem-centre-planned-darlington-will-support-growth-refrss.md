**BUSINESSiQ Editor Mike Hughes gives his own personal view of Darlington - his home for more than 16 years - and the challenge of building a future for the next generation**

Our region is a place to be proud of.

This is where we want to welcome friends and family to show them around and where potential investors from market stalls to billion-pound facilities are blown away by what we have here and the enthusiasm and confidence we have to keep growing.

At its heart, Darlington is a prime example of how to keep that momentum going. This town will never be able to stand still and reflect – it has to set a formidable pace every step of the way.

There will be challenges, of course, there always are. But it is about how they are anticipated and tackled that is the mark of a town - and its astonishing people and businesses - that won’t stop for anyone.

I moved here more than sixteen years ago, after being brought up in Merseyside and working there and in Birmingham before settling in Darlington. Like a lot of people, work was the driving force behind my journey, and the importance of living in a place where there are clear career paths is never lost on me. All of us want young people to be well educated here, find a job within a few miles of their home, and then progress well enough to see that this should be the place where they make their own choice to build their lives.

The £21 million STEM Centre of Excellence planned for the town is a good place to start.

For those behind the project, it represents a statement of intent about the town’s future — and about the role collaboration will play in giving the region the skills, confidence and capacity to grow.

The planned Central Park centre sits firmly within Darlington Borough Council’s wider Place-led regeneration agenda, which is focused on renewing the town centre, attracting investment and creating the conditions for long-term economic growth.

It forms part of a broader story of regeneration already reshaping Darlington, with the council working with public and private sector partners to bring forward strategic sites, improve opportunity and strengthen the town’s role as a place to live, work, invest and do business.

In March 2025, Darlington was awarded up to £20 million over ten years from the UK Government’s Pride in Place programme, to focus on what local people need and care about.

The programme is overseen by the Darlington Neighbourhood Board of local businesses, residents, and partner organisations. The board's goal is to support growth by improving things like buildings and outdoor spaces, as well as community life, to make a real difference to the everyday lives of people in Darlington.

Rose Rouse, chief executive of Darlington Borough Council, said work like the STEM centre meant the town was starting a new era, describing the borough and wider region as “an exceptionally proud, innovative and unique part of the UK”.

She said the town was experiencing “one of the most significant periods of transformation in its history”, with growth, regeneration and investment strengthening its position as one of the North East’s “most connected, inclusive, vibrant and ambitious places to live, work, invest and do business”.

Rose said: “The STEM centre will provide clear pathways from school so local people can access high-value careers in science, technology, engineering and maths.

“The facility will also enable existing businesses and large employers to recruit and upskill locally. This is a huge step forward for our town in terms of regenerating areas which we know are ripe for development and improvement, and an exciting time for Darlington as a whole, as well as ensuring connection, ambition and inclusivity remains at the heart of everything we do.”

Clearly, this is not simply an investment in bricks and mortar, but in the people who will shape the next phase of the regional economy.

The facility will focus on science, technology, engineering and mathematics, providing training linked to some of the fastest-growing areas of the economy, from artificial intelligence and robotics to green energy, advanced manufacturing and data-led innovation. But its significance also lies in the connections it will create between Darlington’s regeneration ambitions and the practical skills businesses will need if they are to expand in the years ahead.

At a time when skills shortages remain one of the biggest barriers to growth across the UK, more than 600 students are expected to take full-time STEM courses, apprenticeships and undergraduate or postgraduate programmes at the three-storey facility, which will include an auditorium, a 350-square-metre community workspace, science and data laboratories, IT suites, seminar rooms and social spaces.

Its location is central to the story. The centre will sit within Darlington’s growing education and innovation quarter, close to Darlington College, Teesside University, the Science Park, CPI’s Darlington operation and other organisations working where research, technology and commercial opportunity meet.

That clustering matters. By bringing education providers, employers, researchers and regeneration partners closer together, the project aims to create a clearer route from learning into work. In that sense, the STEM centre is a highly visible expression of the borough’s joined-up Place strategy to align regeneration, skills, enterprise and investment.

David Gartland, principal and chief executive of Darlington College, described it as “a landmark moment for Darlington and the wider Tees Valley”.

He said: “This investment recognises the ambition of our region and the vital role education plays in driving economic prosperity.

“As industries evolve and technology continues to shape the future, it is essential that we provide local people with access to the very best training and opportunities. The Darlington STEM Centre of Excellence will help develop the highly skilled workforce our employers need while ensuring talent can build successful careers right here in the North East.”

For Philip Probert, director for CPI’s Darlington operation, the practical connection between young talent and applied science is one of the most exciting aspects of the project.

He said: “I want my kids to be able to choose to stay close to me. But I’ve got an opportunity to support that through what we do here, what we do in the region. So I’m not powerless, and we’ve got great universities and the incoming STEM Centre will bring people closer to where we’re doing translational science.”

For him, the concentration of expertise, education and enterprise gives Darlington a rare opportunity to turn potential into tangible progress.

“For us, it’s very positive,” he said. “I’m very passionate about this site, this location because it is a real opportunity for Darlington. To nurture talent, to see people develop and be successful.

“The STEM Centre is exciting to be engaged with, to work with young talent. It’s exciting working with teenagers and graduates so full of energy and possibilities and to hopefully get them involved in what we do.

“We need more of this sort of investment in the region so we can create more. We also need the money to be here, because with money you’ll retain talent.”

That is why the STEM centre is part of a much bigger story. It is about education, confidence and skills, but also about pride in our Place, and ensuring that Darlington and the wider region are not simply responding to change, but shaping it.

This town has a rare chance to strengthen its position as a place where innovation is a tried and tested pathway from classroom to career.
