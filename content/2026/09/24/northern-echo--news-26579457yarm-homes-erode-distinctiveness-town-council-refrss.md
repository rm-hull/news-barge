Drivers on a busy road could see an 81 per cent surge on peak morning journey times if 600 new homes are built, a council has argued.

Taylor Wimpey plans for a new estate off Green Lane in Yarm have been under the spotlight for the last nine days as a public inquiry into the refusal of the bid gets underway.

The developer has argued that the £133m plans were addressing a "serious local need" for housing amid a "significant shortfall" in the area - saying harm caused by the plan "doesn't come anywhere close" to outweighing the benefits.

But as the inquiry drew to a close at Dunedin House in Thornaby today (September 24), Stockton Borough Council once again hit back at the developer, arguing the estate would spark a surge in journey times and "erode the distinctiveness" of the town.

![]()Fields off Green Lane in Yarm, where Taylor Wimpey wants to build 600 new homes *(Image: THE NORTHERN ECHO)*

Traffic has been a major concern among residents, with the estate planning to add two traffic light junctions to Green Lane, a key route, if plans are approved.

Barrister John Hunter, for the council, said the impact would "not be minimal or imperceptible", despite an "adventurous suggestion" from the developer's transport consultant Philip Wooliscroft.

He said there would be "substantial increases in journey times" and "real adverse effects".

![]()Barrister John Hunter at the Yarm public inquiry *(Image: THE NORTHERN ECHO)*

Mr Hunter also noted how Mr Wooliscroft accepted that the Yarm traffic model material did not say what junctions were being "pushed beyond capacity" - saying it was sufficient to show surging journey times but not to show the "full consequences at junctions across the network".

He said if you included all committed development, the estate, and the proposed Gladman development near Morley Carr Farm, the morning traffic peak journey time on Green Lane heading east rises by 81 per cent, an overall increase of four minutes and six seconds.

Leven Road, which is used by drivers avoiding the Crossways Junction from Green Lane, would see a 138 per cent increase (four minutes and 28 seconds) in journey times at the morning peak.

During his closing speech, Mr Hunter also took aim at the developer's ecology and infrastructure arguments, saying the adverse effects of the estate "substantially outweigh the benefits".

He argued that developing on the land would "permanently alter the physical relationship between the town and its surrounding agricultural landscape."

![]()Fields off Green Lane in Yarm, where Taylor Wimpey wants to build 600 new homes *(Image: THE NORTHERN ECHO)*

He noted how Active Travel England's original advice said the estate "lacked proper integration with its surroundings", adding that it would erode its distinctiveness.

When it came to ecology, Mr Hunter said evidence identifies "credible pathways by which deterioration of the ancient woodland would occur".

He said the developer had "failed to provide the assessment necessary to demonstrate that this proposal can be accommodated consistently with the protection of the ecological network".

The council also argued that the site did not offer a "genuine choice of transport modes" and was car-dependent, despite plans for a dedicated cycle and pedestrian active route.

![]()The plans for the new Yarm homes off Green Lane *(Image: P+HS ARCHITECTS / TAYLOR WIMPEY)*

He also said Green Lane was "inherently hostile to pedestrians and cyclists" while branding the new bus route "double-edged", as it will "materially increase journey times" for others and is not secured.

He also touched on "crucial" concerns over the electricity supply, as its current capacity means it could only support 20 to 40 homes.

![]()The Yarm public inquiry *(Image: THE NORTHERN ECHO)*

Anything more than this "depends upon additional capacity" and the planned sub-station at Mount Leven, for which no planning application has been submitted.

Mr Hunter said the evidence "does not demonstrate that development of the scale proposed can be accommodated having regard to the existing or proposed availability of the infrastructure".

Sasha White KC, for Taylor Wimpey, told the inquiry last week that the national housing crisis and a "significant" shortfall of homes being delivered in the area are key issues as to why the development should go ahead.

Mr White also noted how "every statutory consultee with technical expertise supported the application" and that the case in favour of the appeal is "compelling".

He added that the council's highways, transport, and design manager concluded residual impact on the network "would not be severe" following proposed mitigation measures.

A report on Mr White's closing statement will follow.

George Underwood closed the inquiry after eight days and will now make his decision.
