THIS week marks International Day of Sign Languages - set up by the United Nations in 2017 to support the linguistic identity and rights of Deaf people and other sign language users.

The NSPCC believes every child has a right to protection from harm, and research has shown that children and young people who are d/Deaf or have a disability are at an increased risk of being abused compared with their peers.

They are also less likely to receive the protection and support they need when they have been abused.

It can be a challenge for people, including professionals, to identify safeguarding concerns when working with d/Deaf children and children who have disabilities due to a difficulty in communication.

One form of sign is Makaton, a sort of simplified sign language which is used alongside speech by individuals with learning or communication difficulties to help them communicate.

Thousands of children across the country use Makaton to make themselves understood and express themselves every day, and it also helps them understand what their teachers, parents and carers are trying to communicate.

Clear and simple communication is vital when discussing safeguarding issues with children, because by ensuring young people understand what is appropriate and inappropriate, and how they can tell someone whenever they think something might be wrong, the safer they will be.

We are proud of our efforts to ensure children and families with hearing impairments can easily access a variety of support services from the NSPCC.

Our Talk PANTS campaign has been available to children and professionals in Makaton as well as British Sign Language (BSL), for several years, ensuring they can have simple, age-appropriate conversations to help parents, teachers and carers keep children safer from sexual abuse.

In the last 13 years, Talk PANTS has helped more than six million people to educate children and ensure they understand that their body belongs to them, and they should tell a safe adult they trust if anything makes them feel upset or worried.

The campaign teaches children that Privates are private, Always remember your body belongs to you, No means no, Talk about secrets that upset you, and Speak up because somebody can help.

The idea is to Talk PANTS regularly whenever it feels natural, so that young people are comfortable and confident enough to speak openly about anything that might be wrong.

Through the NSPCC website, the Talk PANTS resources are not just available in Makaton and BSL, but sign-a-long PDFs and subtitled videos are also available to download or share, along with translations into other languages and custom-made guides for parents and carers of children with autism.

For older children with hearing difficulties, our Childline website is also available in accessible ways, with a section of the site dedicated to support d/Deaf children.

The Deaf Zone is there to help any young person who is hard of hearing and includes videos which are subtitled, using English or BSL, and offer advice on how children can deal with bullying or discrimination, and how to overcome challenges around d/Deafness.

We know some young people prefer using BSL to email or the Childline website’s 1-2-1 chat service, so there is also an option for children who prefer to communicate through British Sign Language to speak directly to our counsellors through an online video facility.

There are many reasons young people might need support, and our counsellors want to be there to help them through whatever they might be experiencing and however they prefer to communicate.

Whether they need to speak to someone about an issue at school, something they’re going through at home, or anything they’re worried about, Childline counsellors will be there for them around the clock.

Support is available through the Childline website or over the phone, with secure, confidential 1-2-1 chats with our counsellors.
