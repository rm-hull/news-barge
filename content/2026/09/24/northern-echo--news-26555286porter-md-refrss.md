**Kevin Donald meets Fred Wyrley-Birch who, alongside Alison Orgill, has led a management buyout at Anderson & Garland**

In 2007 a teenage porter at the historic Anderson & Garland auction house began to develop a keen fascination for the vast array of artefacts he ferried from warehouse to saleroom.

Leap forward almost 20 years and Fred Wyrley-Birch, now in his late thirties, is about to write a new chapter in the firm’s 186 year history by leading a management buy-out to become its new managing director and majority shareholder.

The deal sees his partner in the acquisition, Alison Orgill, become the first female shareholder director since the auction house was established in 1840

Sitting down with BusinessIQ in their HQ in Westerhope, Newcastle, Fred still displays the boundless enthusiasm of the young student who discovered he couldn’t leave behind his native North East or the auction house that he’d fallen in love with.

He proudly speaks of ‘The Northern Approach’ – a people-first philosophy he believes has given Anderson and Garland a competitive edge over bigger and more widely known rivals and says he’s merely a custodian of a business with ‘an extraordinary heritage and an equally exciting future’.

He remembers those early days fondly. ‘I was literally just moving things,’ he says. ‘Setting things out, shifting furniture, unpacking boxes. All the churning legs that keep the duck moving.’

But even in those first weeks, he found himself stopping mid‑task to ask questions: Why is this vase interesting? What does that mark mean? How do you know it’s this date? Curiosity, it turned out, was the first sign of a lasting career.

‘Eventually someone told me to put on a shirt...’then a jacket. Then a tie.’

He laughs. ‘Now that I own the business, I don’t have to wear a tie any more.’

Those early years taught him the rhythms of the auction world – the cataloguing, the condition reports, the conversations with buyers, the cycle of stock coming in and going out.

But they also taught him something deeper: that the auction house is not just a marketplace, but a custodian of people’s histories. And that custodianship, he says, is the part most people never see.

“People think auction houses just sell things,” he says. “But a huge part of what we do is helping families understand what they’ve inherited, or what they’re leaving behind.”

Probate valuations are the quiet, often emotional work of cataloguing the contents of a home after someone dies, and is one of the often unrecognised but vital services Anderson and Garland provides.

He recalls sitting with an elderly client earlier in the week, a man in his eighties living in a house filled with objects he loved but feared his family would simply throw away.

‘He didn’t want to go into a home. He didn’t want to sell anything. But he was worried his family wouldn’t know what any of it was worth.’

The man wrote Anderson and Garland into his will. Not as beneficiaries, but as administrators. ‘He’s asked us to come in when the time comes, do the probate valuation, sell the contents, and make sure everything is handled properly. His executors hardly need to do a thing. They just need to tell me he’s passed, and everything else is taken care of.’

Probate valuations, he explains, are not about replacement value – the figure on an insurance document – but about realisation value, the actual resale value of the contents.

‘The amount of people who go to an insurance document and say, “The contents are valued at £300,000, so that must be the figure”,’ he says. ‘They don’t realise they’ve just paid tens of thousands more in tax than they needed to. People end up penalising the estate without meaning to. It’s a minefield if you try to do it yourself.’

He has seen families avoid conflict simply because an independent valuation prevented arguments over who gets what. ‘It can be one of the hardest points in people’s lives,’ he says. ‘They’ve just lost someone. The last thing they want is to start arguing over what something is worth.’

The expertise behind these valuations is not accidental. Anderson and Garland has been doing them for decades, and Fred himself is gemologically trained. He talks about insurance valuations with the same precision he applies to probate work.

He says: ‘Some people want full retail replacement value. Others wouldn’t replace the item – it was a gift, or it has sentimental value – so they want a valuation that reflects what they’d actually lose if it were gone.’

High street jewellers, he says, often can’t help. ‘They’re retailers. They know how to sell you things. They don’t necessarily know how to value them.’

He has an array of examples where the firm has helped save families from unnecessary taxation. A watch collection worth £150,000 that didn’t need to be included in a probate valuation because watches are considered ‘depleting assets’ under inheritance‑tax rules. A family Victoria Cross that a client feared would tip them over the tax threshold until Fred explained that family medals passed down through generations are exempt.

‘Why should you be penalised for acts of heroism?’ he says. ‘It’s not your fault it’s now worth a quarter of a million pounds. There are very fine areas of the law. People don’t necessarily know about them. We do.’

The work extends beyond private clients. Museums, universities and institutions rely on Anderson & Garland for valuations, acquisitions and insurance updates.

But the heart of the work remains the human side of valuation. Last year a client in Canada reached out to the Newcastle institution with an extraordinary artefact - a lock of Admiral Nelson’s hair, likely taken after his death at Trafalgar.

The hair was DNA‑tested and accompanied by a fragment of epaulette believed to be from the jacket Nelson wore when he was shot. Other auction houses were slow to respond but Anderson and Garland picked up the phone.

‘Having a conversation with people is an art that’s sorely lacking,’ he says. ‘It makes a colossal difference.’

The item sold for around £17,000. ‘An awful lot of money for a piece of rope and a lock of hair,’ he says with a smile.

‘After nearly 200 years of trading, our reputation is everything to us. It’s local knowledge and global reach. We’re here, and we’re here to help. But we’ve got access to a market that is truly worldwide.’

He recalls the auction house’s record lot, a Banksy work that came through the saleroom in 2022. Merrivale Stable is a small model stable, no bigger than a toaster, dropped by the artist in a Great Yarmouth model village as part of his ‘Great British Spraycation’ project.

The piece had been intended to draw visitors to a declining seaside town in the aftermath of lockdown. For a time, it worked. But the practicalities of securing, insuring and displaying the work became overwhelming, and the owners decided to sell. In a tense telephone sale involving three bidders, the piece went for a remarkable £1m in Anderson and Garland’s saleroom.

Away from the high stakes dealing of the auction room, the firm also works with local schools, museums and universities, offering access to objects that students might otherwise only see in textbooks.

He talks about taking Roman coins into his children’s school, letting pupils handle them rather than peer at them through glass. ‘The engagement was electric,’ he says. ‘They were learning about the Romans, and suddenly they had real coins in front of them. They could see the emperors bust, the designs, the history.’

He has taken medals into classrooms, too – objects that lend themselves to research because British military history is so meticulously documented. He recalls a school group visiting the saleroom to learn how to investigate provenance, using medals as a starting point. ‘They can be quite elaborate, expensive items,’ he says. ‘But they’re also incredibly rich in information. You can research medals to the nth degree.’

Auction houses, he says, are far more accessible than people realise. ‘Even the biggest auction houses in the world struggle to get people in to view,’ he says. ‘People don’t think it’s accessible. They see hundreds of millions of pounds worth of art in one sale and assume it’s not for them.’

Meanwhile, the Tate or the National Gallery will have queues out the door. ‘But the items in auction houses often have never been seen in public,’ he says. ‘It’s newly discovered, which adds to the excitement. And a lot of it ends up here.’

He believes the North East has a particular advantage in this regard. The region’s artists – from industrial landscapes to coastal scenes – resonate with local buyers in ways that national works sometimes do not. ‘A view of Seaton Sluice brings you back to childhood memories,’ he says. ‘Crab fishing off the pier, walking with your dad. You’re not going to get that from an artist in the Tate necessarily, but you’ll find it here.’

It is, he says, about surrounding yourself with things that bring joy, not things that simply fill space.

Too many homes, he argues, are furnished by catalogue, ‘You get this soulless property that doesn’t bring you any real joy,’ he says. ‘Rather than going to find that unique piece that gets you excited.’

He contrasts a £300 IKEA chest of drawers with a Georgian mahogany chest that costs half as much and has already lasted two centuries. ‘It’ll have a knock here and a scratch there,’ he says. ‘But it’s got character. A story. And when you’re done with it, you can bring it back to us to sell on again.’

The same logic applies to art. Galleries do vital work, he says, but buyers often pay for the space, the overheads, the framing, the curation. At auction, those costs are already absorbed.

‘What you’re buying is the aesthetic beauty, the usefulness, the rarity,’ he says. ‘What it is truly worth.’

Cost and worth diverge sharply in the retail world. ‘Ninety‑nine per cent of artists are worth less at auction than what they were selling for in the gallery,’ he says. “That’s just the way it goes.’

Sometimes treasures are found in the most unexpected places, he says. He recalls one such visit in Hetton-le-Hole, County Durham, a standard miner’s cottage, unassuming from the outside. But inside was a medal collection of astonishing breadth: Waterloo medals, naval general service medals, exhibition pieces, discovery medals. ‘He had just about one of everything,’ Fred says.

The collector had built it quietly, week by week, setting aside a portion of his pay packet, buying medals instead of pints. ‘It was rather sweet,’ he says. ‘He used to go drinking with his brother. One would buy one round, the other the next. And every week, he’d say, “I’m a bit shy this week”, so his brother would buy the third pint. Looking back, he realised he’d got quite a lot back from all the pints he didn’t buy.’

The firm is one of the largest provincial auction houses in the country, and its name carries weight. But there is also a practical reality: London houses are selective.

‘They’re after the billionaires,’ he says. ‘They’re only interested in items worth tens, if not hundreds, of thousands per item.’

Collections that fall below that threshold – even if they are culturally significant – sometimes struggle to find a home.

He tells the story of an outsider‑art collection in Southampton. The estate couldn’t find anyone willing to value it. London houses weren’t interested. However, the accountant’s brother‑in‑law happened to be a client of Anderson & Garland, and suggested giving them a call.

One of the team travelled overnight, completed the valuation in a day, and delivered it within the week. The Christian-Leopold Heppe collection of Outsider Art, sold for over £650,000, helped along by a connection to David Bowie, whose collection of similar material drove massive market interest.

‘We’d done the job well,’ said Fred. ‘So they said, ‘Come and get it.’ And it did amazingly well.’

His own journey began at sixteen on a part time basis and he returned at nineteen, finding himself surrounded by colleagues two to three times his age.

He learned by asking questions, handling objects, by watching the auctioneers on the rostrum. After 10 years of valuing and auctioneering, he was offered the chance to buy into the business in 2017. He was 30, had just had a child, and felt young for the responsibility. Fred said: ‘After a decade of doing the same job, it gets cyclical, and although the items are always different, I was ready for the next challenge.’

Over the next decade, Fred became the junior partner, learning the business side as well as the auctioneering side. He discovered he enjoyed making decisions, seeing them through, and watching the results. ‘It used to be I’d have an idea and tell someone, and if they took it, great, and if they didn’t, there was nothing I could do. But making decisions and seeing whether they work – that was really interesting.’

By 2024, Julian was preparing to retire. The opportunity to buy the business would only come around once, and Fred took it. The management buy‑out completed in July, making him managing director and majority shareholder with Alison as his partner. Her role is central, he says. She handles the financial and operational side of the business, the part he freely admits is not his strength.

‘My father’s an accountant, my mother’s a bookkeeper,’ he says. ‘But it’s not my strong suit. I’m client‑facing. Alison takes care of the financial and operational aspects, and it works very well.’

When he first briefed the team after the buy‑out, he asked a simple question: ‘If you were in my position, taking on the MD role, what one thing would you change – other than your salary?’

The answers were revealing: ideas to make jobs easier, suggestions for more staff interaction, thoughts he hadn’t considered because his role is different from theirs. ‘It’s very important,’ he says. ‘The team we’ve collated around us is like one of our auction catalogues. We’re picking and choosing the best people to go into it.’

The team is local, and that matters. ‘Clients enjoy picking up the phone and hearing that accent,’ he says. ‘Whether they’re inside the area or outside it, they know they’re talking to a northern company. They know they’ll get the Northern Approach.’ It is, he says, the mindset of treating clients like family – direct, warm, honest, and invested.

He tells one last story – one that captures the spirit of the region he loves.

He was on the London Underground, talking to an American woman who apologised for speaking to him because a travel book had told her that nobody talks on the Tube.

He told her he wasn’t a local; he was from a town called Hexham in Northumberland. Weeks later, he bumped into her while walking through Hexham and discovered she had travelled north because of that conversation.

Fred said: ‘She told me ‘people in the North are wonderful. I don’t know why I wasted all that time in London”.

‘I think that tells you everything you need to know really.’
