*Why losing Yorkshire farms could put rural pubs at risk - by Kevin Hollinrake, Conservative MP for Thirsk and Malton*

RECENTLY, I launched this year's Thirsk and Malton Pub of the Year competition at the White Swan in Ampleforth. In a village like Ampleforth and many others across the region, the pub and the farm have always gone hand in hand. Both are having a rough time of it, and I'm not convinced this government understands how closely the two are tied.

I started the competition last year because I our pubs deserve a bit of praise. I've run a business myself, and I served as Minister responsible for pubs, so I know how hard landlords work for not a lot of thanks beyond the support of the local customers and community.

The Providence Inn at Yedingham won it last year. They stuck a beer mat with the voting code on their pumps so nobody could order a pint without seeing it. That's Yorkshire enterprise for you. This year Sacha Lord, who is famous for his work in the hospitality and nightlife sector, will be judging the finalists.

Our pubs need the support. Labour's hike in employer National Insurance has landed on top of higher energy bills from both wars and conflict, our Government’s own green levies and customers with less money in their pockets.

Plenty of landlords I speak to are working harder than ever just to stay afloat. A Conservative government would scrap business rates for pubs, and for other hospitality, retail and leisure businesses, up to £110,000 rateable value.

However, a country pub doesn't just live or die on what happens inside its four walls. Its regulars are farmers and the people who work alongside them. Its summer trade comes from visitors who drive up here to see a landscape that farmers built and still look after. If farming goes under, the village pub won't be far behind. And farming is being squeezed harder than I can remember.

To give just one example from my own patch of Thirsk and Malton. There's a family in a North York Moors dale who've farmed livestock there for a hundred years. Their daughter is 18 years old and she wants to carry it on, which frankly is the best news a farm can have. They took on the tenancy of the farm next door and asked Natural England if they could reseed and lime some of the grassland to grow winter feed. They were asked to change their methods, and they agreed. They were then told no anyway. On top of that, they were told to pay for an expensive Environmental Statement, in the very same letter that said permission was unlikely. You couldn't make it up.

Natural England accepts this grass isn't species rich. It only counts as "semi-natural" because it happens to sit inside the National Park. That rule doesn't come from any law passed by Parliament. It comes from guidance.

Now, Natural England has a proper job to do protecting wildlife, and no farmer I know argues with that. However, these sorts of policy choices, on top of the family farm tax, the closure of the Sustainable Farming Incentive, faster cuts to farm payments, and a new carbon tax on fertiliser. Ministers will defend each of those on its own merits. Put them together, though, and the message to rural Britain is pretty plain. You're not wanted.

Up in the hills, the money that used to help farmers buy in winter feed has gone. So they either grow their own or they pack up. When a farm packs up, the vet, the feed merchant and the local mart all lose business. The grazed pasture goes, and the curlews and lapwings go with it. In time, so do the views that keep our cafes, holiday cottages and pubs busy.

None of this is hard to fix. Ministers should get rid of the blanket rule that treats every field inside a National Park as "semi-natural". Natural England should have to consider whether a farm can actually survive. And there should be a quick, free way to get permission for ordinary jobs like reseeding and liming. Big decisions that affect the people who put food on our tables ought to be made by Parliament, not by guidance nobody voted for.

That young lady wanting to take on the family farm deserves a leg up, not a bill for a report that's going to end in a refusal anyway. The countryside is a workplace, not a museum.

So please do vote for your local at kevinhollinrake.org.uk/best-thirsk-malton-pub-2026 before the competition closes on December 1. And next time you're at the bar, spare a thought for the farmers. Without them, there'd be a lot fewer pubs worth voting for.
