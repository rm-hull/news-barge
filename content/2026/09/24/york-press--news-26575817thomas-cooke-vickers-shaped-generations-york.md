## Hidden history of the York factory where generations learned a trade and prospered

VIckers training school in 1966

*How Thomas Cooke and Vickers shaped generations in York, by Ivan Martin, Vice President of Yorkshire Architectural and York Archaeological Society (YAYAS)*

MANY generations of York families owe part of their story to a remarkable self-taught engineer whose name is now easily overlooked.

Thomas Cooke founded a precision engineering business in York that became known around the world and, long after his death, would become part of Vickers.

But perhaps his greatest legacy can be found not in the telescopes and surveying instruments his firm produced, but in the generations of York youngsters who learned their trade at "Cookies", as it was affectionately called.

Many visitors and residents who enjoy Museum Gardens may barely notice York's Observatory. Partly hidden by shrubbery, it is easy to walk past without spotting the blue plaque bearing Cooke's name.

![]()Mr John Cleather, general manager, salutes the workforce at Vickers Instruments,York, on winning two Queen's Awards in April, 1988

Yet his legacy – long before it was associated with Vickers – would become intertwined with one of the great technological advances of the 19th century: the railways.

Developments of the Industrial Revolution led from horse-drawn wagons being pulled on wooden rails, to steam engines pulling wagons on steel rails.

The financial benefits linked to this change encouraged the foundation of many local railway companies. They saw the opportunity to prosper by expanding the range of their limited local operations and promote the transporting of passengers.

To allow the steam engines to work efficiently, the new steel rails needed to run as straight as possible with minimal gradients. This requirement presented the civil engineers charged with the expansion a challenge. Initial expansion routes followed the banks of rivers (many towns or cities are on rivers) or closely followed the canal and road routes constructed by the likes of Thomas Telford (1757-1834), this approach presented an immediate, easy, but limited solution.

![]()Thomas Cooke plaque in York

Further development of the rail network during the 1830s and 1840s, demanding the navigation of the varied countryside and rugged terrain in remote areas, presented a major surveying problem. The number of bridges, tunnels, viaducts, cuttings and embankments requiring to be accurately constructed, all in a very short competitive time scale, was difficult to achieve. The railway civil engineers needed instrumental help.

Thomas Cooke was born in the village of Allerthorpe. He was a self-taught mathematician and engineer who specialised initially in making high-quality turret clocks and telescopes. His first workshop was opened in 1837 at 50 (now 18) Stonegate. The items he produced attracted much acclaim for their accuracy and attention to detail.

This soon led to him to produce theodolites and a range of other surveying instruments which were required by the civil engineers.

The instruments Cooke produced were renowned for their accuracy plus reliability and were in great demand. Business flourished and the railway civil engineers were happy.

As the rail network spread across the UK, other countries world-wide looked on in envy and wanted the same. Overseas orders for everything and anything connected to railways ‘boomed’, including Cooke’s surveying instruments.

To cope with the increased demand, Cooke re-located to a new dedicated workshop, Buckingham Works at Bishophill in 1855.

His surveying instruments were chosen by Captain Robert Scott for his ill-fated British Antarctic Expedition of 1910-13, this was perhaps the greatest accolade Cooke would ever receive; with the resulting publicity greatly enhancing his already bulging order book.

![]()Former York machinist, Thomas Cooke

The resulting fame and success warranted offices as far away as India and Canada; T. Cooke & Son Ltd became a global firm. Later, amalgamation with Troughton and Simms Ltd. in 1922 and then Vickers in 1924, was a period of stability before a move to a new workshop at Haxby Road in 1939 to concentrate on military contracts. This workshop closed in 1988, ending a precision engineering era.

The legacy left by Thomas Cooke will be measured by historians in many ways. But the personal affect on many citizens of York is often overlooked.

![]()VIckers training school in 1966

For many decades, countless 16 year old York boys enrolled as apprentices with ‘Cookie’s’. Most had failed their ‘eleven-plus exam’ and thought their future was grim. Their apprenticeship would generate personal pride, surprise that they could work to the exacting standards required by the firm and also achieve academic qualifications way beyond their expectations.

At the end of their apprenticeships they would step into adult life with a confidence they never thought possible. Their apprenticeships enriched their lives and families, inspired their children, the whole of York benefited.

That is the rich human legacy of Thomas Cooke. For further information about the life of Thomas Cooke visit the website of the York Philosophical Society.
