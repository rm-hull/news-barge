Just days after the latest PISA assessment delivered another shock for Germany’s schools, a new study appears to reinforce the message.

The annual Education Monitor (*Bildungsmonitor*), published by the employer-backed Initiative for a New Social Market Economy (INSM) and compiled by researchers at the German Economic Institute (IW), ranks Germany’s 16 federal states according to the performance of their education systems.

The study has been carried out every year since 2004 and this year draws on 98 indicators using data primarily from 2023 and 2024.

While some states continue to outperform others, the study concludes that educational standards have deteriorated across the country since 2013, particularly in school quality and educational opportunities.

**How do the different states stack up?**

At the top of the table, Saxony retained first place with 62.9 points, comfortably ahead of Bavaria on 57.8 and Hamburg on 53.6. Baden-Württemberg and Saarland rounded out the top five.

Rather than a simple north-south divide, the ranking reveals a patchwork of educational fortunes. Several southern states continue to perform strongly, but eastern Germany also boasts some of the strongest results, with Saxony and Thuringia both placing in the top six.

At the other end of the scale, Bremen remained rooted to last place on 36.1 points. Saxony-Anhalt and North Rhine-Westphalia were only marginally ahead, while Brandenburg and Rhineland-Palatinate also finished in the bottom group.

Berlin landed in 11th place, putting the capital firmly in the lower half of the table.

Perhaps the most striking finding is that even the highest-performing states are losing ground. Germany’s average score fell to 46.2 points, down 2.9 points compared with the 2013 Education Monitor.

**How are the rankings calculated?**

The study compares states across a wide range of measures covering the entire education system, from childcare centres to universities.

Researchers look at factors including class sizes, student-teacher ratios, education spending, full-day childcare provision, school dropout rates, academic achievement, vocational training opportunities and university research performance.

Other categories include digitalisation, internationalisation, labour-market orientation and educational poverty.

The figures are converted into points and assessed from an ‘educational-economic’ perspective, examining how effectively education contributes to prosperity, social mobility and the future skilled workforce.

Around 18.8 percent of young adults in Germany still lack a formal vocational qualification, a factor strongly associated with lower earnings and a higher risk of poverty and unemployment.

There was one brighter note. The researchers found that young people today are somewhat more likely to achieve upward educational mobility than they were 20 years ago, although educational success remains strongly linked to social background.

**The government is promising action**

The publication comes as politicians continue to respond to the latest PISA results.

On Tuesday, Federal Education Minister Karin Prien said Germany must place education much higher on the political agenda.

Talking to Politico, she said that everyone should "feel responsible for providing our children and young people with fair opportunities for a good start in life and a bright future" and added that Chancellor Friedrich Merz had pledged his support.

The minister also announced plans for measures aimed at protecting children and young people from the effects of excessive social media use. According to Prien, a new programme will be developed over the coming months and presented before the end of the year, with a particular focus on young children and public awareness campaigns.
