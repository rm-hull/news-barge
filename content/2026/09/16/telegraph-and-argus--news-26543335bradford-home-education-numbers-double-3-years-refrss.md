As thousands of children return to classrooms this September, a growing number of families are taking a different approach to education.

Recent figures show there were around 1,400 children in elective home education in Bradford in autumn 2025-26, more than double the 690 recorded three years earlier.

While some parents actively choose home education, others say it became the best option after mainstream schooling stopped meeting their child's needs.

I spoke to three parents about their experiences.


## "She's back to her normal bubbly self"

Amy removed her six-year-old daughter from school earlier this year after concerns about her wellbeing.

Her daughter had enjoyed reception but struggled after moving into Year 1.

Amy said: "I watched the life get sucked out of her. She wasn't her happy bubbly self anymore.

"By December she was struggling that much that she didn't want to go to school most days. She was asking why she had to live. Bear in mind she was only five at the time."

After starting home education at Easter, Amy says her daughter's wellbeing improved dramatically.

She said: "We get to spend each and every day together doing things she enjoys, learning about the things she enjoys. Nothing is forced because she's engaging really well and she's thriving."

Learning is now built around reading, maths, English, educational trips and forest school sessions.

"It's not nine until three like school," Amy said. "We fit things around what works best for her."

While positive about the experience, Amy believes more support should be available for families educating children at home, particularly for activities, tutoring and learning resources.

She said: "At first I was trying to mimic school, but my daughter pushed back and refused to learn. When I spoke to the local authority they explained that, for her age, I was probably trying to do too much. It's a lot more relaxed now and we do things at her own pace."

## Learning through life's experiences

For Jodie, who home educates her five-year-old son and runs a Forest School in Tong, home education was a choice she made from the beginning,

She was first inspired to look at alternatives, after working in different schools.

Jodie explained: "I noticed quite a lot of children trying to bid for connection and attention.

"When you've only got two teachers and 30 pupils, some children miss out on that one-to-one support that they desperately want and need."

Jodie says one of the biggest benefits is the freedom to learn through her son's interests.

She said: "We've been to places like Pontefract Castle and spoken to historians, then come home and tied that into maths, English and art."

Jodie also said concerns about socialisation had not matched her family's experience.

She said: "We're in a WhatsApp group with around 30 families and we meet every week. My son has a huge group of friends and, through home education, I've made a lot of friends as well.

Jodie recently launched forest school sessions specifically for home-educated children after noticing a lack of outdoor learning opportunities.


## "I'm a mum, not a teacher"

For Toni, from Queensbury, home education was never part of the plan.

Her 13-year-old daughter, who has autism, has been unable to access full-time mainstream education for around two years.

Toni said moving to secondary school proved overwhelming despite support being put in place.

"It got to the point where there was no way we could drag her out of the car anymore," she said. "Getting her into school was heart-breaking."

Her daughter now attends alternative provision for limited hours each week, but Toni says the family spent long periods trying to fill the gaps themselves.

"I'm a mum, not a teacher," she said. "You can't expect a child to learn at school and then suddenly learn at home.

"The pressure and guilt you feel is unreal because essentially you're the one responsible for making sure they get an education."

Toni said trying to provide learning at home while balancing everyday life was extremely difficult.

She believes more support is needed for children who struggle in mainstream education but do not fit neatly into specialist provision.

"There's no middle ground," she said. "It's either mainstream or super-specialist. These children are lost in the middle."

## A growing trend

The experiences of Amy, Jodie and Toni reflect a growing trend.

Home education numbers in Bradford have more than doubled in the past three years, while figures across England continue to rise.

While their circumstances are very different, all three parents believe children benefit when learning is tailored to their individual needs and circumstances.

Their stories highlight the many different reasons families are looking beyond the traditional classroom.
