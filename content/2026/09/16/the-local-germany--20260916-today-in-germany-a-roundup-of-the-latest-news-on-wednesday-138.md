**Today's top story: Organised crime in Germany becoming more international and digitally connected**

Germany’s organised crime networks are becoming increasingly international, digitally connected and violent, according to the Federal Criminal Police Office's (BKA) latest report.

The number of investigation proceedings into organised crime rose to 683 in 2025, the highest figure since 2021, while the number of suspects increased by more than 13 percent to 7,988 – also the highest level in five years.

While presenting the findings, BKA Chief Holger Münch said: "Organised crime is changing its face: criminal networks today operate in a much more digitally interconnected, international and division-of-labour-based manner."

Drug-related crime once again accounted for the vast majority of organised crime investigations during the reporting period, followed by economic crime, tax and customs offences, migrant smuggling, property crimes and money laundering. A total of 31 investigations focused primarily on violent crime.

The BKA warned of an increase in extreme violence, including cases in which violent acts are commissioned as a service or carried out by minors recruited specifically for the purpose. Perpetrators are increasingly being recruited through social media, with some of those hired to carry out violent crimes as young as 13.

Such criminal acts operate on the principle of "one plans, one pays, one carries out the attack," said Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt (CSU) on Tuesday.

Münch added that criminal networks today also "exploit geopolitical conflicts".

**German drone maker goes underground amid suspected Russia threat**

Changing location every few weeks and constantly accompanied by body guards, the head of a German startup supplying drones to Ukraine has been forced into hiding due to a suspected Russian assassination plot.

"Putin chose this path, and I have to accept that and respond accordingly," Stefan Thumann, CEO of German defence tech firm Donaustahl, told AFP in an interview from an undisclosed location.

The 39-year believes that the saga will only end one of two ways – when "the Russian regime is toppled" or the Russians succeed in killing him, he said.

Thumann is an outspoken supporter of Ukraine's war against President Vladimir Putin and a supplier of attack drones to Kyiv. Authorities first warned him last December that he could be in danger, he said.

Soon after, "I was explicitly told that the threat comes from Russia and that a murder attempt on me is planned," he said.

His life has been turned upside down by being forced to go underground, although he still manages to oversee his company's work.

"I'm more or less completely isolated from the outside world," he said, adding that communication with family was "sporadic" and "only digital".

Contacted by AFP, the interior ministry of the southern state of Bavaria – where Donaustahl is based – said it was aware of the case but declined to comment further, noting that it was under investigation by federal prosecutors.

**School pupils in Würzburg evacuated after gas leak**

Around 2,000 pupils were evacuated from a vocational school in Würzburg, Bavaria, on Tuesday due to a chlorine gas leak at the building's swimming pool.

The incident happened shortly before 10am at the Franz-Oberthür School. Firefighters said two substances used to disinfect the pool had been combined – but because the swimming pool water was missing, the two agents triggered an unwanted chemical reaction.

A total of 14 people were affected, with 10 taken to hospital mainly suffering from respiratory irritation.

The fire service later gave the all-clear after emergency crews removed and neutralised the chemical mixture.

Police are investigating the circumstances of the incident.

**German investor morale misses expectations as Mideast war weighs**

German investor sentiment rose much less than expected in September, a closely watched survey showed Tuesday, underscoring the economy's fragility as the Mideast war persists and suspected attacks on infrastructure unnerve investors.

The ZEW institute's economic expectations index edged up just 0.5 points to 34.7, the survey found, its fifth rise in a row.

That was far below forecasts of a 10-point increase in a poll of analysts by the financial platform FactSet.

Expensive energy resulting from the US war against Iran and the near total closure of the Strait of Hormuz were weighing on sentiment, ZEW president Achim Wambach said.

Petrol prices on Monday reached a record of €2.286 ($2.64) a litre in Germany as the war pushes up oil and gas prices worldwide.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said Tuesday that the government "had to act" on fuel costs, adding that it would have concrete measures to announce "very soon".

**Berlin's run-down public spaces at heart of election campaign**

Politicians campaigning for Sunday's Berlin state election have painted an unflattering picture of the German capital, vowing to clear garbage-strewn streets and promising "syringe-free playgrounds".

Town hall-hopefuls have also pledged to clean up graffiti-covered train stations, end "forever-building sites", fix buckled cycling lanes and take steps to ease a desperate shortage of affordable housing.

Taken together, the list of woes hardens the image of a chronically broke city whose administration often struggles to reliably provide services for its almost four million people.

Certainly, many who live in Berlin love its rich artistic and cultural life, green spaces, lakes and children's playgrounds, vibrant start-up scene and hedonistic, anything-goes nightlife.

But that has gone hand-in-hand with persistent complaints about a slow bureaucracy and dysfunctional services.

Local newspapers often write about Berlin's "crime hotspots" and rising homelessness, and make great sport of documenting ever-new Berlin disasters – from rat-infested police stations to the closure of the Technical University's main campus due to structural defects and fire safety issues.

A spokesperson for the City told AFP, when asked about the garbage problem, that there were "no operational failures", pointing instead to many residents' lack of civic responsibility.
