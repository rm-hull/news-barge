For centuries, international scholars fiercely debated why the masterpiece was configured in such a remarkably long, narrow strip. The answer has officially been uncovered by **Professor Christopher Norton**, Professor Emeritus of Art History, whose meticulous architectural analysis proved the textile was custom-made to fit precisely along the nave walls of Bayeux Cathedral for its consecration in 1077.


**Professor Christopher Norton, whose pioneering research into the French masterpiece originally grew out of his extensive local studies into Norman architecture, stated:**

The research shows that the tapestry was designed to fit specific bays between the west wall and the choir screen. It settles the long-running dispute over its original destination.



The breakthrough carries significant weight for regional historians who have long argued that the tapestry presents a heavily biased, pro-Norman visual narrative. The famous embroidery completely omits the pivotal bloody battles fought across the North of England during the chaotic autumn of 1066, including King Harold Godwinson’s historic, exhausting victory over Viking invaders right before marching south to his ultimate defeat at Hastings.

To provide a vital counter-narrative to the London exhibition, a special local display is launching this Saturday, 19 September, at the regional museum trust. The exhibition will allow visitors to explore rare local artefact hoards, delve into specialised curatorial lectures, and review the deep, lasting trauma the Norman Conquest inflicted on local communities—a perspective completely silenced by the tapestry itself.


**A senior curator involved in the regional project added:** 

1066 is a defining moment, but the tapestry only highlights a fraction of the story. This local exhibition allows us to explore the real archaeological evidence and give residents a rare opportunity to uncover what the Conquest genuinely meant for the people of this region.



While the British Museum’s initial public ticket allocation crashed web servers and sold out within hours, local residents will have immediate, unfettered access to interactive weaving workshops, local historical debates, and expert-led tours running throughout the autumn season.


To celebrate the tapestry’s UK homecoming, the Yorkshire Museum is launching a dedicated “Bayeux Tapestry Celebration” on Saturday, 19 September.
