OPEN LETTER — INTERNATIONAL DAY OF DEMOCRACY

Dear Home Secretary,

Re: Linton-on-Ouse: a test of whether local democracy really matters

Today, 15 September, is the International Day of Democracy — an opportunity not simply to celebrate elections, but to reflect on what democracy means and requires.

The Inter-Parliamentary Union’s Universal Declaration on Democracy identifies freedom, equality, transparency and responsibility as fundamental principles. It emphasises open participation, public accountability and the importance of decentralised local and regional government. Those principles are being put to a serious test at Linton-on-Ouse.

The Government is considering using the former RAF site for asylum accommodation for single adult men, with the site intended to operate for a minimum of three years. The Home Office says no final decision has been made and that necessary planning permissions and approvals will be required. But the fundamental democratic question is already clear: Who gets to decide the future of Linton-on-Ouse?

This is not simply an argument about asylum policy, nor against our obligations to people seeking asylum. It is an argument about how government exercises power.

Devolution cannot mean local power only when Whitehall agrees. The Government says it is devolving power. Yet what is happening at Linton-on-Ouse tells a different story. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has promised to take power out of Westminster and carry it “into every postcode in the land”. His government has described its programme as the “biggest transfer of power from Westminster in a generation”, giving communities greater control over housing, jobs, transport and public services. We welcome that ambition, but it cannot mean local power when Whitehall agrees, and Whitehall power when it does not.

The Mayor of York and North Yorkshire, David Skaith, has made clear that RAF Linton-on-Ouse is the wrong place for large-scale asylum accommodation. He has identified the site as one of the region’s biggest opportunities for housing-led redevelopment — delivering homes, jobs and investment that North Yorkshire urgently needs.

North Yorkshire Council has likewise raised serious concerns about infrastructure, healthcare, education, transport, community safety and public services, and has stated that this is the wrong proposal for the wrong location.

Yet the Home Office retains the power to take this strategic site out of local hands for a minimum of three years to address a national accommodation requirement.

What is the purpose of devolving responsibility for housing and economic development to elected local leaders if Whitehall can simply take control of a major strategic site when the local vision does not align with its own? This is not a minor planning dispute. It is a question about where power resides.

Local planning is part of democracy

We are particularly concerned by the possibility of exceptional or emergency planning mechanisms being used to avoid the normal local planning process. If that is contemplated, the Government must explain why.

The Home Office has stated that no final decision will be made until feasibility assessments, planning permissions and necessary approvals have been properly considered. That commitment should be honoured in full. Local planning is not bureaucracy to be circumvented when inconvenient. It is a mechanism for democratic scrutiny — allowing infrastructure, highways, health provision, environmental impacts, public safety and community consequences to be examined openly, with elected representatives, statutory bodies and residents able to challenge the evidence. Removing that scrutiny would not simply make the decision faster. It would make it less accountable.

What is the emergency?

We understand the Government’s pledge to end the use of asylum hotels but a self-imposed political deadline cannot, by itself, justify removing democratic safeguards.

Nor does the financial case make such urgency self-evident. The National Audit Office found that the Home Office’s large-sites programme was expected to cost £46 million more than using hotels, after the original assessment that it would save money was revised. It also found that the rapid establishment of large sites had increased risk and resulted in losses. The lesson from that experience should surely be better scrutiny and better decision-making, not less.

Democracy is not simply the right to vote

We recognise that Government must sometimes make decisions in the national interest and that local communities do not possess an absolute veto over national policy. However, democracy requires more than the right to vote periodically for representatives whose decisions can subsequently be disregarded.

It requires representation, transparency, participation and accountability. It requires people to have a meaningful voice in decisions affecting their communities and elected local leaders to have genuine influence over the responsibilities they have been given. The IPU’s declaration makes the principle clear: democratic institutions should foster decentralised local and regional government in order to broaden public participation.

Linton-on-Ouse therefore presents the Government with a simple test: will the principles of devolution apply when Whitehall finds them inconvenient?

On this International Day of Democracy, we ask for three commitments:

 1. Respect the local planning process.

 2. Respect meaningful local participation.

 3. Explain the democratic case.

Do not use emergency or exceptional powers to remove RAF Linton-on-Ouse from normal local planning scrutiny without a compelling, transparent and publicly demonstrated justification.

Ensure that the community, North Yorkshire Council, the Mayor of York and North Yorkshire, other relevant elected and public bodies, and the local community can participate meaningfully before, not after, a final decision is made.

Explain how taking a major strategic development opportunity out of local hands for a minimum of three years is consistent with a government whose stated purpose is to transfer power from Westminster to local communities.

The question before the Government is bigger than Linton-on-Ouse. It is whether devolution is a genuine transfer of power or simply a principle that applies until Whitehall wants something different.

On this International Day of Democracy, we ask the Government to demonstrate that local voices, local representatives and local democratic institutions still matter when national government wants to act.

Democracy is not just about who holds power. It is about how that power is exercised. Linton-on-Ouse is a test of whether this Government truly believes that power should be closer to the people.

Kind regards,

The Linton Action Group
