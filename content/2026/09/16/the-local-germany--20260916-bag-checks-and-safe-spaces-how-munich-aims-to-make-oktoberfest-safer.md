Millions of people flock to Oktoberfest – or *Wiesn* as it's also known – in the Bavarian capital of Munich every year. 

While the world-famous festival at the *Theresienwiese* is all about socialising, entertainment and enjoying (often rather a lot of) beer, safety takes top priority.

That's the case more so now than ever amid continued concerns about the threat of terrorism in Germany, as well as the need to manage the risks that come with an event attracting so many people to a relatively small area.

Last year a bomb threat – said to be in connection with a family dispute rather than a terror incident – briefly shut the festival site.

Here's a look at the security plans and rules this year.

Around 600 police officers – alongside additional security staff – will be on duty to help keep the expected more than six million guests safe.

Visitors will once again face bag checks when entering the festival grounds. Dangerous items, such as glass bottles and knives are prohibited. Bags can be a maximum of 20 cm x 15 cm x 10 cm (equivalent to a small handbag or rucksack) and cannot have a capacity of more than three litres.

Security cameras have also been installed throughout the site, allowing police to keep a close check on what's happening.

Camera images will feed into a new security control centre, where artificial intelligence will be used to generate so-called ‘heat maps’.

These are designed to show where crowds are gathering and identify areas where visitor numbers could become too high, allowing measures to be taken before potential problems.

‘Crowdspotters’, who are specialists in constant contact with festival management and the authorities, will also be on hand to help assess the situation.

The system is part of a wider effort to monitor the grounds in real time and respond quickly when large crowds or other incidents develop.

**App provides help in the tents**

An app developed by a Munich-based company is being used to increase safety at the festival.

The free ‘Safe Now’ app allows visitors to request immediate assistance by pressing a button on their mobile phone: for example, in cases of harassment, fights or medical emergencies.

Security staff are then alerted and receive a location accurate to within a metre, helping them find the person who needs help.

The initiative is now in its fourth year, with eight of the larger festival tents taking part. The Oktoberfest medical service is also involved.

**Mini-hospital serves as* Wiesn*Outpatient Clinic**

The main base for the festival's medical service is the *Wiesn* Outpatient Clinic, which is more like a mini-hospital, complete with treatment rooms, small operating theatres and a CT scanner.

Last year, more than 7,000 patients were treated there, with a similar number expected this year. The range of conditions treated extends from alcohol poisoning and injuries sustained in brawls to cuts and lacerations.

Oktoberfest visitors can also be taken to the *Wiesn* Ambulance Centre on stretchers. To ensure rapid assistance even in dense crowds, there are two decentralised medical containers.

In an emergency, help arrives in less than four minutes, said *Wiesn* director Christian Scharpf.

He describes the ambulance service as the "heart" of the service centre, which also helps to relieve the pressure on the city’s hospitals. Only 1.6 percent of patients had to be taken to hospital last year.

A dedicated ‘safe space’ for women and girls will also operate from the ambulance centre.

Experience from previous years shows that women seek help for a range of reasons. Some have become separated from their friends or lost belongings such as mobile phones or hotel keys, while others have had items stolen.

Some visitors also seek help after experiencing sexual or physical violence.

The team behind the *‘Sichere Wiesn’* initiative offers advice and support, accompanies women to other services if needed, and can provide practical help such as blankets and clothing.

But efforts to make the *Wiesn* safer for women do not begin once the festival gets underway.

In the run-up to Oktoberfest, the initiative also calls on men to play an active role in preventing harassment and sexist behaviour.

Among its recommendations are that men should not join in with sexist remarks or jokes at women's expense; they should intervene when someone fails to respect a woman's boundaries; and speak up if friends or colleagues behave inappropriately.
