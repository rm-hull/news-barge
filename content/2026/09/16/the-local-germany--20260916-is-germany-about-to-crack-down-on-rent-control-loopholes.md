For years, Germany's housing debate has centred on a familiar complaint: there are too few affordable homes and rents keep rising.

The 2026 *Mietreport* from the German Tenants' Association (DMB) confirms that these problems remain deeply entrenched. But it goes further, arguing that rising housing costs are no longer just a housing problem but a threat to Germany's broader social safety net.

According to the report, many renters are now cutting back on food, healthcare and retirement savings to keep up with housing costs. The DMB's conclusion is stark: Germany's housing crisis is becoming a crisis for the welfare state itself.

Shortly after the report was published, Federal Justice Minister Stefanie Hubig renewed calls for tougher enforcement of existing tenant protections. So, is Berlin finally listening?

**A housing crisis that reaches far beyond housing**

The *Mietreport's* most striking finding is not simply that rents are high. It’s how vulnerable renters have become.

According to the DMB survey, 58 percent of tenants saw their housing costs rise during the past year, while 29 percent said they couldn't remain in their current home for more than three months if they suffered a major loss of income through illness, unemployment or separation.

The report argues that housing cost pressures are increasingly spilling over into other areas of life. Many respondents reported saving less for retirement, while significant numbers said they had reduced spending on healthy food or healthcare.

**The DMB says government housing policy is falling short**

The report is more than a collection of statistics. It's also a critique of current housing policy.

First, the DMB argues that rent protections need to be strengthened. It says the rent brake (*Mietpreisbremse*) works where it’s properly applied, but that enforcement is weak and too many tenants are reluctant to challenge landlords through the courts.

The association is calling for stricter enforcement of the rent brake, tougher action against rent gouging and the closure of loopholes involving index-linked leases and what it describes as abusive furnishing surcharges.

Second, it argues that social housing policy is moving backwards. Germany had around 1.03 million social housing units in 2025, which is a record low. In the same year, more than twice as many social housing units left the programme as new social housing units were created.

Third, the DMB sharply criticises planned reductions to housing benefit payments (*Wohngeld*). Its argument is simple: cutting benefits will not lower rents or create new homes but could leave more households at risk of rent arrears and homelessness.

Hubig's comments echo one of the report's central complaints: that existing tenant protections are too easy to circumvent.

The SPD politician told Funke Media Group there were still too many "gaps" in tenant protection and too many ways to get around the rent brake.

She has also backed examining fines for landlords who violate the rules and reforms to legislation on rent gouging – and pointed specifically at furnished and short-term rentals as areas where existing regulations may be too easy to sidestep.

The DMB estimates that Germany is short of around 1.4 million homes, but the justice minister has not addressed the issue of social housing. Nor has she responded directly to the DMB's criticism of housing benefit cuts.

Still, the fact that the government is talking about closing loopholes and introducing penalties suggests that at least one of the concerns raised in the *Mietreport* is reaching policymakers.

For renters, that could be a welcome sign that the debate is moving beyond acknowledging the problem – and towards actually attempting to enforce the rules which are already in place.
