IF ever there was a wake-up call, especially to those of us who are getting on a bit, it is surely 70 Up - the final instalment of the documentary series like no other.

What began as Seven Up!, a one-off programme commissioned for Granada Television’s World In Action, became a remarkable series that has captivated the nation for seven decades.

It was, and remains, a study of Britain’s class system. First broadcast in 1963, it took a group of seven-year-olds from various socio-economic backgrounds and asked them about their lives and hopes. Would the class each child was born into define their lives?

That has, on the whole, turned out to be the case. Last night the series ended with 70 Up, a poignant final catch-up with the remaining participants. First introduced as children and filmed every seven years since, they’re now pensioners, reflecting on their lives and the choices they made.

The prep school boys who, aged seven, looked like accountants in shirts and ties and talked of going to Oxbridge, have enjoyed wealth and privilege, following careers that were expected of them.

Tony, the East End lad who dreamed of being a jockey but became a cabbie, is still driving cabs. He cried as he said it broke his heart when he was discouraged from being a jockey. The three working-class girls, Jackie, Lynn and Sue, navigated work and family life, and Suzy, the boarding school girl bruised by her parents’ divorce, and a chain-smoking introvert at 21, went on to find happiness and fulfilment with her husband and children.

Bruce, the rather serious boy who wanted to be a missionary, and at 21 said, with a wry smile, that he was the only socialist in his Oxford college, became a teacher in inner city schools and remains a decent, principled man.

Tellingly, the two boys from a children’s home went on to have large families. Symon and his wife have fostered more than 100 children and Paul too is happily married, surrounded by grandchildren.

It is Neil whose story is seen as most poignant. The bright suburban schoolboy had the world at his feet. By 21 he’d quit university and was labouring on a building site. He became a nomad, moving around the country taking on various jobs. He’s always filmed trudging about, carrying plastic bags in the rain, looking like a lost soul. But there is no self pity with Neil. Articulate and pragmatic, he now lives in France and seems reasonably content.

The one I found most moving was Nick, the Dales farmer’s son who wanted “to learn about the Moon and all that” and became a nuclear physicist. Interviewed at 63, he revealed he was terminally ill and has since passed away.

Lynn died in 2013 and Michael Apted, who directed the programmes, died in 2021.

I’ve long been fascinated by the Up series and found 70 Up, directed by Asif Kpadia, particularly moving. It’s a reminder of how short life is, and how we should make the most of what’s left. As a viewer, you can’t help but reflect on your own life. I sometimes wonder if I made the right decision to go to university; it was drummed into us at school that having a degree would boost our careers and earning potential, but in my experience that wasn’t the case. I worked hard at school and spent years sitting exams. While I enjoyed university, having a degree hasn’t benefited me financially. I’ve spent my adult life fretting about money, whereas my brother left school at 16, didn’t even turn up for his exams, and earned twice as much as me, retiring in his 50s.

Life is a series of choices. What I have benefited from, and am grateful for, is the freedom and opportunities that my working-class grandparents didn’t have. My grandma was working in a mill at 14 and was married at 18.

Seven Up! was of its time; we wince at moments such as Apted asking one of the girls why she’s not married at 21, and how 14-year-old Symon feels about being “illegitimate”. It was of its time - that was the point - and it was often unsettling to watch. But there will never be another television series with the same impact.
