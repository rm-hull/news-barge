Alarm sirens rang out in cities across Germany on Thursday morning at 11am. Mobile phones made shrill alarm noises and displayed warning text messages. Official warning apps triggered notifications.

If you live in Germany you would (hopefully) get an emergency warning in this form in the event of a major natural disaster in your area, or in the event of a military attack.

But don't worry -- there was no danger on Thursday. This alarm was just a drill.

It comes as an annual test of Germany's national, state and local emergency broadcast channels, which happens every year on 'Warning Day' (*Warntag*).

*Warntag* has been an annual practice in Germany since 2020 -- coming each year on the second Thursday in September. This year the warning was scheduled for September 10th at 11am.

There are also other regional warning days in certain states in Germany. Bavaria, Hesse, Rhineland-Palatinate and North Rhine-Westphalia also have state-wide warning system tests in March.

Officials say regular tests of warning alert systems are crucial to make sure that they work, and that people can be reached effectively.

"Basically, we are simulating an emergency: Will the warning be received? And do people know what to do with it?" explains the Bonn-based Federal Office for Civil Protection and Disaster Assistance (BBK).

The main warning is triggered via the federal government's modular warning system, which relies primarily on data transmission by satellite, but also by cable for safety.

The alarm is spread via sirens, digital display boards, apps on the mobile phone, FM broadcasting, digital radio and cell broadcast technology.

At the same time, municipalities can test their own warning channels, for example by sounding an alarm on loudspeakers.

If you hear an alarm near you, rising and falling siren howling sound means warning. A continuous tone signals the end of the test alarm or, in an emergency, that the danger has passed.

An all clear signal was to be sent at around 11:45 am.

"It is just as important for the population to know when a danger has passed," says the BBK.

**48,000 sirens**

Not everyone in Germany will have heard a siren on Thursday.

Participation in *Warntag* is voluntary at the municipal level, so some cities that do have sirens may not not trigger them. In other places there is no siren nearby.

Last year, according to the BBK, 66 percent of people in Germany were reached via a siren.

States and municipalities are responsible for maintaining their own sirens. The BBK knows of around 48,000 active siren locations.

**Cell broadcast**

A warning signal is set to be sent to mobile phones. This message should also reach tourists, and other people with foreign mobile phone numbers, who are currently in Germany.

According to the BBK, the procedure is anonymous. Unlike sending by SMS, for example, mobile phone numbers and other data of the recipients does not have to be known.

This year, for the first time, the all-clear will also be sent via cell broadcast.

According to the BKK, more than 90 percent of participants were reached via at least one warning channel last year.

To receive the mobile warning, your phone needs to be on and not in airplane mode. In addition, the German government advises keeping the operating system up to date.

There are also warning apps, such as Nina, that provide another means for these and other official warnings to reach you. The Nina warning app is specifically intended to alert people about local disasters like fires or floods.
