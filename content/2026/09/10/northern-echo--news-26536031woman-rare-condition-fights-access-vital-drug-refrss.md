A woman living with a rare condition that has progressively robbed her of her ability to walk says she feels "isolated" as she fights for access to a vital drug she believes could slow its devastating effects.

Georgia Hart, 29, from Hartlepool, was diagnosed with Friedreich's ataxia (FA) when she was just 15.

Fifteen years on, she is a full-time wheelchair user, has significant problems with balance and coordination and has suffered heart damage as the condition continues to progress.

  Georgia Hart was first diagnosed with FA when she was 15-years-old 

*(Image: PROVIDED)*

Now Georgia, her mum Bev and others living with the rare disease are campaigning to have the drug omaveloxolone (OMAV) made available on the NHS in England.

The drug can slow the progression of FA and has been approved in the UK, but patients in England are still unable to access it through the NHS because funding has not been put in place.

It is available in Scotland, Northern Ireland and elsewhere in Europe.

The Northern Echo contacted the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), who decide which drugs or treatments can be available on the NHS, which said it was unable to make a recommendation on omaveloxolone after manufacturer Biogen withdrew its evidence submission.

NICE said it cannot recommend a drug if the company does not take part in its appraisal process, but added that it is ready to reopen the assessment if Biogen submits new evidence.

For Georgia, the fight has become deeply personal. She says her friends around the world who also live with Friedreich's ataxia are now taking the drug, while she remains unable to get it.

Georgia said: "The petition is so important, we need 100,000 people to take two minutes out of their day to ensure the drug is debated in parliament.

"As you can imagine having a rare disease can be really isolating so the online community I have found is priceless. These groups are made up of people from the UK, Europe, America, Canada, Australia etc. I have made some of my best friends this way, this online community has become a daily part of my life.

"However, for the last few years the focus of these online spaces has been on OMAV and now I feel once again, isolated. It feels like England is the only country without access to the drug.

"Time is of the essence for people with FA, I have an incredible life and I want to enjoy it for as long as I can."

  Georgia and other petitioners calling for the vital drug to be available to all in the UK 

*(Image: PROVIDED)*

The condition is a rare neurological disorder which progressively damages the nervous system.

It can cause problems with muscle coordination and walking, as well as heart and spinal complications.

Bev said Georgia had led a normal life before her diagnosis, taking part in theatre school, horse riding and gymnastics.

But after a podiatry appointment raised concerns about her balance, Georgia was eventually referred to a genetics clinic where the family was given the devastating diagnosis.

Bev said they were initially told there was no cure and no treatment for the condition.

Instead, Georgia has had to receive treatment for its individual effects, travelling to London to see a neurologist and receiving specialist care for her heart and orthopaedic needs.

Over the years, the condition has continued to take more from her.

Georgia gradually lost her ability to walk and now relies on a wheelchair full-time.

But despite the progression of the disease, Bev said her daughter had continued to achieve the things she had set out to do.

Georgia gained a psychology degree from Teesside University and remains a strong advocate for people with Friedreich's ataxia and other disabilities.

She also writes for publications about disability and equality.

Her family says omaveloxolone is not a cure and will not reverse the damage already caused by the condition but instead, can slow its progression.

Bev says that could mean giving Georgia more time and making her life more comfortable.

  MP Jonathan Brash with Georgia and her mum Bev 

*(Image: PROVIDED)*

The campaign has already reached Parliament, with local MP Jonathan Brash raising the issue.

Mr Brash said: “From the moment I met Georgia and her mum Bev last year, I promised them that I would do everything I could to support their campaign and make sure their voices were heard in Parliament. Georgia has shown extraordinary determination on this campaign, and Bev has fought tirelessly alongside her for access to the treatment that could slow the progression of this cruel condition.

"Since then, I have raised Georgia’s case at Prime Minister’s Questions, secured a meeting with Ministers and officials at the Department of Health and Social Care, and brought MPs together in Parliament to meet people living with Friedreich’s ataxia and hear directly about the reality of the condition.

"That meeting with the Department did secure commitments to improve the monitoring of patients so we finally get a clearer picture of how many people are living with FA, but it did not deliver what Georgia and other patients desperately need, which is access to omaveloxolone.

"We are now pushing the parliamentary petition and I have been applying for a debate in Parliament so that Ministers can be brought before the House to answer questions and we can keep up the pressure for action.

"The decision in Ireland makes the position here increasingly difficult for the NHS to justify. As more countries around us approve this treatment and make it available through their health services, patients in Britain are understandably asking why they are still being forced to wait.

"For a progressive condition, every month matters. I made Georgia and Bev a promise that I would keep fighting alongside them, and I intend to keep that promise."

The family has also been working with Ataxia UK on an open letter calling for action, while MPs from across the country are being urged to join the campaign.

  Georgia in Parliament 

*(Image: PROVIDED)*

Bev says the family has already gone through the official processes but believes England is now moving too slowly.

She said the situation was "a nonsense" when patients could potentially access the drug simply by moving across a border.

The family has met with a rare disease minister and NHS England, with discussions taking place around possible interim access while funding issues are addressed.

But Bev says more pressure is now needed, she said: "We're not gonna roll over, you know, we're not.

"We just can't stay still. Not when there's a treatment, which can potentially help people."

The Northern Echo contacted the National Institute of Clinical Excellence (NICE), who decide which drugs and treatments are available via the NHS.

They said: “NICE was unfortunately unable to make a recommendation on omaveloxolone (Skyclarys) for treating Friedreich’s ataxia in people 16 years and over because Biogen withdrew its evidence submission.

“We provide rigorous, independent assessment of complex evidence for new health technologies to make sure the NHS can continue to offer the most effective treatments to everyone.

“NICE must balance the benefits that a new medicine offers with the savings that have to be made from other vital patient services elsewhere in the system to pay for it.

“Unfortunately, NICE cannot recommend a drug if the company does not take part in our work, and the outcome of an evaluation cannot be assumed.

“NICE stands ready to work with the company and will reopen the appraisal if the company decides to make a new evidence submission.”
