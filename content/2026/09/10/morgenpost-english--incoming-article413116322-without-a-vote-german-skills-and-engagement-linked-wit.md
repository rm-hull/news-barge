Giving a voice to people without voting rights – that was the aim of StimmlosTrend, a project by the initiative WahlheYmat, whose media partner is the Berliner Morgenpost. According to the Berlin-Brandenburg Statistics Office, 976,000 people without a German passport live in Berlin. Of these, 851,000 are of voting age but are not allowed to vote in elections to the House of Representatives because they do not have German citizenship.

As part of StimmlosTrend, which was modelled on Infratest dimap’s BerlinTrend, participants were asked about their views on the capital. The final results have now been published: 96 percent of respondents would vote if they were eligible to do so. Just under three quarters view Berlin’s future with concern or great concern (72.4 percent) and a similar share feel they have little or no influence (73.9 percent).

### Berlin Election 2026: What Matters to People Without Voting Rights

“The results show very clearly: not having the right to vote does not mean that people have no political interest or do not feel responsible for Berlin. On the contrary. Many want to help shape the city, but feel that their own opportunities to do so are limited,” says Esther Nass, project manager of StimmlosTrend at WahlheYmat e.V.

Other findings reinforce this picture. Those who volunteer and have good German language skills are more optimistic about the city than those who do not volunteer and do not speak German. Nevertheless, negative views of the city’s future predominate across all groups.

Among German-speaking migrants who are actively engaged, 47 percent are both concerned and feel that they have no influence. Among those who do not speak German and are not actively engaged, the figure is as high as 82 percent. An equally interesting trend, however, is the link between length of residence and satisfaction with Berlin politics. As detailed in StimmlosTrend’s final report, people who have lived in Berlin for longer also tend to be more dissatisfied with the Senate’s work. Those who have only arrived recently, by contrast, initially display a higher level of trust.

### Rents and Housing Are Also Migrants’ Top Concern

Rents and housing top the list of the most important issues for respondents, as they do for eligible voters. A total of 63.6 percent of StimmlosTrend participants name these issues as their first or second choices when asked about the city’s biggest problems. The next most frequently named issues are the shift to the political right, racism and discrimination (14.3 percent), homelessness and drugs (14.1 percent), and transport and mobility (13.9 percent). A total of 1,009 open-ended responses were evaluated in this section.

StimmlosTrend received a total of 577 responses. The survey is not representative, but it nevertheless offers an initial picture of the views and concerns of this population group. The online survey was available in eight languages, and WahlheYmat worked with numerous migrant organizations to distribute it. One model was the KFF/New York Times 2025 Survey of Immigrants in the US, which conducted a nationally representative survey of 1,805 immigrants in the United States. The project was funded by the Senate Department for Labour, Social Affairs, Equality, Integration, Diversity and Anti-Discrimination as part of the Participation and Integration Programme.
