New head announced for the main public-sector union

Sarah Basford Canales

The main public-sector union has appointed a new national secretary after its former head Melissa Donnelly was named the Australian Council of Trade Unions’ secretary last month.

This morning, the Community and Public Sector Union’s deputy secretary, Rebecca Fawcett, was promoted to national secretary. Fawcett worked as the CPSU’s lead in the first round of APS-wide bargaining and played a role in the Your Rights at Work campaign against the Howard government’s WorkChoices laws in the mid-2000s.

Fawcett said:

CPSU members do essential work for the Australian community every single day. They deserve secure jobs, fair pay, safe workplaces, and public services that are properly staffed, properly funded and properly valued.

Together, we have won important improvements, but there is more to do. We will keep fighting to bring public sector work back in-house, strengthen protections around AI and automated decision-making, and make sure workers have the pay and conditions they need during a cost-of-living crisis.

Carbon credits for native forests bill expected to pass, despite Greens split

The plan to allow states to earn carbon credits for protecting native forests that had been earmarked for logging is expected to pass the Senate despite three Greens crossing the floor.

It will be backed by Labor, seven Greens and other crossbenchers, including independents David Pocock and Lidia Thorpe. The vote has been brought forward to this afternoon.

The Greens leader, Larissa Waters, said in a statement that the party would support the disputed carbon credit method after improving it, but would oppose other upcoming carbon credit legislation that would produce a larger number of credits.

The fight against Labor’s carbon credit greenwashing starts with the Greens opposing 40% of the offsets used by these big polluters to hide their emissions.

For years, Labor has created a system that allows big polluters to buy get-out-of-jail-free cards instead of cutting their emissions, and the consequences of this broken system are being felt around the country by communities living with the floods, fires and heatwaves. That ends now.

Waters did not directly address in the statement that three senators would cross the floor. The Greens said the deal with Labor would ensure credits from a native forest project could not be sold to coal and gas companies if the state government promised that would be the case.

The NSW premier Chris Minns said this for the koala park, but his commitment was not legally enforceable.

Other changes include an increase in how much logging has to be reduced in an area for a project to qualify for any carbon credits – rising from 20% to 30% from 2030 – and closing a loophole that could have been used to classify regrowing native forests as a loggable plantation.

Majority believe government moving too slowly on domestic violence, polling shows

Nearly three quarters of Australians believe the federal government has been moving too slowly on domestic violence, while just over a quarter (27%) of Australians believe the federal government’s response to recent high-profile gender-based violence instances has been adequate, according to new polls.

Polling for the Women and Girls’ emergency centre, conducted in July and September, of more than 1,000 Australians shows the majority believe the government isn’t doing enough to curb domestic violence and would support an increase in funding for services.

The July poll found 71% Australians believe Labor has responded either “much too slowly” or “somewhat too slowly” to domestic and family violence.

Nicole Yade, the chief executive of WAGEC, said two years after the government declared domestic and family violence as a national crisis, “Australians are clear that the pace of change does not match the scale of the crisis”:

The message from the community is simple – Australians are saying enough is enough. Action simply cannot wait until after yet another woman or child is harmed.

These results that show that the response to the devastating headlines that have dominated the news over recent weeks simply does not meet the moment.

Greens call on Labor to abandon gas reservation plan and legislate gas export tax

The Greens say Labor should ditch the gas reservation plan – which will require producers to hold up to 20% of their production for domestic use – and slap on a 25% gas export tax instead.

The government this morning released draft legislation for the scheme, with a slight watering down of the requirement from producers to hold a firm one-fifth of produce to “up to 20%”.

The Greens have been on this train for an export tax for a while – and just a few months ago so was seemingly every second person on social media (recall David Pocock’s viral video asking department officials why Australia makes more revenue from the beer tax than the petroleum resource rent tax).

The government says the reservation scheme will lower power bills, but Greens senator Steph Hodgins-May says it “won’t raise a cent for Australian households”:

When gas corporations say jump, Labor asks how high. Now the 20% reservation requirement is being watered down to appease the gas industry.

A gas export tax does what Labor claims its reservation scheme will do, but it also raises revenue to compensate Australians who have seen gas prices triple since exports started a decade ago … A gas export tax would raise at least $17bn a year. That’s our gas and our money.

NSW government’s Great Koala national park carbon credit plan to go ahead

Adam Morton

A controversial plan for the Minns government to allow big industry to use a new Great Koala national park to offset its climate pollution will go ahead after the Greens reached a deal with Labor, and decided not to vote with the Coalition to block it.

The minor party and environment advocates have been divided over a regulation introduced by the Albanese government that lets state governments earn carbon credits for protecting native forests that had been earmarked for logging.

The Nationals want to kill the regulation, known as a carbon credit method, on the grounds it would hurt the forestry industry. They have introduced a disallowance motion scheduled in the Senate next Tuesday.

The Greens have the casting votes. They want the park – a promised 476,000 hectare reserve, including 176,000 ha of newly protected areas - but have been divided over whether it should be allowed to happen this way.

They reached a decision this morning to allow the carbon credit to stand. The details of the deal with Labor are expected shortly. More to come then.

NSW police rule out connections between fatal Punchbowl shooting and recent two shootings

NSW police have said a fatal shooting in Sydney on Wednesday night does not appear connected to recent fatalities allegedly related to an “ongoing feud” between “two organised crime networks”, but the victim was known to police.

A man was fatally shot in the car park of a Punchbowl community centre in the city’s southwest on Wednesday evening. Police have said that a car was spotted driving away from the scene.

In a press conference about the incident on Thursday, acting assistant commissioner Grant Taylor, told reporters the “shooting does not have the same hallmarks as the other shootings that have occurred over the last several months.”

He said:

This shooting appears to be an individual person acting alone at this stage. We don’t believe in the infancy of its investigation that it does relate to the ongoing feud between the two organised crime networks you are aware of.

The person who was killed last night is known to us. He is known to the Queensland police service. There are links to outlaw motorcycle gangs, and it’s not impossible that that’s why he was killed.

The fatal shooting comes two days after the New South Wales premier, Chris Minns, announced measures to curb gangland violence in Sydney.

New laws being introduced in the state include new aggravated offences for conspiracy to commit gang-related murder. The maximum penalty will be increased to life imprisonment.

Last week, police confirmed that shootings in Glen Alpine on Tuesday and in Merrylands on Wednesday were both being investigated as mistaken identity killings by organised crime groups.

Bowen says no backdown gas reservation – but it’s starting late

Circling back to the revised plan to reserve up to 20% of locally produced gas for Australian use, Chris Bowen says it’s not a backdown but a few key elements have changed.

The reservation will now kick in from July 2028 instead of July 2027, which the government said would align with companies’ cargo planning.

The resources minister will have discretion to cut companies’ supply obligations all the way to zero if they wish. Bowen said:

Some people said to us in feedback ‘make sure you don’t oversupply the market so much that it’s counterproductive’.

The government says the reserve should push prices down but Bowen said it was not setting any target or ceiling on prices.

They have also split out the west coast market from the east coast market. The resources minister, Madeleine King, said the government was not looking to mess up Western Australia’s reservation and would release the state from federal obligations if it was already well supplied. King said:

If they are working through that well, then they should be able to continue to do that.
