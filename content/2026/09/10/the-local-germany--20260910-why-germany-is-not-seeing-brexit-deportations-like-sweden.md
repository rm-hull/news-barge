While British nationals living in the EU before the Brexit cut-off date were assured they would retain their residency rights under the Withdrawal Agreement (WA), the reality has been very different in some countries.

The situation is particularly troubling in Sweden where 2,500 British nationals have received deportation orders, according to Eurostat figures, and reportedly 458 have been deported or forcibly removed since Brexit.

The UK Foreign Office calculates that 27.5 percent of Britons in Sweden were refused post-Brexit residency, compared to an EU average of under 4 percent.

Cases such as that of Joyce Thomas, a 78-year-old retired nurse who has lived in Sweden for 21 years and now faces deportation because of a late application for post-Brexit residency rights, have made headlines around the world.

While Brits in Germany have faced hurdles when applying for or extending their residence permit (more on that below), those living here have – thankfully – not experienced the same treatment.

**What's different about Germany?**

The rights of Brits who were living in Germany up to December 31st 2020 are safeguarded under the WA. People in this category are entitled to an *Aufenthaltsdokument-GB,* which can be used to document their rights. After five years of residence, they become eligible for permanent residence.

**READ ALSO: How Brits who arrived in Germany before Brexit can get permanent residence**

But there is a key difference in the way that Germany chose to recognise rights.

Under the WA, EU countries could decide how to document the rights of British nationals. Germany opted for a "declaratory" system, meaning Britons automatically hold rights if they qualify, even if they do not report to the authorities.

Countries using a "constitutive" system – like Sweden – require people to apply for a residence status in order to have their rights formally recognised.

Matt Bristow, of rights group British in Germany, said this is one of the main reasons why Germany has not seen deportations and permit refusals like Sweden. During the Brexit process, British in Germany called for a declaratory system.

"The fundamental difference is that Germany chose a declaratory system rather than a constitutive system," said Bristow. "And so people had their rights if they fulfilled the criteria without making an application.

"That is something that we were always concerned about at British in Germany, that if Germany had chosen the same sort of system that Sweden had, they would have ended up with this exact problem here as well, that people would be refused a residence permit or be asked to leave the country."

Experts add that Sweden has an unusually strict interpretation of EU residence requirements which makes it somewhat of an outlier, even among countries that have a constitutive system.

Another issue affecting foreigners in Sweden is tougher immigration rules.

Our sister site, The Local Sweden, notes that since 2022, Sweden has "massively raised the minimum salary requirement for a work permit, and also raised the maintenance and housing requirements for family reunion, making it difficult for Brits who are not in well-paid jobs to renew their temporary permits".

The most recent wave of deportation orders is said to stem from earlier rejections being finalised.

"People were given these temporary reprieves, but Sweden has really toughened up its national residence laws, so it has become very difficult for these people to go on and get permanent residency under national law, and they just end up leaving," David Milstead, a Stockholm University physicist who volunteers for the Brits in Sweden group, told The Local Sweden.

Although Bristow said he is concerned about the possibility of anti-immigration policies in Germany as well as cuts to organisations that assist non-EU citizens, particularly in states where parties like the Alternative for Germany (AfD) are popular, he said overall Brits are in a strong position.

"In lots of ways, we're in a very lucky position in Germany and I'm thankful for the path that Germany took to protect our rights," said Bristow.

**What are the problems faced by Brits in Germany?**

That doesn't mean British residents in Germany have not come across difficulties. As The Local has reported, some people have struggled to get their permanent residence title, mostly because immigration offices are unclear about the rules.

"[Some British nationals] are getting very conflicting information or sometimes being told 'no, they don't qualify for that', even though they should... but that doesn't mean they need to leave the country," said Bristow.

![A sign for the immigration office in Frankfurt am Main.](https://apiwp.thelocal.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/rsz_386492468_1.jpg)


Bristow said British in Germany was not aware of large numbers of people being ordered to leave.

He said advisors at British in Germany more commonly see Britons being told they should apply for a residence permit – rather than getting their WA rights recognised.

"We've definitely heard of people being given the wrong advice," Bristow said.

"There's still a lot of stress for some people and some people were being asked to jump through many extra hoops that they shouldn't have been asked to jump through."

He advised British nationals to be aware of their rights under the Withdrawal Agreement.

"The absolute overwhelming majority of people in Germany now should have that permanent status because they have been here for more than five years," said Bristow.

Another issue in Germany is the lack of statistics showing how many Brits in this group have been refused a residence permit or permanent status. It makes it hard to get the full picture on what's going on.

A spokeswoman from Germany's Interior Ministry told The Local that statistics on whether individuals have been refused residence permits are not recorded.

"However, between 2015 and 2025, the number of British nationals required to leave the country did not change significantly when comparing the periods before and after Brexit," said the spokeswoman. "The number of such individuals at the end of each year consistently remained in the double figures."

The Interior Ministry added that around 77,500 British nationals were living in Germany at the end of 2025.

*British people in Germany can find details on the Withdrawal Agreement rights on the German government website and by looking at British in Germany resources*
