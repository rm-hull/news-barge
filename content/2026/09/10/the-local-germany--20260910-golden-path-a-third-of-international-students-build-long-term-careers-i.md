Germany's universities are doing something many countries struggle to achieve – attracting international talent and convincing a large share of it to stay.

That's the headline finding from a new study by the German Economic Institute (IW), commissioned by the German Academic Exchange Service (DAAD) and published on Wednesday.

The new research uses German Microcensus data to examine what international students actually do after arriving in Germany.

Most notably, the researchers estimate that around one-third of international first-year students ultimately remain in Germany until retirement, suggesting that many who come to study end up building entire careers and lives in the country.

**Retaining international students boosts tax revenue**

For years, German policymakers have argued that international students can help address the country's shortage of skilled workers. The IW's latest study suggests this is already happening.

Roughly one in three (34 percent) of international first-year students remain in Germany as academically qualified professionals until retirement, according to the IW's Microcensus analysis.

For the cohort of nearly 82,000 international students who began degree programmes in 2023, the study calculates a lifetime value-added contribution of around €127 billion and a long-term public finance surplus of €23.1 billion.

Those figures are notably higher than the IW's central estimate in its 2025 study, which projected a surplus of €15.5 billion under a medium-retention scenario.

The study also found that taxes and social security contributions paid by international graduates exceed the public costs associated with their education just two years after graduation.

Germany's performance also stands out internationally. The report draws on earlier OECD research showing that Germany had the highest measured retention rate among the countries examined ten years after international students first enrolled, narrowly ahead of Canada.

Discussing the findings, IW researcher Professor Axel Plünnecke argued that international students in Germany often have an easier path to long-term integration than many other highly qualified migrants.

"There is a great deal of data suggesting that international students are more successful than immigrants with college degrees in establishing a firm footing in Germany over the long term," he said.

The reason, he argues, is simple. Unlike immigrants who arrive directly for work, students spend years building language skills, professional networks, friendships and a deeper understanding of German society before entering the labour market full-time.

University study therefore acts as a bridge between arrival and integration. Or, as Plünnecke put it, a "golden path to immigration" ('*Königsweg**der** Migration*').

That idea is backed up by another finding in the report. A recent DAAD survey of around 21,000 international students found that 35 percent "definitely" wanted to stay in Germany after graduation and a further 29 percent probably wanted to stay.

Only eight percent said they "probably" or "definitely" did *not* want to remain. The rest were undecided.

For international students weighing up where to study, the findings offer a rare glimpse of what happens after graduation.

The new Microcensus-based approach is significant because it allows researchers to track longer-term outcomes, including employment and living situations, while also capturing students from both EU and non-EU countries.

The picture that emerges is strikingly positive. Rather than treating Germany as a temporary stop, many international students appear to be finding jobs, building careers and, in many cases, staying for decades.

For Germany, that means a stronger supply of skilled workers at a time of growing labour shortages.

For international students, it suggests that studying in Germany is not only an educational opportunity but also a realistic pathway to long-term integration and life in the country.
