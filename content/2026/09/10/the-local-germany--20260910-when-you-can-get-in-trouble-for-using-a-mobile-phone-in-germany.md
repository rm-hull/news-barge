In Germany the consequences of reaching for your phone at the wrong time can range from a fine and penalty points on your licence to being removed from a venue or, in some cases, even facing criminal charges.

Germany's rules on phone use are generally driven by one of three broad concerns: road safety, privacy and the right of businesses and institutions to set rules on their own premises.

Keep these principles in mind and most of the country's phone-related restrictions will make sense.

**No phones while driving**

The clearest and strictest rules apply when you're operating a vehicle.

Many people know that using a phone while driving a car is illegal in Germany. The same basic principle extends well beyond cars to include motorcycles, bikes, e-scooters and other vehicles.

In practice, if you're actively operating a vehicle, you shouldn't be holding your phone.

That means no texting, checking messages, scrolling through social media, tapping a navigation app or even quickly checking the time if you need to pick up the device to do it.

Hands-free systems, voice controls and phones mounted in a cradle are generally permitted, provided they can be used with only a brief glance away from the road.

One area that regularly catches drivers out is phone use at traffic lights.

Many people assume that if the car isn't moving, phone use becomes legal. That's not the case. Being stopped in traffic, waiting at roadworks or sitting at a red light does not create an exception.

Germany's rules even address modern start-stop systems. Drivers are not permitted to pick up their phone just because the vehicle has temporarily turned itself off.

A standard handheld-phone offence for a motor vehicle driver starts at €100 and one point on the Flensburg driving register. Fines can increase significantly if another person is endangered or an accident occurs.

Cyclists face a lower fine but can still be penalised for using a handheld phone while riding.

**Invading someone's privacy**

Germany's privacy laws are among the strictest in Europe, reflecting a long-standing and hard-earned sensitivity towards surveillance and invasions of personal privacy.

This means that secretly recording non-public spoken words is generally illegal – whether that's a disagreement with a neighbour, a workplace meeting or a conversation with a police officer.

Taking photos or videos of people in highly private settings – such as homes, changing rooms or bathrooms – can also constitute a criminal offence.

That doesn't mean photography in public is generally forbidden in Germany. But publishing images of identifiable people without their consent is legally risky, particularly when those individuals are the main subject of the image rather than simply appearing in the background.

Germany also takes a tough stance against so-called gawkers. In practice, that means filming accident victims, seriously injured people, helpless individuals or even the dead can cross the line into criminal territory.

Not every phone restriction comes from a law passed by parliament.

Many of the situations in which someone can legitimately tell you to put your phone away arise from something known in Germany as *Hausrecht*.

Roughly translated, this means the right of the owner or operator of a property to decide what rules apply there. These rules are often written down in a 'house code' (*Hausordnung*).

This is why you'll frequently encounter phone restrictions in places such as schools, workplaces, courts, concert venues and swimming pools.

**Schools:** While there’s no nationwide ban on smartphones in schools in Germany, the overall trend is towards tighter regulation. Several states have already expanded restrictions and a federal expert commission recently recommended banning private smartphone use in schools up to seventh grade. It remains to be seen whether the proposal will become law.

**Workplaces:** Employers can often restrict personal phone use during working hours, particularly where safety, confidentiality or productivity concerns are involved.

Nightclubs, concert halls and museums are all well within their rights to introduce restrictions on filming and photography, while swimming pools, saunas and wellness facilities are among the places most likely to ban phones altogether due to privacy concerns.

If you ignore these rules, the consequences aren’t usually criminal. But staff may ask you to stop using the device or leave the premises. In some cases, they could also ban you from returning.

**Courts, airports and hospitals**

In Germany, courts frequently prohibit photography, filming and audio recording, while airport security areas may impose restrictions for security reasons.

Hospitals increasingly allow ordinary smartphone use in many areas, but intensive care units and other sensitive departments may still have stricter rules.
