Just weeks before Atom bank announced that its co-founder and chief executive Mark Mullen was stepping down, he gave BUSINESSiQ a characteristically candid interview. Now, his comments offer a revealing final perspective on the bank, its North East roots and the industry he never intended to join.

Mark Mullen has never sounded much like a conventional banker.

Sitting in Atom bank’s new headquarters at the Pattern Shop in Newcastle, the co-founder and chief executive of Britain’s original digital bank delivered his assessment of the profession with a grin.

“It’s the money that is interesting,” he said. “Banking is boring. Bankers think they’re interesting, but there’s nothing more soul-destroying than bankers.”

The remark was vintage Mullen: provocative, funny and underpinned by a serious argument about what a bank should be.

But the interview has acquired an unexpected significance. It was conducted by our writer Kevin Donald just days before Atom announced that Mullen was stepping down after 12 years at the helm. Andrew Marshall, the bank’s chief financial officer, has since become interim chief executive while its board searches for a permanent successor. Atom said Mullen and the board had agreed it was the right time for new leadership to drive its next phase of growth.

His departure followed reports over the ownership of the Newcastle-headquartered bank. Reports said a sale process, which had generated interest from private equity firms and building societies, ended without a firm bid. Atom described the change as an agreed leadership transition.

“I think it’s our job to charge people as little as possible for borrowing and pay them as much as possible on their savings,” he told us. “You won’t hear that from many banks.”

Atom was founded in 2014 and developed as the UK’s first app-based bank.

Yet he was reluctant to present banking as something more glamorous or complicated than it needed to be.

“Banking is a dematerialised business model,” he said. “We don’t produce cash anymore. It’s a glorified rule book dealing in a high-value commodity with no tangible reality. If you can’t digitalise banking, what can you digitalise?”

Customers, he argued, are not looking for excitement from a bank. They want speed, consistency and reliability.

“Consumers are not in banking for the adventure,” he said. “You can’t be good on a Tuesday and bad on a Friday. A brand exists because you create standards and operate within them all the time.”

Technology can help banks meet those expectations. However, Mullen warned that it should not become an excuse to exclude people or reduce opportunities for the next generation.

“I’m excited about technology,” he said, “but there’s no point in using technology to pull up the ladder and stop opportunity for young people.”

That focus on people is reflected in Atom’s move from Aykley Heads in Durham to the restored Pattern Shop in Newcastle’s Stephenson Quarter.

Mullen described the building, once part of the works associated with the birth of the railways, as a natural home for a technologically led company with deep North East roots.

“If you were writing a fairy story and you thought about where you’d put a modern, technologically-led company, you’d put it here,” he said. “It’s a symbol of who we are as a brand. Its deep roots in the North East industrial heritage mirror our ambition to build on tradition with bold, modern thinking.”

The decision was practical too. Atom wanted a base close to public transport and deliberately moved away from a workplace model dominated by car parking.

The Pattern Shop now operates as the cultural centre of a hybrid organisation, offering collaborative areas, meeting rooms and broadcasting facilities alongside conventional workspace.

“The most important leases we have are the leases with our own employees,” Mullen said.

“The company is the people who built it and run it. Without them, there is no company. Atom is a service business. It doesn’t own machinery or factories. Its assets are its people and they’re not prisoners.

“We owe them more than they owe us, particularly talented people. Talented people get to make choices.”

The move also forms part of Atom’s environmental ambitions. The bank wants to become carbon positive by 2035 and has offset the carbon it says it has produced since its foundation.

Mullen was scathing about companies retreating quietly from environmental commitments and rejecting responsibility for decisions made by their leaders.

“There’s no such thing as a company deciding,” he said. “People decide. Chief executives, chairmen, boards. Don’t hide behind the legal structure.

“Principles are expensive things. That’s why they’re in short supply.”

For Atom, he said, environmental responsibility means occupying a reused building, sourcing energy carefully, reducing employee travel emissions, planting trees and financing businesses with sustainable models.

“Unless you’ve been living under a stone, global warming is a reality,” he said. “It’s destructive and it’s accelerating.

“If you’re running a company, then you have, as far as I’m concerned, a responsibility to try not to do anything to make it worse and, if at all possible, invest to make it better. We’re firmly in the latter camp.”

Mullen was equally direct about the role Atom should play in the North East.

“The reason we’re here, headquartered in the North East, is to create prosperity in the North East,” he said. “We didn’t have to do it. We wanted to do it, because that’s where it was needed most.”

He pointed to Atom’s 600 employees and the value of the skilled jobs it has generated during its development.

“These are not chicken-feed jobs,” he said. “For the last 12 years, we’ve been doing this. If you think about all of the people we’ve employed, that’s the real value we’ve created in the region.”

He also called for greater cooperation between Newcastle, Middlesbrough, Darlington and the North East’s other centres.

“It’s not a big enough region to have interregional rivalry of the sort that it has,” he said.

“There is competition between the cities, but I’m not sure that it’s always healthy or necessary. There should be more collaboration and cooperation. A win for one is actually a win for everybody.”

Mullen described himself as an “accidental banker”. After studying history at Trinity College Dublin, he entered finance because he had student debt and needed employment.

“I didn’t plan on spending my career in banking, but I was successful,” he said. “Either nobody found out, or I wasn’t revealed as a faker.”

Twelve years after helping establish Atom, however, his impact is difficult to disguise.

His final message from the chief executive’s office was not principally about transactions, valuations or potential owners. It was about customers, employees, place and the responsibilities carried by those who run companies.

In Atom’s official departure announcement, Mullen said it had been a privilege to build the business and that he was “immensely proud” of creating a bank capable of competing with the high street institutions.
