*As the TEWV public inquiry finally progresses with the appointment of a chair, Minister for Social Care and Mental Health Alison McGovern writes in The Northern Echo about her hopes for what the probe can achieve.*

It’s a betrayal of trust.

When someone is in the grip of a mental health crisis, they deserve the certainty of a safe and caring environment, a place to feel protected, and a space in which to heal.

Tragically, for many people accessing mental health services at Tees, Esk and Wear Valleys NHS Foundation Trust, that promise of support and empathic care was broken, time and time again.

For years, families across this region have lived with unimaginable grief after the preventable deaths suffered by their loved ones, including those of vulnerable young people such as Christie Harnett, Nadia Sharif and Emily Moore.

  Nadia Sharif, Emily Moore and Christie Harnett (L-R) 

*(Image: THE NORTHERN ECHO)*

Their families’ determined pursuit of answers has exposed grave shortcomings in patient safety, inappropriate care and, worst of all, a failure to listen when relatives raised concerns.

It goes without saying, the vast majority of staff across NHS mental health services in England provide compassionate and expert care every single day, often under immense pressure. They save lives and prevent heartbreak for thousands of families.

   

*(Image: SARAH CALDECOTT)*

But successive independent reports and prosecutions by the Care Quality Commission have laid bare unacceptable failings, a culture of denial and, most damaging of all, a profound loss of public trust.

That is why in December 2025 the Government committed to a full statutory public inquiry to investigate deaths and critical shortcomings in care across the trust’s mental health services.

His Honour Judge John Potter has now been appointed to chair that inquiry.

A retired Circuit Judge and former Deputy Judge of the Court of Appeal, he brings more than 40 years’ experience in public service, judicial leadership and the administration of justice.

  Judge John Potter. 

*(Image: DHSC)*

He has led large organisations, evaluated complex evidence and worked with bereaved families and vulnerable people.

These skills, together with his independence and judgement, will be essential as the Inquiry examines the circumstances surrounding these events and identifies lessons for the future.

As they have a right to expect, those affected will be at the heart of this process.

The Department of Health and Social Care has met with and spoken to families and patients to hear their views on matters they would like the Inquiry to consider, and on the qualities needed in its chair.

Of course, no inquiry can undo the pain suffered by survivors, families and loved ones. But it can provide much needed answers and help make sure the same failures are never repeated.

Rebuilding trust won’t be easy.

But with openness, action, and a sustained commitment to do better, I firmly believe we can restore faith in mental health services across the region.

We owe it to everyone affected to support Judge Potter’s work, learn lessons and deliver the lasting change these families have fought for with such immense courage.

We can’t bring back those who tragically lost their lives, but we can make safer, kinder, more responsive care their legacy.
