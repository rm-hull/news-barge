When The Local recently asked readers to share their top tips for a successful Oktoberfest, the advice was strikingly consistent – reserve early, bring cash, avoid overdoing it and have a plan.

I wish I’d had this advice to hand on my disastrous first visit to Munich's famous beer festival.

That trip took place many years ago on a stag weekend. I wasn't the groom or the best man and had played no role in choosing Oktoberfest as the destination.

But as the only member of the group living in Germany, and one of the few who spoke any German, I somehow acquired responsibility for rescuing the situation when things went wrong.

And things went wrong almost immediately.

So here’s your advice – and a cautionary tale of what can happen when it’s disregarded.

**Mistake number one: arriving without a reservation**

Around two thirds of respondents to our survey described Oktoberfest as either "the best time of their life" or "brilliant fun".

While a minority said they’d had "had better times in life" or dismissed the event as "totally overhyped" the overall picture was clear. Most readers believe Oktoberfest is worth experiencing – but that this generally relies on having some kind of plan.

Reader K. Munson got to the heart of what this means in just two words: "Tent reservations."

Another respondent said that "not having a reservation for a table in a tent" is the most common mistake a first-time visitor can make.

When I first went to Oktoberfest, the decision was made for me and far too late – long after it was possible to secure a reservation. To make matters worse, we were visiting on the festival's middle weekend, traditionally one of the busiest periods of the entire event.

After spending hours trying unsuccessfully to get into a beer tent on the Saturday, our group of twelve eventually admitted defeat and retreated to Munich's Ratskeller beneath the Neues Rathaus on Marienplatz.

It’s a wonderful place in its own right, with vaulted halls and excellent Bavarian food, but it’s not Oktoberfest. Simultaneously disappointed and overwhelmed by the sheer scale of what was happening around us, we wedged ourselves into a corner table and tried (and mostly failed) to attract the attention of the waiting staff (I wouldn’t have gone anywhere near us, either).

Eventually and sheepishly, we shuffled off to bed – determined to be up at dawn and first in the queue for the tents on the following morning.

**Mistake number two: dressing as an English cricket team**

Readers also had strong views on clothing.

One respondent warned visitors not to wear "cheap fake *Tracht*".

This is sensible advice. Bavarians generally appreciate traditional clothing, but they can spot a costume-shop imitation from a considerable distance.

Our solution was arguably worse. Rather than cheap lederhosen, we decided to dress as an amateur English cricket team. I think the organisers hoped this would mean we attracted attention wherever we went, but in a good way (hence cricket team rather than football team).

Instead, amid the sheer spectacle of Oktoberfest, we were either invisible to everyone but ourselves or seen as a mild irritant.

For first-timers, the lesson is simple. Rent authentic *Tracht* if you want the full experience, buy quality second-hand clothing if your budget stretches to it, or simply wear ordinary clothes. All of these options are preferable to fancy dress.

**Mistake number three: treating Oktoberfest like a sprint**

Many of us were new fathers enjoying our first weekend away from nappies, sleepless nights and irrevocable life decisions – and determined to make the most of it.

On Sunday morning, we were back at the *Wiesn* before 8am. We were shown to places in a tent at 9am and our first litres of strong German beer arrived just moments later.

One respondent to our survey advised visitors to "drink responsibly and take care of yourself and your friends".

The serving staff in the tent just asked if we were ready for another round.

Another respondent wrote that visitors should pace themselves. "There’s lots to see. The tents all have their own character. And remember the beer is stronger than normal Helles."

That last point bears repeating. A *Maß* of Oktoberfest beer is not the same as having a couple of pints in your local pub.

**Mistake number four: wanting too much**

Reader Larry Schulz has arguably cracked the Oktoberfest code: “Get there early, before the boisterous crowds show up and the music starts with full blast. Our group has been going on Sunday mornings at 10:30am and we stay until 2pm.”

If we had taken Larry’s advice and left a 2pm, at least some of us would have stood a fighting chance of reaching the airport on time.

One of the most useful reader tips we received concerned something many visitors overlook: the importance of agreeing on a meeting point.

"Lost my son, who did not speak German at age 13, just after we told him if he got lost to go to the Löwenbräu tent and we would find him. We did."

Alas, we weren’t as well prepared. Within a remarkably short period of entering the tent, our group had splintered in multiple directions. Some were exploring the fairground, some were attempting to visit other tents and some had vanished entirely.

By 1pm nobody seemed to know where anybody else was.

It was only much later that we started congregating again, largely by chance, on a grassy slope behind the tents. We later learned that it’s widely known by Oktoberfest regulars as *Kotzhügel*, or "Vomit Hill" – and for very good reason.

**What does it all mean?**

As our readers made clear, while Oktoberfest may reward bravado to some degree, it rewards preparation much more generously.

So reserve if you can. Bring plenty of cash. Pace yourselves and wear something appropriate. Above all, keep an eye out for each other and agree on where you'll meet if separated.

And if all else fails, try not to end the day flat on your back on *Kotzhügel* wondering what’s happened to your friends, your dignity and your return flight home.
