It's been quite the intriguing Berlin election, at least when it comes to election posters.

And the ones that have arguably stood out the most have been from one of the smaller parties.

Volt, a progressive and pan-European party, has raised eyebrows with its 'unf*ck Berlin' poster campaign that aims to highlight that the capital can do things a lot better.

**READ ALSO: What is Volt and how does it plan to 'unf* ck Berlin'?**

Now, ahead of the election for Berlin's House of Representatives (also known as the state election) and local districts on Sunday, Volt has another provocative question: 'Who the f*ck ever asked you?'

The idea behind it is to draw attention to a question that is rarely asked: who actually gets to have a say in the future of this city? And who doesn't?

The poster states in English: "You work here, pay taxes, raise your kids, are part of the community. You make this city what it is. But your voice is never heard? It's time we change that."

Only people aged 16 and over with German citizenship can vote in the state election, while EU citizens can also cast ballots in the district council elections (the *Bezirksverordnetenversammlungen* or BVV).

Volt says this means that EU citizens "are completely excluded from one of the most important decisions for Berliners".

The party is therefore calling for full voting rights for people with EU citizenship.

"Of course, the slogan immediately catches your eye," said Matt Bristow, Volt candidate in Neukölln, who came up with the idea for the campaign posters.

"But what I really find obscene is the enormous democratic deficit we have in Berlin: almost a quarter of a million EU citizens alone are not eligible to vote in elections to the House of Representatives."

For Bristow, the demand goes beyond the issue of voting rights. He referenced the recent election in the eastern German state of Saxony-Anhalt where the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD), classed by state intelligence as an extremist organisation, managed to secure nearly 40 percent of the vote.

"Saxony-Anhalt shows that we need to ‘dare more democracy’," he said. "People don’t just want to put a cross on a ballot paper every few years. They want to know that their voice actually makes a difference: in how money is spent in their neighbourhood and in decisions that affect their everyday lives.

"Too few people currently know how they can get involved in political issues in their own neighbourhoods. Even worse: the results of greater public participation processes, such as the international competition on the future of Tempelhofer Feld, are simply ignored by the Senate."

Bristow called on voters to choose Volt on election day so the party can give "power to the people".

On the topic of edgy posters, Bristow said Volt's campaign has created a buzz.

"The unf*ck Berlin campaign is a bit like Marmite: people seem to love it or hate it," he told us. "But everyone has an opinion. In general, though, I've heard overwhelmingly more positive than negative reactions when I've been out canvassing.

"And even when people haven’t liked the sweary style, they have appreciated the substance of our manifesto."

The party says the message has sparked a conversation about the city.

Bristow says while preparing for the campaign, people stopped him in the street to ask what the message meant.

"I explained that there were lots of things in this city that simply don’t work and that we believe that not only can Berlin do better, but it deserves better too," he said. "As a message that instantly resonates with people."

But does the language go too far?

"For Volt, as a young, international party, a word like 'unf*ck' that is common in the tech and startup world feels like a natural fit for a team that wants to get things done," said Bristow.

"Besides that, Berliners are known for telling it like it is and not sugar-coating things. Maybe “unf*ck Berlin” is the Berliner Schnauze of 21st-century Berlin?"

Bristow admits such a campaign might not work somewhere like Munich, which is more traditional than Berlin.

"That said, we’ve already had the team in Volt España contact us to tell us how they want to 'unf*ck Madrid' and 'unf* ck Barcelona' in elections there next year. So it’s definitely struck a chord beyond Berlin," said Bristow.
