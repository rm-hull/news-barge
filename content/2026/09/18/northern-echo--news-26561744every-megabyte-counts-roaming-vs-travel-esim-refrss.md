You need the internet from the very first minutes of a trip. Checking a map, finding somewhere to have lunch, booking a transfer, sending a quick message home.

Over the course of a fortnight away, hundreds of these small actions add up, and every one of them uses data. It usually happens without you noticing, which is exactly why it can end in an unpleasant bill.

When your connection depends on roaming, you can't see your data draining away, you don't know the final total in advance, and you hit your limit precisely when you need the internet most.

More and more Britons are getting around this by setting up a travel eSIM service before they leave home — from the Swiss company Yesim, for example. It gives you a straightforward data package with a price and an allowance you know in advance, and it switches on within seconds of landing. Before we get into the details, though, it's worth working out where your megabytes actually go on a trip, and why roaming turns that into a problem.

## 
  **Where your megabytes go when you travel**

On a trip every megabyte counts, especially when you're paying roaming rates for it. Here's how much data the popular apps and tools we're all so used to relying on while travelling get through:

  **Google Maps services** are the most economical thing on the list, getting through around 3–5 MB per hour of ordinary navigation. Outside the city you can drive around all day and still use less than 50 MB. There's a catch, though: satellite mode or 3D view together push that up to 15–30 MB an hour and beyond. And abroad the consumption often doubles (10–30 MB an hour), because your phone is loading unfamiliar sections of the map for the first time.

  **Messengers such as WhatsApp** are almost weightless — a quiet text conversation costs just 5–10 MB an hour, and once you add voice notes and photos, up to 30–50 MB an hour.

  **AI assistants — ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude.** They're handy for asking about a route or reading through reviews. If you type and stick to text, it costs next to nothing: around 1–5 MB an hour. Voice mode is another matter entirely, using dozens of times more data, roughly 1–2 MB a minute (that is, 60–120 MB an hour).

  **A video call home over WhatsApp or FaceTime** is where the drain really starts to make itself felt. That's about 5–15 MB a minute, or roughly 200–400 MB an hour. A single long evening catch-up with the family can easily come to hundreds of megabytes.

  **Instagram and other social networks** — around 100–200 MB an hour for simply scrolling the feed, but switch to stories and it's already 300–500 MB an hour, while reels and video can drive it up to 1 GB an hour.

  **Posting your own photos and stories is fairly light in itself.** A single photo is 1–5 MB, or 10–20 MB at high quality. An hour of uploading and watching video stories, on the other hand, can come to 150–300 MB.

  **TikTok** is one of the biggest data guzzlers on the entire list: 500 MB to 1.2 GB an hour.

  **Streaming video on YouTube or Netflix** runs from 500 MB to 1–2 GB an hour even at standard quality, and in HD it's several gigabytes an hour.

## 
  **Why roaming is a financial trap**

Roaming doesn't just make all of this expensive. There are three specific reasons why it's no longer a workable model.

The first is that you can't see what you're spending until it's too late. Data leaks away in the background, and the final figure often only becomes clear once you're back home. Plenty of British tourists have no idea that their operator charges separately for calls, messages and internet abroad, on top of their home plan. And other travellers never check roaming rates before a trip at all.

The second is that the allowance included in your plan can run out faster than you'd counted on, especially if you settle into a video call or scroll through reels for an hour. Buying more has to be done once you're there, and it's usually pricier — at separate roaming rates rather than domestic ones.

The third is that the price depends on exactly where you are, yet most people only find that out on arrival. Roaming carves the world up into zones, and it's rarely obvious in advance which one your particular country falls into. Spain or Greece is one thing; Egypt, Morocco or India is a completely different and far more expensive zone.

All three of these traps can be avoided. A travel eSIM gives you a known amount of data at a fixed price.

## 
  **How an eSIM differs from a travel eSIM**

An eSIM is simply a built-in chip, found in every iPhone from the XS onwards, in every Google Pixel, in recent Samsung and Motorola models, and in most current mid-range and higher Android phones. It lets you load an operator profile remotely, with no trip to a phone shop.

A travel eSIM is the service that uses that chip: an app, a range of plans, technical support and the ability to manage data across several devices at once.

## 
  **Conclusion**

Megabytes really do matter on a trip — the only question is who counts them, and how. With roaming, the operator does the counting: after the fact, by its own zones and its own rules, and you often only see the total once the trip is over.

With a travel eSIM you count for yourself: an allowance chosen in advance at a price known in advance, with nothing left to guess at. For anyone heading abroad the choice is fairly simple — a travel eSIM package bought beforehand instead of an anxiety inducing meter ticking away in your head.
