A PLAY play about Ian Curtis, troubled frontman of Joy Division, was always going to be bleak. What I wasn’t expecting was the humour, essentially of the northern variety, that drove much of this compelling production.

New Dawn Fades, at St George’s Hall as part of an acclaimed UK tour, is the story of four lads who formed one of the country’s most influential bands. Narrated by impresario and Granada Reports presenter Tony Wilson, who gave Joy Division their TV debut, the play weaves strands of Manchester’s history through early scenes - a quirky, witty device that sets a stirring social backdrop for the band’s story.

  The play is a moving homage to an influential band. Images: New Dawn Fades 

*(Image: New Dawn Fades)*

Empowered by the legendary Sex Pistols’ 1976 gig at the Lesser Free Trade Hall, Salford Grammar School pals Bernard Sumner and Peter Hook decide to form a band. Approached by Ian Curtis, a shy lad from Macclesfield - “Can he sing?” asks Hooky. “Does it matter?” shrugs Sumner - the seeds are sown for Warsaw, later Joy Division. With a dark, atmospheric sound, fuelled by Curtis’ enigmatic lyrics and intense stage presence, they were the post-punk pioneers whose debut album Unknown Pleasures, released through Wilson’s Factory Records, was hailed as a masterpiece.

This is a moving, soul-stirring play, guiding us through the band’s story and the demons that tormented Curtis, leading to his suicide at the age of 23. It’s written by Brian Gorman, who is excellent as Tony Wilson, capturing his speech patterns, mannerisms and look, down to the specs on the end of his nose.

The stage is stark, allowing the audience to be immersed in the band and its music and Manchester in the late 1970s; still reeling from wartime bombings, entering a post-industrial age, but the beating heart of a new cultural wave.

The entire cast is a class act, led by Josh Lonsdale - spellbinding as Ian Curtis. He delivers a deeply moving portrayal of a young man who is overwhelmed by life; despair at his epilepsy, his failing marriage, and not wanting to let people down. Watching him perform Shadowplay, with Curtis’ jerky movements and haunting deep voice, is eerie and mesmerising.

  Josh Lonsdale is spellbinding as Ian Curtis 

*(Image: New Dawn Fades)*

The actors playing the band are impressive musicians; it feels like a treat to see them embody the band’s spirit and sound. Paddy Echlin as Peter Hook, Sam Glyde-Rees as Bernard Sumner and Kai Ross as Stephen Morris were terrific as a band on the rise, but out of their depth with a complex frontman losing control.

Excellent performances too from Lauren Greenwood who plays Curtis’ wife, Debbie, and is also the director, and Charlie Shaw and Neal Ward, who brought an array of characters, from Friedrich Engels to the Buzzcocks’ Pete Shelley and producer Martin Hannett, beautifully to life.

With a sparkling, often witty script and memorable performances, this is a moving tribute to a much-loved band and a tortured soul whose spirit still shines bright.
