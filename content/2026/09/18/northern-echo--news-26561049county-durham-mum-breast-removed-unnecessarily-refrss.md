A County Durham mum told she did not need a mastectomy says the operation robbed her of the person she was before surgery.

Denise Howarth, 51, from Consett, was diagnosed with grade-one cancer in her left breast in February 2023.

She first had a lumpectomy and lymph nodes removed, but was later told cancer cells remained and that she needed a mastectomy.

The mastectomy was carried out just days later.

But in September last year, County Durham and Darlington NHS Foundation Trust told Denise the mastectomy had not been necessary.

She said: “They called me at home to say I needn’t have had my breast removed and it was something which really knocked me off my feet.

“I was completely shocked.”

Denise said she had endured a deeply difficult time after the surgery and had only just begun recovering when the call came.

She said: “I’d just reached a point where I was beginning to focus on myself and to look forward, and then this news came like a bolt out of the blue.

“You put your trust in professional people. To be so badly let down is something I’ll never be able to come to terms with.”

Denise is one of 20 women who underwent an unnecessary mastectomy.

Her case is part of a wider investigation into treatment in the Trust’s breast service.

Of 514 cases reviewed, 315 patients were found to have suffered harm, including 77 who were significantly harmed. One patient was known to have died.

*(Image: SUBMIT)*

Denise had her mastectomy on a Saturday, just days after being told she needed it.

Looking back, she believes the process was rushed.

She said: “I was told at my review that there was bad news and good news.

“The bad news was that the lumpectomy had not removed all the cancer, but the good news was that I could have the mastectomy the following Saturday.”

Denise said she thought the operation had been arranged unusually quickly but felt unable to question the medical advice.

She said: “When you are told it’s needed to remove cancer, you don’t question things.

“I just wanted them to do what was needed, and I put my complete faith and trust in them, as they are the doctors and experts.

“I’d seen too many people die of cancer to take any risks.”

  Denise Howarth during treatment 

*(Image: SUBMIT)*

Denise later learned that her surgery had been carried out by Cathedral Surgical Services (CSS), one of two private providers contracted by the trust, as part of the private breast cancer clinics funded by the NHS trust.

Amir Bhatti, who at the time was the trust’s consultant clinical lead in breast surgery, was a director of the company.

An external governance review found that CSS carried out Saturday surgery under a payment-per-patient deal that created a “clear financial incentive” for “higher patient throughput”.

Mr Bhatti was subsequently restricted from performing breast cancer surgery by the trust.

Durham Police has also interviewed Denise as part of its investigation into possible criminal offences in the breast service.

The trust has since sent Denise a written apology.

The letter said a second wide local excision would have been suitable but was not discussed with her, leaving her with only the option of a mastectomy.

Denise said the apology could never make up for the operation’s impact on her life.

She said: “An apology is not enough. This is life-changing.

  Denise Howarth 

*(Image: SUBMIT)*

“I’ve lost so much confidence. I feel robbed of the person I was before.

“As a woman, losing a breast has a huge impact on you.”

She said friends often tell her she looks well and praise her strength, but privately she is struggling.

“I fear I will never be the same person again, although I have tried to be strong,” she said.

“The reality is it’s a huge struggle. I’ve broken down many times.”

Denise said even choosing clothes had become difficult.

She added: “I find it difficult to choose clothing I feel comfortable and happy in, and it worsens at this time of year as I can’t really wear the summer tops with straps. I always need to wear a T-shirt.”

She also criticised the support she received over breast reconstruction.

Denise said she raised reconstruction at several appointments but was repeatedly told it was either too soon or major surgery.

Although she was not in the right frame of mind at the time, Denise insists she never ruled out reconstruction.

She later received a letter which, she said, wrongly recorded that she had turned down reconstruction.

“That was not the case at all,” she said.

“I just feel there’s been no compassion and care throughout all my treatment.”

Denise has instructed Hudgell Solicitors to take legal action against the trust.

She said she had usually “kept quiet” but now felt compelled to speak out.

“I feel we all do, all the women who have been affected and harmed,” she said.

“We are stronger together.

“We should be compensated, and we need people at the Trust, from management to the consultants, to be held to account.

“This simply should not be happening to one person, let alone hundreds as it has.”

Rachel O’Connor, of Hudgell Solicitors’ medical negligence team, said Denise’s case was not isolated and the firm was advising other women told they had undergone unnecessary surgery.

She said breast cancer treatment was physically and psychologically difficult even when care was appropriate, making the discovery of avoidable harm especially distressing.

Ms O’Connor has called for a public inquiry into the failures and why they were allowed to continue.

Board papers published before the trust’s extraordinary meeting on September 24 included a further apology for wider failures in its breast services.

The trust said: “We acknowledge that, during the period under review, the standards our patients had a right to expect were not consistently met and that failures in care, governance and oversight caused distress, uncertainty and harm for some patients and their families.

“We offer our sincere apologies to everyone affected and remain deeply sorry for the impact this has had.

“We recognise their lasting impact and remain committed to learning from the past, building on the significant improvements already made, and ensuring patients receive the safe, compassionate and high-quality care they deserve.”
