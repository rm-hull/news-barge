An extra £100 in your pay packet might sound like an easy way to ease the pressure on household finances. But for some workers, there could be a much bigger price to pay further down the line.

Pausing workplace pension contributions could give an average earner around £1,000 more in take-home pay, but leave their retirement pot more than £12,000 smaller, according to new analysis.

The warning comes as one in five UK adults (20%) say they have either paused or are considering pausing their workplace pension contributions.

And it is not always an emergency forcing people to make the change.

More than a quarter of adults, 27%, say they would consider reducing their pension savings simply to have more disposable income to spend today.

That could prove a costly trade-off.


Moneybox modelling suggests that for an average earner, a 12-month pause could mean an immediate boost to their finances, but potentially leave them more than £12,000 worse off by retirement once lost contributions and compound investment growth are taken into account.

So what looks like an extra £1,000 today could ultimately mean thousands less when the money is needed most.

The £12,000 shortfall is also equivalent to almost an entire year's spending for a single person on a minimum retirement lifestyle, according to the latest Retirement Living Standards.

## The pension decision hiding in your payslip

The temptation is easy to understand.

An extra £80 or £100 appearing in your monthly pay packet is money you can see immediately. The pension money you give up, however, is money you may not need for decades.

That difference can make it much easier to prioritise today's spending over tomorrow's retirement.

Moneybox's Psychology of Retirement research highlights this as present bias, where people tend to place greater value on rewards available now than potentially larger rewards in the future.

Just 35% of UK adults recognise the importance of time and compound growth when building long-term pension wealth.

That means many people may not fully appreciate what happens when contributions are stopped, even for a relatively short period.

And the money lost is not necessarily limited to what comes directly out of a worker's salary.

Depending on the pension scheme, reducing contributions could also mean missing out on employer contributions, tax relief and years of potential investment growth.

## What could you lose by stopping pension payments?

Time can be one of the most valuable ingredients in retirement saving.

Money invested for longer has more opportunity to generate returns, which can then themselves be reinvested and generate further growth.

That is why a contribution missed today can have an impact far beyond its original value.

Brian Byrnes, Head of Personal Finance at Moneybox, said: "When household budgets are stretched, an extra £80 or £100 in your monthly pay packet can understandably feel much more valuable than money you won't access for decades.

"But our analysis shows just how unequal that trade-off can be: for an average earner, around £1,000 in additional take-home pay today could ultimately mean more than £12,000 less in their pension by retirement, once lost contributions and potential compound growth are taken into account."

He adds: "Your current contributions are only part of the picture. Track down any old workplace pensions and check how they're performing and what you're paying in fees. You may find it helpful to consolidate old pots where appropriate, making your pensions easier to manage and helping ensure your retirement savings are working as hard as possible for your future."

## Should you ever reduce your pension contributions?

For households struggling with immediate bills or essential spending, having more money available now can be necessary. But the figures highlight why anyone considering a pause should understand exactly what they could be giving up.

Where possible, a reduction should be treated as a temporary measure, with contributions reviewed when finances improve.

Workers should also check whether reducing contributions means giving up employer matching, as this could make the long-term cost of the decision even greater.

And anyone with old workplace pension pots may want to check where their money is held, what fees they are paying and how the investments are performing.
