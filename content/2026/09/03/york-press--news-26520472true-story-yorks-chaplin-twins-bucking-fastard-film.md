## From Tang Hall to Hollywood: the mysterious York twins who fascinated the world

Freda and Greta Chaplin - screen shot taken from A Pair of One, documentary by Juliet Darling

  *From York to Hollywood: the true story of the Chaplin twins, by MAXINE GORDON*

FOR decades, Freda and Greta Chaplin were among York's most recognisable and mysterious residents.

Now, more than five years after Freda's death, the extraordinary lives of the inseparable twins from Tang Hall are set to reach a whole new audience.

The story of York's most unusual twins – who dressed identically and walked and talked in unison – has inspired a major Hollywood film, Bucking Fastard, and, following its recent premiere, it is due for wider release this autumn.

In Bucking Fastard, Rooney Mara (famous for her roles in The Girl With The Dragon Tattoo and Carol) and her real-life sister Kate (who starred in Brokeback Mountain and Fantastic Four) play inseparable sisters Jean and Joan Holbrooke, modelled on the lives of Freda and Greta.

  Rooney Mara, left, and her sister Kate, right, play sisters in a new film inspired by the lives of York twins Freda and Greta Chaplin


The film – directed by award-winner German film-maker Werner Herzog and starring Hollywood A-listers Orlando Bloom and Domhnall Gleeson – brings to the screen a tale that has fascinated journalists, documentary-makers and the public for more than 40 years.

Born in April 1943, Freda and Greta grew up in Tang Hall and spent almost every moment of their lives together. As children they appeared much like any other pair of twins, but by the age of 11 they had become ultra-dependent on one another. They left school at 15 and became recluses, often staying in their bedrooms.

  Exterior of the Chaplin family home in Tang Hall ,York, which went on the market in 2020 following the death of Freda


They grew eerily dependent on each other, acting as mirror images in movement, dress, and speech. It was a relationship that many observers found both remarkable and puzzling.

Their lives first attracted widespread public attention in the early 1980s.

Appearing before York Magistrates' Court, they were prosecuted for breach of the peace following an incident involving local lorry driver Ken Iveson. The court proceedings became famous not only because of the case itself but because of the twins' unusual behaviour. Speaking together and protesting their innocence in unison, they made headlines far beyond York.

  Press cuttings from the Chaplin case at York Magistrates' Court, with inset of Ken Iveson taken from The Twins documentary


What might otherwise have been a routine local court case sparked international fascination.

Newspapers around the world reported on the York sisters and the public became captivated by their unique story.

That interest led to a series of documentaries, including Julia Darling's acclaimed A Pair of One in 1987 and Journeyman Pictures' The Twins in 1994. The films offered a rare glimpse into the sisters' private world and helped establish Freda and Greta as some of Britain's most unusual sisters.

Behind the fascination, however, lay a more complicated story.

As adults, the twins struggled to live independently and spent periods in institutions, hostels and supported accommodation after their parents were no longer able to cope with the demands of caring for them.

Mental health specialists attempted to understand their behaviour, but no straightforward explanation was ever found. Their intense attachment to one another remained one of the defining features of their lives.

  Chaplin twins, Greta and Freda, - still taken from the documentary, The Twins


For a time they lived in London with Jack Davenport, an elderly man who had read about their circumstances and offered them a home. Later, in the mid to late 1990s, they returned to York, moving back into the family house in Tang Hall following the death of their parents.

It was there that the sisters spent their later years together until tragedy struck.

Greta died from cancer in 2007 at the age of 64. Friends recalled how a heartbroken Freda would visit her sister's grave at Fulford Cemetery whenever she could.

Freda found comfort in her beloved pets and remained devoted to animal welfare. When she died in May 2020, aged 77, she left the bulk of her £177,000 estate, including the family home in Tang Hall, to the Cinnamon Trust, the national charity that helps elderly and terminally ill people care for their pets.

In keeping with her wishes, she was buried beside Greta.

  Fulford Cemetery, where Freda Chaplin is now buried next to her sister Greta


Today, the sisters rest together in Fulford Cemetery, just as they spent their lives together.

Yet their remarkable story continues to resonate. What began in a modest Tang Hall home has now reached Hollywood, ensuring that a pair of York sisters who once puzzled, fascinated and moved so many people will not be forgotten.

The film, inspired by their lives, Bucking Fastard, premiered at the International Venice Film Festival this week and will be released in cinemas this autumn following screenings at other international festivals.

The movie's director Werner Herzog has long been fascinated by the story of the York twins.

In his memoir, Every Man for Himself and God Against All, Herzog recalls meeting the Chaplin twins and being struck by their uniqueness, particularly the way they walked and talked.

He recalled their meeting: "We know that twins sometimes develop their own secret language when they are all alone by which they can exclude the rest of the world, but Freda and Greta spoke the same words at the same time.

"They wore exactly the same clothes, hairstyles, shoes. Their handbags and umbrellas were identical.

"When they walked, they didn’t walk in step like soldiers, left-right, left-right, but they had their inside feet together and kept time with their outside feet.

  "You could have folded a picture of them, and the two halves would have matched."
