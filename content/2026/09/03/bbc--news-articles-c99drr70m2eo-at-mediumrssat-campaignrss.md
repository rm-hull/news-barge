**Sleep scores, magnesium, white noise machines - what was once the simple act of sleeping has become a minefield.**

Trying to optimise sleep in this way is called "sleepmaxxing". Take it too far, and you could end up with "orthosomnia" - the clinical term for an obsession with achieving perfect sleep.

While it's not formally recognised as a condition in the UK, doctors tell the BBC they've noticed signs of orthosomnia in their patients, with some bringing their sleep tracker data into appointments convinced, wrongly, that they have a sleep disorder.

That fixation with sleep is actually making people's sleep worse, they warn.

So how and why did sleep become so complicated? And if sleepmaxxing isn't the answer, what is the elusive secret to a good night's sleep?

## 'Sleep scores are completely made up'

The first commercial fitness tracker to incorporate sleep analysis was the Fitbit Classic in 2009.

Since then, many others - including Garmin, Whoop and Apple - have integrated sleep trackers into wrist-worn devices, as have smart rings such as Oura, Ultrahuman and RingConn.

These devices vary, but in general they all provide users with a report of their night, broken down by sleep stages - time awake, light sleep, deep sleep, and REM (rapid eye movement). Many also give users a "sleep score".

But the concept of a sleep score is "completely made up", according to Dr Alanna Hare, consultant of sleep medicine at the Royal Brompton Hospital in London.

"There's nothing recognised in sleep science that is a 'sleep score'," she tells the BBC. "That's just been created by manufacturers."

How sleep-tracking can make your sleep worse

Hare says she's noticed trackers causing anxiety among her patients.

"I've noticed more patients coming to me with data from various trackers, with lots of questions about sleep that... is probably contributing to their sleep difficulties," she says.

Even though some have had medical tests with reassuring results, this hasn't allayed their fears, she adds.

"It's great that there's more focus on sleep as part of wellness," she adds. "But sleep just does not respond well to attempts to perfect it."

One proposed lawsuit against Oura, filed last month in San Francisco, alleges that Oura rings are unable to accurately measure sleep quality and sleep stages, and instead rely on AI-generated estimates that have "a coin flip's chance of being correct".

In response, Oura told the BBC that it "stand[s] behind our science, research and accuracy claims".

"We're committed to clearly communicating what Oura measures, what it estimates, and how members should use that information," the company added.

## Daytime impact

Studies also show sleep trackers can affect how you function during the day, even if they're not accurate.

In one study, external from the University of Oxford, participants were given sleep trackers which assigned them a sleep score.

The scores were randomised, however, and had no relation to how the participants had actually slept.

![Illustration of a man wearing a sleep tracking device with a pink ring, with pink rings emanating from his head. He has his head in his hand and looks anxious](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/1920/cpsprodpb/4f4d/live/6ae10c80-a6d3-11f1-aed2-8d6da8d75094.png)

The next day participants rated their mood, fatigue levels and alertness, before tests checking their focus and reaction times.

Researchers found that the people told they'd slept badly reported feeling more fatigued, and were less mentally alert - even though the sleep scores were all fake.

## 'Cycle of worrying'

This is something Jess Hill says she noticed in herself after getting a Garmin watch several years ago.

"I started to rely on the watch as my Bible for whether I slept well or not," the 32-year-old HR professional from London tells the BBC.

"I would think, 'I've had awful sleep so I'm going to really struggle today', even if I actually probably had okay sleep. If I'd slept really well, I'd be convinced I'd have to be on top - even though I was tired, I was pushing through things."

Jess Hill was "obsessed" with her Garmin's sleep scores for about three years

Her "obsession", as she describes it, with getting a high sleep score lasted three years, until earlier this year, when she started to notice the watch wasn't reflecting how she'd actually slept.

"I was awake the other week for four hours in the night, and my watch was like 'wow, great sleep!' - I literally was awake from 01:00 until 05:00," she says.

The BBC has approached Garmin for comment.

Jess's sleep scores weren't matching how she actually felt

Dr Neil Stanley, sleep expert and author of the book How to Sleep Well, argues that gamifying sleep is creating more disordered sleeping.

"The thing with tracking your sleep is that it gives you a number, and this means you have a target," he tells the BBC. "So you get into this cycle of worrying about your quality of sleep or the quantity of sleep based on figures."

He says he carried out research into actigraphy - wrist-worn sleep trackers - around 40 years ago.

Though commercial trackers now are a bit more sophisticated, they work in much the same way - by measuring your movement - and have the same limitations, he says.

"If you want to measure sleep, you have to measure the brain," Stanley adds. "Unless you're measuring the brain's activities, you're not measuring sleep."

## So how can we actually sleep well?

![Five illustrations showing the different tips Alanna Hare recommends for good sleep](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/1200/cpsprodpb/8505/live/ce369570-a6d3-11f1-aed2-8d6da8d75094.png)

Hare recommends:

- Keeping your bedroom cool, dark and quiet - within reason. "That doesn't mean it needs to be pitch black, exactly 16C or totally silent," she says. "It should be coolish, darkish and quietish."
- Seeing bright light in the morning, which is good for regulating your circadian rhythm - that is, your body's natural clock.
- Doing light exercise, and eating a "healthy, Mediterranean-style diet with plenty of plant variability".
- Above all, keeping it simple - "anything else, you don't need it".
