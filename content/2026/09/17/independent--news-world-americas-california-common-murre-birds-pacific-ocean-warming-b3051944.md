Penguin-like sea birds known as common murres have been dying in droves along the California coast over the last two months.

The birds are starving to death because warming Pacific waters, fueled by climate change and a “super” El Niño, are making underwater creatures who compete for their food sources hungrier. Murres can dive to depths of up to 300 feet to hunt for prey but predator fish are outpacing the birds, leaving many to starve. 

Without enough energy from food, the birds are not be able to molt. Molting is a natural process that helps murres swim, stay warm and buoyant and maintain waterproofing.

Dozens of emaciated seabirds have been taken to International Bird Rescue centers in California to receive critical care.

“This time of year, Bird Rescue normally receives large numbers of orphaned chicks with a few adults. However, this year, more adults than chicks are coming into care,” the non-profit said in a blog post last week.

![Common murres are starving because warming Pacific waters are helping competitors gobble up more of their prey](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/17/14/47/usfws-common-murres-ledge-large-1.jpeg)

Severely emaciated murres have been brought into rescue centers with worn and broken feathers.

“Not having the energy to grow new feathers is kind of like not having enough money to buy a new wetsuit and having to use a ratty old one with lots of holes in a cold ocean,” Dr. Rebecca Duerr, Senior Director of Research and Veterinary Science, explained.

The center’s staff provides specialized care to help the birds regain their strength and eventually return to the wild. Some are also treated for a red blood cell parasite called *Babesia uriae* that leads to anemia and weakness in seabirds. 

Unfortunately, this is not the first time common murres have been threatened.

The birds experienced the largest known die-off of a single bird species after an Alaska ocean heat wave known as “the blob” that started in late 2014 and lasted for two years.

![Common murres experienced the largest known die-off of a single bird species in Alaska in 2014](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/17/13/16/GettyImages-486497092.jpeg)

Some four million murres - or around half of the state’s murre population - died from starvation during the die-off, a 2024 study found.

During that time, more than 500 birds were treated at the rescue organization’s San Francisco Bay-Delta center.

"Most heat waves come and go within a few weeks, maybe a few months. This one was different. It hung around for two years," Fish and Wildlife researcher Heather Renner told *CBS News San Francisco* Wednesday. 

"And over the course of those two years, it really changed the marine ecosystem in ways that we're only now beginning to understand."

Murres spend most of the year on the open ocean before colonies of thousands migrate to tall seaside cliffs to lay teardrop-shaped eggs, according to the Oregon Coast Aquarium. Newborn chicks will jump into the water just a couple of months after birth.

Because murres often mate for life and only lay one egg a year, the species’ recovery has been slow, and makes saving them even more critical.

Anyone who sees a murre that appears to be in distress in an unusual location, isolating from other birds, entangled in fishing gear or allowing people to approach should contact local animal control or the Bird Rescue’s Bird HelpLine at 866-SOS-BIRD (866-767-2473).
