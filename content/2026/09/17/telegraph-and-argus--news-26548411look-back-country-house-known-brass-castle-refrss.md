JUST off Long Lane in Harden there is a house known as Brass Castle.

The name brass is said to be a corruption of brae, or braes, a northern and Scottish word for a hillside, or brow, which had its origins in old Norse.

There has been a house on the site from at least the 17th century, but it was substantially rebuilt in the 1790s by Benjamin Ferrand of Harden Grange.

Little remains of the old house, with perhaps the exception of the front entrance that overlooks the Goit Stock valley. Here the original heavy door lintel with its shallow Gothic arch has been retained.

The Gothic style was very much the theme of Ferrand’s rebuild.

The window openings, both on the ground and the first floor are tall and narrow with pointed Gothic arches. They are side by side in groups of three, or more, to allow in maximum light. The windows give the property an almost ecclesiastical look.

In addition to the Gothic windows an ornamental, crenelated, parapet was added around the roof, giving the house the appearance of a small castle.

At the time that Ferrand rebuilt it he also built a folly in the wood at the other side of the valley. This consists of a tower with the same Gothic openings and a stone Gothic archway. Above the doorway in the tower is inscribed - BF 1796.

It is difficult to know what Benjamin Ferrand had in mind when he rebuilt the house with crenelations and a rather curious stone cross that he added to the apex of one of the gables. However, he was undoubtedly looking back to antiquity, perhaps to the days when land in the neighbourhood was under monastic control.

The house and surrounding land were let as a farm unit during the 19th century.

In the 1850s it leased to William Foster, a grocer and farmer.

His youngest daughter, Elizabeth, was born here in 1853. She married James Roberts the son of an Oakworth weaver, who proved to be an astute businessman and was later the owner of Salts Mill, in Saltaire.

When he was knighted, she became the first Lady Roberts.

Following the departure of William Foster a Bingley corn miller, Henry England, took the property.

In 1891 he brought a mains gas supply to the house and turned the farm over to raising poultry.

He bred Dark Brahmas, Speckled Hollands and a long-forgotten breed known as the Burton’s strain.

After England left the country for Massachusetts in America, Brass Castle was tenanted by a dairy farmer but he left after his daughter was badly injured in an accident on the farm.

Shortly after 1911 it was occupied by Charles Dixon who once again returned it to poultry rearing. When he sold up in 1925, he had 400 chickens and pullets, including such now rare breeds as White Wyandottes, Buff Rocks, Leghorns and Rhode Island Reds.

The same year that Dixon left the Ferrand’s sold the property to Asa Binns, a Bradford wool merchant with a business in Cheapside.

His youngest son, Frank, lived at Brass Castle. A keen cricketer he was captain of the Harden cricket team.

During the Second World War he served as an advisor to the Ministry of Labour for which he received a knighthood in 1947.

Asa Binns sold the house in 1935 for £1,300 and moved to Cottingley.

The next occupant was James Sykes a Bingley market gardener who was followed by Henry Entwistle a Bradford businessman whose later hobby was breeding fox terriers.

During the war the land was ploughed for growing potatoes.

Today the property remains largely unchanged and stands as a reminder of the eccentricities of an 18th century Yorkshire gentleman.

The name has now reverted to Braes Castle.
