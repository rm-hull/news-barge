If you work in Germany, the latest anti-discrimination figures are worth paying attention to.

The Anti-Discrimination Association of Germany (advd), an umbrella organisation representing independent anti-discrimination counselling centres, this week published its third Civil Society Report on Anti-Discrimination (for the year 2025).

Based on data from 29 counselling centres across the country, the report suggests that discrimination complaints are rising sharply – and that the workplace remains the most common setting in which people seek help.

**What does the report say?**

Last year, counselling centres affiliated with the advd documented 4,106 new discrimination cases – a 19 percent increase compared with the previous year and a 35.9 percent increase compared with 2023.

Because only 29 of the advd's 40 member organisations participated in the analysis, the authors of the report say the actual number of recorded discrimination cases is likely to be significantly higher.

Racism and antisemitism remain the most commonly reported forms of discrimination, accounting for 54.6 percent of all documented cases. Discrimination based on gender, disability or chronic illness, sexual identity and age also increased compared with previous years.

The report highlights discrimination across almost every area of life, including education, housing, healthcare, government services and employment. However, the workplace emerged as a hotspot for discrimination complaints.

More than a quarter of all reported cases, 26.7 percent, occurred in the workplace. Compared with 2023, the number of workplace-related cases per counselling centre rose by 54.2 percent.

Nearly half of workplace discrimination cases involved racial discrimination. Gender discrimination accounted for a quarter of cases, while approximately one in five involved disability or chronic illness.

Reported experiences included racist insults, sexual harassment, unfair performance evaluations, unequal pay, missed promotion opportunities and exclusion by colleagues or managers.

**What can you do if you experience discrimination at work in Germany?**

Germany's General Equal Treatment Act, known as the AGG (*Allgemeines Gleichbehandlungsgesetz*), is the country's main anti-discrimination law.

It prohibits discrimination in the workplace on grounds including ethnic origin, race, gender, religion or belief, disability, age and sexual identity.

Companies are required to take measures to protect employees from discrimination and must provide a designated complaints mechanism through which workers can report discriminatory treatment. Employers are also obliged to review complaints and may not penalise workers for making them.

**Independent advice**

For anyone experiencing discrimination, the advd and its counselling network advise seeking independent advice as early as possible.

The organisation's counselling centre directory can help people find specialised support locally. Counsellors can then help assess a situation, explain legal and non-legal options and assist people in asserting their rights.

In addition, anyone who believes they are being discriminated against is advised to keep a detailed record of incidents, including dates, times, locations and exactly what happened.

Relevant emails, messages, performance reviews and other documents should be preserved, while potential witnesses should also be identified.

Seeking independent advice can be particularly important because many people experience discrimination in areas where the law offers only limited protection or none at all.

According to the advd report, almost one-third of documented cases occurred in public institutions – such as schools, universities, government agencies, the police or the courts – that are largely outside the AGG's scope.

The report also notes that some forms of discrimination, including cases linked to language or nationality, may not be clearly covered by the legislation.

Independent counselling can help clarify whether a case falls under the AGG, whether other legal protections may be relevant (including labour, civil, criminal or asylum law), and what practical steps can be taken next.

**Why it’s important to act quickly**

Under the current AGG rules, anyone seeking compensation or damages for workplace discrimination generally only has two months to make their claim in writing after becoming aware of the discriminatory act. Missing this deadline can make it much harder to pursue a legal claim.

The advd argues that this timeframe is too short, as many people need time to process what has happened, gather evidence and seek advice. A reform currently moving through Germany's legislative process would extend the deadline to four months, but the advd says even that still may not give victims sufficient time to understand their options and enforce their rights effectively.
