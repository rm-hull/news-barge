**Thursday's top story:Germany targets social security fraud with ten-point plan**

The German government has unveiled a ten-point plan aimed at tackling social security fraud, with a particular focus on organised criminal networks exploiting welfare systems through abuses of EU freedom of movement rules.

The measures, developed by Labour Minister Bärbel Bas (SPD) and Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt (CSU), are expected to be introduced by the end of the year and include stricter checks on benefit claims, expanded data-sharing between authorities and stronger action against employers involved in undeclared work.

Under the proposals, individuals subject to enforceable arrest warrants could lose access to basic income support. Local authorities would also gain new powers to tackle so-called “junk properties” (residential or commercial properties whose renovation or repair costs significantly exceed their actual market value), including repair orders and, in some cases, expropriation or restrictions on use.

Ministers say organised gangs often recruit vulnerable people from poorer EU regions, house them in substandard accommodation and use fictitious employment contracts to obtain benefits such as child allowance. Authorities argue that many of those involved are themselves victims of criminal exploitation.

The government says the reforms are necessary to protect public confidence in the welfare state, while critics from The Left have accused ministers of focusing on fraud rather than strengthening social security provision for those in need.

**Workplace discrimination drives surge in reported cases across Germany**

Cases of discrimination reported to advocacy centres in Germany rose sharply in 2025, with the workplace emerging as the most common setting for incidents. According to the Anti-Discrimination Association of Germany (advd), 4,106 new cases were registered nationwide last year, representing a 19 percent increase on 2024.

More than a quarter of all documented complaints related to discrimination in the workplace. Nearly half of those cases involved racial discrimination, while incidents linked to gender, disability, chronic illness and age were also reported at above-average levels.

Trade union leader Anja Piel described the rising figures as a “warning sign”, arguing that stronger protections are needed to ensure workplaces provide equal opportunities and respectful treatment for all employees.

The report also highlights mounting pressure on discrimination counselling services. Compared with 2023, the number of workplace-related cases handled per counselling centre increased by 54.2 percent, stretching organisations that are already facing financial uncertainty.

The advd has called for long-term funding for anti-discrimination services and urged lawmakers to strengthen proposed reforms to Germany’s General Equal Treatment Act, arguing that victims need more time to seek advice and pursue legal claims.

**Germany’s justice system strains under record case backlog**

Germany’s criminal justice system is facing mounting pressure as an unprecedented backlog of cases builds up at public prosecutors’ offices across the country.

According to the German Judges’ Association (DRB), more than 1.1 million criminal cases were awaiting processing by the end of the first half of 2026, marking the first time the backlog has exceeded the one million mark.

The association warns that prosecutors are struggling to keep pace with a growing workload. More than 2.7 million new cases were filed during the first six months of 2026, around 52,000 more than in the same period last year.

If current trends continue, the total number of new cases for the year is expected to exceed five million, setting a new national record.

The backlog has risen by 13 percent compared with June 2025 and continues a long-term upward trend. For comparison, there were just over 709,000 pending criminal cases nationwide at the end of 2020.

**Saxony loosens wolf hunting rules after livestock attacks**

The eastern German state of Saxony has approved new legislation making it easier to cull wolves following attacks on livestock.

The measure, passed by the state parliament, implements recent changes to federal hunting and conservation laws. Under the new rules, a wolf may be shot if it can be shown to have injured or killed livestock after breaching existing herd protection measures.

Environment and Agriculture Minister Georg-Ludwig von Breitenbuch said the changes would help authorities “become capable of taking action” and demonstrate that the concerns of livestock farmers are being taken seriously.

Official monitoring identified 35 wolf packs, ten wolf pairs, and one territorial lone wolf in Saxony during 2024-25. Most are concentrated in the Upper Lusatia region.

By the end of July this year, farmers had reported 116 suspected attacks, with wolves considered responsible in 73 cases. Around 200 livestock animals, primarily sheep, but also goats and cattle, were killed.

The reforms remain controversial. Critics from the Green Party argue the legislation goes beyond federal requirements and lacks detail on hunting qualifications, culling limits, scientific oversight and reporting obligations. Farmers will continue to receive compensation for verified wolf-related losses.

A clear majority of people in Germany support a temporary halt to the development of advanced artificial intelligence systems if experts believe the risks cannot be adequately controlled, according to a new survey.

The YouGov poll, commissioned by the *Süddeutsche Zeitung*’s Digital Policy Briefing, found that 64 percent of respondents would back a pause in the development of particularly powerful AI technologies under such circumstances. Only 20 percent opposed the idea, while 17 per cent were undecided.

The survey of 2,161 people was conducted between Friday and Monday, following renewed debate about AI regulation after several recent incidents involving AI systems reportedly operating beyond expected controls.

Support for a development freeze cuts across political divides. Backing was strongest among supporters of the Greens and the Social Democratic Party (SPD), at 72 and 70 percent respectively.

But majorities of CDU/CSU and AfD voters, 63 and 61 percent, also favoured a temporary pause. Among voters who supported The Left in the last federal election, 67 percent were in favour.
