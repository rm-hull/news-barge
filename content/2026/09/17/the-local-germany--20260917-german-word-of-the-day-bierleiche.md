**What does* Bierleiche*mean?**

*Bierleiche* literally translates as “beer corpse”. It’s a humorous, informal German noun used to describe someone who has drunk so much alcohol they’ve fallen into a state of complete drunken exhaustion.

Grammatically, *Bierleiche* is a feminine noun:*die Bierleiche*. The plural is* die Bierleichen*.

Like many German words, it’s a compound noun made up of *das Bier*(beer) and* die Leiche* (corpse).

Because the final element of a German compound determines the gender, the word takes the feminine article *die*.

Despite sounding rather dramatic, *Bierleiche* is not considered particularly insulting. At festivals, student parties and local celebrations, Germans often use it with a shrug and a laugh rather than as a serious criticism.

You may also come across a few related expressions. For example, a *Schnapsleiche* is someone who has got themselves into a similar state via spirits rather than beer.

**Why do I need to know* Bierleiche*?**

In a word: Oktoberfest.

While the term is understood throughout Germany – and used at any time of year – there’s arguably no greater concentration of *Bierleichen* anywhere in the country than at Munich’s Oktoberfest.

Inevitably, after a few hours spent drinking litres of strong beer, some visitors take a turn for the worse. Many of them eventually make their way to the legendary *Kotzhügel* (“Vomit Hill”) – the grassy slope behind the festival tents.

Despite its unappetising nickname, the hill offers a place where exhausted revellers can sit down, recover, get some fresh air, or occasionally become a textbook *Bierleiche* under the watchful eye of medics and security staff.

**Use it like this:**

*Nach der langen Feier lag eine Bierleiche im Garten.*

The beer corpse was lying right in the garden after the long party.

*Die Sanitäter mussten auf dem Festivalgelände mehrere Bierleichen versorgen.*

The paramedics had to take care of several beer corpses on the festival grounds.
