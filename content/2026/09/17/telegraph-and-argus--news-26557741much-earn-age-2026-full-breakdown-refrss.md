How much should you really be earning at your age? New salary benchmarks reveal what the typical UK worker takes home at different stages of their career – and the figures may not be what you expect.

Salary remains one of the great British taboos. We might openly discuss our relationships, mental health and even what we paid for our homes, yet asking a colleague what they earn can still feel wildly inappropriate.

That means many workers have little idea whether their pay is broadly in line with people at a similar stage of their careers – or whether their salary has fallen behind the market.

The question is particularly relevant in 2026, with UK wage growth slowing to 3.8% in the three months to January, according to the figures supplied by employment specialist Bridgewater TI.

Meanwhile, payroll employment among workers aged 34 and under has fallen by almost 220,000 since its mid-2024 peak, suggesting younger workers have faced particular pressure.

## So, are you earning what you should be in 2026?

Martin Balcombe at global employment specialist Bridgewater TI has broken down the figures, including the typical salary at different ages and the warning signs that could indicate your pay deserves a closer look.

Based on the latest median monthly PAYE figures, these are the approximate salary benchmarks for UK employees in 2026:

1. 
    **18–24:** £1,830 a month / £21,960 a year
2. 
    **25–34:** £2,820 a month / £33,840 a year
3. 
    **35–49:** £3,062 a month / £36,744 a year
4. 
    **50–64:** £2,698 a month / £32,376 a year
5. 
    **65+:** £1,582 a month / £18,984 a year

These figures can provide useful context, but nobody should see them as a deadline they have failed to meet.

A 30-year-old earning below that national median isn’t automatically behind, just as somebody earning above it isn’t necessarily being paid fairly for their particular role.

## Your 20s: The decade with the biggest salary jump

The figures suggest one of the sharpest pay increases occurs between 18-24 and 25-34, when median monthly earnings rise from £1,830 to £2,820, a difference of almost £12,000 a year.

For many, their 20s involve moving from entry-level employment into roles requiring greater experience and responsibility. However, that pay increase rarely happens automatically. There’s an assumption that working hard and staying loyal means your salary will rise at the right pace. That isn’t always true.

If you joined as an assistant but now manage projects, train colleagues or work directly with clients, compare your current responsibilities, not your original title, with similar vacancies.


## Your 30s: When the pressure to be ‘successful’ gets loud

By their 30s, workers are often expected to earn more, lead a team, buy property and build a pension, all at once.

Median earnings peak among those aged 35 to 49 at £3,062 a month, but that figure hides considerable differences between industries, locations and circumstances.

Salary comparison can become particularly intense in your 30s, when people assume your earnings should reflect years of uninterrupted progression. But careers are rarely that neat. People retrain, have children, relocate, experience illness or choose greater flexibility. None of that means they have failed.

## Your 40s: Are you being paid for the job you do now?

Experience does not always guarantee fair pay. Long-serving employees can discover that newer colleagues earn more because market rates have increased while their own salary has barely moved.

This is known as salary compression, where the pay gap between experienced and newer employees becomes unexpectedly small. Instead of asking, ‘Am I earning enough for my age?’, ask, ‘What would my employer pay to replace me?’

If similar vacancies consistently offer significantly more, that provides a much more useful comparison than a broad national average.

## Your 50s: Why earnings don’t always keep rising

Median monthly pay falls from £3,062 among 35- to 49-year-olds to £2,698 for those aged 50 to 64. This does not necessarily represent pay cuts.

The group may include more people reducing their hours to look after children, changing careers or prioritising flexibility to care for aging parents.

We often imagine earnings as a staircase, rising with every birthday and promotion. In reality, pay can plateau or fall for many reasons. Some people willingly trade salary for fewer hours or less pressure. What matters is whether that was a genuine choice or whether their experience is being undervalued.

## What counts as a ‘good’ salary in 2026?

The median annual salary for a full-time UK employee reached £39,039 in 2025, up 4.3%.

Meanwhile, workers needed to earn around £77,000 to enter the highest-paid 10%, according to ONS figures reported by Reuters.

But a ‘good’ salary is not one fixed number. Someone earning £35,000 while living with family may have more disposable income than somebody earning £50,000 and renting alone in London.

The headline figure doesn’t tell you everything. Pension contributions, annual leave, flexibility, working hours and progression opportunities all matter. A higher salary can quickly become less attractive if it involves an expensive commute, unpaid overtime and very little security.

## 5 signs you could be underpaid

1. Your responsibilities have increased but your salary hasn't
2. New vacancies at your company offer more for similar work
3. Comparable jobs repeatedly advertise significantly higher salaries
4. You've gained valuable skills or qualifications without a salary review
5. Your employer cannot give you clear criteria for reaching the next pay level

Go into a salary conversation with evidence. Make a clear list of how your role has changed, the results you have delivered and what comparable positions currently pay.

‘I need more money’ is understandable, particularly when living costs are high, but ‘my responsibilities have expanded in these five measurable ways’ gives your manager a stronger business case to respond to.

## How much do you need to earn to live comfortably in your region?

A £35,000 salary can provide a very different lifestyle depending on where you live. The Joseph Rowntree Foundation estimates that a single working-age adult needs approximately £30,500 a year to reach a socially acceptable living standard, covering essentials alongside some socialising.

However, housing costs vary dramatically. In June 2026, average UK private rent reached £1,388 a month, ranging from £781 in the North East to £2,302 in London, according to the Office for National Statistics.

Based on these figures, these are the broad salary ranges a single private renter might need to live reasonably comfortably in each region:

- 
    **North East:** £29,000–£32,000
- 
    **Wales:** £29,000–£33,000
- 
    **Northern Ireland:** £29,000–£33,000
- 
    **Yorkshire & Humber:** £30,000–£34,000
- 
    **North West:** £31,000–£35,000
- 
    **East Midlands:** £31,000–£35,000
- 
    **Scotland:** £32,000–£36,000
- 
    **West Midlands:** £32,000–£36,000
- 
    **South West:** £35,000–£39,000
- 
    **East of England:** £36,000–£41,000
- 
    **South East:** £39,000–£44,000
- 
    **London:** £50,000–£58,000

These estimates assume a single adult renting privately without children. National comparisons can be misleading.
