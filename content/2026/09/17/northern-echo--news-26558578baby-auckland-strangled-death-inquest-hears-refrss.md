A baby whose skeleton was discovered beneath floorboards in Bishop Auckland more than a century after his passing was likely strangled to death, a coroner has concluded.

The remains of the infant, known as "Baby Auckland", were uncovered during renovation work on Fore Bondgate in July 2024.

An inquest at Crook Coroners' Court on Thursday (September 17) heard the baby boy's body was found beneath floorboards wrapped in pages of The Umpire newspaper dated June 1910, with twine tied around his neck.

After hearing evidence from forensic experts, police and pathologists, senior coroner Crispin Oliver concluded the child had been "unlawfully killed" by strangulation.

  CSI at the scene 

*(Image: TERRY BLACKBURN)*

Mr Oliver said: "The conclusion I am driven to on the balance of probabilities is that the twine was applied to Baby Auckland after he was born while he was still alive.

"The most likely explanation is that the person in a position of care for him while he was most vulnerable had previously taken the decision to end his life by means of strangulation using the twine as a garrote.

"As such, this was a homicide."

  Twine found with Baby Auckland 

*(Image: DURHAM POLICE)*

The inquest was held to establish who the child was and how, when and where he died.

Describing it as a "sad and singular case", he said the investigation had been unusual because the baby's identity remains unknown despite extensive inquiries.

The court heard the "critical piece of evidence" was the twine found around the infant's neck.

Alternative theories that the twine formed part of a religious practice, or had been used in a parcel wrapping process, were considered and ruled out during the investigation.

Mr Oliver said the evidence pointed to the infant being strangled before his body was concealed beneath the floorboards in the newspaper.

  Twine and newspaper found with Baby Auckland 

*(Image: DURHAM POLICE)*

Detective Chief Inspector Mel Sutherland, of Durham Constabulary, told the hearing police were called shortly after 11am on July 18, 2024, after contractors carrying out renovation work uncovered what appeared to be human remains.

The court heard the skeleton was found around a metre below the floor with twine tied around the neck.

Builder Peter Moody, who has owned the property for around 18 years, described the moment the remains were discovered.

  The floorboads of a Bishop Auckland flat where Baby Auckland was found 

*(Image: DURHAM POLICE)*

He said: "I picked up what appeared to be a ball and then realised 'this is a baby'.

"It was a skull and bones. I could see the little feet."

Forensic anthropologist Dr Micol Zupello told the court the remains were partially mummified and skeletonised.

Experts recovered the body using archaeological techniques and found the infant curled “in a foetal position.”

Analysis showed the baby was full-term and had died between 38 and 40 weeks of gestation.

However, specialists were unable to determine whether the child had been stillborn or born alive.

A post-mortem examination by Home Office pathologist Dr Louise Mulcahy said the twine was found looped around the baby's neck three times before being knotted.

Dr Mulcahy said it was impossible to determine how tightly it had been applied because of the passage of time and deterioration of the remains. The official medical cause of death was therefore recorded as unascertained.

Radiocarbon dating established the child could not have died after 1955, but experts could not pinpoint an exact year. Possible date ranges stretched from as early as the 17th century to 1955.

  Funeral held for Baby Auckland 

*(Image: SARAH CALDECOTT)*

Investigators believe the baby was most likely born sometime after June 1910 due to the newspaper wrapping and the age of the property.

Durham Constabulary carried out extensive DNA enquiries and followed a number of public leads in an effort to identify the child and any surviving relatives, but none resulted in a match.

Mr Oliver said what happened to Baby Auckland was “terrible thing” and praised the community for its response.

He said: "In the great majority of cases, the questions of who, when and where are readily apparent, leaving the 'how' question the focus of the inquest.

"In this inquest, at its conclusion, it still had all four questions to address and, if achievable, to answer."

He added: "This is a terrible thing that happened to a defenceless infant.

"It happened in our community but happened over a century ago, and it is heartwarming to see how the public have stepped up to do the right thing."

Detective Chief Inspector Sutherland said leading the investigation had been both frustrating and challenging because of the lack of evidence available after so many years.

He added: "It brings it home how sad this case is. The baby never had the opportunity to live a life, but it has been heartwarming to see the response from the community here."
