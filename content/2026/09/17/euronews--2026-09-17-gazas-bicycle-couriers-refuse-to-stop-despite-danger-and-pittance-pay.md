## Between 40 and 50 workers have died in the Israel-Hamas war at one delivery company alone, yet hundreds remain on the roads, covering routes by bicycle after fuel shortages and the destruction of 74% of Gaza's road network made motorised delivery impossible.

Gaza delivery workers pedalling through rubble on bicycles can spend 40 of their 50-shekel (€12) daily earnings fixing a single puncture.

Fuel shortages, transport disruption and the destruction of Gaza's road network have made bicycles — known colloquially as "baskleet" — the primary means of delivery across the Strip.

Workers who spoke to Euronews described a job that keeps them under relentless pressure: the physical strain of cycling through rubble, the cost of constant repairs, and the danger of passing daily through areas still under bombardment.

In the Maghazi area in central Gaza, Mohammed Abu Amra works for the delivery company Barq.

Trips he used to complete by motorbike in under 10 minutes now take him 30 or more by bicycle, with heat, rain, road conditions, and the distances involved making it much more challenging.

Tareq Abu Zekri launched Barq during the second ceasefire in January 2025, then expanded by hiring workers who used their own bicycles.

When he advertised for riders, 109 applications arrived within a day and a half, among them university graduates with few other options in what remains of the Strip's labour market.

The company now has more than 50 employees operating across three branches in Gaza City, central Gaza and Khan Younis.

Orders are transferred between areas by whatever means are available before couriers complete local deliveries, covering shop orders as well as goods sold from tents and homes.

Bicycle delivery predates the 2023 Israel-Hamas war: a pilot scheme in Rafah began in March 2021, and since October 2023, bicycles have become standard as fuel has run short and vehicles have been destroyed.

## **Repairs that cost more than a day's work**

Abbas Al-Souri estimates his daily earnings at between 50 and 60 shekels (€12 to €14), depending on how many orders he completes.

A puncture repair costs between 30 and 40 shekels (€7 to €10). A worker who earns 50 shekels and pays 40 to fix a tyre is left with 10 shekels — roughly €2.

Larger repairs cost more. Abu Zekri estimates that fixing wheels, hubs and pedals runs between 200 and 500 shekels (€48 to €120), depending on what needs replacing.

Spare parts are unreliable. When a bicycle breaks down, workers strip components from bikes too damaged to ride and use them to keep others moving.

"From broken bikes to other bikes — it's patchwork on top of patchwork," Al-Souri said.

A joint assessment published in April 2026 by the World Bank, European Union and United Nations found that around 74% of Gaza's road network — approximately 4,500 kilometres — had been classified as destroyed, with a further 13% partially damaged.

The assessment put total damage to the transport sector at approximately $3.21 billion (€2.97 billion) and losses at around $1.18 billion (€1.09 billion), covering roads, bridges, vehicles and transport facilities. The figures cover damage recorded up to October 2025, calculated primarily from satellite imagery.

Barq attempted to introduce electric bicycles to reduce the strain on workers. The experiment stalled when charging costs proved prohibitive: powering an electric bike costs around 30 shekels (€7) — roughly half a day's earnings.

## **Danger beyond breakdowns**

On 29 August, delivery worker Raed Adel Mohammed Alyan was killed alongside Abdel Fattah Abu Al-Eesh when a strike hit an electric bike in the Tel al-Hawa neighbourhood of south-west Gaza City, according to local reports citing medical and local sources. Three others were wounded, one critically.

Alyan worked for the Hamama delivery company and was on a delivery run at the time, according to the company's director Jamil Al-Yazji.

Between 40 and 50 bicycle couriers at Hamama have been killed in the Israel-Hamas war, yet around 400 continue to move packages through damaged streets and active bombardment, according to Al-Yazji.

Abu Zekri said some of his workers were wounded in a strike in Khan Younis and others narrowly escaped shelling near them in the Al-Jalaa area of Gaza City.

Despite the losses, both companies continue taking on workers. For Abu Zekri, the project is partly about providing income when other options have gone. What workers need to keep going, he said, is straightforward: passable roads, repairable bikes and available parts.

"Let us bring in pedals, half-frames, tyres, patches for the bikes," he said.
