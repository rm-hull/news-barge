  *It was once considered ‘the impossible restoration’. Celebrations were held in August to mark the 25th anniversary of the UK’s longest, deepest and highest canal tunnel reopening*

'It was a truly transformative moment – it reconnected communities, revived a historic route across the Pennines and created a destination that continues to inspire.’

Catherine Clayton, operations manager at the Canal & River Trust, speaks with pride as she reflects on the huge significance of the restoration of the Huddersfield Narrow Canal and Standedge Tunnel.

  Catherine Clayton 

*(Image: Supplied)*

The grand reopening in 2001 – attended by the then-Prince Charles – marked the culmination of a huge, years-long multi-million-pound project.

Today, the tunnel is on the bucket list of many boaters, whilst visitors can add it to theirs, with guided boat and canoe trips.

What could have been lost forever is now a thriving attraction where passionate volunteers keep its history alive, and events were held back in August to celebrate 25 years since the tunnel and canal reopened.

‘It’s a hidden gem – you wouldn’t expect it. You go through Marsden, which is a beautiful village, and suddenly there’s the canal,’ says Catherine, who looks after the team that maintains the canal and tunnel.

  The tunnel, which runs to Diggle in Lancashire, can be explored by canoe 

*(Image: Supplied)*

‘You go along the canal, which is lined with trees. It’s got gorgeous birdsong as you walk along, and you come to the mouth of the longest, highest, deepest canal tunnel in the UK, so it’s really special. It’s just an incredible space.’

She adds: ‘Twenty-five years later, it’s vibrant, it’s alive, people absolutely love this space, and they want to come and celebrate that again. We’ve got the café open – run by volunteers, we’ve got the boats running regularly – manned by volunteers, and it’s a special place where people can have a really great day out.

‘People around here are really invested in the canal because it’s a beautiful space, it’s really good for ecology, it’s really good for their mental wellbeing.

‘One of the straplines of the Canal & River Trust is that life is better by water, and I genuinely believe that’s true.

  The Huddersfield Narrow Canal is a serene place for a walk. Picture: 

*(Image: Jill Jennings)*

‘If I ever feel stressed, I just walk along by the canal and take a few deep breaths, listen to the birds, and feel a million times better.

‘It really is a lovely environment and we’ve had a lot of people come to volunteer with us who have mental health problems but say that coming here makes them feel better; this is what keeps them on an even keel, it gets them back to feeling happy – you can’t say anything more powerful than that, I don’t think.’

Stretching 20 miles across the Pennines, the canal connects Huddersfield in West Yorkshire and Ashton-under-Lyne in Greater Manchester, passing through dramatic landscapes and historic mill towns.

At its heart lies Standedge Tunnel, cutting a remarkable 3.25-mile route beneath the hills between Marsden and Diggle in Lancashire.

   

*(Image: Charlotte Graham)*

Dug by hand over 17 years, the tunnel was completed in 1811. It was a monumental feat of Industrial Revolution engineering, originally constructed to enable the movement of goods across the country along the canal.

Before motorised boats, journeys through Standedge relied on ‘legging’. This was a gruelling process that saw boatmen lie on their backs and walk along the tunnel walls or roof to propel boats through darkness, often taking many hours.

Following decades of decline and closure in 1944, the canal and tunnel were brought back to life through a complex restoration, made possible through the determination and advocacy of volunteers and organisations including the Huddersfield Canal Society.

Recent towpath improvements by Canal & River Trust, in partnership with West Yorkshire Combined Authority and Kirklees Council, mean towpath users can enjoy enhanced access along Huddersfield Narrow Canal to enjoy the stunning surroundings of the Pennines.

   

*(Image: Charlotte Graham)*

Families visiting the tunnel can explore a small boat-themed play area, look out for resident ducks and explore the onsite nature reserve using the Admiral’s Trail.

Despite its success, the trust says caring for such a complex and historic waterway remains an ongoing challenge.

‘Twenty-five years after its reopening, the work to protect Standedge Tunnel and the Huddersfield Narrow Canal continues,’ says Sean McGinley, regional director, Yorkshire & North East at Canal & River Trust.
