Following a wave of support for anti-immigrant parties in recent state elections, the next Berlin elections – held on Sunday, September 20th – feel especially consequential for foreigners.

For many, it will also represent their first chance to go to the polls in Germany.

Since the last vote, a record-breaking number of people have applied for – and been granted – German citizenship. This includes 9,041 people in 2023, 21,811 people in 2024, and 39,041 people in 2025.

While no final statistics have been released for this year, the LEA predicts that around 36,000 people will be naturalised this year.

If these figures are accurate, that would mean that around 25,500 people could have received their German passports in 2026 in time for the September elections.

In total, that means that around 95,393 new citizens will be eligible to cast their vote on Sunday, representing around 3.83 percent of the total electorate. What's more, anyone over the age of 16 will be able to vote in the elections for the first time after the voting age was lowered.

With just a few percentage points between many leading parties, this influx of fresh voters could prove decisive on election day.

**Why are there so many "new Germans"?**

The last Berlin elections were held in February 2023. A little more than a year later – in June 2024 – a law to permit dual nationality came into force in Germany. At the same time, the residence requirements were reduced from eight years to five in ordinary cases, giving many more people access to citizenship.

At the start of 2024, meanwhile, the city-state centralised and digitalised its naturalisation process, speeding things up significantly.

From less than 10,000 applications per year in 2023, the *Landesamt für Einwanderung (LEA)* has ramped up to processing almost 40,000 applications per year.

**How to decide how to cast your vote** 

The two frontrunners – the Left Party and the CDU – have widely differing views on immigration. The former wants to remove barriers to citizenship, including income requirements and language tests. The latter, meanwhile, places emphasis on integration and learning the German language.

In addition, issues such as the housing crisis, affordability, education, local transport, cycling infrastructure, culture funding and dealing with waste are all on the agenda – so there's plenty at stake for residents.

**A quarter of residents still blocked from voting**

While almost 100,000 new citizens have gained voting rights since the 2023 elections, a far larger number are still left out of the process.

Of Berlin's 3.9 million population, around 976,000 adults don't hold German citizenship. That means they are unable to vote in state and federal elections, and only those with EU citizenships can vote in local and EU elections.

The number is so large that the Green Party – who have called for an expansion of voting rights – organised a symbolic election for internationals in Mitte as a way of raising awareness of the issue. At the start of September, foreigners were invited to "vote" at a series of polling stations in the capital's centre to help make their voices heard.

Among those excluded from voting are some 40,000 people still waiting for their applications to be approved by the LEA.
