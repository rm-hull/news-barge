Former players and footballers’ families have united to demand government action on heading in professional and grassroots football, warning: “The time for action is now, not just shock and condolences”.

Ex-pros and the families of former Middlesbrough players and coaches launched a petition at Middleton Hall near Darlington on Wednesday (September 9), calling for less heading in training to protect footballers’ brains.

Head Safe Football’s campaign is backed by former players affected by dementia, chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) and other brain diseases linked to heading during their careers.

Families of former Middlesbrough players and coaches who suffered sports-related brain disease were the first to sign the petition on Wednesday.

They were joined by John Stiles, son of Nobby Stiles, Alan Mcllmoyle, son of Hughie Mcllmoyle, and former Middlesbrough player Gary Pallister, in calling on the government for action.

"But we can, and must, protect the players who are playing today, and those who will play tomorrow.

"We've previously focused on blame and shame of governing bodies in football, but it's now about protecting the next generation of footballers and that's what this event was about."

Head Safe Football has five demands, including a parliamentary debate on the petition, a national “Think Head Safe: reduce heading in training” campaign and recognition of CTE from repeated head impacts as a public-health issue.

They also want governing bodies to cut heading in training through policy and accredited coach education, while the Department for Education embeds brain-health education and HeadSAFE in teacher training and PE.

This issue of CTE was put into the spotlight earlier this year when a coroner ruled that heading a ball in former footballer Gordon McQueen's playing days “likely” contributed to the brain injury that was a factor in his death.

At the hearing at Northallerton Coroner's Court in January, the senior coroner for North Yorkshire and York, Jon Heath, gave a narrative conclusion, finding that McQueen died from pneumonia and vascular dementia, with a post-mortem examination also indicating CTE.

Mr Heath told the court: “I am satisfied that on the balance of probability, repeatedly heading footballs contributed to his developing chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE).”

The inquest heard that repeated head impacts during his career may have contributed to his death and years of neurological decline, including losing the ability to make tea and do the crossword.

Professor Willie Stewart told the inquest that CTE cannot be fully diagnosed during life and was identified in McQueen's brain after his death.

Outside the court, after the inquest concluded in January this year, Mr McQueen's daughters, Hayley McQueen and Anna Forbes, said that "football must change" if it wants to protect footballers of future generations.

Head Safe Football says research has found CTE in the brains of professional and amateur footballers, including young people, with evidence also pointing to increased neurological disease risks among footballers.

Head Safe is now asking who knew what, when they knew it and whether football’s governing bodies are doing enough.

Judith added: “With more than five million children and 10 million adults playing football in England, this is not simply a professional football issue. It is a national and even a worldwide issue.”

“This is not about removing heading from football. It is about asking whether we need to expose players to repetitive heading in training when we have an opportunity to reduce that exposure.

"It's not a North East issue, it's a global issue. We're working with people across the world, in Australia, the US, India, Africa, it's a problem that needs solving around the world."

Judith also thanked Middleton Hall, where her husband Bill spent the last year of his life, for volunteering to host the petition event.

Head Safe has also revealed that they have made Hayley McQueen a Head Safe Guardian, meaning she's now an ambassador for the footballing charity.
