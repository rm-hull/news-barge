THE Tradesmen were in existence for only three years, from 1963-1966. Starting the group showed what can be done with talent and determination, even when you have no funds to speak of.

Although they didn’t make it to the top, they fully deserve a place in the annals of Bradford’s rock ’n’ roll.

It all came about when Mike Betts, who’d been taking guitar lessons, got together some friends. The line-up was John (Andy) Anderson, lead guitar and vocals, Mike Betts, rhythm guitar and vocals, Mick Higgins, bass guitar, and Robert Castle, drums. John and Mick had no guitar-playing experience and had to be taught the basics, while the drummer taught himself.

  Mike Betts, who was taking Saturday morning guitar lessons, set up the group with friends.


They set about purchasing guitars, drums, amplifiers and a PA system - quite a challenge, as they were all apprentices, earning £8 to £10 a week. Robert bought a cheap drumkit and took lessons on Saturday mornings. Mike Betts had his own guitar, while Mick and Andy purchased secondhand guitars. Not able to afford a bass guitar, they took the strings off one of them, purchased four bass strings and converted it. With the equipment complete, Mike set about teaching the other two guitarists with sheet music from Fred Power’s bookshop in Kirkgate Market.

  Mick Higgins and John Anderson on stage .


The next item they needed was a cheap amplifier with a couple of speakers. They practised at Mike’s house but amplification was a problem, and his mum didn’t always go out! As two of the lads were in the Boys Brigade at Idle Baptist Church, the deacon let them practise in the Sunday School, which had a stage. Progress was slow, but each week they improved.

  An advert for Moore's on North Parade. 

*(Image: When Bradford Rocked)*

Soon they could play songs by Beatles, Rolling Stones, Hollies and Shadows instrumentals. It wasn’t enthusiasm letting them down but equipment, so they decided to pool their money and purchase two amplifiers from Harmony House, one used by the bassist and the other by lead and rhythm guitarist. Complementing these were two Goodman’s speakers. Putting down a deposit and buying on hire purchase was a massive undertaking.

  Drummer Robert Castle in action


On the day they got all this equipment they took it to the Sunday School. They were now a new group with a very different sound - it had been worth the many months of practice.

After purchasing another amplifier, it was time to show off their repertoire. The Tradesmen’s first booking was a youth club dance at St Luke’s Church on Barkerend Road early 1963. It was a thrill for the lads when they were well received and had their first payment of £20!

They were later asked to play at their youth club at Idle Baptist Church - for nothing, as they used the Sunday School for practice. They agreed it would be a good venue, especially as the audience would contain many of their friends. They went down well and two 21st birthday bookings followed, including one at Idle Working Men’s Club.

When not practising or playing The Tradesmen went around pubs and clubs listening to other groups. At Greengates Social Club, known as the ‘Red Den’, they had their first top booking. The concert secretary, in a broad Yorkshire accent introduced them: ‘I would like you to give a big hand to our special group of the night as they are all local lads living in Idle - let’s hear it for The Tradesmen.’ They were nervous, but after a few rocking numbers and applause they were a hit. They were on their way, especially as the concert secretary gave them another booking and advised them to contact other club secretaries. Soon they were playing Saturday evenings and lunchtimes. A friend borrowed his brother’s Austin A55 van and later a Bedford Workabus with seats and sliding doors.

The Tradesmen were starting to be noticed, leading them to invest in new outfits, which a friend offered to make, payable monthly. The new attire consisted of black dinner suits. It was also time to upgrade their instruments; Robert went back to Harmony House in Bradford to trade in his old kit for an upmarket Rodgers drumkit and top of the range Zym cymbals. The guitarists went to Kitchens of Leeds for a part exchange on their guitars. Mick purchased a Fender Solid bass, John a Gibson Semi-Acoustic and Mike a Gretsch Chet Atkins Tennessean semi-acoustic with a Bigsby Tremolo arm. Mike’s guitar cost around £178, a fantastic sum in 1965, although guitar cases, extra strings and plectrums were given to them.

The Tradesmen filled their repertoire with numbers of The Beatles, Billy J Kramer, The Shadows, Bob Dylan, Donovan, The Hollies, Joe Brown and others. Now established on the club circuit, they earned enough to pay for suits and instruments, with a little left over. Clubs they performed at included Laisterdyke, East Ward Labour Club and Woodend Club in Windhill. At the Red Den they were first choice for 21st birthday parties. One November 5, they played a Bonfire Night gig at Bolton Villas Cricket Club in Wrose, on the edge of the cricket pitch, with extension leads from the clubhouse supplying the power. About this time the Top Twenty Club opened in Idle, promoted by Frank Thorpe of Thorpe’s IceCream. It was popular, with Garth Cawood at the helm. Frank let the group practise there and gave them bookings. In 1966 the mother of one member wrote to Hughie Green, suggesting The Tradesmen for TV’s Opportunity Knocks. The group forgot all about it and continued to play, but girlfriends were on the scene and other priorities took over. Eventually they realised they’d never make it big and in mid-1966 they closed the book on The Tradesmen, selling their equipment back to the shops. Three months later a letter arrived, asking if the group could audition at The Gaumont for Opportunity Knocks. It was too late: The Tradesmen were no longer available. Much of what happened to them happened in some way to other local groups. Few who saw them would have any idea of the effort it took to put them on stage, smartly dressed, performing well, all smiling and hoping.
