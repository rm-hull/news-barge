In a bit more than the five years I lived in Berlin I moved five times.

From a seven person shared flat way out in the eastern suburb of Biesdorf, to a 'student' studio with a broken stove near Hermannstrasse, to an architect’s flat down the street – where I succeeded in lowering the rent by invoking the rent price brake – to a very short-term stay on Sonnenalle, to my final sublet in Wedding where I fell in love with Sprengelkiez; I have fond memories from each of my various Berlin homes, but with each move the lack of permanence and security in my housing situation grew harder to bear.

The last move in particular was hell. I spent three months submitting hundreds of applications and visiting dozens of flat viewings, and ultimately I failed to land an unlimited contract offer on an apartment of my own. (I'll tell you how it ended below.)

It was only after the clock ran out on my last search that I decided to write an article on ‘Expert tips for finding an apartment in Berlin’. In my research for that article I spoke with flat finding professionals, who had literally made finding apartments in Berlin their business, and some of their insights were quite useful.

So for anyone currently going through the hell that is apartment hunting in one of Germany's tightest housing markets, here are the best tips I picked up, so you don’t have to learn them the hard way.

Note that I've written this specifically about Berlin, but these tips can be applied to flat hunting anywhere in Germany.

**Define your search and stick to it**

Given the limited number of flats, and the overwhelming amount of competition for them, apartment hunting can quickly feel like a numbers game.

During my search I felt like I needed to send 10 applications to get a viewing, and I (wrongly) assumed that the more viewings I went to, the better my chances of being offered a contract.

This amounted to checking listings on ImmoScout 24 and similar platforms several times a day, spending hours writing messages and filling out applications and then more hours running around town trying to attend every listing I was invited to.

I was taking my lunch breaks at odd times of day to ride my bike across town to a viewing and then hurrying back, sometimes carving out time before or after my work to do the same.

For one viewing I even took a ferry to Kladow, on the far side of Wannsee.

All of this proved to be wasted effort. None of the listings I liked worked out, whereas the few offers I got were for places that were far away, too small and too expensive for what they were.

When I spoke to Graham Pugh, founder of BerlinRelo, about the best tactic for landing a rental in Berlin I was surprised that he recommended quite the opposite approach.

Pugh told me that he often finds himself trying to convince clients *not* to go to viewings, that is viewings for apartments that don’t fit what they’re looking for.

Set a clear target for yourself in terms of size, number of rooms, price etc and then really focus your efforts on the options that fit what you're seeking.

That’s not to say you can afford to be too picky. If your goal is an affordable *Alt-bau* in Prenzlauer Berg, you’ll probably need to check your expectations.

But if your target is a two-room flat somewhere close to the city centre, then don’t bother looking at a studio in Spandau. It’s a waste of time and you’ll quickly burn out.

By limiting the number of viewings you plan to attend you can afford to spend a bit more time preparing.

Regarding preparation, the pro-tip here is to find ways to make an impression and present yourself as an ideal candidate to the landlord. This could be anything from asking the right questions to making the landlord laugh to showing up with your application in a bright pink folder to make an impression.

Pugh suggests thinking deeply on the question, "Do I make sense as the person most likely to rent this place long term?"

**Money or time: Be prepared to spend what you have**

Another insightful tip of Pugh's: Finding the right apartment at the right price requires either money, time or luck – and usually some combination of all three. The more you have of one of any of these, the less you need to rely on the others.

So my tip to current flat hunters is if you can afford to invest a bit in your flat search, do it! It’ll save you time, make your life easier and increase your chances of finding the right place for you.

The minimum here would be shelling out for a ‘Plus’ account on ImmoScout 24. It's an annoying service to have to pay for, but it makes applying to listings much faster and easier.

Going further, you might hire services which apply directly to listings for you, such as Get the Flat, or even more involved placement services like BerlinRelo, which work with you directly through the entire house hunting process.

Given my own budget at the time, I stubbornly refused to pay money for help with the process. Instead I ended up paying pretty significantly in time.

People say flat hunting in Berlin is a full-time job, and that's not really an exaggeration.

So if you already put around 40 hours a week into work, it's probably worth looking into paying for a service that can reduce some of the hours for your house hunt.

This one genuinely surprised me, but the experts I spoke to confirm that some landlords will try to avoid people from certain professions.

Of course having a job – especially one with an unlimited contract – is a huge plus as a potential tenant. But landlords do take your profession into account when considering your application.

Kevin Hiller, founder of Get the Flat, told me that private landlords “are actually interested in who this person is that's going to be renting their property.” He added that about half of the apartments in Germany are rented out by private landlords.

Pugh confirmed to me that sharing the fact I work as a journalist could actually be hurting my chances.

The reason being landlords want a tenant who's going to move in, pay the rent on time and not create any problems.

Architects, lawyers and journalists are perhaps more likely to cause ‘problems’ for landlords. An architect might be expected to try and renovate a flat. A lawyer or journalist might be more likely to challenge aspects of a rental contract.

(It’s not an entirely wrong assumption. As mentioned above, my own reporting on the rent brake did lead me to challenge my own rent and have it lowered on one occasion.)

Of course you shouldn't lie about who you are on your application, but it may help to not emphasise your job title in these cases.

For example, if I were to apply for a flat now, rather than writing ‘Journalist’ for my occupation, I might instead go with ‘Writer’ or ‘Editor’. In conversation with a potential landlord I might emphasise the cultural topics I cover, and definitely not mention my reporting on illegally high rent prices in Berlin.

**Get out and talk to people**

When time gets tight and the flat search is stressful, some people tend to skip social events and stop seeing friends, thinking, ‘I just need to focus on this until it's over’.

But in my experience this is exactly the opposite of what you should do.

In fact I’d suggest that putting yourself out there, meeting new people and talking about the fact that you’re desperately looking for an apartment, is just about the only way you can actively increase the luck factor in your search.

This is exactly how my own flat search ended. I had given myself three months to find an unlimited contract apartment before my sublet ran out, and was within days of my deadline with no suitable leads.

To relieve some stress I went to a running club meet-up with a friend, and while discussing my trials and tribulations another runner chimed in that they needed to quickly find someone to take over their flat.

It wasn’t the unfurnished, unlimited contract flat that I had been seeking exactly. But it would shelter me for another year in Berlin, and ended up being quite a nice fit.
