Anyone who has lived in Germany for a while has probably encountered the famous “cash only” (*Nur Barzahlung*) sign.

Whether buying a coffee here, paying for a haircut or grabbing something from a neighbourhood shop, it’s surprising how often a lack of cash can lead to a wasted trip.

That could change as early as next year following news that the federal Finance Ministry – led by SPD Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil – is preparing legislation that would require businesses to offer at least one digital payment option.

The proposal is currently being coordinated within the government and is expected to be presented to the cabinet before the end of the year.

The stated aim is not to replace cash, but to ensure consumers have a choice. Customers should be able to decide whether they want to pay with notes and coins or digitally using a card, smartphone or smartwatch.

**What would change?**

If the plans become law, retailers, restaurants and service providers would have to accept at least one commonly used digital payment method whenever customers pay on site.

Businesses would be free to choose which payment systems they offer, although at least one European payment option would need to be accepted. There would be no minimum purchase amount for card payments and no extra fee for using a card.

That means buying a €2 coffee should be just as easy with a card as paying for a larger purchase.

Euro notes and coins would continue to be legal tender – and the government has repeatedly stressed that the proposal is about expanding options rather than limiting them.

**Why has this taken so long?**

For many foreign residents, Germany’s attachment to cash can come as a surprise. While countries such as Sweden have embraced cashless payments for even the smallest purchases, Germany has long remained one of Europe’s most cash-oriented societies.

Historically, many Germans have valued cash for privacy, budgeting and security reasons. The country also developed its own debit card culture through the Girocard system, often still referred to as the “EC card”.

Even so, payment habits are changing rapidly, with supporters of the proposal arguing that digital payments are now an everyday expectation for many consumers.

The Finance Ministry has also cited the potential benefits for tackling tax evasion, money laundering and other financial crimes, since electronic payments leave a traceable record. But not everyone is convinced, with retail groups warning that card terminals, transaction fees and administrative requirements could create additional costs for smaller businesses.

For now, the proposal still has to pass through the legislative process. Assuming it’s approved, Germany’s familiar “cash only” signs could soon become a thing of the past.
