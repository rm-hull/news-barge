Andy Burnham will not take part in today’s crucial vote on assisted dying as the future of the controversial legislation rests on a knife edge.

MPs are preparing to once again debate and vote on the law after it previously cleared two Commons votes before running out of time in the Lords in April, leading supporters to denounce a "denial of democracy" over hundreds of amendments.

The Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill will be reintroduced in the Commons on Friday, giving MPs a second chance to consider whether the legislation should continue its journey through parliament.

The proposed legislation puts forward a change to the law allowing adults in England and Wales, with fewer than six months to live, to apply for an assisted death subject to the approval of two doctors and an expert panel.

Last year, the bill was passed by a majority of just 23 votes, but it is likely Friday’s vote will be even closer.

![The bill will be reintroduced in the Commons on Friday](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/10/15/01KZJN3HHRD5F4BBZQ6AAKXS0P.jpg)

Since the last vote, two MPs who voted in favour of the bill – Sir Keir Starmer and Josh Simons – are no longer in Parliament.

Sir Keir is yet to be replaced and Mr Burnham, who replaced Mr Simons, will not be present for the vote.

This means it will take just 12 MPs to change their minds for the bill to fall.

A bigger issue will likely be convincing MPs to remain in Westminster to attend the vote at all.

Parliament does not usually sit on a Friday, and with recess starting again next week, many MPs may have already headed back to their constituencies.

The bill remains highly controversial, with the government remaining neutral and allowing MPs to vote according to their consciences, rather than along party lines.

Though the new prime minister will not be voting to avoid “unduly influencing the debate”, he said in July that he believes the funding of palliative care needs to be fixed before consideration can be given to legalising assisted dying.

![The new prime minister will not be voting to avoid ‘unduly influencing the debate’](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/10/15/252c1f67c39bc2b0a02b4c55e4e4a29bY29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzg5MTM1OTE1-2.85735890.jpg)

Those in favour of assisted dying, where it is legal for a doctor to prescribe life-ending medication to a consenting terminally ill adult, argue the changes mark a “long overdue reform”.

They have also highlighted what they called a “denial of democracy” in the upper chamber, as peers face accusations of blocking the bill despite it being passed by the Commons.

Some have suggested the Parliament Act – a rarely used piece of legislation which allows for bills that have been backed by the Commons in two successive sessions but rejected by peers to pass into law without Lords approval – could be used to get the bill through.

Friday’s debate will begin at 9:30am before a vote takes place at no later than 2:30pm.

Labour MP Lauren Edwards has reintroduced the bill, which was originally brought forward by Kim Leadbeater last year.

She said people were “quite rightly frustrated by the process not being seen to conclusion in the last parliament” and that she “felt a responsibility to those people who really wanted this legislation to be brought in”.

![MP Lauren Edwards is bringing the Bill back to Parliament (Aaron Chown/PA)](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/07/00/5c21f8649c21713a989675d5ae8bffe4Y29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzg4NjEwMzgz-2.85735912.jpg)

She has appealed to her colleagues in the Commons to vote for the bill “so that we can respect the will of the chamber and we can have it go through the usual processes in the House of Lords, just like any other form of legislation”.

Those in opposition to the legislation have raised “significant concerns” about protections for the vulnerable against coercion and access to high-quality end of life care.

Labour MP Preet Kaur Gill wrote earlier this week that MPs have a “responsibility to reconsider previous devisions when new evidence comes to light”, as she insisted it would not be wrong for her colleagues who previously voted in favour of the bill to change their minds.

“Parliamentary scrutiny has inarguably exposed unresolved problems in the Bill since the last time we were called upon to vote,” she wrote in an article for Labour List. “This should be of significant concern to members across the House, irrespective of our party affiliation or position on the principle of assisted dying.”

Meanwhile, Labour MP Ashley Dalton, who has terminal cancer, said the bill is “unsafe and unworkable” and argued that palliative care should be fixed first.

![Opponents and supporters of assisted dying have demonstrated outside Parliament on many occasions during the debate (Stefan Rousseau/PA)](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/04/24/15/7f795367eec3648d78364c3bee7f31f9Y29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzc3MTI0NjU3-2.78335510.jpg)

Meanwhile, Tory leader Kemi Badenoch said she would again vote against the bill, arguing scrutiny in the House of Lords should “not be dismissed as obstruction”.

New polling shows 70 per cent of Britons believe parliament should prioritise sorting out palliative, social and end-of-life care before considering whether to introduce assisted dying.

The survey, carried out by Whitestone Insight for think-tank The Other Half, also found that 78 per cent agree MPs who supported the previous bill should judge the current bill on the evidence now available, rather than feel bound by how they voted before.

New analysis of government figures has also suggested four people a week could die an an assisted death in which money played a part if the bill became law.

The bill's impact assessment raised the central projection of assisted deaths by 60 per cent, to 3,502 a year within a decade of the law taking effect.

The document also cites data from US state Oregon, which shows that "financial implications of treatment" featured among the recorded reasons in 6 per cent of assisted deaths.

Commenting on the figures, Labour MP Naz Shah said: “We would be legislating for assisted deaths in a country where you can wait weeks for a care package and a long illness can still cost a family their home.

“In those circumstances, some people will choose to die for reasons that have nothing to do with medicine. International numbers tell us that happens every day.”

## What happens next?

If MPs vote the bill through on Friday, it will be subject to further debate, amendments and they will have another vote on the Bill as a whole at what is known as Third Reading, before passage to the Lords for scrutiny and then back to the Commons.

While it passed two previous votes in the Commons, it ran out of time in the Lords in April after peers tabled hundreds of amendments and effectively talking the bill out.

While legislation has to pass both Houses to become law, supporters have previously suggested a rarely used piece of legislation could be invoked to pass the bill this time around if it is stalled again in the Lords.

The Parliament Acts allow for bills that have been backed by the Commons in two successive sessions, but rejected by peers, to pass into law without Lords approval.

If the bill is not passed today however, it will not pass through to the Lords and it’s passage into law will effectively be stopped.
