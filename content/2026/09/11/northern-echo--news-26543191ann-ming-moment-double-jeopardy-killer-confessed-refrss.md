Ann Ming still remembers the last time she saw her daughter Julie Hogg alive.

It was around 2.30pm on November 15, 1989. Ann had gone to Julie’s home on Grange Avenue, in Billingham, to pick up her grandson Kevin so she could go to work that night.

It's a sequence of events that Ann, now 80, relives, ending with a promise to wake Julie up at 7.30am the next morning.

But it was a call that was never answered.

“I walked away from there with Kevin and little did I know that would be the last time I'd ever see her alive,” Ann says.

After racing to her home, she found Julie had vanished.

It would be an agonising 80 days before Ann would make the tragic discovery of her daughter's body, stuffed behind a bath panel in her own home.

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

This was the start of tireless 17-year campaign to bring Julie’s killer to justice - and it was 20 years ago today (September 11) that her murderer, Billy Dunlop, stood at The Old Bailey in London and confessed to the killing.

Dunlop, a former labourer, had been tried twice for the murder but was acquitted - and the 800-year-old double jeopardy law meant he could not be tried twice for the same offence.

   

*(Image: North News and Pictures)*

But Ann’s determination, backed by The Northern Echo, saw her take her fight to the country’s corridors of power, demanding change. And change is what she got.

“I can remember that day, so it was yesterday,” Ann said, speaking to the Echo at the home of her grandson Kevin, 40, on Friday.

“I can't tell you what it felt like, 17 years after he killed Julie, to admit that he killed her and saying guilty in The Old Bailey.

“I was just crying all the time because I kept thinking we'd fought so long to get the double jeopardy law changed.

  Ann Ming 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

“We'd finally got a conviction, so he wasn't going to be out in the streets bragging he'd killed her and got away with the perfect murder.

“But at the end of the day, she's never coming back, thanks to him.”

## 
  **How ‘ordinary’ Ann made legal history**

Ann describes herself as an ordinary, northern, working class woman.

She’s far from it.

Across 17 years, she tirelessly fought for Julie, 22, through her Justice for Julie campaign, and eventually went on to take on the law and win.

  Ann Ming during a press conference at Snow Hill police station, London on the day Billy Dunlop admitted that he killed Julie Hogg 

*(Image: PA)*

But before the victory, the family had to endure agonising pain as brutal killer Dunlop, who faced two trials in 1991 for Julie’s murder, was formally acquitted.

“We thought he wouldn't do anything else,” Ann said. “But he was bragging within weeks.

“He had an acquittal party in a local pub, the police came to tell us about that. And then he was bragging in pubs and whoever he could talk to.

“I thought he wouldn't do anything else because he got away by the skin of his teeth. But no, you're talking about the mind of a psychopath.”

He laughed at the legal system once again by telling a prison officer that he had indeed killed Julie, while he was in jail for a separate offence.

At the time, he was right, he could not be jailed for her murder - but was instead charged with perjury and jailed for a further six years.

But it wasn’t enough for Ann.

   

*(Image: (c) NORTH NEWS & PICTURES)*

Armed with a petition signed by hundreds of Echo readers, Ann went to London in 2000 to meet then Home Secretary Jack Straw to demand change.

The campaign was also backed by the parents of Stephen Lawrence and Maureen Harvey, whose son, Lee, was stabbed to death by his girlfriend, Tracie Andrews, and MPs.

“I was pleased that the government listened to me and took it all on board and every step of the way I went,” she said. “[But] some politicians opposed it.

“I tried to get in touch with them to see why they were opposing it. I couldn't see what the problem was.”

In July 2002, the then-Home Secretary David Blunkett announced a White Paper which revealed plans to abandon the double jeopardy rule.

But the bill had to then move through the House of Commons and House of Lords.

“People think I had a team of lawyers but I was a one-man band,” she said.

“Obviously I had the support of my family and especially Julie's son.

  The house in Grange Avenue, Billingham, Teesside, where Julie Hogg was murdered by Billy Dunlop 

*(Image: PA)*

“There were times I had really bad days when I used to sit and cry.

"Even my solicitor had said to me in the beginning, they will never ever change a law, you're wasting your time and it's going to make you ill.

"But I never gave up.”

Ann’s courage and hard work saw the 2003 Criminal Justice Act finally introduce the change in law, permitting a second trial if compelling evidence became available indicating guilt.

And in October 2006, Ann Ming watched as Dunlop received a life sentence, with a minimum tariff of 17 years, for murdering her daughter

## The fight continues

Dunlop has been in jail ever since, but Ann and Kevin, who was just three at the time of his mother's murder, have had to endure numerous parole hearings in recent years.

The most recent hearing saw then-Justice Secretary Shabana Mahmood block a parole board recommendation that he be moved to an open prison.

  Ann Ming with her grandson Kevin Hogg 

*(Image: ANDY FUTERS)*

Dunlop's next hearing is scheduled for October 21.

“I was hoping he would die within the 17 years, to be quite honest, because we knew that after the 17 years he'd be eligible to apply for parole,” Ann said.

Ann said the pain is something you are "living and breathing all the time" - and is now fearing what the parole board will recommend next month.

“They're going to look at it and think he's all right to come out," she said.

“What about public protection? That's supposed to be at the forefront of everything to do with victims. Would you like him living next door to you?

“I don't see any reason to bring him out and put people at risk.”

But Ann said that all of the years of fighting were worth it as there have now been 22 double jeopardy retrials and cases since the law was reformed.

“Every time I see one come at The Old Bailey, I think, thank God you carried on Ann,” she said.

“It doesn't mean to say they're all going to get a chance of a retrial, but there was no chance at all before. But now there's a chance for some people.

“It's the difference between living in a state of limbo for the rest of your life or having some form of closure.”
