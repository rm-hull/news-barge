IT was 25 years ago today that terrorist attacks devastated America and changed the world forever.

Four passenger planes were hijacked by al-Qaida terrorists; two of them flew into the World Trade Center in New York, one hit the Pentagon outside Washington and a fourth crashed into a field in Pennsylvania. A total of 2,977 people, 67 of them British, were killed.

As events take place today to commemorate the 25th anniversary of the attacks on September 11, 2001, Bradford people have been reflecting on a day they will never forget.

Diane Duguid left Bradford nearly 60 years ago to live in New York with her American husband George. On the morning of September 11, 2001 Diane was at home in Long Island, watching the terrible events unfold. Her husband and two sons were officers with the New York Police Department.

It was mere chance that they weren’t at the tragic site that day. George narrowly avoided flying into New York from South Carolina, where he was picking up a prisoner. Oldest son, George, a detective, was due in court in Lower Manhattan, near the Twin Towers, but the time was changed, otherwise he’d have been one of the first at the scene. His brother Andrew was assigned to his Queens County precinct.

In the aftermath of the towers collapsing, George (junior) was recovering and trying to identify bodies. He also helped with the massive clear-up operation at Ground Zero and at Staten Island, where the debris was later taken.

In 2002 Diane and her husband presented the T&A with a plaque featuring special badges produced by the New York Police Department and Fire Departments to commemorate officers in the 911 tragedy. Diane at the time: “I thank God my family was safe but everyone in New York knows someone who lost loved ones.”

Reflecting on that September day, 25 years on, Diane said: “Even now, reading stories is so heartbreaking. In our local paper today are stories from people who were born on that day. Two men from our town, one a firefighter and one a police officer, were never found. Their father went every day to Ground Zero until he died.

“The first person to die helping console people at the scene was Father Judge, who came from our town of Deer Park. So many funerals and so many without loved ones to bury.”

Vicki Garwood of Denholme flew to New York to help recover bodies from the site. She spent four weeks as a volunteer with the American Red Cross, working 12-hours shifts in the still burning wreckage. She told the T&A: “It was like a big graveyard. We were encouraged to talk to firemen about things like football, to take their minds off it.”

  Firefighters in the World Trade Center rubble on September 11, 2001. Alamy


Ex-army driver Vicki transported rubble from the site to warehouses. “What really got me was the last day when I looked around Ground Zero in daylight. I just stood and cried,” she said.

Anna Pratt of Silsden was on the New York underground when the train suddenly stopped. Anna was 19 and in Manhattan sightseeing with her boyfriend. They were heading for lower Manhattan when both towers were hit. “The train lost power and halted. We were at a station two blocks from the World Trade Center,” said Anna. “It started to fill with white smoke, we were told to get to the back. I wondered how the hell we were going to get out. The doors wouldn’t open and people were beginning to panic. Finally a fireman came and opened them, there was dust and debris everywhere. They told us to keep walking north.”

Suddenly police told them to run down a side street. “The tower was on fire, you could see people at the windows trying to escape. People were screaming and injured on the floor.”

Former T&A news editor Martin Heminway was on honeymoon in New York on September 11. He and his wife were queuing in the Empire State Building for tickets when the planes hit the Twin Towers. “A gaggle of security guards began hovering, taking messages on their walkie talkies,” recalled Martin. “At about 9.10am a senior officer told everyone there had been a major incident down town and the Observatory wouldn’t be opening. An increasing air of panic took over as people hurried up the escalator into the foyer at street level.

“Out on the streets, crowds were running down Fifth Avenue, a huge column of smoke had enveloped the World Trade Center in the distance. A passing policeman said a plane had crashed into the building. The first howls of fire and ambulance sirens filled the streets as convoys of emergency vehicles raced to the scene. Queues of people were forming at every phonebox as people phoned friends and relatives who worked at the World Trade Center.

“News quickly spread through the crowd that two planes, not one, had crashed into the towers, and that they may have been deliberately flown into them.”

Michael Quain of Keighley was working in a hotel room five miles from the World Trade Center early that morning. “I took a break to watch the start of a beautiful clear blue day,” he recalled. “From my sixth floor window I could see the Manhattan skyline with the Empire State Building on my right and Twin Towers on my left. I set to work and three hours later stood up and looked out. There was thick black smoke. I switched on the TV. It was like watching Towering Inferno, only this was real. I called my wife, as we were talking I looked out of the window and said: ‘Oh my God, one of them is falling down. There will be thousands dead’.”
