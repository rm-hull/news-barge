THE “I Love NY” T-shirts were faded and frayed, the handwritten messages scrawled across them long smudged.

Laminated letters read of grief and despair, some still clinging to hope, and the faces of the beloved – sons, sisters, husbands, mothers, and FDNY’s finest – gazed out from sun-bleached photographs fluttering in the breeze.

Leaning in to look at this collage of loss – railings covered in cards, banners, candles, trinkets and US flags – just a few yards from where the Twin Towers had come down, I felt like an intruder.

Six months after the September 11 attacks or, as we came to know that day, 911, Ground Zero had become a bleak tourist attraction.

Someone was selling Twin Towers baseball caps as I joined a crowd shuffling along the railings where loved ones had pinned messages and tributes to those who went to work that day and never came home.

You could pre-book tickets for a viewing platform overlooking the debris.

I nearly didn’t go.

The sense of grief and shock was still very raw in New York and it felt horribly intrusive to head down to Lower Manhattan and gawp at the site where more than 2,700 people had lost their lives.

But, despite the horror and grief of that place, it was somewhere that New Yorkers urged us to visit.

In a city where just about every window had a stars & stripes flags hanging from it, Ground Zero was regarded as a place to pay your respects, and to remember.

Today is the 25th anniversary of the September 11 attacks. I’d first visited New York in late spring 2001, a few months before the attacks.

I remember looking up at the Twin Towers that dominated the World Trade Center and being in awe of the size of them.

In a city full of skyscrapers, they were giants, rising so high you could barely see where they ended. I’d never encountered anything quite like those towers.

When I returned to New York the following year, the towers were gone. I felt the city’s sadness, and its defiance.

The bars were lively, Broadway was twinkling, tourist shops were filled with commemorative keepsakes, from Twin Towers fridge magnets to glossy 911 coffee table books.

Manhattan’s financial district was bustling; sharp-suited city boys hailing cabs and grabbing lunch from street delis.

But walking towards Ground Zero, where I’d last looked up at the World Trade Center stretching into the sky, it was eerily quiet.

The only noise was the clanking of cranes from the massive clear-up. Twisted steel girders were all that remained of the mighty towers.

People were gathered around a railing that had started off as a kind of noticeboard and quickly became a shrine.

It was covered in handwritten letters, cards and appeals for information.

“This is our son, he worked in the South Tower” was one of the desperate messages, hastily pinned up in the days after the attacks.

The South Tower was the second to be hit and, less than an hour later, the first to collapse.

There were more recent messages too. A photo of a smiling middle-aged woman was pinned to a birthday card from her brother. “I think of you every day,” he’d written.

And everywhere we looked were tributes to New York’s police and fire service.

Particularly poignant were the pictures drawn by schoolchildren.

Sitting quietly on a bench, among the tourists, was a burly cop who looked like he’d stepped off the set of Hill Street Blues. I wondered what he’d witnessed here that September morning.

As we left, the street sellers were flogging badges, caps and T-shirts. It felt oddly appropriate. New York is built on graft.

In that makeshift shrine, where time had stood still and sorrow was all around us, a bunch of guys making a fast buck seemed to be a sign that life in the city was going on, just as it always had.
