TWO Bradford MPs have spoken out after Parliament voted against legalising assisted dying.

During a more than four-hour debate in the House of Commons on Friday, MPs rejected the Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill by a narrow majority of 16 votes, with 286 against and 270 in favour.

The Bill would have allowed adults in England and Wales with fewer than six months to live to apply for an assisted death, subject to the approval of two doctors and an expert panel.

Imran Hussain, Labour MP for Bradford East, said: "I’m glad Parliament has rejected this dangerous and deeply flawed Assisted Dying Bill.

"I voted against it because it is wrong in both principle and practice, and I could not support legislation that risks putting vulnerable people in danger.

"We should be investing properly in palliative care instead."

Shipley MP Anna Dixon said: "I voted against this Bill today, and I'm glad the House has decided not to proceed with it in its current form.

"I did so because I believed that this Bill was not safe or workable for the NHS and for patients.

"We must first reform and invest in social care, palliative care, and end-of-life care.

"I was particularly concerned by the powers this Bill would have handed to future ministers to reshape assisted dying without full Parliamentary scrutiny, and by a four-year implementation deadline with no readiness check for a health service already under significant strain.

"This does not mean the underlying questions go away, and I recognise how strongly people on all sides feel about this issue.

"I have consistently said that investment in palliative and social care must come before any change of this magnitude, and with an estimated 170,000 people a year still dying without access to the palliative care they need, that must now be the priority.

"I will continue to press the Government to invest fully in palliative and end-of-life care, so that everyone facing a terminal diagnosis has access to good care and genuine support, whatever the future of this debate."

Bradford West MP Naz Shah and Keighley and Ilkley MP Robbie Moore also voted against today's Bill.

Andy Burnham had previously said he would not vote on the Bill to avoid "unduly influence the debate."

The Government took a neutral stance, allowing MPs to vote according to their consciences rather than party lines.

Opponents of the Bill raised concerns about inadequate safeguards and the potential for coercion, warning that vulnerable people might choose assisted death because they feel burdensome to others.

Archbishop Richard Moth, leader of the Catholic Church in England and Wales, described the Bill as "wrong in principle" and "deeply flawed."

Dame Sarah Mullally, Archbishop of Canterbury, also voiced strong opposition.

Medical colleges, including the Royal College of Psychiatrists, raised "serious concerns", saying there were "too many unanswered questions about the safeguarding of people with mental illness."

Mr Burnham has previously said that funding for palliative care must be addressed before any consideration is given to legalising assisted dying.

However, Bill sponsor Lauren Edwards, who re-introduced the Bill, argued that both issues could be addressed together.

## MP Kim Leadbeater introduced the first Bill

Batley and Spen Labour MP Kim Leadbeater introduced an initial Bill on assisted dying in October 2024.

Speaking about the issue in the House of Commons in May 2025, Ms Leadbetter said: "Put simply, if we do not vote to change the law, we are essentially saying that the status quo is acceptable.

"Over recent months, I have heard hundreds of stories from people who have lost loved ones in deeply difficult and traumatic circumstances, which show that that is clearly not the case.

"Too many have seen their terminally ill loved ones take their own lives out of desperation, or make the traumatic, lonely and costly trip to Switzerland, and then face a police investigation while dealing with their grief and loss."

The October 2024 debate was the first time the topic has been debated in the House of Commons since 2015, when an assisted dying bill was defeated.

## Charities remain neutral

Marie Curie, Hospice UK, and St Christopher’s Hospice remain neutral on the principle of assisted dying.

The charities have repeatedly warned that end-of-life care is inconsistent and underfunded across the UK.

They said: "The need for more urgent and ambitious action to fix end-of-life care could not be clearer."

Demonstrators for and against the Bill gathered outside Parliament during the debate.

Ms Edwards, who reintroduced the Bill after it ran out of time earlier this year, warned that the issue could take another decade to return to Parliament.

The Bill had previously passed two Commons votes but ran out of time in the Lords in April, after peers tabled hundreds of amendments.
