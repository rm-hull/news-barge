Money raised from a proposed visitor levy in York “must stay in York”, an MP in the city has said.

Rachael Maskell’s call comes after the county’s Labour mayor, David Skaith, told The Press that any money raised in York from the levy, dubbed a “tourist tax”, would go into a “wider pot for York and North Yorkshire” to be distributed across the county.

  It follows a report by York St John University, commissioned by the mayor, finding that a £1-a-night visitor levy on overnight stays in York and North Yorkshire **could raise up to £26 million-a-year.**

Ms Maskell, Labour MP for York Central, said “small levies on overnight stays on hotels and Airbnb-type accommodation would raise significant income for York” but added: “I have made the case that revenue raised should come direct to York, with a proportion spent to improve our city's infrastructure, to benefit residents and tourists and half should go to local community projects in our city. Money raised in York must stay in York."

She also called for children to be exempt from the levy, as well as “camping and hostel accommodation”.

  Rachael Maskell, MP for York Central 

*(Image: UK Parliament/PA Wire)*

The MP added that “for sustainable tourism, local residents need to see the benefits from guests staying in their city”.

City of York Council's leader, Cllr Claire Douglas, also said it is "right that York sees its fair share of income raised through the levy, reflecting its almost six million overnight stays in 2025".

“As a hugely popular visitor destination there are significant costs to York that can be mitigated with a visitor levy," the Labour council leader added.

Mr Skaith has ruled out money raised from the visitor levy in York being kept solely for the city, saying: “It will be a levy raised region-wide across York and North Yorkshire. And it will be spent region-wide across York and North Yorkshire.

“That’s the biggest opportunity we have to get the bigger investment and bigger opportunities we have across York and North Yorkshire.”

The tourist tax is being proposed after the government announced it would give mayors in England greater economic power in their regions.

Local leaders will set the fee as a percentage of the cost of accommodation, rather than a flat rate, which ministers argue will protect budget trips.

The levy will have no upper limit, though government sources said mayors are unlikely to make it too expensive, with most indicating it would be a few per cent.

Ministers plan to introduce legislation establishing the levy “in due course”, with mayors expected to set out their plans by March 2028.

  David Skaith, Mayor of York and North Yorkshire 

*(Image: Supplied)*

In York and North Yorkshire, Mr Skaith said the region’s combined authority, which he leads, has been working with industry bosses to determine what money from the levy would be spent on.

The mayor would not be drawn on specific projects where the money would be spent but did single out Parliament Street in York, saying: “We’ve known for years that’s needed investment to make sure that’s in a much better position.

“We’ve got the opportunity now to actually do that investment. Previously, we wouldn’t have been able to go anywhere near that project – it would have cost too much money.”

He also said the money could be used to improve public transport across York and North Yorkshire and to promote the area by staging more major events in the region, calling it a “huge opportunity".

Mr Skaith said it was too early to say how money raised from the levy would be distributed, but it would “probably be a combination” of it being given to businesses, events and local authorities.

Critics have argued that introducing the overnight levy could deter people from visiting York and North Yorkshire, but Mr Skaith said York St John University’s report had not found that not to be the case.

The report pointed to visitor growth remaining steady in places that have already implemented a levy on overnight stays, such as Edinburgh, Manchester, Paris and Barcelona.

Conservative MPs in North Yorkshire have opposed the visitor levy, saying it would hit people taking family holidays in the county and the “family businesses that depend on them, at a time when both are already facing significant cost pressures”.

But Mr Skaith said: “We’ve got an opportunity here to raise much-needed capital for the region.”

He added: “I’m happy to work with any politician or anyone in the region to make sure this works.”
