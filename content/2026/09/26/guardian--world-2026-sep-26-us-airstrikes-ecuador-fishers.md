While the US secretary of state, Marco Rubio, was being decorated in Quito by Ecuador’s president for “helping to promote peace in the region”, 10 Ecuadorian fishers whose boat had been blown up by US forces were adrift in the eastern Pacific.

![Two men put a metal on a third man in the center](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/f41609563a2b6b9472f7c3b63b26ce0151a27d7b/260_0_6091_4615/master/6091.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

On 8 September, a day before Rubio arrived, a US patrol vessel intercepted the fishing boat Don Rufo in international waters near Ecuador.

“The gringos were all armed as if they were going to war,” said the boat’s captain, Mero Calderón, 50. “I tried to show them our catch to prove that we were there working, but they wouldn’t listen,” he added.

![A still from a video of two boats in water](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/cd59b4ac6fe23c79d823bde85f93d305bd5c422b/216_143_2903_1933/master/2903.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

“They brought two huge bombs aboard, took us to their ship, handcuffed and blindfolded us, and blew our boat to pieces,” said Calderón, who along with five other fishers, was handed over to local authorities.

But another 10 crew members were fishing farther out to sea in a smaller boat that was not equipped to make it back to shore. The Ecuadorian navy only rescued them on 10 September, when Rubio was already in Peru preparing to return to the US.

![People sitting in a line, many of them wearing yellow rain boots](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/f2c02fbc6fdd652841e5c9c97d4825ba2cab6f38/59_1_1221_977/master/1221.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

US Southern Command posted an edited 14-second video of the operation and said “intelligence confirmed” that the vessel was being used by a gang as a “floating refuelling station” for speedboats allegedly carrying drugs – which the fishers deny.

It was one of eight operations involving Ecuadorian boats in just more than two weeks – the latest on 16 September – to follow the same pattern. Unlike the nearly 70 US airstrikes in the Pacific and Caribbean that have killed 234 people since last year, crews were removed before the vessels were sunk.

![A still from a video of a bigger and smaller boat in water](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/310ee8f9116a3ffefc3e3794594b37acc36776ca/51_24_3403_1945/master/3403.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

Also, rather than unilateral US attacks, the new wave of operations has the open support of Ecuador’s far-right president, Daniel Noboa, who is calling them “joint operations”.

Donald Trump has compared the drug trafficking operations to the campaign against the Islamic state. “Like ISIS, they should be killed, exiled or detained as enemy combatants without the possibility of release, which is what we’re doing,” Trump said on Tuesday.

Some analysts see the shift as an indication that the US’s military strategy against drug trafficking in Latin America has entered a new phase.

![A person lifts leg onto one boat from a smaller boat](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/bd5b092804cb66f473f72dcef2b7cdf9f2100d9b/104_0_1696_1200/master/1696.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

“Noboa has offered Ecuador as a ‘laboratory’ for the implementation of this new phase,” said Will Freeman, a fellow for Latin America studies at the Council on Foreign Relations.

Although Ecuador does not produce cocaine like neighbouring Colombia and Peru, over the past decade it has become a major trafficking hub because of its access to the Pacific, including the area where the attacks on boats have been taking place.

Noboa has responded with a controversial “iron-fist” approach to the gangs, issuing repeated states of emergency and expanding the role of the armed forces.

![People in the street, some with flowing yellow skirts trimmed in red, green and black](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/7b7e345402a7583ebf55a1ac1d8fb6fa9f849ac1/0_0_4528_3016/master/4528.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

This crackdown however has done little to reduce violence, and instead has eroded human rights. The most notable example of human rights abuse came last year in the case of the “Guayaquil Four”, the forced disappearance of four Afro-Ecuadorian boys between the age of 11 and 15. All four boys were found dead, there bodies charred and dismembered.

Despite pushback from voters, Noboa signed an agreement with the White House authorising joint operations and granting US military personnel involved in counter-narcotics operations immunity from criminal prosecution. The operations have also extended to land, with US troops taking part in raids last Thursday.

“Noboa is interested in the optics of a very lethal crackdown and in making Ecuador a place where the Trump administration can refine those methods and show them off,” said Freeman, who has also reported on the boat attacks in Ecuador for the New Yorker magazine.

![Two soldiers standing with guns and a man on his knees between them](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/cadf21cdcf19731d525de43e24e6727bbd1d3541/0_0_2200_1650/master/2200.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

Trump has pressured Latin American presidents to join an anti-drug cartel coalition he created called Shield of the Americas. With Peru and Colombia recently joining, the group now includes all but the few countries still governed by the left, such as Mexico and Brazil – where a far-right senator is a serious contender in October’s election.

So far, the coalition’s primary goal appears to have been increasing joint operations against alleged drug traffickers.

Adam Isacson, director for defence oversight at the Washington Office on Latin America, expects joint operations to expand across the region.

![People on a rowboat in water](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/4a99ebe3d5dabf682979ccac463de9000ec4d11b/0_0_6500_4335/master/6500.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

Isacson stressed that though more people are being turned over to local authorities instead of killed, the US is “still doing the bad old stuff”. On 19 September, four people were killed in the Caribbean. As with the other airstrikes, the US has not disclosed their identities or any of the alleged evidence linking them to criminal activity.

During his trip to Ecuador, Rubio said the decision on what approach to take was “a strike-by-strike or operation-by-operation determination” that depended on the threat and location of the boat, but that the US would “still blow up ships if need be”.

Human rights organisations have described the airstrikes as extrajudicial killings, and a special rapporteur for the UN human rights council has concluded that there are “reasonable grounds to believe” that they constitute “crimes against humanity”.

Despite Trump’s claims, available data show they have had no impact on the volume of cocaine entering the country.

Journalists and human rights groups have managed to identify some of the victims, who in many cases were innocent or, when suspected of involvement in criminal activity, poor people at the bottom of trafficking networks.

![People near wood fencing](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/87555e96befeefb072585b7f85845c7e5cdcbcdf/0_203_3431_2453/master/3431.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

In Ecuador, the local NGO Permanent Committee for the Defence of Human Rights estimates that more than 220 fishers have been subjected to “violent boardings, arbitrary detentions, enforced disappearances, torture and the destruction of vessels” in this year’s US operations.

None of the fishers remains in custody and, while some are still facing prosecution, many have already been acquitted for lack of evidence.

“Fishers are losing their livelihoods when the US destroys the boats. Besides, if the US says the vessels were being used to refuel drug boats, why destroy the evidence?” said the committee’s lawyer, Fernando Bastías.

A US Southern Command spokesperson said it conducts operations “in compliance with US and international law” and that leaving boats “intact could permit their reuse by criminal networks”.

Although the US and Ecuador have celebrated the recent operations on social media, three cases from earlier this year remain unclaimed.

The first was on 20 January, when the fishing vessel Fiorella disappeared with eight crew members on board; days earlier, some of them had told relatives they were being monitored by drones and by a vessel on which they said they had seen a US flag.

Human rights organisations and journalists identified two more cases with similar patterns in late March, although the fishers survived. The Washington Post reported that the three cases were part of a covert CIA programme, and the agency declined to comment.

A US Southern Command spokesperson said it had no knowledge of, nor were its forces “involved in the incidents described in reports involving the Fiorella”.

Both the husband and brother of María Mero, 43, were aboard the Fiorella: Gabriel Aladino Mero Delgado, 44, and Jefferson Ariel Mero Cueva, 25. “Where did they take them? Do they have them in the US, in a prison somewhere? We don’t want any trouble; we just want them to send them back to us,” she told the Guardian.

The families say they have spent months trying to get answers from the Ecuadorian government, so far without success.

“They were fishers, not criminals. Let the great president come here to my house and see whether I have luxuries, anything that would show I’m a drug trafficker. No, because all of us here are poor, and we just want our loved ones back,” added Mero.
