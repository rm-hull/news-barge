Residents have compared the reopening of Whorlton Bridge to the fall of the Berlin Wall as communities separated for nearly seven years were finally reunited.

Crowds packed both sides of the River Tees on Friday to watch the ribbon being cut on the historic crossing, ending a closure that began in 2019 when safety inspectors deemed the bridge unsafe.

For many, the reopening was about far more than restoring a road link.

It marked the return of family visits, friendships, walks and connections that had been interrupted for years.

One resident, Nicky, summed up the mood among many residents gathered for the ceremony.

"I said to somebody earlier on, it's a bit like the Berlin Wall coming down," she said.

"We've been waiting for so long for this to happen, so it's quite a special day really."

![]() *(Image: SARAH CALDECOTT)*

Among those celebrating was Cath Campion, 67, who said the closure had turned a short trip to her daughter's home into a lengthy detour.

She said: "I'm very grateful it's open.

"Some people are not keen because of the traffic and I just think, no, we bought a house with a bridge.

"It was my worst nightmare when the bridge shut.

"I used to say to my daughter, 'I'll be around for a coffee'. It'll take me 20 minutes."

![]() *(Image: SARAH CALDECOTT)*

The Grade II\* listed suspension bridge links Whorlton, in County Durham, with communities on the North Yorkshire side of the river.

Generations of residents had relied on it before it was closed on safety grounds.

Its restoration became a major engineering challenge after specialists discovered roughly 96 per cent of the bridge's 1,800 components were beyond repair.

The project eventually cost more than £12 million, with much of the structure rebuilt using replica parts designed to match the original appearance while meeting modern safety standards.

The closure even forced some residents to find extraordinary alternatives. In 2024, Wycliffe resident Eddie Peat revealed he had begun rowing across the River Tees in a small boat to avoid a 40 minute detour caused by the bridge's closure.

![]() *(Image: SARAH CALDECOTT)*

The reopening attracted visitors from further afield, including Archie McEwan, who travelled from Hamilton in Scotland to witness the occasion.

He said: "My friend lives at the top of the hill here. It's a momentous occasion.

"I've been coming down for around four years, so it's nice to see it complete."

Lower Teesdale councillor Richard Bell said the bridge had been a familiar part of local life for decades.

"My earliest memories of the bridge were after I passed my driving test, which is some decades ago now, because I used to love to drive across the bridge slowly and hear the ka-clunk, ka-clunk as the rafters all rattled," he said.

"I didn't know if they were meant to rattle, but I used to love that.

"So I'm looking forward, when it's a bit quieter, to driving over again and renewing my acquaintance with it.

"But it's a great structure and it looks beautiful and being rebuilt and strengthened will hopefully, if not last another 200 years, at least last many, many decades before it needs attention."

![]()George Stastny and Jo Harper were the first people to walk the rennovated crossing. *(Image: SARAH CALDECOTT)*

Friday's celebrations also featured a folk song written by villagers about the bridge's importance and a performance by Barnard Castle Band, which played as residents gathered to watch the crossing reopen.

Opened in 1831, Whorlton Bridge is one of the oldest suspension bridges in the country.

After years of uncertainty over its future, residents say its return has restored far more than a route across the river.

For many, it has brought their communities back together.
