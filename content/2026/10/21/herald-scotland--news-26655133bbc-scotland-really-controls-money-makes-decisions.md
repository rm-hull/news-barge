**When designing the BBC Scotland headquarters at Pacific Quay, the architects aimed for “an enclosed yet publicly accessible" building that allowed people to see the BBC at work while maintaining tight security.**

In other words, be welcoming yet fortress-like. Public and private. Today BBC Scotland remains a glass citadel of contradictions. What other organisation inspires both affection and angry, Saltire-waving protests?

Now, with the long-anticipated closure of the BBC Scotland channel finally announced, The Herald shines a light on “PQ” and its leadership.

**Who runs this place?**  
The first question any reporter should ask, according to lore. With BBC Scotland, this one is easy, at least on the face of it. Hayley Valentine is the director of BBC Scotland. Appointed in November 2024, she earns around £180,000 a year. That is £44,400 more than Scotland’s First Minister takes home, and £26,750 more than the basic salary of an NHS consultant.

Valentine is responsible for programmes, staff, commissioning, and generally keeping the show on the road. But some commissions for Scottish programmes come from London, and some staff work for other parts of the BBC.

Below Valentine are the heads of news, commissioning, sport, radio/audio, Gaelic Services, the BBC Scottish Symphony Orchestra, and corporate affairs.

Sounds crowded, but we are just getting started. Above Valentine is BBC Nations, which has overall responsibility for Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland and the English regions. On top of Nations sits a 12-strong executive team, headed by Matt Brittin as director general (salary £565,000 a year).

Finally, there is the BBC Board, which is meant to set the general direction of travel. Muriel Gray sits on the Board as the representative for Scotland. Reappointed for a further four-year term last January, she is paid £38,000 annually.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/20472581.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Hayley Valentine, former Question Time producer, now BBC Scotland director *(Image: BBC)*

By now the BBC family tree looks less like Who Do You Think You Are, with clear lines and connections, and more like the tangle of wires sprouting from the back of a computer. Overall, the BBC employs around 20,000 people across the UK, including an estimated 1250 in Scotland. That is more than twice the 8,641 full-time equivalent civil servants directly employed in the Scottish Government’s core workforce.

At the last all-staff meeting in October, Brittin heard a lot of complaints about the rank and file being disproportionately hit in the drive to cut 1,800-2,000 jobs. In Scotland the redundancy process has already begun. According to a Substack post by Douglas Fraser, the former BBC Scotland business and economy editor, the cuts include trimming the team of senior presenters, which includes Martin Geissler, Gary Robertson, and Laura Miller, from nine to seven.

The golden rule when trying to find out where power resides in any organisation.  But once more we wander into Alice in Wonderland territory, where things are not what they seem, or so we are told.

On page 231 of the BBC’s 2025/26 annual report, you will find a string of numbers relating to Scotland. These tell us that in the year in question, Scotland raised £313 million from the licence fee. But the amount spent here was £278 million, leaving a difference of £35 million.

The same calculation for Wales gives £93 million raised and £216 million spent (a difference of £123 million in Wales’s favour). For Northern Ireland, £101 million raised, £100 million spent (a £1 million difference). England is top of the loser board, with £3,256 million raised and £2,262 million spent (a difference of £994 million).

Not so fast, however. According to the BBC, it is not a simple matter of money paid in, money paid out, surpluses and shortfalls. Money is not ring-fenced by nation. Other, general items need to be paid for, such as the World Service, licence-fee collection, corporate overheads, and so on.

Others dispute this complex interpretation and say the basic point remains: certain nations, Scotland among them, get back less than they put in.

Angus Dixon has 20 years’ experience as a freelance journalist and TV producer, working on programmes including Location, Location, Location and Antiques Roadtrip. Now a senior lecturer in broadcast and online journalism at Glasgow Caledonian University, he says research being funded by his department raises “serious doubts” about the £278 million figure for spending in Scotland.

“A closer gap between what is raised and what is spent would be helpful,” he says.

**The spender in chief**  
That brings us back to Hayley Valentine. Of the £278 million spent in Scotland, exactly how much is under her direct control? Who has the final authority over the remainder? Can she refuse a saving imposed by London?

I put those questions to BBC Scotland and await the answers. I also asked, again, for an interview with Ms Valentine.

Knowing how much she has to spend could be the biggest clue to her power, or lack of it, when up against London. When she appeared before Holyrood’s culture committee last February, Ms Valentine said: “I do not recognise the idea that we are stymied by London. We run our own newsroom, and I do not receive instructions from anyone on how to run it.”

She also complained about having to live with “a fairly hostile media all the time in Scotland”.

**Transparency, transparency, transparency**  
Getting staff at any level in BBC Scotland to go on the record has proved impossible. Perhaps no surprise when jobs are already at risk. Those who did talk did so on condition of anonymity. Independent production companies are similarly reluctant to bite the hand that feeds them with commissions. Frankly, I have investigated arms firms that were more forthcoming than dear old Auntie.

With the channel closure out of the way, Valentine’s next big test will be the launch of her new dramas. Only then will viewers see if her gamble with River City money has paid off.

In the meantime, a more open BBC Scotland would be welcomed by many. Catherine Happer, professor of media sociology and director of the Glasgow University Media Group, was a programme maker at the BBC before taking voluntary redundancy.

She recognises the BBC feels “under siege”, but says the corporation cannot respond by turning in on itself.

“You’ve got to be answerable, accountable, visible, transparent.

“I don’t think any of this is transparent.”
