# DWP state pension triple lock pay rise confirmed - but with three huge catches

## Harvery Jones wades in with good news for state pensioners but there are triple lock disappointments too

This morning's figures from the Office for National Statistics revealed that consumer price inflation climbed to 3.5% in September, up from 3.1% in August. September's inflation reading is one of three measures that determines the annual state pension rise under the triple lock mechanism.

The remaining two are average earnings growth and a 2.5% minimum guarantee. With earnings already known to have increased by 3.9% in the three months to July - exceeding September's inflation rate - this figure will almost certainly dictate next April's state pension uplift.

The full new state pension is projected to increase by 3.9%, rising from £12,547.60 annually to approximately £13,036.96. That represents a boost of £489.36, pushing it beyond £13,000 for the first time. While this appears positive for pensioners, there's a significant caveat. By the time next April arrives, this may not seem quite so generous, given that inflation is predicted to continue rising.

Bank of England forecasts suggest it could reach 4.2% early next year. Should this prove accurate, the state pension will effectively decline in real terms, squeezing pensioners' spending power, reports the Express.

Despite the triple lock's effectiveness in enhancing pensioner incomes, it may still deliver a below-inflation increase next year. There's also a second drawback. Older retirees on the basic state pension will similarly receive that 3.9% increase. Their annual payments will climb from £9,614.80 to £9,989.20, representing a rise of £374.40.

Regrettably, this remains more than £3,000 annually below the full new state pension. In reality, numerous basic state pensioners receive that amount supplemented by additional pension forms, including SERPS and the state second pension. While some receive more than the full new state pension overall, others are disadvantaged. It remains a persistent source of frustration, with the divide between old and new state pensions set to grow wider annually. Both benefit from the same triple lock increase, yet the new pension starts from a considerably higher baseline.

As though that weren't problematic enough, there's a third complication. With the full new state pension rising to nearly £13,037, it will surpass the frozen £12,570 personal allowance for the first time, by approximately £467. mIn theory, a basic-rate taxpayer might face an income tax liability of roughly £93 on that surplus.

Former chancellor Rachel Reeves pledged that pensioners whose sole income source is the state pension wouldn't be required to pay tax simply because it exceeds the personal allowance. However, that will only assist around one in 20 pensioners in practice. Anyone with supplementary taxable income, such as a private pension or other state pension payments, will pay tax in the standard manner.

Some might encounter an unexpected tax bill simply for earning £1 from a savings account, private pension, or employment, for instance. Key details about how this mechanism will operate remain uncertain, and pensioners will be seeking clarification in next week's Budget. This issue will only worsen.

The personal allowance remains frozen at £12,570 until 2031, while the state pension continues to increase. Consequently, more pensioners will find themselves pulled into the tax system as their incomes grow, even if their actual spending power barely changes.

There's a fourth concern, as Andy Burnham intends to weaken the triple lock from April 2030 by scrapping the wages element. This potentially leaves pensioners with smaller rises in years when wages soar. It would have happened this year. It all contributes to the uncertainty.
