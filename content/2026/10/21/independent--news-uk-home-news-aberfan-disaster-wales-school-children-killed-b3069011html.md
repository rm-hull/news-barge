On the morning of 21 October 1966, nothing had appeared out of the ordinary in the mining village of Aberfan in Wales.

By midday, it had turned into one of the darkest days in Welsh history, with 144 people killed after an avalanche of coal slurry slid down the nearby mountain - with 116 children among the dead.

Complaints had been made to the National Coal Board in previous years about the dangers of the slag heap being positioned above Pantglas Junior School - yet no action was taken and the tip continued to build.

At 9am, a coal worker spotted the tip had begun sinking and attempted to warn his bosses – yet the order stop adding to the coal heap had been given too late, with 240 pupils already making their way into their classrooms.

Minutes later, 150,000 tonnes of slurry would slide down the mountain and crash into the school, killing 109 pupils, and five teachers. Some staff died protecting the children, with deputy headmaster Dai Beynon using a blackboard to shield himself and five of his pupils - he and all 34 children in his class were killed.

Several houses along Moy Road were also destroyed, with another seven children and 23 adults killed as a result of the avalanche.

The tragedy has led to a number of royal visits, documentaries and memorials - yet after the news headlines moved on, it has remained woven into Aberfan’s daily life, with an entire generation lost under the rubble.

On its 60th anniversary, residents have continued to suffer with medical issues or post-traumatic stress disorders - with these images depicting the devastation wrought by the disaster.
