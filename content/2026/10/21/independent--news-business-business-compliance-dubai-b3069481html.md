Many founders do their homework on the aspects that receive the most attention: 100% ownership; the corporate tax threshold; how fast a Dubai free zone license can be issued. However, some may be less familiar with a filing that can carry significant penalties for non-compliance and may receive less attention during the setup process.

## **Understanding the UBO declaration**

It’s called the Ultimate Beneficial Owner declaration, and companies operating in the UAE, whether on the mainland or in a Dubai free zone, are generally required to file one. The rule exists to identify the real natural person who owns or controls a company once every corporate layer is stripped away, and it applies whether that’s a straightforward single-founder structure or something routed through a UK holding company first.

Under Cabinet Decision No. 109 of 2023, anyone holding 25% or more of a company’s shares or voting rights, or who can otherwise appoint or remove the majority of its directors, must be identified and declared to the relevant registrar. Where no individual meets that threshold, the person holding senior management authority is deemed the beneficial owner by default, so there’s no version of company ownership the rule doesn’t reach.

## **How founders fall out of compliance**

A new company has 60 days from incorporation to file its initial declaration, which most people manage fine because it’s built into the setup process. What trips people up is that the obligation doesn’t end there: any change in ownership or control must be reported within 15 days, and some authorities may also require an annual confirmation, usually tied to licence renewal.

A founder who brings on a co-founder, restructures shareholding to bring in an investor, or simply forgets to reconfirm at renewal time can find themselves out of compliance without ever having done anything that felt like a mistake at the time.

Penalties may escalate when non-compliance continues, which means the potential consequences may be underestimated. Depending on the circumstances, an initial lapse may result in a written warning, while continued non-compliance can lead to increasing fines, into the tens of thousands of dirhams, with licence suspension a real possibility for continued non-compliance.

And the obligation doesn’t disappear when a company closes, either: the underlying registers must be retained for five years after deregistration, so this follows a business through its wind-down as well as its growth. Some founders may assume that closing a dormant company automatically ends every obligation attached to it; this is one that doesn’t.

## **The wider tax implications**

It also isn’t purely a standalone compliance box, so an outdated UBO register can quietly complicate a tax position that otherwise looks clean on paper. Founders who treat the declaration as separate from their other filings may encounter related complications elsewhere in the compliance process.

## **Making compliance part of the setup process**

What I’d say to any British founder currently weighing up a Dubai free zone against a mainland structure, or against staying in the UK altogether, is this: build the UBO declaration into your business setup in Dubai checklist with the same seriousness as the trade license itself, not as a follow-up task for month three.

The declaration can often be managed relatively easily during the initial setup process but may become more difficult to address if an outstanding filing affects license renewal.
