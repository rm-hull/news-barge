Trump says he will pay settlement money to veterans affected by contaminated water at marine base only if Republicans retain control of Congress

Donald Trump said that there will be “no money” to settle claims related to water contamination at Marine Corps Base Camp Lejeune if Republicans are not victorious in the upcoming midterm elections.

“If we win the House and the Senate, I am directing the Department of Justice to settle all remaining legitimate claims as soon as possible, and get it off the books and get it finished,” he told supporters during a rally in North Carolina. “If we don’t win, there’s no money. FEMA to states: Prepare to handle your own disasters, Politico reports

The Trump administration wants states to shoulder more of the financial responsibility for disasters, Politico reports.

“Federal dollars should be building greater capability, not creating greater dependency,” Federal emergency management agency (FEMA) administrator Cameron Hamilton told state officials at an Arkansas conference, in a recording exclusively obtained by the Politico. He said each state should have the “appropriate financial reserves.”

“A future that I hope we would shoot for is one where we wouldn’t require as many disasters because the capability of states is so high and so substantial,” Hamilton also said, noting that changes could begin in the next six months.

Under Trump’s second administration, FEMA has seen deep cuts in funding and the exodus of over 2,400 personnel. Former employees and emergency response experts have voiced concern over its capability to respond to future natural disasters.

Trump loyalist and rightwing influencer Laura Loomer tore into the Department of Justice on Tuesday, saying the agency was belittling veterans, who were exposed to contaminated drinking water at a Marine Corps base decades ago, with “lowball” settlement offers.

“This is a political disaster waiting to happen. Let’s be clear: Democrats are 100% going to weaponize this against President Trump and try to hurt him with veterans heading into the midterms in two weeks,” Loomer wrote in a X post, blaming associate attorney general Stanley Woodward Jr.

In a thinly-veiled rebuff, attorney general Todd Blanche appeared to suggest Loomer’s diatribe was financially-motivated.

“You should be transparent with your followers, and you are dead wrong about Stanley,” he responded on X.

“We love our veterans and we want to get these cases right. Find a different obsession other than going after one of DOJ’s best fighters.”

Loomer shot back, saying Trump wanted to fire Woodward, but took pity on him “because his lefty wife is divorcing him,” she claimed.

Some outlets, including Bloomberg, reported earlier this year that Harmeet Dhillon, an assistant attorney general in the DOJ’s civil rights division, would be promoted to Woodward’s post. She was not.

Trump's use of an antisemitic slur for a second time draws belated, indirect criticism from ADL

The Anti-Defamation League, which calls itself the world’s “leading anti-hate organization”, founded in 1913 “to protect the Jewish people”, denounced the use of the term “shylock” as antisemitic on Tuesday, without explaining that the slur had been used one day earlier by Donald Trump in a speech in Kansas.

The ADL’s brief social-media post made no mention of Trump at all and offered no explanation as to why the group found it necessary to explain, today, that anyone equating an avaricious person to Shylock, the villainous Jewish moneylender in Shakespeare’s Merchant of Venice, is using an antisemitic trope.

“The term ‘Shylock’ is rooted in a longstanding antisemitic stereotype,” the ADL posted on X but not its other social media accounts. “Regardless of intent, its use can reinforce harmful tropes about Jews and normalize language that has long been used to target the Jewish community.”

Critics who accuse the ADL of using accusations of antisemitism selectively, to attack people who are critical of Israel, suggested that the Jewish-American civil rights group was going easy on Trump because of his support for Israel.

Matt Duss, a former foreign police adviser to senator Bernie Sanders, the most prominent Jewish American politician, responded to the ADL’s failure to mention Trump by suggesting its president would have been more direct had someone else used the slur. “If a Democrat said it Jonathan Greenblatt would be knocking people over to get in front of a camera to condemn them by name, but it was Trump so it’s just a general public service announcement,” Duss wrote.

In 2014, after then vice-president Joe Biden described people who take financial advantage of American service members as “shylocks”, the Anti-Defamation League’s national director accepted his apology and called the remarks “a teachable moment”.

Trump’s use of the slur, at a rally in Kansas on Monday night, came just one day after he had endorsed Israel’s prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu, on Monday, ahead of Israel’s elections.

Trump invoked the antisemitic stereotype as part of his defense of the Republican senator, Roger Marshall, a doctor who has been criticized for filing lawsuits against more than 700 patients with outstanding bills, leading to 18 of them being arrested.

The president began by claiming, falsely, that Marshall had been required by law to take legal action over unpaid bills, even one for just $101.

Marshall, Trump told supporters at a rally outside Wichita on Monday, “was given a bum rap because he was mandated by law to request strongly, money owed from patients. You’re not allowed not to do it. You have to, by law, go after those people.” There is, in fact, no such law.

Turning to Marshall’s critics, including his Democratic rival, Adam Hamilton, who featured some of Marshall’s patients in a campaign ad, Trump said: “they made it like, oh, he was some kind of a Shylock-type guy.”

Kansas has not sent a Democrat to the Senate since 1932 – does Adam Hamilton have the courage, brain and heart to cause an extraordinary upset?

Just two years after Donald Trump won Kansas by 16 points in his fight to regain the White House, Republicans in the state are scrambling to retain their dominance. The battle for one of its two seats in the US Senate is too close to call, setting the stage for a potentially extraordinary upset.

Some recent polls even put Democrat Adam Hamilton on course for a historic victory. Kansans have not sent a representative of the party to the US Senate since 1932.

On the campaign trail, Hamilton regularly points to one of his state’s most famous residents: Dorothy from Wizard of Oz. Recalling a visit he made to Liberal, Kansas, and its Oz museum, he asks the audience to recall what Dorothy’s companions on her journey need. The lion needed courage, the scarecrow a brain and the tin man a heart.

Washington needs more of all three, he says, and he can deliver it. He decided to run for office after seeing the Trump administration act in ways that were “not good or kind or compassionate or humble”, Hamilton – in the energetic cadence of the pastor he is – told a crowd of business leaders in Topeka on Friday. He was waiting for Roger Marshall, the state’s Republican incumbent senator, to stand up for his state and its people.

“I would have arguments with Roger Marshall in the shower. He was not in the shower with me,” Hamilton joked. “And I would just say, Roger, please stand up for your people. You were not hired to be a spokesperson for the White House. You were hired to fight for your people.”

Hamilton’s role as senior pastor of the country’s largest United Methodist congregation comes with name recognition and speaking skills. But Marshall’s liabilities as his opponent – namely, the fact he sued hundreds of his patients for unpaid bills, resulting in the arrests of dozens of them – have contributed greatly to Hamilton’s prospects of flipping the seat.

Electorally, he’s trying to win over independents and Republicans, calling himself an independent-minded Democrat. It would be valuable, he told the business leaders last week, for Kansas to have a Democrat like him and a Republican in Jerry Moran representing them in the US Senate – working both sides of the aisle.
