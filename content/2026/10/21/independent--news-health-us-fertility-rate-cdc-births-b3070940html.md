The U.S. fertility rate has hit another record low.

For every 1,000 women, aged 15-44, approximately 53 gave birth last year - down from 54 the previous year, according to new data from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

Birth rates decreased among those up to age 29 but increased for women aged 30-44, the CDC found.

The findings are indicative of a broader U.S. trend in recent decades and one that the Trump administration and its MAGA allies, including Elon Musk, have sought to reverse.

President Donald Trump was considering a proposal to offer $5,000 “baby bonuses” to new moms last year, according to *The New York Times*, and previously told the Conservative Political Action Conference he wanted to spur another “baby boom.” MAGA ally Elon Musk, who has 14 children and the president’s ear, fears declines in fertility rates could mean the “end of civilization.”

![Billionaire Elon Musk and President Donald Trump have both called to reverse falling fertility rates. The Trump administration considered a proposal to offer $5,000 ‘baby bonuses’ for new moms](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/21/17/02/GettyImages-2299253024.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

However, there are multiple factors complicating efforts to reverse the trend. Greater access to IUDs and birth control have helped reduce teen births, which has long been a goal for U.S. policymakers because of the complications young mothers with underdeveloped bodies face during pregnancy.

“People are able to avoid having kids at points in their life when they don’t feel it’s the right time,” Karen Benjamin Guzzo, director of the Carolina Population Center at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, told NBC News Wednesday.

A shift in women’s education has also had a major impact. Women now make up the majority of the college-educated workforce, according to the Pew Research Center.

Those with college degrees generally have fewer children, 2022 University of Pennsylvania research shows. That’s largely because of a focus on establishing careers, traveling or finding a romantic partner.

![A lot of the change in birth and fertility rates has to do with women’s health and lifestyle. Women make up the majority of the college educated workforce and women who hold college degrees generally have fewer children](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/21/17/03/GettyImages-2220800665.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

But many women who want to have children are facing major headwinds - economic ones. The average cost of raising a child in the U.S. has ballooned to more than $300,000, the financial marketplace LendingTree says, rising nearly 2 percent from a year ago. That’s increased from $230,000 in 2000, the bank SoFi says.

And while marriage could help distribute some of that financial burden between two, fewer women are heading to the altar. The marriage rate has fallen 60 percent over the last five decades, according to 2023 research.

“Examining age-specific fertility rates, marriage rates and trends in women’s educational attainment, it is clear many women are delaying fertility and ultimately having fewer children,” the university said.

Beyond the U.S., global fertility rates have dropped from an average of five births per woman in the 1950s to around 2.25-2.3, non-profit tracker Our World in Data says.

![This CDC graph shows fertility rates in the U.S. from 1909 through 2018. The orange line shows birth and the blue line shows overall fertility. The difference lies in the measurement. The birth rate measures live births per 1,000 people and the fertility rate measure the average number of children a woman will have between ages 15-44](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/21/17/42/BIRTH-RATES-1909-2018.jpeg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Researchers at the University of Texas at Austin say global birth rates could continue to drop. An analysis published last month found that “where and when fertility has fallen low, it has tended to keep falling.”

The human population is aging, too, decreasing the likelihood of surging fertility. By 2030, 1 in 6 people in the world will be aged 60 years or over, according to the World Health Organization.

Much of the concern about falling fertility rates is tied to concern about senior caregiving and economic strain, according to the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health. With more seniors, there could be fewer people to care for them in later years. Lower fertility rates could also mean fewer workers and spenders to fuel the economy.
