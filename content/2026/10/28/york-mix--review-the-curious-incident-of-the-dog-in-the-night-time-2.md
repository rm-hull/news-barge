Something curiously brilliant is unfolding at the Grand Opera House York this week.

An innovative new production of *The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-Time* has arrived in York this week on its UK tour.

Inspired by the multi-million-selling novel by Mark Haddon, it is adapted by Olivier and Tony Award-winning playwright Simon Stephens and directed by Ned Bennett – and has been heralded as ‘this century’s most popular murder mystery’.

It feels a crime in itself then that I haven’t read or seen this iconic work before yesterday evening, but what a delight it was for this version to be my first time with this story.

In this new staging of the show, which originally premiered at the National Theatre in 2012, it sees the first-ever female casting of the neurodivergent teenage protagonist, now named Chris Boone.

Chris Boone is gifted with a brilliant mind for numbers but unsettled by the unpredictability of people and everyday life. When a neighbour’s dog is discovered killed, she finds herself under suspicion. Determined to prove her innocence, Chris becomes both detective and suspect, drawn into a mystery that grows darker and more complex with every clue she uncovers.

We join Chris on, not just a geographical journey, but a personal one too as her world widens in an utmost test of courage and independence.

![](https://wp.theyorkmix.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/The-Curious-Incident-of-the-Dog-in-the-Night-Time-Johan-Persson-4.webp)

The multi-roling ensemble are a joy to watch as they cavort, cartwheel, and careen across the stage as supporting characters in Chris’ journey, and sometimes literally supporting her as they wheel on set blocks and lift her about the stage. Their continuous energy is captivating and they punctuate the play with pockets of humour.

Bridgette Amofah as Siobhan is the teacher we all wish we had growing up. She grounds Chris, and the audience, keeping us on track and guiding us through the narrative. Amofah imbues the character with a solid kind strength, and never wavering on her professional encouragment.

Charlie Brooks as Judy, and Joe McFadden as Ed are Chris’ parents and have the difficult task of portraying their characters as sympathetic. We as the audience are firmly inside Chris’ head, in her world, so we feel every hurt and betrayal. McFadden as Ed keeps his anger and grief bubbling very close to the surface, but as the play develops his portrayal reveals that perhaps Ed is the most lost character of all.

Judy is a mother who has been shut out, through her own choices and not, and Brooks breathes a spine of steel into her that translates into a fierce protectiveness over her daughter.

![](https://wp.theyorkmix.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/The-Curious-Incident-of-the-Dog-in-the-Night-Time-Johan-Persson.webp)

At the heart of the play, of course, is Chris, played by Alex Keenan (and alternating with Imi Price. Keenan is a revelation in this part. Chris is an extremely demanding role – she never leaves the stage and is constantly moving, and Keenan commands the stage with physicality and emotional intensity.

Every moment of joy, wonder, doubt, fear, anger, tribulation, is projected across Keenan’s face, and it is a deeply authentic, sensitive and touching performance. This is also her professional debut, and it’s a name I’ll remember for a very long time.

The design of this new staging (Eleanor Bull) is extremely effective – the stripped back set allows simple objects to become trains, rocket ships, and police stations, adding the element of child-like imagination to what’s happening on stage.

![](https://wp.theyorkmix.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/The-Curious-Incident-of-the-Dog-in-the-Night-Time-Johan-Persson-2.webp)

The way the lighting (Laura Deward) and costume (Isobel Pellow, Debbie Williams) work together is ingenious. Chris’ world is in block colour – her dad is in greys, her teacher in green, her neighbours in various blocks of pastel colour, and Chris herself is in blue with highlights of red, her favourite. It’s only when Chris ventures outside her known world that clashing colours and patterns appear.

When Chris is uncomfortable or upset or scared, the stage floods with a yellow light, her most hated colour. The visual storytelling at play is very clever, and also simply beautiful to look at.

The video projections (Hayley Egan) are another creative highlight in the show. When they’re used with intention, particularly in the second act when Chris is navigating London, is when they truly shine and add a unique element to the show that creates another level into Chris’ mind.

This is the ‘century’s most popular murder mystery’ for a reason – a timeless story of bravery from a young person to exist in a world exactly as they are. And we, the audience are there to meet them every time.
