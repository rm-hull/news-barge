Welcome to the strange case of the new Scottish arts company that seems to have come out of nowhere, is championed by John Swinney and has a £5 million annual budget attached to it – but appears to be unwanted by the sector it is meant to champion.

One of the flagship new cultural policies of the SNP ahead of the Holyrood election has become a headache for the Scottish Government within just six months of the idea emerging from the ether.

The First Minister’s new culture secretary, Màiri McAllan, is now having to deal with a mini-rebellion from the leaders of arts organisations who have been scratching their heads - and pointing the odd finger behind the scenes - as they try to figure out exactly how a proposed National Performing Company for Traditional Music, that nobody appears to have lobbied for, has become a major priority.

While it is not exactly unheard of for Scottish artists and arts organisations to put their names to letters of concern to a culture secretary it is usually a sure sign that something has gone badly wrong somewhere.

That certainly seems to be the case with this new company, which has sparked warnings that the government risks damaging the country’s existing traditional arts “ecosystem” and raised fears that the new company may “unintentionally privilege” certain artists and art forms.

Traditional arts organisations now find themselves in the bizarre position of urging the government to slow down progress on an initiative which they only appear to have been consulted about after it became official government policy.

The new company was effectively launched at an invite-only gathering of the great and the good of the trad music scene hosted by the First Minister at Bute House during the final week of Edinburgh’s summer festivals.

It was only after that behind-closed-doors event, which I only found out via a flurry of social media posts from invited artists and performers, that the government launched an official consultation about the new company, with the lack of detail made available and the questions which were posed triggering rumblings of discontent behind the scenes.

That consultation was already up and running before Mr Swinney’s culture secretary, Mairi McAllan, chaired a “roundtable” meeting at the start of September with existing arts organisations and event organisers to discuss the government’s plans for the new national company. I have no idea what kind of reception she was expecting, but I am told they did not land well in the room, provoked more questions than answers and ultimately led to a letter sent to Ms McAllan last week setting out wide-ranging concerns about the new company.

The SNP’s concept is fairly straightforward – a new organisation which will ensure traditional music has the “same platform” as opera, ballet, theatre and classical music on Scotland’s stages.

But nothing is ever straightforward in the world of Scottish culture funding.

If you believe everything the government has said in the last few years, things have never been better for the culture sector.

If you look at what some arts organisations have said in recent years, they suggest things have never been worse.

It is less than a year since the five existing national companies - Scottish Opera, Scottish Ballet, the National Theatre of Scotland, the Scottish Chamber Orchestra and the Royal Scottish National Orchestra – issued a scathing joint condemnation of the government.

A decision to leave them on standstill funding in the last Scottish Budget before the election provoked a furious response from the five companies, who said they were “baffled” and “at a loss to understand” why they had been denied a share of the £100m of new culture spending which the SNP had been promising since 2023.

With more than £140m of new arts investment promised in this parliamentary term and the countdown well underway towards the next Scottish Budget, the leaders of those five companies are still arguing for what they say would be their first meaningful uplift in 15 years, while trying to get their heads around the fact that the government has green the light to a sixth national company since they protested about their “inexplicable” treatment.

MSPS were warned last month that all five national companies are projecting budget deficits next year and face having to scale back their work in future years unless there is a significant shift in their direct government funding.

They also want assurances that the new music company will be funded “in addition” to that uplift and will not be created “at the expense of those already delivering for Scotland”.

Yet the overriding impression is that the government has decided to prioritise the new music company over ensuring much-needed stability for the existing five organisations.

This would perhaps be understandable if there was a huge groundswell of support for a new traditional music company.

But the reality is the government has been forced to justify and explain its plans for the new company – and is reluctant to even say where the idea came from in the first place.

Ms McAllan looked uncomfortable at Holyrood’s culture committee this week when she asked who had been consulted about the new company before it was proposed in the SNP manifesto. She did not provide much of an answer. But it is not a complete mystery.

A major clue lies in another behind-closed-doors event on Scottish culture hosted by Mr Swinney at the start of last year.

I don’t recall anything being announced by the Scottish Government before or after the intimate gathering.

But those in attendance were happy to pose for a photograph in the stairwell of Bute House alongside Mr Swinney and his then culture secretary, Angus Robertson.

I did not recognise absolutely everyone in the picture, which was shared on social media, but I did recognise three of the leading figures in the trad music scene - Donald Shaw, creative director of Glasgow’s Celtic Connections festival, folk singer Karine Polwart, and Gary Innes, the trad musician, broadcaster and event organiser behind the Hoolie at the Hydro event in Glasgow.

The idea of launching a new national folk orchestra at Celtic Connections was subsequently pitched by Mr Shaw to the government for support via its “expanded festivals fund” which now supports special projects and commissions at events across the country.

The Grit Orchestra, which Celtic Connections created more than a decade ago to revive and reimagine the music of the late Martyn Bennett, will indeed be relaunched at the forthcoming event in January, but with the same name, largely due to the behind-the-scenes concerns laid out in that letter to Ms McAllan, which Ms Polwart, who will be among the performers at next year’s festival, has signed.

For as long as I can recall, I have written about the case for Celtic Connections to be much better resourced. Its funding is pitiful compared to the Edinburgh International Festival, the most comparable event in terms of international reach and audience.

But there must be better ways addressing that and ensuring traditional music has “parity” with other art forms without undermining what already exists.

It is simply not good enough for the SNP to have now spent three years talking about investing more money in culture without addressing the funding situation of national companies which are reliant on its support but have yet to see any benefit.

The most sensible course of action for Ms Swinney and Ms McAllan is to press pause on the new traditional music company for now and focus on the existing organisations they are already responsible for.
