The contrast between John Healey’s first Budget and Rachel Reeves’s debut in October 2024 could hardly have been starker.

Mr Healey delivered a Budget which contained relatively small and cautious moves, manoeuvring within tight fiscal headroom and amid challenges presented by higher UK Government borrowing costs and a sharp cut to next year’s growth forecast in which higher energy prices were a major factor.

Conspicuous by their absence were the types of huge calls in Ms Reeves’s debut Budget, which included a hike in employers’ national insurance contributions expected to raise £25 billion a year.

One of the more eye-catching moves from Mr Healey, such as they were, was a three-percentage-point increase in the bank surcharge to 6%. As he revealed this temporary tax rise for the banks, with this increase effective from January for 15 months, he flagged the support given to them by the British people “20 years ago”, amid the global financial crisis.

There was certainly huge support for the banking sector nearly two decades ago now, back in autumn 2008, when then prime minister Gordon Brown and chancellor Alistair Darling acted swiftly and impressively to stabilise the UK financial system.

Mr Healey, as he unveiled this tax rise for banks framed as enabling the help being given to households on energy bills, struck a diplomatic tone.

He told the banks: “I celebrate and am grateful for the role you play.”

While Mr Healey highlighted an upgrade to the UK growth forecast for this year by the independent Office for Budget Responsibility, a downgrade to the OBR’s projection for 2027 expansion was far more significant. That said, there was more positive news on upgrades to the growth forecasts further out.

The Chancellor flagged the impact of the “Iran war”, which has pushed energy prices higher, as he majored on support for households on this front.

Summing up the impact on gross domestic product (GDP) growth and borrowing costs, the OBR said: “Energy prices have risen sharply this year, pushing up inflation, lowering real incomes, and reducing GDP growth next year. Higher inflation and interest rates drive up the forecast for government borrowing. Budget decisions further increase borrowing in the near term and reduce it in the medium term.”

It highlighted the “small margin” on Labour’s self-imposed fiscal rules.

The OBR declared: “The fiscal mandate is met with a reduced margin which is small relative to risks around the forecast.”

Mr Healey noted the headroom against the Government’s stability fiscal rule is £15bn in 2029/30, rising to £22bn in 2030/31.

And he highlighted his use of the buffer, which had been built up previously, to absorb the impact of the Iran conflict.

Financial markets took the Budget in their stride. Sterling was little changed against the dollar and euro in the immediate wake of the Budget.

This was in stark contrast to the wild market gyrations following the mini-Budget of Kwasi Kwarteng in autumn 2022, during Liz Truss’s short time as prime minister, a fiscal event highlighted by Mr Healey as he delivered his Budget.

Matthew Amis, investment director at fund manager Aberdeen, said: “The gilt market wanted dull and they got exactly that.

“With oil and gas prices this high and with one eye on France, a dull Budget is what was needed.”

Mr Amis added: “All of the major obstacles presented to Chancellor Healey were avoided, as such gilt yields are slightly out-performing. Is this a gilt-positive budget? Not particularly, but in the context of this year that is good enough. 2027 is going to present another fiscal challenge for Chancellor Healey, but today’s Budget should provide enough to reassure gilt investors going into the final months of 2026.”

The upgrade to the UK GDP growth forecast this year by the OBR – which was highlighted with something of a flourish by the Chancellor – is modest. The growth projection has been upgraded by the OBR from 1.1% in March to 1.2%.

Next year’s growth forecast has been cut from 1.6% to 1% – news which drew a somewhat noisy reaction from the Opposition in the chamber when it was delivered by Mr Healey.

The 2028 forecast is the same as in March – 1.6%.

On a positive note, the 2029 forecast has been raised from 1.5% in March to 1.8%. The OBR’s 2030 prediction is also upgraded – from 1.5% to 1.7%. Growth in 2031 is now forecast at 1.6%.

So it was something of a mixed bag in terms of those changes to the growth projections over the forecast horizon.

That said, the most significant change in the growth forecasts is surely that cut in the projection for next year, which highlights the scale of the challenges in the near term.

The major change to the triple lock on state pensions had been announced weeks ago.

However, Mr Healey highlighted the big numbers on resultant savings for the public finances from that looming reform on the pensions lock – with the Chancellor putting these at £15bn a year by the end of the 2030s and £50bn by 2050.

The other side of that is obviously much less money in pensioners’ pockets in decades to come, which will have implications for aggregate demand in the economy.

From April 2030, state pensions will rise each year by the highest of annual UK consumer prices index inflation, 2.5%, or “the amount required to return or maintain the value of the state pension relative to earnings”.

This third element replaces annual average earnings growth.

Meanwhile, Mr Healey declared: “Since 2024, we've rejected austerity.”

Given the UK economy’s weak performance amid the Conservatives’ austerity, it would be difficult to dispute the merits of Mr Healey’s sentiment on this front from an economic perspective.

Jonathon Marchant, fund manager at Mattioli Woods, highlighted “disciplined” messaging from the Chancellor and Prime Minister Andy Burnham.

Mr Marchant described Mr Healey’s first Budget as “relatively uneventful”, while also flagging Mr Burnham’s “ambitious” rhetoric.

The fund manager said: “Under Andy Burnham and John Healey, messaging was notably more disciplined, with consistent pre-Budget speeches, limited leaks and a clearer articulation of the Government's priorities.”

He added: “Healey's first Budget was relatively uneventful, but Burnham's broader rhetoric remains ambitious. Strong polling could encourage the Government to seek a fresh mandate, and this Budget may lay the foundations for a 10-year strategic plan expected early next year. With many political parties continuing to move away from the centre ground, the range of potential outcomes at the next general election appears wider and more polarised than has been the case for some time.”

The contrast between the low-key nature of Mr Healey’s Budget and the boldness of Mr Burnham on key policy issues, notably in reopening the debate on Brexit in a meaningful way, is striking.
