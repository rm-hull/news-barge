Police Scotland has been told to overhaul support for officers facing misconduct investigations after a watchdog found that welfare arrangements were inconsistent and "perceived as inadequate", with some officers reporting severe mental health problems.

It has emerged that four of the 20 serving police officers and staff who died by suicide in Scotland between 2013 and 2024 were subject to live complaints or disciplinary proceedings at the time of their deaths, according to a May analysis by HM Inspectorate of Constabulary in Scotland (HMICS).

The inspectorate also identified delays in investigations, weaknesses in the handling of whistleblowing allegations and concerns about the way potential wrongdoing by senior officers was assessed.

Inspectors found that low-level performance issues were frequently referred for consideration as possible misconduct problems rather than being addressed through performance management. They said this resulted in disproportionate use of formal conduct procedures.

Investigators found that some officers described severe mental health problems, suicidal thoughts and fears of losing their jobs and homes when facing misconduct charges.

The inspectorate's assurance review found that the impact of conduct and disciplinary proceedings on Police Scotland officers’ mental health and wellbeing “can be significant”.

On the need for reform, HM Chief Inspector Craig Naylor’s said: “These public servants deserve to have confidence in conduct and discipline processes that are fair, proportionate and prompt.”

And on the need to restore public confidence he said: “The recommendations set out in our report provide a clear pathway to enhance professional standards and restore confidence in conduct and disciplinary arrangements.”

Inspectors heard from people who described feeling isolated, let down and uncertain about their futures, with one saying: “I felt that I might not recover from this process, I felt very isolated.”

The context is important here. HMICS’s report, published on 26 May 2026, places the quote in its section on the wellbeing of officers and staff subject to conduct and disciplinary proceedings.

Another who was subject to conduct and disciplinary proceedings said: “I have had suicidal thoughts on multiple occasions through the last four years."

One officer described support they received in stark terms: “Concern for my wellbeing from Police Scotland was non-existent, I barely had contact with my welfare point of contact, I had one five minute face to face chat with them the rest was via text and once via [Teams]."

HMICS called for changes to welfare arrangements, including a review of how support is provided and better training and guidance for those acting as welfare contacts. It said support should be tailored to individual needs and that officers and staff should have an opportunity to contribute to the choice of the person supporting them.

It also recommended that, where an officer or member of staff dies by suicide while subject to ongoing or recently concluded disciplinary proceedings, Police Scotland should explicitly include that information in its report to the Crown Office and Procurator Fiscal Service.

Police Scotland said it supported improvements across police conduct and discipline and has completed all recommendations raised by the HMICS.

The findings come as former Scottish Police Federation general secretary Calum Steele has separately urged MSPs to reject proposed changes to police misconduct regulations, warning that they could undermine officers’ human rights and expose the disciplinary system to further legal challenges.

In a new letter seen by ministers, Mr Steele argued that the proposals risked creating a fundamental inequality between junior and senior officers by failing to provide independent legally qualified chairs for misconduct hearings across all ranks.

Mr Steele who served as the federation’s general secretary from 2008 to 2023, said the committee faced a decision with consequences extending beyond the technical wording of the regulations. He argued that the proposed arrangements departed from the longstanding principle of parity between junior and senior officers, despite the Angiolini Review having recommended independent legally qualified chairs for both.

He raised particular concerns about Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights, which protects the right to a fair hearing, arguing that people conducting misconduct proceedings could be required to determine complex legal questions without the necessary legal qualifications.

He warned that the provisions could leave officers having to challenge the fairness of proceedings through appeals or judicial review after decisions had been made, bringing additional legal costs, delays and uncertainty.

Mr Steele urged MSPs to recognise the significance of the decision before it and reject the changes in their current form.

The former federation chief argued that the proposed rules could create particular difficulties where legally represented parties raised complicated questions of law before a hearing. He said the arrangements risked errors in decision-making and subsequent challenges, with officers and the public purse bearing the consequences.

HMICS inspectors reviewed 107 concluded police officer conduct cases and 23 police staff disciplinary cases, alongside interviews with officers, staff, representative bodies and other organisations as part of a probe.

They found that Police Scotland’s performance appraisal system was widely regarded as ineffective and was being used by relatively few staff. Only 47% of the workforce completed the MyCareer performance and development process in the year to March 31, 2025, according to the report, while fewer than five per cent had completed it between April and September 2025.

HMICS also said officers and staff had limited understanding of how to raise protected disclosures, while records did not always explain why concerns were or were not treated as whistleblowing.

It recommended that the Scottish Government add the Police Investigations and Review Commissioner to the list of prescribed bodies under whistleblowing legislation, enabling officers and staff to raise concerns with an independent oversight organisation outside Police Scotland.

The inspectorate also raised concerns about allegations involving senior officers. It found that reports of potential wrongdoing received by the Scottish Police Authority had sometimes bypassed its complaints team and newly appointed conduct facilitators, contrary to its documented procedures.

In some cases, records did not show that an initial assessment had taken place or explain the reasons for decisions. Inspectors also heard that senior authority officials had sometimes sought advice from senior Police Scotland officers about concerns involving their peers, a practice HMICS considered inappropriate.

The investigators said close working relationships between senior managers at the authority and chief officers could help day-to-day operations but could also create concerns about impartiality when allegations against senior officers were assessed. Some officers, staff and representative bodies believed chief officers were treated more leniently than those they managed.

HMICS recommended that all allegations of potential wrongdoing involving senior officers be shared with the Scottish Police Authority, including anonymous reports, and that a complete and auditable record be maintained.

Chief Superintendent Neil MacDougall said: “Holding the office of constable is a great responsibility, and our officers are expected to exemplify our values at all times to ensure public confidence in policing. Where any officer or staff member falls below the standards expected they will be held to account.

“The health and welfare of our officers and staff is one of our highest priorities and is always a key consideration during misconduct cases."
