John Healey will promise "fresh hope for the future" when he delivers his first Budget today.

But the Chancellor faces a sceptical public. New polling by Ipsos found that 64% of British adults expect the economy to get worse over the next 12 months. Only 14% expect it to improve and 18% think it will stay the same.

Seven in ten think it likely that Labour will increase the taxes they personally pay over the next year, against 17% who think it unlikely.

That is down from 77% in October 2025 and 78% in June 2025.

Two-thirds believe it is likely Labour will increase government borrowing and the national debt, while 51% expect an increase in spending on public services.

Few want broad tax rises. If taxes had to go up, 44% would prefer higher income tax on earnings above £125,000. Only 4% back a rise in basic-rate income tax and 3% a rise in VAT.

The Chancellor is expected to freeze fuel duty and offer help on energy bills as well as extra cash for defence.

The Prime Minister has also ordered him and Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh to examine scrapping standing charges from gas bills.

A series of "everyday fixes" announced on the eve of the Budget includes replacing the Carers' Allowance cliff edge with a taper and making a 55p tax-free mileage allowance permanent.

Tariffs on a range of popular foods will be cut, and parental pay will be extended to more than 150,000 parents a year. The minimum wage is reported to be rising by nearly 4%.

The Chancellor has also promised to fill a £4.7 billion hole in the defence investment plan, lifting defence spending to 2.7% of GDP by 2027.

He has limited room for giveaways. Borrowing costs have risen because of the Iran war, and the Resolution Foundation estimates that headroom against his fiscal rules could have halved from the OBR's March forecast of £24 billion to £12 billion.

In a video message released by the Treasury ahead of today's statement, Mr Healey said: "Breathing space for today and fresh hope for the future. That's what today's Budget will deliver.

"The world's a highly uncertain place. Conflicts abroad are pushing up energy prices, and the cost of living here at home — piling pressure on the families, on businesses, and on our public finances.

"But Britain's economy is showing real signs of resilience: investments up, productivity is growing, and we had the joint fastest growth in the G7 in the first half of this year.

"We can't stop these shocks that hit Britain, but we can make Britain more resilient when they do."

The Chancellor said the Budget was "about getting through this difficult moment" and "about putting Britain on a future path, a better future path".

Scotland's Deputy First Minister Jenny Gilruth said the Chancellor had a chance to "change the direction of the UK Government and finally end austerity budgets by taking real action on the cost of living crisis".

She added: "He can choose to invest in public services and to help hard-pressed families by taking action to stop soaring fuel costs and energy bills — which are hundreds of pounds higher than when this UK Government came to power, despite Scotland being one of the most energy-rich nations in the world."

She also warned that on current plans, the Scottish Government 's resource and capital block grants are both forecast to fall by 1% in real terms in 2027-28.

Ms Gilruth said Holyrood was allocating more than £3.5 billion this year to tackle poverty and support people with the cost of living. She added: "But we want to do more and if the UK Government won't take action to address this, then the Prime Minister should give us more powers to support people in Scotland and grow our economy."

Meanwhile, Offshore Energies UK urged the Chancellor to bring forward a new Oil and Gas Revenue Levy to replace the 78% Energy Profits Levy from January 2027. The trade body says the move could unlock £50 billion of investment in North Sea projects by 2035.

Chief executive David Whitehouse said: "Businesses need certainty to invest. Bringing forward the OGRL to January 2027 would create a permanent framework that taxes windfalls when prices are high while creating the confidence needed to invest when prices are lower."

The Child Poverty Action Group called for a £10-a-week rise in child benefit, saying it would lift more than 20,000 children in Scotland, and 400,000 across the UK, out of poverty. It says child benefit is worth £5 a week less than in 2010.

John Dickie, director of CPAG in Scotland, said: "Scotland deserves better. We need both our UK and Scottish governments boosting social security to prevent this avoidable poverty."
