**City of Edinburgh Council is set to adopt a new code of conduct urging councillors to eschew ‘derogatory language’ and ‘personal attacks’.**

Council officers have recommended that the capital incorporate the COSLA Civility Charter, which was drawn up by the local government association earlier this year, into its Committee Best Practice Guide.

“The charter sets out the expectations for conduct and behaviour during political meetings and interactions at COSLA, both online and in person,” a report submitted ahead of Thursday’s full council meeting states.

“Whilst the charter is principally applicable for meetings involving COSLA, the principles enshrined in it are appropriate for application within the council.”

These principles include ‘respect for all’, ‘constructive engagement’, ‘professional in all settings’, ‘respect for officers and their role’, ‘safe and inclusive spaces’, and ‘shared responsibility’.

![Free transport to school by City of Edinburgh Council has been scrutinised.](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/20170667.jpg?type=mds-article-620)City of Edinburgh Council is poised to adopt a new code of conduct. *(Image: NQ)*

Councillors will be asked to “treat all individuals with dignity, courtesy, and respect, regardless of political affiliation, role, or background" and to “avoid personal attacks, derogatory language, or dismissive behaviour”.

They will also be encouraged to “listen actively and seek to understand differing perspectives with the intent of reaching consensus wherever possible” and to “uphold the council’s reputation by modelling respectful conduct in all meetings and external engagements”.

Councillor Alys Mumford, equalities spokesperson for the Green group on the council, has welcomed the report’s recommendations.

"Democracy is becoming increasingly polarised, and the behaviour of elected representatives towards each other in the chamber can help to legitimise this,” Councillor Mumford said.

“While we continue to debate and disagree about some of the existential challenges facing us - how to end the scandal of child poverty, the best routes out of the climate crisis, and what the future of Edinburgh should look like - we should all try to do so in a way which upholds civility.

“This is not always easy to do, but it is vital to ensure people - especially women, young people, people of colour, and other groups whose voices are so needed for a healthy democracy - aren't put off from engaging in politics because of toxic behaviours.”

Councillor Mumford went on to say she was pleased the Greens had submitted the original motion which led to the recommendations.

“We hope to see improvements in the behaviour of all councillors in Edinburgh as a result,” she added.

The report acknowledges that the local authority is limited in its ability to deal with councillors accused of breaking the rules.

Instead, complainants must pursue action via the Ethical Standards Commissioner and the Standards Commission for Scotland.

The report explains: “Whilst parts of the COSLA Civility Charter (particularly when referring to complaints and resolution during meetings) are not applicable for adoption as they are contrary to the council’s standing orders, the six principles as detailed above could be adopted by the council.

“However, where allegations are made that one or more of these principles has been broken, the council has limited powers to undertake any mitigating actions, save those contained within standing orders and the right of individuals – elected members, officers or members of the public – to make a complaint to the Ethical Standards Commissioner.”

![Scottish Green councillor Alys Mumford.](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21034079.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Scottish Green councillor Alys Mumford. *(Image: Scottish Greens)*

According to Section 29.1 of the council’s standing orders, councillors can be kicked out of a meeting if they “behave obstructively or offensively”, provided the majority of their peers vote to remove them from the room.

The adoption of the charter could be seen as an attempt to self-police conduct, as Scotland’s standards watchdogs have come under fire from city councillors in recent months.

In September, councillors voted to condemn the Ethical Standards Commissioner and the Standards Commission.

SNP councillor Adam Nols-McVey said that the watchdogs had failed in their mission to “uphold the Nolan principles of public life”.

“We have a unanimous position which states that Edinburgh formally declares that it has no confidence in the Ethical Standards Commissioner and Standards Commission,” he said.

“Their position is now untenable and they need to recognise that. Their job as an organisation is to uphold the Nolan principles of public life. That is their job and it's a mission they have fallen so irretrievably far from delivering.”
