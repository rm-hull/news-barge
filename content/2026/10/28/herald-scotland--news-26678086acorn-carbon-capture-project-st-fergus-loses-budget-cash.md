Scotland’s only large-scale carbon capture project has been left out of the Budget, a year after Ed Miliband promised it £200m.

The Acorn carbon capture project at St Fergus near Peterhead has been left without a confirmed funding commitment after the UK Government cut millions from carbon capture capital grants to help pay for increased spending on defence.

Aberdeen & Grampian Chamber of Commerce said the move was a "hammer blow" for the north-east of Scotland, while the SNP said it was “the gravest betrayal of Scotland’s energy sector".

But Scottish Secretary Douglas Alexander said there “significant challenges” around the project and ministers had a "responsibility to think carefully about the use of public money".

Acorn is Scotland's only large-scale carbon capture and storage (CCUS) project. It aims to capture carbon dioxide emissions from industrial facilities and power plants, transporting them offshore to be permanently stored in depleted gas fields beneath the North Sea.

The project plans to re-use existing oil and gas infrastructure, notably pipelines that already run from St Fergus out to former drilling sites.

Acorn missed out on support in 2021, when funding instead went to two areas in the north of England. It was placed on the Track 2 reserve list for future backing.

A 2023 impact assessment estimated that Acorn could support 15,000 jobs across construction and long-term operation, with many roles directly re-using skills from the offshore oil and gas sector.

Last year, the then UK Energy Secretary Ed Miliband promised the scheme £200m.

He said he wanted to see significant progress "by the turn of the decade" but would not commit to a firm timetable.

However, the Treasury's Budget document says the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero (DESNZ) is "reprioritising its budget to contribute towards the Defence Investment Plan".

It says the Government is "prioritising delivery of mature carbon capture, usage and storage (CCUS) and hydrogen projects at the Teesside, Merseyside and North Wales clusters, maintaining grant funding for the Viking cluster".

Acorn is not among the projects named for grant funding.

Instead, the document says the Government is "actively looking at how loan or equity investments can support the continued development of future CCUS and hydrogen projects this Parliament, including for the Acorn Cluster, while ensuring a return on any investment for taxpayers".

Russell Borthwick, chief executive of Aberdeen & Grampian Chamber of Commerce, said: "This is yet another hammer blow for the North-east Scotland economy and comes only a year since former Energy Secretary Ed Miliband said the UK Government would put 'its money where its mouth is' and back the Acorn Project, protecting 15,000 North Sea jobs. All talk, no action, like so much of the transition we were promised by this government.

"This is an abject betrayal of that commitment and, yet again, communities across our region, which has powered the UK's energy and economic security for decades, are left to face the consequences of inaction and indifference from our political class."

Seamus Logan, the SNP MP whose Aberdeenshire North and Moray East is home to Acorn, said: “This is the gravest betrayal of Scotland’s energy sector and is an utter disaster for our energy security, our net zero ambitions and for Scottish jobs.

"This treacherous decision comes after explicit and repeated commitments from the UK Labour Government who previously assured workers and the industry that capital was secured.

“Acorn will now be listed alongside Grangemouth and Labour’s tax on Scotland’s energy - a list that depicts the shameful destruction of Scotland’s industrial base by the Labour Party.

“History is repeating itself. The Labour Government comes to Scotland, takes our resources and gives nothing back - our energy story is one of betrayal, abuse and outright lies.

“Today will live long in the memory. This budget has put in black and white the case for our independence with our energy wealth syphoned off and our resources asset stripped."

Speaking to journalists after the budget, Mr Alexander said there were “significant challenges” with Acorn around both the "market readiness of the technology and in relation to the commercial arrangements around the project".

He added: "We have a responsibility to think carefully about the use of public money, at the same time as ensuring that we continue to facilitate the transition in the North Sea.

"That's why we welcomed, and I welcomed the decision in relation to Jackdaw last week as a win for Aberdeen, a win for Scotland, and a win for the UK's energy security.

"That is but one piece of the jigsaw. There are other steps that we are taking in terms of the North Sea transition, but we have a responsibility to look at each project on a case by case basis, and we've reached important judgments in relation to Acorn.

"And let's also recognise the importance of the defence dividend that is differentially benefiting Scotland."

North East Green MSP Maggie Chapman said the news called into question the Scottish Government’s decision to approve the construction of a controversial new gas-fired power station at Peterhead.

The scheme, by SSE and Equinor, will generate about 900 megawatts of electricity and includes provision for carbon capture.

Ms Chapman said: "The case for Peterhead relied on it working with Carbon Capture and Storage Facilities, the viability of which is now in doubt.

"The Chancellor’s decision will cause concern and uncertainty for workers in the NE, but it highlights the urgent need to create an economy on high value, high skilled jobs that do not rely on fossil fuels”.

Friends of the Earth Scotland’s head of campaigns, Caroline Rance said it was right to cut Acorn's funding, given the technology is unproven: "It has been over 20 years of delays, failures and sucking up public money and the so called carbon capture industry has not stored a drop of carbon. No amount of public money can make this pig fly."
