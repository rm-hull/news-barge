Carlo Carozzi is the chef-owner of Brasas by The Peruvian at Edinburgh Street Food and owner of The Bonnington in Leith.

Before launching his own business, Carozzi worked in Edinburgh bars including The Voodoo Rooms and Dragonfly. He started The Peruvian as a small street-food stall during the Edinburgh Festival Fringe, later trading at markets and events including The Pitt and Glasgow’s Platform and earning recognition at the Scottish Street Food Awards.

The business has since evolved into Brasas by The Peruvian, where Carozzi has put fire cooking at the heart of a menu inspired by his homeland. In late 2024, he also took over The Bonnington, an independent neighbourhood pub in Leith, bringing subtle Peruvian influences alongside a modern pub menu.

This week, he takes on our chef Q&A:

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21640230.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The Peruvian began as a small street food stall during the Edinburgh Festival Fringe *(Image: Supplied)*

**When did you realise you wanted to be a chef?**

I never imagined I would end up being a chef. Even now, I don’t really think of myself as a “chef chef”. What I discovered was a passion for hospitality as a whole. I’ve worked my way through the industry, from kitchen porter to head chef, and from cellar boy to general manager. Now I have two businesses of my own.

There wasn’t one particular moment when I decided that this was my future. One role led to another as I followed what I loved. It takes a huge amount of hard work, but when you’re passionate about it, you put your heart into it.

**What was your first kitchen job like?**

My first kitchen job? I must have been about six, in my grandmother’s kitchen. The hours were questionable, and the pay was non-existent, but I was very happy there.

I would go to the market with my grandparents to buy the week’s ingredients. Once we got home, my grandmother would put me to work shelling peas, washing potatoes and stirring whatever stew was bubbling away in the pot. Even now, those smells take me straight back to my childhood. They never paid me a penny, but they gave me something much more valuable. I owe them so much of who I am today.

I also loved going fishing with my grandfather. On the way, we would always stop for pan con chicharrón: sandwiches filled with pork and sweet potato. I’d had allergic reactions to chicharrón before, but one day I completely forgot and ate one. Hours later, my brother and grandfather looked at me and said, “Have you realised what you ate? And nothing happened!” Discovering I could enjoy those glorious sandwiches was a very big day.

**What does “good food” mean to you?**

Good food means everything to me! Somewhere along the way, I think we’ve lost a little of its magic. People seem to think it has to cost a fortune, and the smaller the portion, the better the food. Sometimes I look at a plate and think, “Even a newborn would want a second helping.”

I understand making food look beautiful for Instagram, but what happened to cooking with love, from the heart?

You don’t need a mountain of food on the plate. For me, it’s about flavours that are balanced and complement each other, where all the ingredients come together in one delicious mouthful. Food that makes you slow down because you want to enjoy every bite. That’s good food.

**Can you share a memory of your worst kitchen disaster?**

How long have you got? I’ve had oil spills, dropped food, cornflour everywhere and burns. Choosing just one is difficult.

But the biggest disasters started at home with my brother. Once, our grandparents brought something back from the USA to help you go to the toilet. We told our friends it was a new American jam and encouraged them to try it on toast. Before long, everyone was fighting over the bathroom.

**Where is your favourite place to eat out?**

I’ve been so busy over the past year that I haven’t had much chance to eat out. Dogstar is top of my list to try properly. A lot of my mates work there, and Jimmy is a fantastic chef with such an eye for detail, but I still haven’t sat down for a full meal.

And, honestly, whoever said chefs don’t eat their own food hasn’t met me. I love eating at Brasas. I’m at Edinburgh Street Food whenever I get the chance, either working or sitting down with my family.

I’m proud of what we’ve created. The smoked pork, with the sweetness of honey and sweet potato, the sharpness of lime and the crunch of onions, has that balance of flavours that keeps you coming back for another bite. It’s addictive. And the rotisserie chicken is out of this world. I eat both whenever I get the chance.

**What’s the best thing you’ve cooked recently?**

It has to be the smoked pork with glazed sweet potatoes and salsa criolla from our new menu at Brasas at Edinburgh Street Food. We’re going back to our roots, cooking over wood and charcoal, taking our time and giving the ingredients the care they deserve. That love for food carries through to the plate.

Ask me again tomorrow, and I might say the rotisserie chicken! With our homemade potatoes, salad, chimichurri and ají verde, it’s something special. Pour on the sauces, get stuck in and enjoy—that’s my kind of eating.

**What do you think is the biggest misconception people have about working in a professional kitchen?**

That it’s easy and anyone can do it. Working in a professional kitchen takes a particular kind of person. You need stamina, a thick skin and the ability to keep your head when everything is happening at once.

And you have to be a little bit mad. In fact, we’re all mad. But there’s something special about a group of people who can get through the chaos of a busy service together and still come back the next day to do it all again.

**If you weren’t a chef, what do you think you’d be doing instead?**

Honestly, I really don’t know. I wish I could give you an impressive answer and say I’d be a doctor or something, but hospitality is where my passion has taken me, and I’m so happy it did.

I get to share the flavours of Peru with you lovely people in Scotland. It’s hard to imagine another job that would mean as much to me.

A Peruvian! Every kitchen should have one.

Failing that, some Peruvian chillies. They bring so much more than heat—they have fantastic flavour and character. Everyone should have some in their kitchen. It’s a little taste of Peru at home!

**What’s been the standout highlight of your career so far?**

I don’t think there’s one single moment. For me, every person we cook for and every plate we serve matters.

The real highlights are when someone comes up to the kitchen after eating and tells me it was the best meal of their life. How do you beat that?

Or when people go off and explore other food, then come back and say, “For me, it’s still yours.” That means everything. Knowing that something we’ve cooked has made someone happy is what fulfils me and keeps me going
