Islanders are demanding an independent investigation into whether the CalMac is putting the needs of communities first accusing the state-owned ferry operator of systemic failures after two incidents in a week left islanders facing lengthy delays.

Cumbrae’s ferry committee and community council have written to transport secretary Stephen Flynn asking him to reverse his decision not to commission an external review of CalMac’s handling of its Clyde and Hebrides ferry contract, following disruption on the Largs–Cumbrae route on October 20 and October 27.

Angus Campbell, chairman of the Cumbrae Ferry Committee, said: “This was not one bad day. CalMac promised capacity that never arrived, ignored warnings we gave it in advance, and then failed to deliver its own plan for a spare vessel. When the same failures keep happening, that is a system, not an accident.”

The move has come a matter of months after The Herald revealed minsters scrapped a 20-year-old system of fines for ferry failure in Scotland’s new multi-billion-pound lifeline services deal with CalMac.

The **Scottish Government** has been criticised over the move which comes despite repeated promises of tighter scrutiny, stronger performance measures and greater accountability after the ferry operator came under immense criticism over how it manages the service.

**CalMac** was fined more than £20 million for failing island communities before being handed the new £4 billion contract last year. Performance deductions imposed by ministers - when CalMac fails to meet reliability and service measures on the contract - hit a record £4.335m in its final year alone.

The Cumbrae committee says the first incident saw passengers face delays of three to four hours after CalMac failed to provide the replacement capacity it had previously indicated would be available while MV Loch Shira underwent planned maintenance.

The larger vessel completed its final scheduled sailing at 8.40pm on Monday, October 19, and did not return to service until 6.35am on Wednesday, October 21. On the intervening Tuesday, the route was served by MV Loch Tarbert, which the committee says has around half the capacity of Loch Shira.

Mr Campbell said: “Islanders sat in queues for three and four hours while a bigger vessel sat tied up with its maintenance finished. CalMac’s contract says it should put communities at the centre of its decisions. On Cumbrae, that is not what we are seeing.”

A briefing paper titled Systemic Failures within CalMac prepared by Mr Campbell demands an inquiry.

“Our clear ask is for an independent external review of the community focus of CalMacs CHFS contract. This review should develop clear action plans that change the company culture, management structure and processes to put the communities at the forefront of the CalMac organisation," it said.

The briefing says that residents had contacted CalMac weeks earlier to establish what arrangements would be in place for the maintenance period. It says they were told two smaller vessels would provide capacity equivalent to Loch Shira, but only Loch Tarbert operated.

According to the committee, vehicle queues had exceeded an hour and a half by 9.30am, with delays of three to four hours affecting journeys in both directions for much of the day. It says families, people travelling to healthcare appointments and island deliveries were among those affected.

The committee says it asked CalMac to consider using spare vessels reportedly at Sandbank or Rothesay, or returning Loch Shira to service once its maintenance was complete. It says it was told neither option was possible.

The paper states that the maintenance work was finished by 5pm on October 20, but Loch Shira remained alongside and its crew went home. Mr Campbell says CalMac chief executive Duncan Mackison subsequently acknowledged that mistakes had been made in allocating vessels and crews.

The committee argues that the incident raised questions beyond a single allocation error, including whether staff had sufficient authority to challenge plans when the replacement capacity was not in place and whether practical steps were taken to reduce the disruption once queues began to build.

The second incident came a week later, when Loch Shira was moved from Largs to Garvel Wall at James Watt Dock in Greenock ahead of forecast storm conditions overnight from October 26 to 27. The committee says it accepts the need to protect vessels during severe weather, but questions the arrangements for bringing the ferry back into service.

Mr Campbell says the committee had raised concerns on October 23 about the arrangements at Garvel Wall, where access to the vessel depended on a substantial gangway being put in place by crane, as well as suitable wind conditions and the availability of dock staff.

The committee says it was assured that procedures were in place, but that the ferry was then held at the dock for a full day after wind speeds had dropped. It argues that the risks identified beforehand were not adequately addressed and that alternatives suggested by residents were not properly considered.

![CalMac: ](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/17569602.jpg?type=mds-article-620)CalMac comes under fresh fire from islanders. *(Image: Agency)*

One option raised by the committee was Gourock, which it says had previously been used as a safe haven for Loch Shira. The paper suggests a lightweight gangway could have been transported from Largs or carried aboard the vessel, while the ship’s generator could have been used if shore power was unavailable. These are the committee’s proposals; the feasibility of the alternatives would need to be established with CalMac.

The committee also says it was told twice in the preceding week that no relief vessel was available because MV Isle of Cumbrae was uncrewed. It points to CalMac’s corporate plan for April 2026 to March 2027, which commits to “fully crew all small vessels to ensure a spare vessel is available as relief to support planned maintenance activities, overhaul cover and vessel breakdowns in the small ferry fleet”.

The committee says the lack of a crewed relief vessel raised questions about whether the published commitment was being put into practice when Cumbrae needed cover. It argues that the two incidents point to problems with planning, communication between departments and the consideration given to local knowledge.

The briefing states: "Taken together, the events of 20 and 27 October 2026 raise serious concerns that extend beyond isolated operational mistakes. They identify systemic failures in planning, interdepartmental coordination, local staff empowerment, contingency arrangements, relief-vessel crewing, customer welfare and the consideration of community needs.”

The groups acknowledge that CalMac operates an ageing fleet and that difficult weather can disrupt sailings. The committee’s paper says those challenges are not solely the operator’s responsibility, pointing to a lack of investment in replacement vessels by Transport Scotland and the Scottish Government between 2010 and 2020 as a major underlying reason for the age and unreliability of the fleet.

However, it argues that the way decisions are made within the company can make the effects of those problems worse for individual communities. The committee says its concern is that decisions may be shaped by what suits the organisation rather than what a route needs to keep people moving.

The call for an independent review follows a meeting between the committee and Mr Flynn in Edinburgh on September 29, when the groups first raised their request for an external examination of the community and service focus of the ferry contract.

They are now asking Mr Flynn to reconsider his decision and are seeking support from leaders of political parties in the Scottish Parliament. They also want MSPs to raise the matter with the Cabinet Secretary, a parliamentary committee to examine the concerns and a meeting with a delegation from Cumbrae.

The proposed review would examine whether CalMac’s management structure, planning and working practices consistently put the needs of island communities first, and recommend practical changes with clear accountability to the communities affected.

The ferry committee and community counci say they are not seeking to undermine the safety responsibilities of skippers or dismiss the practical difficulties of running an ageing fleet in severe weather. Their request is for an independent assessment of whether the company’s decisions, processes and culture are delivering the service the contract requires.

In the paper, the committee says the aim is to ensure “that the importance of service to island communities is central to operational decisions”.
