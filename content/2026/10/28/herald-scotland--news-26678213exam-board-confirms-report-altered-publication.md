Scotland’s exam board has been forced to alter a key subject report after it had already been published, The Herald can exclusively reveal.

The organisation is facing growing criticism after official data revealed incredibly low national average scores across one crucial aspect of the exam.

The Herald has also been made aware of an open letter signed by dozens of History teachers expressing “significant concerns” about the exam board’s handling of the Higher exam for their subject.

Course reports are published each year for all subjects. They are “written by assessors” and are supposed to “highlight learner successes and areas for improvement.” Qualifications Scotland (formerly the SQA) encourages teachers to read the reports for their subject to use the “valuable insight” they provide in order to “prepare learners” for exams.

Following an approach from The Herald, an official confirmed that Qualifications Scotland altered the course report for Higher History on Friday 23 October. This happened after the Scottish Association of Teachers of History (SATH) challenged a claim about pupil performance in the 'evaluation' component of the examination.

The organisation wrote to a key official at the exam board raising multiple significant concerns and calling on the Qualifications Scotland to "acknowledge that there is a serious problem."

Initially, an exam board official described the editing of the 2026 Higher History course report as a change to “one word” of the document, but The Herald has confirmed that the alteration in fact has a specific and significant statistical meaning.

The first paper of the Higher History exam requires students to select and answer one question on British history and one on European or world history. Candidates are expected to write short essays in response to the questions, with a total of 22 marks available, four of which relate to evaluation.

The original version of the 2026 Higher History course report claimed that “some” pupils had done well in evaluation tasks, with this word defined as a reference to between 25% and 49% of pupils.

The reissued document now states that “few” pupils did well in this area, meaning than the figure is somewhere below 25%.

However, figures released by the exam board suggest that the true number is extremely low, rather than marginally beneath the 25% threshold.

Official data confirms that the national average score for evaluation components in the 2026 Higher History exam was equivalent to just 3.1% of the marks available. Despite this, Qualifications Scotland argues that the paper "performed as expected."

In the most commonly answered question for paper 1 (question 11), which was completed by almost 80% of students, the national average score for the evaluation component was 0.11 marks out of a possible 4, equivalent to just 2.8%.

Across the whole paper, half of questions (21 out of 42) had a national average score of 0.1 marks or less for the evaluation component, with three of those recording a national average score of zero marks out of four.

In contrast, Knowledge and Analysis components of the exam questions returned national averages of 80.2% and 67.8% of available marks respectively.

Speaking exclusively to The Herald, a spokesperson for SATH said: "There is a lack of clarity surrounding these marks, and that Higher History is not in line with other language based, essay writing subjects in terms of the standard being asked for. Teachers and learners have become discouraged to the point where learners don't even attempt evaluation. It is an important skill, and we would like to see our most able learners being rewarded for demonstrating it, not strategically missing it out because it is perceived as unattainable. The evidence now appears to back up this perception.

"We would like the issue to be addressed for this session's learners, not pushed back for qualification reform to deal with, given that concerns over a lack of clarity in how these marks are assigned was raised in the 2024 survey."

The “inaccessibility” of evaluation marks is one of the main concerns raised by teachers in a separate open letter to be sent to Qualifications Scotland, and of which the exam board is already aware.

It states that problems in this area “have been a concern for teachers for some time, but the recently available statistics show the stark reality of the situation.”

Teachers have also highlighted a series of other problems, including the “post-exam alteration of marking standards”, noting that a senior member of the Qualifications Scotland History team “publicly admitted on social media that this definitely happened in 2026.” The Herald has previously reported these allegations, and the exam board has not denied that such changes took place.

The letter also highlights the “erratic standards” in the Scottish History paper of the Higher course, explicitly referencing a “dramatic drop in marks for this paper in 2024 was widely covered in the press and raised many questions, none of which have been answered to the satisfaction of the profession.” It says that the exam board’s explanation, which places the blame with teachers and students, is “simply not plausible.”

In addition, concerns are also raised about the “whitewashing of colonial history”, which is described as “deeply concerning.”

Ultimately, signatories argue that the “net effect” of the various problems with Higher History is that “students are being driven away from what is a core subject”, with the exam itself described as a “disgrace.”

They add: “The response of Qualifications Scotland to date has been to refuse to address any of these specific concerns directly and to repeatedly state that the 2026 Higher exam ‘performed as expected’. This is simply not good enough.”

Speaking to The Herald, Paul Cochrane, Vice President of the Scottish Secondary Teachers Association, told The Herald that action must be taken to address the problems with Higher History, and that the national exams system must be “beyond reproach at all levels.”

He continued: “To have repeated questions directed at any subject is a matter of grave concern. The situation in History demands the highest level of transparency and honesty from Qualifications Scotland. We would also expect that Qualifications Scotland would be seeking to reassure learners, teachers and parents that such anomalies have not occurred in other subject areas.

"Our examination and certification system requires an unimpeachable level of performance and trust. It is very concerning that this incident may be damaging public and professional trust in aspects of performance from Qualifications Scotland.”

Katherine Sangster MSP, Scottish Labour spokesperson for education, Katherine Sangster told The Herald that the change to the Higher History course report raises “serious questions that must be answered.”

“Pupils and teachers across Scotland deserve confidence in our qualifications system.

“History teachers’ concerns must be listened to, not dismissed. Qualifications Scotland must explain why the wording was changed, what the 3.1% average score for evaluation questions tells us, and whether previous reports require further scrutiny.

“We need transparency about how this situation arose, whether there are wider problems with assessment and what action will be taken to put things right. Our young people deserve a fair and reliable qualifications system, and our teachers deserve to have their professional expertise respected. Restoring confidence must be the priority.”

Scottish Conservative education spokesperson, Meghan Gallacher MSP described the latest revelations as another “quango scandal", and said that there are "serious questions over transparency and an exam body that appears to have brushed aside teachers’ warnings.

“Qualifications Scotland must stop trying to minimise this and explain why a crucial report was changed after publication.

“Teachers have been ignored for too long. SNP ministers must get a grip, establish whether pupils have been let down and hold those responsible to account.”

Scottish Liberal Democrat education spokesperson Duncan Dunlop MSP said:

“This is the latest worrying chapter in the marking of the Higher History exam.

“These revelations suggest an evasive and secretive approach. Pupils, parents and teachers all deserve to know exactly what went wrong here.

“There are many young people for whom the results of this exam really matter, which is why it is so important that Qualifications Scotland are clear about how the marking of this exam was handled.”

A Qualifications Scotland spokesperson said: “The original reference in this year’s Higher History course report to 'some' candidates demonstrating evaluation skills well, referred to the proportion of those candidates who attempted that part of the question paper.

“Following feedback from SATH, we updated the report to reflect the performance of all candidates who sat the Higher History paper – many of whom did not attempt the evaluation part of the question paper. The amendment does not alter the advice provided in the course report, which helps prepare candidates for the assessments.

“We are working hard to ensure both teachers and learners alike are confident in their understanding of what is required of them to successfully demonstrate all skills, including evaluation. We welcome and encourage feedback from teachers, and we have invited many of those who have expressed their views about the Higher History course to work with us. The door remains open.”
