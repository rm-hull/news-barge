A tax rise on alcohol could prevent 420 deaths in Scotland by 2032, according to a public health group representing Scotland’s medical royal colleges.

The Scottish Health Action on Alcohol Problems (SHAAP) is calling on Chancellor John Healey to bring back an annual alcohol duty escalator, increasing tax by two per cent above inflation each year. The proposal is based on modelling by the University of Sheffield.

SHAAP’s briefing to the Treasury says the measure could also prevent 8,719 hospital admissions in Scotland and save the NHS £46m by 2032. The figures are projections, not guaranteed outcomes, and form part of the group’s call for tougher action on alcohol harm.

In an analysis for ministers ahead of the Autumn Budget, SHAAP argues that recent falls in alcohol-specific deaths should not be taken as a reason to ease pressure on the industry, and says restoring the escalator would raise money for the public purse while reducing the toll on health services.

The number of alcohol-specific deaths in Scotland fell by three per cent in 2025, but the gap between the richest and poorest communities widened.

National Records of Scotland (NRS) figures show 1,152 alcohol-specific deaths were recorded in 2025, down from 1,185 in 2024.

But the death rate remained far higher in the most deprived areas, at 40.2 per 100,000 people, compared with 8.3 in the least deprived communities. The gap widened to 31.9 deaths per 100,000.

The group told UK ministers: “These gains are fragile and are not a signal to stop.”

It also says: “Alcohol duty is one of the few fiscal levers that raises revenue, reduces pressure on public services and prevents deaths at the same time.”

SHAAP, a partnership of Scotland’s Medical Royal Colleges and the Faculty of Public Health, based at the Royal College of Physicians of Edinburgh set out four proposals for the Chancellor: restoring the escalator, retaining the system that taxes drinks according to their alcohol strength, keeping tax relief for beer and other drinks sold in pubs, bringing cider duty into line with beer of the same strength, and directing a share of alcohol duty revenue towards treatment and prevention.

The escalator was introduced by then Chancellor Alistair Darling in 2008, when alcohol duty was increased by two per cent above inflation each year. It was phased out in 2014, after which duty freezes and cuts reduced the amount paid in real terms on many drinks.

SHAAP cites modelling by the University of Sheffield, prepared for the Institute of Alcohol Studies in 2019, which estimated that restoring the escalator would prevent 4,710 alcohol-attributable deaths in England and 420 in Scotland by 2032.

The same modelling estimated there would be 160,760 fewer hospital admissions in England and 8,719 fewer in Scotland, with NHS savings of £794m and £46m respectively.

The group also points to the potential revenue from increasing duty on alcohol sold outside pubs and other licensed premises.

It cites analysis by the Alcohol Health Alliance estimating that applying an escalator to non-draught alcohol could raise £3.4bn over five years, equivalent to the salaries of more than 37,000 nurses or police officers over that period.

SHAAP says the approach would give the Treasury a more predictable stream of revenue and remove the uncertainty surrounding annual decisions on duty rates. It argues that the changes could be made within the existing tax system, without introducing a new method of collecting duty.

They said that the escalator was "sound tax design". "It gives producers certainty, removes the annual uncertainty around uprating and requires no change to how duty is collected. Tobacco duty already works this way," they said.

Alcohol-specific deaths in Scotland fell by seven per cent in 2024 to 1,185and was the lowest annual total since 2019, but the group says Scotland’s rate of 20.9 deaths per 100,000 people at that ponit remains among the highest in the UK.

SHAAP argues that the figures show progress has been made but that the level of harm remains high. It points to Scotland’s minimum unit pricing policy, which sets a minimum price for each unit of alcohol, as evidence that measures affecting the price of alcohol can reduce harm.

An independent evaluation published in The Lancet in 2023 estimated that minimum unit pricing was associated with 13.4% ewer deaths wholly attributable to alcohol than would otherwise have been expected during the first 32 months of the policy. SHAAP’s briefing says the policy averted an estimated 156 deaths and 411 hospital admissions each year, with the largest reductions among men and people living in the most deprived areas.

The group says duty increases and minimum unit pricing work best in combination, arguing that duty applies across the UK while minimum pricing is a devolved measure in Scotland.

They also argue that recent increases in duty have not restored the purchasing power of alcohol taxes to earlier levels. Although duty rose in line with inflation in February 2025 and February 2026, SHAAP says rates remain well below their 2012-13 levels after allowing for inflation.

Its figures show duty on beer is 32% lower in real terms than in 2012-13, while duty on draught beer is 42% lower. Duty on cider and spirits is 26% lower, draught cider is 36% lower and wine is 19% lower.

SHAAP estimates that cumulative duty cuts and freezes since 2013-14 will have cost the Exchequer £31.2bn by 2031 compared with a policy of increasing duty in line with inflation. Alcohol duty raised around £12.4bn in 2025-26, according to HM Revenue and Customs figures.

The analysis compares that revenue with the estimated cost of alcohol harm. It cites a figure of £27.4bn a year for England and an estimated £5bn to £10bn annually for Scotland, based on research by the Institute of Alcohol Studies and the Social Market Foundation.

SHAAP said: “Duty does not come close to meeting the alcohol harm related costs the public purse carries.”

The proposals also seek to change how duty applies to different types of alcohol. The system was reformed in 2023 so that duty rates are more closely linked to the amount of alcohol in a drink. SHAAP says the principle should be retained and opposes a return to the previous wine tax concession.

They also call for a share of alcohol duty revenue to be directed towards treatment and prevention through sustained, multi-year funding. SHAAP cites estimates that every £1 invested in alcohol treatment in England returns £3 in social value, rising to £26 over ten years.

The group says alcohol treatment is often under-represented in combined drug and alcohol funding and calls for specific, sustained investment, including funding for Scotland through the relevant budget arrangements.

The Treasury has been asked to respond to SHAAP’s proposals ahead of the Autumn Budget.
