The principal of Scotland's largest independent school group has warned that the political battle over private school VAT risks creating a false divide between the state and independent sectors.

Anthony Simpson, principal of the newly merged Erskine Stewart Melville (ESM) school in Edinburgh, said the debate surrounding Labour's decision to impose VAT on private school fees had become increasingly divisive and overlooked the extensive partnership work taking place between schools.

His intervention comes at a time of significant change for independent education, with many schools across the UK assessing the impact of the loss of the sector's longstanding VAT exemption.

However, Mr Simpson said the discussion had become dominated by politics rather than the reality of how many schools operate.

"The VAT narrative and language has created an awful lot of division," he said.

"It is really deeply frustrating to have that constant political agenda that's almost put the schools at loggerheads."

Mr Simpson argued that independent schools and their state-sector counterparts frequently work together through shared projects, community programmes and educational partnerships that benefit pupils on both sides.

He pointed to initiatives involving local schools, including STEM programmes led by senior pupils, wellbeing events and sports inclusion days that bring children from different educational backgrounds together.

"Our senior school children who go and do a term's worth of STEM activities in a local primary school get as much benefit as those primary school children get from working with us," he said.

His comments come just months after the launch of the new ESM school, created through the merger of The Mary Erskine School, Stewart's Melville College and the ESMS junior school and nursery.

The move has created a co-educational school serving pupils aged three to 18 and represents one of the most significant restructurings in Scottish independent education in recent years.

Mr Simpson said the merger was conceived before the VAT changes and was designed to future-proof the school while improving the experience of pupils.

The transformation has included major investment in facilities, giving younger pupils access to specialist science, robotics, product design, drama, music and computing spaces previously associated with senior-school education.

A new wellbeing hub has also been established in a former boarding house, reflecting what Mr Simpson described as growing parental expectations around mental health and pupil support.

"When I arrived five years ago, learning and wellbeing were on different pedestals," he said.

"We've got a senior deputy learning and a senior deputy wellbeing in both schools. They're on equal footing."

While the school's facilities and structure have changed, Mr Simpson insisted its underlying purpose remains the same.

"As a school, you want to be brilliant at learning, and you want to have amazing wellbeing, and you want to look after your community," he said.

"In that, nothing has changed."
