Jenny Gilruth has suggested Andy Burnham's cautious Budget could be a sign he is holding back for an early general election.

The Deputy First Minister said the low-key tax and spending plans did not necessarily match the Prime Minister's rhetoric.

She told The Herald: "This budget, if you're a new Prime Minister or a new Chancellor, coming in with all these opportunities at your disposal, it's quite a restrictive budget. It's about maintaining spending limits.

"It's about really I suppose not rattling the markets. It's not about helping folk necessarily. It's not about turning on the tax for public services. So I think that's interesting in many ways."

She added: "You could also argue that the PM might be deciding to hold things back for an early general election."

On whether the SNP wanted one, she said: "We're always prepared in the SNP for an early general election. We would welcome it, of course. But, you know, it remains to be seen."

Ms Gilruth said: "I do think today's budget there's so much that's not there. If you compare the content of today's budget with the PM's speech at Labour Party Conference, the two do not match. So there's an incoherence there which suggests perhaps that something might be around the corner."

She added: "But we'll have to wait and see. As with all UK governments, we never know who will be Prime Minister next week."

The Prime Minister was asked if he would call an election in the next year as he visited a community centre in central London after the budget.

He said: “I don’t know how many times I’ve done this now, but yes, I can rule out calling a general *election* in the next 12 months.

“I have said repeatedly since coming into this job, I’m not thinking about myself.

“I’m not thinking about my own party and its prospects.

“I am thinking about people, and I’m thinking about what I can do to help them to have that care for people, which I spoke about on my first day in Downing Street.”

Meanwhile, Ms Gilruth confirmed the Scottish Government will pass on the £100 energy payment announced in the Budget, but warned it leaves Holyrood facing a real-terms cut of around £650 million next year.

The payment for households in council tax bands A to D in England was Chancellor John Healey's big rabbit out of the hat in his first Budget.

He told MPs the £2.6 billion scheme would "take the pressure off in the coldest months". It will be paid from January 2027 as a council tax rebate. As he set out his tax and spending plans in the Commons, he called on the Scottish, Welsh and Northern Ireland governments "to do the same".

![Chancellor of the Exchequer John Healey and Prime Minister Andy Burnham meet with local residents in the cafe of the Abbey community centre in Westminster](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21643619.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Chancellor of the Exchequer John Healey and Prime Minister Andy Burnham meet with local residents in the cafe of the Abbey community centre in Westminster *(Image: PA)*

The Treasury says the Budget brings an extra £600 million for the Scottish Government between 2026-27 and 2029-30, most of it consequentials from the payment and from business rates relief in England. That is on top of £1.7bn from the 2025 Budget and Spring Forecast 2026.

However, Ms Gilruth said Scottish Government analysis suggests the headline figure hides a cut. She said the resource block grant will fall by 1.3% in real terms in 2027-28, around £550m, and the capital budget by 1.5%, around £100m.

Speaking to journalists at Holyrood, she said: "The estimates I have from the officials just now is £650 million real terms cut for next year. That's going to make the decisions I take next year even harder."

Ms Gilruth added: "I'm not going to sugarcoat it. It's really challenging just now in terms of the gap."

In their analysis, the Fraser of Allander Institute said there would be an increase in Barnett consequentials on the revenue side and reductions on capital in the years ahead, and that the capital cuts appeared to reflect the reprioritisation of capital spending to support defence.

It said the picture was complicated by a technical adjustment affecting public sector pensions. Pensions Minister Torsten Bell said that adjustment would add to Scotland's spending power on top of the £600m, because Scotland has a larger public sector workforce relative to its population. Ms Gilruth said officials were still seeking further clarity from the UK Government on some of the points.

The FAI also said part of the new money will come as Financial Transactions funding, which can only be used for loans and equity investments, rather than day-to-day spending.

The Office for Budget Responsibility's new forecasts, which the FAI summarised, show inflation peaking at 3.8% at the start of 2027 and staying above the Bank of England's 2% target until mid-2028.

Asked whether the settlement would force her to accelerate public sector reform, Ms Gilruth said it would change the decisions she could take. She said she and Public Service Reform Minister Ivan McKee would engage ahead of the Scottish Budget on December 3.

Ms Gilruth said the Scottish Government welcomed the payment but that it fell "far short" of what was needed. She said it does not come close to the UK Government's promise to cut energy bills by £300. She said: "The UK Government has failed to meet the moment and has failed to keep its promise."

Asked how the payment would reach the right households, she acknowledged problems with council tax bands, which date back to the 1990s. She said reform was not possible before the money was due: "We're not going to be able to fix council tax banding before this funding comes. We need to get this funding out of the door. We'll need to help folk this winter. That's what this money is about."

The minister said the government would work with Cosla on the best way to get it into people's bank accounts. Asked if she would go beyond the £100, she suggested that would be for budget negotiations with the other parties.

She also attacked the Prime Minister over devolution: "The Prime Minister has previously claimed that he would respect devolution and the Scottish Government — but his actions do not match his words. Cutting Scotland's budget shows that, yet again, we are being treated as an afterthought."

She said the Budget contained no further devolution of powers, adding: "It's one thing to come forward with the rhetoric, but it's another to deliver action through your budget."

The Chancellor also announced a new British data centre covenant, with strict tests requiring data centres to invest in local communities, create local jobs and protect local water supplies. In return, he said, critical data centres will get the grid connections they need and, where necessary, be allowed to build onsite generation while they wait.

Ms Gilruth said the plans mirrored work the Scottish Government set out in August. She said: "The judgements we've arrived at in Scotland are those balancing community risks, community engagement, community views with that of the need for economic growth. So, we think there's some overlap here in terms of the approach."

The Budget also contained several Scotland-specific measures.

The Treasury has promised £18m to fund a UK Advanced Chip Design Academy led by the University of Edinburgh, and £6m, subject to a business case, will expand Glasgow's Scottish Event Campus, which hosted COP26 and the Commonwealth Games.

A further £20m will create two Defence Technical Excellence Colleges, in the east and west of Scotland.

The 4% tariff on kerosene will be removed, which the Treasury says will help the 142,000 Scottish households that heat with oil.

Scottish Secretary Douglas Alexander said: "This is a budget that puts money back in the pockets of Scottish households, backs businesses, and puts Scotland firmly at the heart of the UK government's plan for growth and reindustrialisation.

"We're helping families tackle the cost of living by cutting the standing charges from gas bills, saving households around £45 a year, then looking to remove them altogether. We're also extending the VAT cut on energy bills, as well as removing the tariff on heating oil.

"But we're not just easing the pressure today, we're building Scotland's future. £18 million for a semiconductor manufacturing research centre at the University of Edinburgh, £6 million to expand the Scottish Event Campus and grow on the legacy of COP26. That's creating jobs and good growth in every postcode."

Scottish Labour leader Michael Marra said the Budget gave "breathing space for today and fresh hope for tomorrow". He said the energy payment money "should be used to give Scots breathing space, not to plug holes in the Scottish Government Budget."

Scottish Conservative leader Russell Findlay said: "People across Scotland and the rest of the UK will be forgiven for asking 'is that it?'. John Healey's first Budget was a damp squib which will do nothing to boost economic growth."

He said the Budget was also "a massive disappointment" for the oil and gas sector because Rosebank had not been approved and the energy profits levy had not been scrapped.

Scottish Liberal Democrat leader Alex Cole-Hamilton said: "This Budget was missing a real growth plan with Europe at its heart. Without this it can't match up to the scale of the cost of living crisis."

He welcomed the removal of standing charges but said it fell far short of his party's energy guarantee.

Scottish Greens co-leader Ross Greer said: "This budget was a chance to make oil and gas giants fund a cut in household energy bills by taxing their huge profits. Instead of taking that action, the Chancellor has let these companies off the hook." He said Mr Burnham should "at the very least" deliver on further devolution.

Citizens Advice Scotland said the payment "amounts to a fraction of the rise to bills this year alone, never mind the huge hikes predicted for in January". Unite said it and the removal of gas standing charges "will not touch the sides" when many forecasts show bills jumping by over £400 in January.
