# Sir Titus Salt: The Yorkshire industrialist who was one of the pioneers of labour welfare in the UK by building the model village of Saltaire

## Sir Titus Salt was a Yorkshire industrialist, politician and philanthropist who helped to revolutionise labour welfare by building the textile model village of Saltaire.

Sir Titus Salt is best known for having built Salt’s Mill, a large textile mill, along with the attached village of Saltaire in West Yorkshire.

Another side to him that wasn’t often publicised was his involvement in politics both actively and financially.

In the 1830s, both he and his dad, Daniel, were founders of the Bradford Reform Party and he made a gift to the Bradford Mechanics Hall Building Fund, signalling support for the education of working people.

![Sir Titus Salt. (Pic credit: Hulton Archive / Getty Images)](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/heritage-and-retro/heritage/smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65.jpg)


## **Early life of Titus Salt**

Titus was born in 1803 to Daniel Salt, a drysalter, and Grace Smithies, the daughter of Isaac Smithies, of Old Manor House, Morley, where the Salt family lived.

In 1813, he and his family moved to a farm at Crofton near Wakefield and his dad became a sheep farmer.

Titus attended a day school that is considered to be connected with Salem Chapel in Wakefield and later he moved on to Batley Grammar School.

![Salt's Mill. (Pic credit: English Heritage / Getty Images)](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/heritage-and-retro/heritage/smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65.jpg)


He was close friends with his teacher at the day school called Enoch Harrison. In 1853, Enoch was a guest at Salt’s Mill’s opening banquet on Salt’s 50th birthday.

## **Titus Salt’s career**

His first job was stapling wool in Wakefield, but the family moved to Bradford in 1820, bringing that job to a close.

Whilst his dad was a dealer in wool, Titus spent two years learning about textile manufacture at William Rouse and Sons before joining his father’s company which traded in Russian Donskoi wool among others.

![Victoria Hall at the Saltaire Village World Heritage Site in Bradford. (Pic credit: Christopher Furlong / Getty Images)](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/heritage-and-retro/heritage/smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65.jpg)


Donskoi was commonly used in the woollens trade but not in the manufacture of worsted cloth. Titus tried to convince the Bradford spinners to use Donskoi in worsted manufacture, but was unsuccessful.

So the father and son decided to utilise it themselves and set up as spinner and manufacturer which turned out to be a success.

The Salt business went through various significant changes between the year of 1833 and 1835.

Daniel retired and Titus’s brother Edward joined the business before Titus branched out on his own and the family business was dissolved.

![A mill built by Sir Titus Salt next to River Aire. (Pic credit: Bethany Clarke / Getty Images)](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/heritage-and-retro/heritage/smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65.jpg)


During this timeframe, Titus experimented with alpaca wool from Peru and the first record of Salt encountering the wool is in his Day Book. On June 27, 1835, Salt recorded one bag of ‘Peruvian wool’.

While he was not the first to work with fibre in England, he was the manufacturer of the lustrous and subsequently popularised cloth called ‘alpaca’ and it was this that transformed Titus from a successful young businessman to the largest employer in Bradford.

In around 1850, he decided to build a mill large enough to consolidate his textile manufacture in one place.

## **The beginning of Saltaire**

Titus felt that Bradford was already overcrowded and didn’t want to add to it, so he bought land three miles from Bradford, in Shipley, next to the River Aire, the Leeds and Liverpool Canal and the Midland Railway.

In 1851, the construction of the factory started and Saltaire Mills, now known as Salt’s Mill, opened with a grand banquet on Titus’s 50th birthday, September 20, 1853.

The village of Saltaire was built with houses, almshouses, shops, schools, an infirmary, a club and institute, baths and washhouses in 1854.

In 1858-1859, he built the church, now called Saltaire United Reformed Church, later donating land where the Wesleyan chapel was built by public subscription in 1866-1868.

He famously banned ‘beershops’ in Saltaire, but it isn’t confirmed whether he was a teetotaler himself.

Titus was a private man and left no written statement of his purposes in creating Saltaire, but he told Lord Harewood at the opening of Salt’s Mill that he had built the place ‘to do good and to give his sons employment’.

An Australian economist Emil Sax wrote a book called Die Wohnungszustände der arbeitenden Classen und ihre Reform in 1869 in which he wrote that Titus’s main motive for building dwellings for his workers was to increase their productivity by shortening their walking commute, and interpreted the projects as ‘housing reform’.
