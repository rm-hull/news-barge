# How national education policy is holding back Yorkshire’s schools - Dr Gurmit Singh

## If you want to understand why young people across Yorkshire are being let down by national education policy, talk to a teacher trying to navigate the latest batch of directives coming out of London. You will not hear about a lack of ambition or a shortage of talent. You will hear about a system choked by administrative bloat, where managing compliance matters far more than actual teaching.

For years, successive governments have promised to raise standards across the North. We have seen endless initiatives, target-setting exercises, and reform packages passed down from civil servants who rarely step foot inside a school in Bradford, Leeds, or Hull.

Yet the reality on the ground remains unchanged. Teachers spend their evenings logging behaviour points on software platforms, filling out multi-page risk assessments for basic field trips, and auditing spreadsheet metrics designed in Westminster.

This is the administrative trap. It operates on the flawed assumption that educational success can be measured entirely on a spreadsheet. Instead of trusting headteachers and experienced educators to make decisions suited to their own communities, central government treats regional schools like branch offices of a national franchise.

![School children in a classroom. PIC: Danny Lawson/PA Wire](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/news/opinion/columnists/smart&trim=&width=640&quality=65.jpg)


The consequence for Yorkshire is severe. When rigid EBacc performance tables dictate every timetable, practical vocational links with local engineering firms in Sheffield or green technology hubs in Grimsby are quietly squeezed out. A school in a former industrial town faces entirely different economic and social realities than one in a wealthy London borough. Yet both are forced into the exact same narrow academic mould. When local leaders are forced to spend their energy pleasing distant inspectors rather than serving their students, regional talent suffers.

We see this most clearly in how classroom discipline and curriculum choices are handled. Teachers are forced to adopt one-size-fits-all behaviour policies and standardised marking schemes that prioritise audit trails over common sense. Rather than fostering genuine learning, schools are pushed into teaching to the test just to avoid an Ofsted downgrade. The passion that brought talented people into the profession is steadily replaced by bureaucratic fatigue.

Furthermore, this centralising urge treats regional disparity as an afterthought. When national funding formulas and curriculum changes are drawn up in Whitehall offices, they reflect the assumptions of the capital. They ignore the specific industrial heritage, transport networks, and community needs of Yorkshire towns. Local headteachers know where investment and flexibility are needed most, yet their hands are tied by national quotas and reporting requirements.

Yorkshire has never lacked talent, creativity, or grit. Our schools are full of capable young people who deserve an education that reflects their potential, delivered by teachers who are free to teach. But as long as education policy is driven by London-centric bureaucracy, our regional schools will remain handcuffed.

Real reform does not require another high-sounding strategy document from Whitehall. It requires simple common sense. We need to strip away the mountain of paperwork that ties teachers to their desks. We need to restore real authority to local headteachers who understand their pupils and their towns. Most of all, we need to stop treating education like an industrial assembly line.

If the government is serious about delivering for the North, it should start by trusting Northern educators. Hand control back to the classrooms, cut the administrative dead wood, and let our schools get on with the job.

Dr Gurmit Singh holds a PhD in education from the University of Leeds.
