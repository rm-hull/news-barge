Patients face a postcode lottery in access to some of the latest medicines, according to a new report.

The Association of the British Pharmaceutical Industry (ABPI) has published a report revealing significant disparities in the prescribing of innovative treatments between NHS boards in Scotland.

The report, released on Thursday, October 29, suggests that these differences may be due to "unwarranted variation" in patient access and avoidable barriers within the system.

Titled Analysing the adoption of SMC-accepted medicines in Scotland, the report was supported by analytics firm LCP Health and examines NHS prescribing patterns across all 14 Scottish NHS boards from September 2020 to August 2025.

It is the first study to compare several types of medicines across different regions in this way.

In Scotland, the Scottish Medicines Consortium (SMC) determines whether a new medicine is clinically effective and offers value for money.

Boards are expected to choose within 90 days whether to add it to their local list of medicines, known as a formulary.

Victoria Jordan, executive director of patient access at the ABPI, said: "Scotland has a strong and respected system for assessing new medicines.

"But this report shows that the use of some accepted medicines can vary widely between NHS boards, and that differences in patient need do not fully explain this.

"While the data we have does not tell us everything, it does show we need to look more closely at this issue.

"When avoidable barriers slow things down, patients can wait longer than they should for medicines already judged effective and good value.

"We need to plan and work together across government, the NHS, clinicians, patients, academia and industry.

"That way, we can help make sure national SMC decisions have benefit to patients, wherever they live."

The report found the greatest variation in access to GLP-1 medicines, which are used to treat type 2 diabetes and, in some cases, for weight management.

By the end of the study period, the Western Isles NHS board was prescribing these drugs at rates nearly 10 times higher than the lowest-prescribing board, Shetland.

This gap could not be fully explained by differences in diabetes and obesity prevalence.

In some areas, it took more than a year to add GLP-1 medicines to the local formulary.

The report also identified significant variation in the prescribing of non-statin cholesterol-lowering medicines for patients at high risk of heart attack and stroke.

By the end of the study, prescribing rates differed by a factor of eight to 10 between the highest-prescribing board, Highland, and the lowest, Forth Valley.

Boards in the west of Scotland generally had higher prescribing rates than those in the east.

The report suggested that the organisation of local services plays a key role.

Areas with stronger links between specialist lipid clinics and GPs tended to have higher prescribing rates.

However, the analysis did not adjust for differences in patient need or service provision.

The ABPI warned that many limitations in the available data restrict the conclusions that can be drawn and emphasised that the report does not rank NHS boards or provide explanations for individual variations.

The report makes seven recommendations for the Scottish Government and NHS Scotland, including earlier planning to ensure services are ready before SMC advice is published, greater transparency around formulary decisions, and additional support for complex medicines that require specialist services.

Other recommendations include piloting protected funding for high-impact medicines and closer collaboration between government, the NHS, academia, and industry.

The ABPI stressed the need for a "triple helix" approach to ensure that national decisions translate into real benefits for patients across the country.
