*(Image: Dumfries and Galloway College)*

Dumfries and Galloway College generated an estimated £142.1 million in annual economic and social benefits for Scotland last year, according to a new report.

The independent assessment, commissioned by the college and carried out by BiGGAR Economics, found that Dumfries and Galloway College delivered £12 in benefits for every £1 of public funding it received in 2024/25.

Its estimate combines £18.5m in economic impact from the college’s operations, supply chain, staff spending and investment; £58.4m in wellbeing benefits; £46.3m in social value linked to qualifications; and £19m in higher earnings associated with those qualifications.

The report comes at a time when Scotland’s college sector faces significant financial pressures, with multiple institutions forecasting cash flow difficulties over the next three years and a review underway to examine how the funding model might be reformed to change the status quo that multiple watchdog reports have described as “unsustainable”.

Douglas Dickson, principal and CEO of Dumfries and Galloway College, said that rural colleges and their students face significant transportation, economic, and social barriers. And yet, he said, the BiGGAR report highlights the "significant contribution” that further education makes to wider society.

“This report puts a clear value on what Dumfries and Galloway College delivers every day, both in terms of the outcomes our students achieve, and the opportunities those outcomes create for learners, employers and communities across our region and further afield.

“From developing skilled workers in essential sectors, to supporting communities and widening opportunities, our impact reaches far beyond the classroom.”

The report also outlines the challenging landscape in which the college operates.

Over the past 20 years, Dumfries and Galloway’s working-age population fell by 5,239 and is expected to fall by a further 12,451 by 2047. Although overall deprivation is comparatively low, the report identifies access to services as a significant issue: 39% of the region’s small areas fall within Scotland’s most access-deprived fifth.

Mr Dickson argued that “rural poverty is often hidden poverty” because on-the-ground challenges don't align with the most common measures, such as the Scottish Index of Multiple Deprivation (SIMD).

![Dumfries and Galloway College](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21642922.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Dumfries and Galloway College principal Douglas Dickson said the college has had to undergo several rounds of cuts to reach a level of balance. *(Image: Dumfries and Galloway College)*

As much as distance is an issue for rural students, he said, the availability of transportation is an entirely different challenge.

“Whilst I admire the efforts of the Scottish government to have a cap on travel and free bus passes for those that are under 22, the question for our students is not about being able to show a bus pass. It is: Is there a bus? And how does that bus link?"

Mr Dickson said the college has responded to this challenge by maintaining much of the online and hybrid delivery model that grew out of necessity during the Covid-19 pandemic. Around one-third of the total curriculum is now delivered online to some extent. However, Mr Dickson said the college’s strategy and mission should not rely too heavily on online delivery.

“What we are really conscious of is that online is not a solution to everything. It is a solution to transport, but there is a need for interaction.”

![Dumfries and Galloway College](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21642926.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The rurality of Dumfries and Galloway College presents unique challenges for its students. *(Image: Dumfries and Galloway College)*

He said hybrid learning provides opportunities for students who might otherwise be shut out of education because of care responsibilities, part-time work, or unreliable travel.

“If a student misses a bus in the morning, the cost of a taxi is £50 or more,” Mr Dickson said. “So instead, they are able to go back home and still participate in the class.”

He also explained how digital tools have replaced a “rainforest worth of paper” for brickwork apprentices, who can now record work on their phones, submit photos and video to lecturers, receive feedback, and maintain a digital assessment tracker they can also share with employers.

The BiGGAR report credits the college with supporting 320 jobs across Scotland in 2024/2025, 290 of which are in the local authority area. It also estimates that college-issued qualifications generated £19m in wage premiums. The report described the college as offering a high proportion of permanent contracts and above-average pay.

But Mr Dickson said the college achieved this position after a series of difficult internal restructuring efforts. When he joined the college more than six years ago, he said it ran a £1m deficit and had weak systems for financial and curriculum planning.

“We had three voluntary severance runs. At every round, what we asked ourselves was: What do we need for the future? And actually, is there a need to create some headroom to be able to do something different?”

![Dumfries and Galloway College](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21642923.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Renewable energy, engineering, childcare, and health and social care are among the in-demand skills courses for which the college has reached capacity. *(Image: Dumfries and Galloway College)*

As with most colleges, staff pay remains the highest cost, at just under 70% of total expenditure.

Mr Dickson insisted that making decisions early, before the deficit grew too wide, allowed the college to restore financial balance while retaining enough to invest in digital learning, local partnerships and skills.

Like many in the sector, he said the college has aligned its course offerings with in-demand sectors of the economy, including renewable energy, construction, early years and social care.

However, demand already outstrips the college’s capacity in many areas.

“Over this year, in the areas of engineering, construction, early years, and health and social care, I could not accommodate over 200 people who were interviewed and were suitable for college,” Mr Dickson said.

This challenges the assumption that demographic decline necessarily reduces demand, he said.

A partnership with the University of the West of Scotland (UWS) offers degree opportunities to local students, and the Stranraer campus hosts Skills Development Scotland and other important community resources for students and non-students.

“It would be naïve to assume that a college can do everything itself,” he said. “But funding a rural college makes a significant difference.”
