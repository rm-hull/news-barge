ATO could have done better on credit card ban, Mulino says

Sarah Basford Canales

The assistant treasurer, Daniel Mulino, says the tax office was dealing with a “complex” problem when deciding how to address the ban on credit card payment surcharges but, with the benefit of hindsight, it could have done better.

Speaking on ABC’s Radio National this morning, Mulino said the tax commissioner, Rob Heferen, said the reforms were really important and the Australian Taxation Office had been concurrently trying to negotiate with credit card companies while determining an adequate transition period.

The ATO stopped accepting credit card payments after the ban came into effect at the beginning of October. Heferen previously warned it would cost the ATO about $200m a year if it could not pass on the banking and credit card fees on to customers.

The ATO will now extend accept credit card payments for an extended transition period until the end of June next year.

Overnight, Heferen told a Senate estimates hearing he first realised the Reserve Bank’s surcharge ban would be a “real issue to grapple with” back in April.

The tax boss said he had received a letter from Mulino on 28 April informing the tax office of the RBA’s reforms, which brought the proposal to a “sharp head”.

Heferen told the hearing:

If we accept credit cards, it needs to be funded, and ceasing credit cards will have an effect on people who use credit cards. And some of our discussion was, ‘OK, what’s the appropriate transition for people up to transition through?’ And so that awareness, fair to say, around June, July [2026], we had staff before that who were already thinking about the issue, already formulating proposals.

Andrew Hastie says a lot of report ‘rings true’ with his experiences

Continuing from the previous post, Greens senator David Shoebridge argued there was a lack of accountability for the SAS leadership.

The thing that is eroding faith and trust … is this seeming accountability gap between what happens if you don’t have gold braid on your shoulders, where the book gets thrown at you … and the people who are in leadership positions who were either aware of it, or had the data that should have triggered that [awareness], and have not taken action.

Outside parliament, opposition frontbencher and former SAS captain Andrew Hastie said the report’s findings resonated with his experience in the regiment.

I lived a lot of what I read in that report. There’s almost 50 findings and a lot of it rings very true to me as a junior officer at the time.

He said officers were responsible for the actions of those they commanded.

‘Unacceptable behaviour’ within SAS, ADF chief says after report findings

Some Australian SAS officers knew that soldiers under their command were using “throwdowns” – planting weapons on dead Afghan civilians to portray them as combatants – but did nothing to stop the unlawful practice, a previously unpublished military report has said.

The executive summary of the report by the inspector general of the Australian defence force was quietly published online this week. And the chief of the ADF, Admiral Mark Hammond, was interrogated on the report in Senate estimates Thursday morning.

He said he agreed with the report findings, stating there had been “serious failures” within the SAS and its command structure. He said the report revealed:

Unacceptable behaviour … it is extremely unprofessional, that behaviour is absolutely not in concert with our values, our professional standards, the behaviour that we expect of our personnel

Hammond was asked specifically about the finding that legal officers within the defence force obstructed investigations into allegations of war crimes.

Cross-parliamentary women’s network launches, inspired by Haines

Female politicians across federal parliament will launch a women’s network to build stronger connections across parties after the alleged attack on Helen Haines and to encourage more women into politics.

The finance minister, Katy Gallagher, shadow minister Melissa McIntosh, Greens senator Sarah Hanson-Young and independent MP Rebekha Sharkie announced the establishment of the Network of Women in Parliament (NOW) this morning.

“Our purpose of NOW is to build connections, strengthen relationships and create an environment where women in parliament can share experiences, support one another and learn from each other beyond political lines,” they said in a joint statement.

While Labor has internal women’s groups, female MPs from other parties have told Guardian Australia they have struggled to establish similar networks of their own due to smaller numbers.

The statement launching NOW said the group came amid “a growing recognition of the value of stronger connections between women in parliament”.

The MPs said:

The devastating [alleged] attack on our parliamentary colleague Helen Haines has further highlighted the importance of coming together in support, solidarity and friendship across political divides.

Helen has always demonstrated the importance of kindness, compassion and genuine connection across political divides. In many ways, she embodies the very spirit of what NOW hopes to achieve.

We recognise that while our political views may differ, we share many of the same experiences, challenges and responsibilities as women in public life. Through friendship, trust and solidarity, we want to strengthen the voice of women in parliament, encourage more Australian women into public life and stand together when it matters.

As Indi MP, Helen Haines, continues to recover in hospital, her staff are preparing to reopen her electorate offices in Wangaratta and Wodonga. They will be open from 10am to 3pm, three days a week, between now and the end of the year.

“Thank you to everyone who continues to show such care and support for Helen, her family and our team,” Haines’s team said in a statement on Thursday.

We know there continues to be great concern for Helen. We won’t be providing updates on her health or circumstances, and we ask that the privacy of Helen and her family continues to be respected.

Thank you for your patience, kindness and understanding as we navigate the weeks ahead.

Haines has lost both of her legs as a result of last week’s alleged attack in Wangaratta. A 43-year-old man has been charged and the incident continues to be investigated by police.

Darwin teenager to face court on charges of collecting documents likely to facilitate a terrorist act

Nick Visser

An update from the AFP as the prime minister urges the Senate to pass his government’s latest social media reforms.

A teenager in Darwin is set to appear in children’s court today over charges of collecting documents likely to facilitate a terrorist act, as well as schematics for 3D-printed guns.

The Australian federal police said officials began an investigation earlier this month. They allege the teenager was the operator of several social media channels that accessed, discussed or reposted violent extremist material.

A search warrant was undertaken on Wednesday, where officials allegedly found documents and electronic devices that contained violent extremist material and instructions for the making of 3D-printed firearms.

The teenager faces several charges, which carry a maximum sentence of between five and 10 years’ imprisonment each.

NSW government says it’s not ‘sitting back and waiting for rain’ at Menindee

The NSW water minister, Rose Jackson, says “no one is underestimating the challenge at Menindee this summer” after water volumes at the Menindee Lakes fell to their lowest point in five years.

As we have reported this morning, experts have warned that unless conditions improve there is potential for fish kills in the region that suffered millions of fish deaths in 2018, 2019 and 2023.

Rose Jackson. Photograph: Steven Markham/AAP

Jackson says:

No one is underestimating the challenge at Menindee this summer, with lake levels now at their lowest point in five years and the prospect of another hot summer.

Our focus is on doing everything we can to protect water quality and native fish through the months ahead.

Significant inflows would improve conditions but we aren’t sitting back and waiting for rain.

Temporary restrictions are in place for some upstream water users. Jackson says the government has also installed a temporary fish passage to allow for better movement of native fish.

Taylor says country needs to get ‘balance right’ on datacentres

Angus Taylor, the opposition leader, said the country needs to get the “balance right” on AI, but powering datacentres with renewables only would not deliver for Australians.

Taylor spoke to reporters earlier, saying the debate around datacentres was “pretty commonsense”.

It’s like gas, you’ve got to get the balance right. And the balance is, put them in the right locations where there’s community support, and there will be locations with community support.

Critically, make sure you’ve got the energy you need. So these datacentres are not going to drive up the cost of energy for Australians.

I tell you, a renewables-only strategy is not going to deliver on this.

Man cautioned after allegedly stalking two SA members of parliament

A man who allegedly targeted two South Australian member of parliament has been cautioned.

SA police issued stalking cautions to the 44-year-old man on Tuesday afternoon and said the cautions related to separate alleged incidents involving two MPs that were not connected.

He was also arrested for a separate alleged breach of bail.

The state’s education minister, Lucy Hood, and another Labor MP, Ella Shaw, spoke about their alleged experiences during a press conference on Wednesday.

Shaw alleged she was involved in an incident in her community while working and said she made the report because she didn’t want “any young person in my electorate to feel the way I was made to feel”.

The education minister said it was “frustrating and unfortunate”, telling reporters:

It is a sad occurrence that the political discourse has moved to such a level that we see young women in our parliament who are having to put up with this behaviour.
