**Bus franchising process begins in York and North Yorkshire says DAVID SKAITH**

At the Labour Party Conference last month, the Prime Minister, Andy Burnham, set out Labour’s vision for Britain. One rooted in hope and aimed at addressing the failures of the last four decades of economic policy, which have put profit before people.

He has made some significant contributions to giving people some breathing room now, while also setting out some of the fundamental changes he wants to make to rebuild hope in this country.

We saw that plan in more detail at yesterday’s Budget. But you’ll be able to find plenty of commentary on that in today’s paper and over the next few weeks.

In my column today, I want to focus on putting the public at the heart of more of the industries and services they rely on. There’s a reason why London’s public transport system has been the envy of the rest of the country for years: it operates under a model of public control.

Transport for London sets the routes, fares and timetables, and specifies what buses should look like. Any profits from buses or the tube go straight back into those services, rather than lining the pockets of shareholders or investors.

That’s one version of a franchised public transport system that mayors across the country have decided to introduce in their areas. Greater Manchester’s Bee Network is already seeing passenger numbers grow, alongside greater opportunities for the public to shape what services look like. Liverpool City Region has just started introducing its franchised system, with South and West Yorkshire set to follow in the years to come.

I know that bus services in York and North Yorkshire are not good enough. While York is a national success story for bus services, with passenger numbers on the rise, bus companies can extract profits from our county to pay dividends to their shareholders. And if a route stops making money, they withdraw from it. This forces the taxpayer to step in and fund subsidised services. Operators can name their price, and in many cases the public purse cannot meet those exorbitant costs.

This model of private operators and public subsidy costs York and North Yorkshire’s taxpayers millions of pounds every year. I don’t think that’s how a public service should be run.

That’s why, last month, we took the first step towards greater public control of bus services in York and North Yorkshire.

You’ll have to forgive me for getting a bit technical here, but I think it’s important to set out exactly what happened and what it could mean in the years to come. Regaining control from private operators won’t be quick, easy or cheap. But it will be worth it.

Last month, I signed the first statutory document in the franchising process. The notice has a long, very formal-sounding name, but it is essentially the starting gun in a marathon. Issuing the ‘Statutory Notice of Intent to Prepare an Assessment of a Proposed Bus Franchising Scheme’ is a mayoral power, and I’m very proud to have been able to use it.

It means we now have greater access to information from operators, allowing us to develop a full business case for a franchising scheme in York and North Yorkshire. That work will take time. We expect a full public consultation on our proposals, alongside any alternative arrangements that operators wish to put forward, to take place in late spring or early summer 2028.

If the public consultation is positive and everything is in place, the mayor (who, as the eagle-eyed among you will have noticed, may or may not be me after May 2028) will make a final decision on whether to introduce a franchising scheme across the area.

Experience elsewhere shows that this stage can also take a few years, with the scheme introduced in phases across the area.

This has never been done in a rural area like York and North Yorkshire, and I’m aware of the additional complexities this creates for our ambitions. But I don’t think it’s right to say that, simply because it could cost a little more or be more difficult, it isn’t the right thing to do.

North Yorkshire lost more bus mileage from 2010 than any other area in the North. That’s not fair on people living in Skipton, Scarborough, Harrogate, Thirsk, Selby or any of our market towns and rural villages.

This is about ensuring the public get the services they deserve. This is about finally saying enough is enough and refusing to accept the managed decline of rural services. This is about putting the public back at the heart of public transport.
