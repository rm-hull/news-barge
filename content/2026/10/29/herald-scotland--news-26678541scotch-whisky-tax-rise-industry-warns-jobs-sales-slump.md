Scotch whisky drinkers are handing over 74p in every pound to the taxman as the industry warns that soaring costs and falling sales are threatening jobs across Scotland.

The Scotch Whisky Association (SWA) says VAT and excise duty together account for 74% of the price of an average bottle, following three increases in excise duty in three years. It has been pushing for a cut in spirits duty in the arguing that the tax system is hitting whisky producers harder than makers of other alcoholic drinks.

In a briefing seen by ministers, the trade body warned that the industry had already been forced to pause production, restructure operations and close sites or visitor centres as businesses struggle with rising costs and an uncertain global market. It said further pressure could cause "lasting damage" to an industry that supports 41,000 jobs in Scotland.

The industry has already suffered significant job losses. The association said that more than 1,000 direct jobs had been lost in the 10 months following the October 2024 Budget, equivalent to 2.7% of the sector's directly employed workforce at the time.

The association warned: "The Scotch whisky sector is currently facing a range of challenges and opportunities as it looks towards the coming decades. Despite its strength, the sector will not be able to continue delivering growth on the scale seen previously without the support of both the UK and Scottish Governments.

"We have found that government does not always take a joined-up approach to the sector and can concentrate its support on whisky’s global trading success, without as much focus on its domestic position.”

The association says the current tax system is not delivering the revenue the Treasury expected. Its September evidence states that spirits duty has risen by 17% over three years, but receipts from the tax fell by £94m in 2025-26 compared with the previous financial year.

The association argues that higher taxes have pushed up prices and discouraged consumers from buying spirits, undermining the aim of raising more money for public services. It wants a Treasury review of alcohol duty to address what it sees as a structural disadvantage for whisky and other spirits.

![Graphic of John Healey](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21632744.jpg?type=mds-article-620)John Healey *(Image: NQ)*

But on Wednesday, the Chancellor John Healey confirmed in the Autumn Budget that spirits duty will rise by 3.9% in February 2027 under an inflation-linked tax hike. The increase is expected to add roughly 35p to 37p to the price of a standard bottle of gin or whisky, dealing a major blow to distillers.

Under the current system before the latest budget announcement, duty on spirits was at £33.99 per litre of pure alcohol, compared with £30.62 for wine, £19.45 for draught beer and £10.39 for cider, according to the SWA. A 40% strength Scotch whisky therefore pays around 1.7 times as much duty per litre of alcohol as draught beer, compared with 1.5 times before the 2023 reforms.

The trade body says the difference is particularly stark when individual drinks are compared. A 25ml measure of Scotch whisky at 40% alcohol contains one unit and attracts around 34p in duty, while a pint of 6.7% cider contains around 3.8 units but attracts the same total duty under the draught relief arrangements.

The SWA argues that the system penalises spirits even when consumers drink less alcohol than they would in some other drinks. It says the difference is also affecting the prices charged by shops, pubs and restaurants, with spirits becoming more expensive relative to beer.

After allowing for underlying producer costs, spirits prices in shops had risen by close to 18% relative to beer since August 2023, according to the association's evidence. In pubs and restaurants, the comparable price ratio has risen by around 17%.

The SWA says sales volumes have fallen alongside the relative price increases. Spirits volumes sold through shops dropped by around 12% since August 2023, compared with a fall of around 5% for beer. In pubs and restaurants, spirits volumes fell by around 16%, four times the decline in beer.

The association says the impact extends well beyond distilleries. Spirits account for 33% of sales and 38% of profits in hospitality, according to their analysis meaning that lower sales can affect the financial position of pubs, bars and restaurants already facing higher operating costs.

After the Chancellor's latest announcement, the SWA said the fourth consecutive duty increase on spirits would have "damaging consequences for the sector and supply chains" and said it was pressing the Treasury for "systemic reform to provide greater fairness for Scotch Whisky".

![Mark Kent, chief executive of the Scotch Whisky Association](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/17825498.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Mark Kent says the latest duty increase was a "disappointing blow to the Scotch Whisky sector" *(Image: SWA)*

Mark Kent, chief executive of the SWA said the latest duty increase was a "disappointing blow to the Scotch Whisky sector, its supply chain and the local communities it supports".

He said: “We’ve... warned that increasing the spirits super tax will not only pile further costs on distillers, but will harm the supply chain and reduce Treasury revenue. Nobody wins when hard-pressed businesses are taxed to the hilt.

“The UK Government should listen to business and pay attention to the evidence."

The industry is also under pressure from overseas markets, where around 90% of Scotch whisky is sold. Exports were worth £5.36billion in 2025, but their value fell by 1.8% and volume by 4.3% compared with 2024, according to the SWA.

The United States, the industry's largest export market by value, was particularly affected by tariffs introduced in April 2025.

The association says export volumes to the US fell by 15% over the remainder of 2025 and by a further 10% in the first six months of 2026. The tariffs were lifted in July 2026, but the SWA says the benefits of improved trading conditions will take time to be felt.

The cost of energy is another concern. The association cites UK Government figures showing that average industrial electricity prices in 2024 were the highest among countries covered by the International Energy Agency, standing 63.1% above the agency's median.

Some distilleries have reported waiting times of up to eight years for electricity network upgrades, according to the SWA. It says those delays restrict investment in electric boilers, heat pumps and other equipment that could reduce emissions and improve energy efficiency. Switching from natural gas to electricity can increase costs by more than 200% for a distiller, the SWA say.

The association argues that reducing the tax burden would help businesses invest, protect employment and compete in overseas markets. It says a more competitive home market is essential if the industry is to turn its international opportunities into new investment and jobs in Scotland.
