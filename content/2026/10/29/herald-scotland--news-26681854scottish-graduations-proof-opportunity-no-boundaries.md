*Celebrating graduates’ achievements while also championing the power of flexible learning to unlock talent and opportunity is the goal of The Open University in Scotland, writes Principal Martin Boyle*

Graduation is one of the most rewarding moments in any university’s calendar. This year’s ceremonies for The Open University in Scotland were particularly special, not only because of the qualifications our students earned, but because of what those achievements represent for their families and communities.

Among this year’s graduation cohort of 1,735, a total of 815 told us that neither parent had attended higher education. That is almost half of our graduating class.

For those students, crossing the stage meant more than receiving a qualification. It was a family first, achieved through years of determination, often alongside work, caring responsibilities and the demands of everyday life.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21647110.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Principal Martin Boyle *(Image: Supplied)*

Our recent ceremonies in Glasgow also recognised four remarkable honorary graduates: Paralympic cyclist Jenny Holl, education advocate Marlyn Somers and Scottish writers Jenni Fagan and Len Pennie.

Their different paths reflect a principle at the heart of The Open University; education should be shaped around people’s lives, rather than requiring people to reshape their lives around education.

That principle matters well beyond graduation day.

Across Scotland, talent and ambition reside right across our towns and cities, to our island and rural communities, and everyone should have the same opportunity to develop their skills, advance their careers and fulfil their potential.

We believe, for example, that when physical mobility is restricted, educational mobility must expand.

It should not be necessary to move away from home, family or employment to access high-quality learning. Flexible education makes it possible to gain qualifications while remaining rooted in the communities that need those skills.

The timing could hardly be more important. Rural and remote Scotland is entering a period of extraordinary economic opportunity, with more than £100 billion of projected investment linked to renewable energy, infrastructure, advanced manufacturing and innovation-led industries.

Investment on that scale offers real promise, but buildings and technology alone will not deliver lasting prosperity. People with the right knowledge and skills will. That is why flexible ‘earn and learn’ routes must be central to Scotland’s workforce planning.

In sectors such as nursing, social work, teaching, engineering and digital industries, employers need routes through which existing staff can develop professionally and new entrants can build careers without stepping away from their income or community.

![Open University in Scotland graduates Jill Wardrop and Rachael Stirling with their tutor Dawn Davison](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21647091.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Scottish writer Len Pennie *(Image: Supplied)*

Our approach allows students to combine supported learning with their other commitments.

It also creates opportunities for employers to develop and retain local talent.

For rural communities, that connection between education and employment can be transformative.

The Open University in Scotland brings more than flexible courses to this challenge.

Through partnerships with employers, entrepreneurial support programmes and decades of experience in supported online learning, we can help connect opportunity with the people best placed to seize it.

Our ambition is not simply to prepare students for jobs, but to enable them to contribute to the future of the places they call home.

In the 2026 National Student Survey, 90.8% of our students in Scotland were satisfied with the overall quality of their course, placing us second in Scotland for student satisfaction.

Those figures matter, but they are not the whole story. Each graduate has navigated a different set of circumstances to reach this point. Some are beginning new careers, others are progressing in roles they already hold, and many are demonstrating to their families that higher education can be within reach.

As we reflect on this year’s ceremonies, I hope the message travels far beyond the graduation hall. Scotland’s future depends on opening doors to people wherever they live and whatever their background.

As Scotland’s national university for widening access and lifelong learning, our commitment is to keep those doors open, helping more people learn, earn and build their futures, whatever their circumstances and ambitions.
