CNN poll finds Democrats leading Senate races in Texas and Michigan, tight contests in Maine and Kansas

With days until the midterm elections, a new poll from CNN places Democrats well in the lead in key Senate races in Texas and Michigan, while projecting tighter contests in Kansas and Maine.

In Texas, the poll found 49% of likely voters would back James Talarico, the Democratic nominee, over Republican Ken Paxton, who netted just 43%.

In Michigan, it found Democrat Abdul El-Sayed had received support from 49% of likely voters compared with 42% for his opponent, Mike Rogers.

The poll found no clear winner in the Senate contests in either Maine or Kansas. Maine was once viewed as Democrats best chance to flip a Senate seat, as they aimed to oust Republican incumbent Susan Collins. But the race has been marred by the late replacement of the original Democratic nominee, populist Graham Platner, with Troy Jackson, after Platner dropped out following allegations of sexual misconduct.

Kansas has been a far more unexpected battleground, with Democrat Adam Hamilton mounting a stiff challenge against Republican Roger Marshall in the normally deep-red state.

Senate race in once reliably Republican South Carolina tied in final stretch – poll

Joseph Gedeon

A US Senate race in one of the most Republican states in the country is now a dead heat, according to a new poll showing Democrat Annie Andrews tied with Darline Graham, the Republican incumbent senator.

The Palmetto State has not elected a Democrat to the Senate since 1998, and Donald Trump won it by nearly 18 points in 2024, but a New York Times/Siena poll this week has the two candidates neck and neck, at 48% each.

Graham was appointed to the seat after her brother, Lindsey Graham, died in July, and she entered the race at Trump’s urging, without having ever held elected office.

Her inexperience has become one of the defining aspects of the campaign, which culminated in an August debate when she admitted she was “not that informed on national security” when asked about US policy on Taiwan. In the poll, 51% of voters said she lacked the right experience to be an effective senator.

Andrews, a pediatrician from Charleston, has meanwhile built her campaign around her medical career. Her solution to better access to doctors and climbing medical bills has been to advocate for a public option and cheaper prescription drugs.

Obama-era investigation reportedly found some evidence against Ilhan Omar's marriage

An Obama-era federal investigation of Minnesota representative Ilhan Omar found some evidence suggesting that she entered into a sham marriage in 2009 to help a man suspected of being her brother obtain a green card, according to the New York Times (paywall).

At issue is Omar’s 2009 marriage, when she was already a US citizen, to Ahmed Nur Said Elmi, a British citizen whom she divorced in 2017.

Current and former officials told the paper that the FBI and the Department of Homeland Security investigated the marriage in the summer of 2016, but the case stalled chiefly because prosecutors concluded that the suspected crime fell outside a five-year statute of limitations.

The Times notes that it remains unclear what evidence investigators had that suggested the marriage was fraudulent and that the person Omar married was her brother.

Marrying someone for the sole purpose of helping them obtain a green card is illegal under federal law, while marrying a sibling is illegal under state law.

Omar has denied the allegations. Spokesperson Jackie Rogers told the NYT in a statement:

Rep. Omar has repeatedly and unequivocally denied this baseless, disgusting conspiracy theory. This is a desperate political smear designed to distract from Trump’s failed presidency and two years of Republican failure in Congress.

Judge rebuffs Trump DoJ’s bid to force California to set aside 20,000 ballots

Sam Levine

A federal judge declined to order California election officials to segregate and not count ballots from people the Trump administration claimed were non-citizens, rebuffing a legally thin request from the justice department that appeared designed to cast doubt over the results of the midterms.

The ruling from US district judge David Carter, nominated to the federal bench by Bill Clinton, comes about two weeks after the justice department delivered a USB drive to election officials in Los Angeles and Sacramento that it said contained the names of 20,000 non-citizens on the state’s voter rolls. There are more than 23 million registered voters in California and non-citizen voting is extremely rare.

“This case is about the right of every American citizen to vote in a free and fair election,” Carter wrote in his Wednesday evening ruling. “Never before in American history has the federal government, on the eve of an election, sought to coerce a state and local government to abandon their own Constitutional elections procedures and bend to the will of the Executive Branch.”

“In bringing this lawsuit, the Executive would have this Court believe that it reigns as a sole sovereign,” Carter continued. “But California and its people are sovereign, too.”

The justice department gave officials just 48 hours to review the list and said it would sue the state if officials did not sequester ballots from those whose citizenship it could not confirm. The department refused to provide more details about its methodology and Los Angeles officials found preliminary indicators the list was inaccurate. The justice department then sued California on 15 October to try and force the state to sequester the ballots and verify citizenship.
