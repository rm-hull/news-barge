Scientists have given a human patient a virus designed by artificial intelligence for the first time, in an effort to fight infections that no longer respond to antibiotics.

California-based AI startup Radical Numerics said a patient with a serious bacterial infection received an experimental treatment containing viruses created by its AI model, Omnii-1.

The model designs bacteriophages, or phages, which are viruses that infect and kill bacteria without targeting human cells. Doctors already use phage therapy as an experimental treatment for serious bacterial infections, particularly when patients have few other options.

Rather than searching for a virus that could kill the patient's bacteria, Omnii-1 redesigned part of a virus so it could better recognize and attack the specific bacterial strain causing the infection. The company says the AI-driven process could eventually take just days.

The treatment was administered through a special program that allows doctors to try experimental therapies when conventional options are limited.

![Radical Numerics’ AI model, Omnii-1, designs bacteriophages, or viruses that kill bacteria without targeting human cells, for use in experimental treatments for serious infections](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/29/19/05/Untitled-design-(2).png?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Radical Numerics said this was the first time AI-designed phages had been used to treat a human patient. The announcement was published on the company's website but has not been confirmed in a peer-reviewed study, and the company has not said whether the treatment helped the patient.

The approach could offer another option for treating hard-to-treat infections. Doctors typically have to find a naturally occurring virus that can target the specific bacteria causing a patient's illness.

Radical Numerics hopes its AI model can speed up that process by designing viruses to target a patient's particular infection, potentially helping doctors find a treatment faster.

The company said it tested its AI-designed viruses against bacteria taken from the patient before administering the treatment. The virus mixture eliminated the patient's antibiotic-resistant bacterial sample in laboratory testing, according to the company.

However, it has not released details about the patient's diagnosis, the treatment's outcome, or whether the infection was ultimately cleared.

The development comes amid growing interest in using AI to accelerate medical research, alongside concerns about how the technology could be misused.

In August, researchers at Stanford University and the Arc Institute announced that they had used AI to design previously unseen viruses capable of killing E. coli bacteria in laboratory experiments. Of nearly 300 AI-designed viral genomes synthesized and tested, 16 produced functional viruses. Those experiments were conducted in bacteria, not humans.

Researchers have warned that AI systems capable of designing new viruses raise biosafety concerns, particularly if similar methods are adapted to create viruses that infect humans.

The Stanford team deliberately focused on viruses that attack bacteria rather than humans or animals, but scientists have called for stronger safeguards as AI-driven biological research advances.
