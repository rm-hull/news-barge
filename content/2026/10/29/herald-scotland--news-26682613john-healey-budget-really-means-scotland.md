For once, there's a clear consensus: John Healey's first budget was very low key. It may have contained lots of measures, but few were of much consequence; total tax and spending commitments were a fraction of those announced by Rachel Reeves and her Tory predecessors over recent years.

The intention seemed to be to try to support struggling households and stabilise (or, at least, not worsen) consumer sentiment while avoiding anything that might have spooked markets.

The Chancellor and his officials are likely to have felt quietly satisfied as Thursday's media reported calm bond markets and a rising FTSE.

Tax measures were, in the grand scheme of things, trivial. The temporary increase in the bank surcharge was met with the obligatory mumps and moans but share price movements confirmed that the market had been expecting something much harsher.

The bigger picture on tax is that we're still living with the consequences of Starmer's staggeringly irresponsible manifesto commitment not to raise income tax, VAT or employee national insurance.

At a time when the country has been crying out for broad-based, high revenue tax measures, the government continues to wring small gains from an increasingly complex system.

Big spending decisions were postponed and the forthcoming spending review looks increasingly difficult for all but health and defence.

The Chancellor, wisely in my view, decided to await the Milburn and Timms reviews before making potentially seismic decisions on social security spending.

Much of the political rhetoric around the budget — including, predictably, the leader of the opposition's response — was based on the assumption that rising 'welfare' spending is the root cause of the UK's relatively high long-term interest rates. This is, not to put too fine a point on it, nonsense.

As the IMF recently reported, the UK's fiscal consolidation is much more extensive than any other G7 nation.

Its total debt, current and forecast deficit and debt interest payments as a proportion of GDP all compare relatively well. Higher borrowing costs are a result of pessimism about UK inflation relative to other nations; largely a result of the UK's especially high exposure to soaring energy prices.

The sickness and disability component of social security spending has risen and this is a genuine concern, especially since the factors driving the increase are not well understood.

But the Chancellor is right to be cautious before taking decisions which threaten the living standards of some of society's most vulnerable people.

What did the budget mean for Scotland? Well, £600 million in additional finance over the next three years, for a start.

Yes, next year's Scottish budget will see a real-terms cut but this is attributable to the additional cash received this year.

While welcome, £600m is hardly a gamechanger for a Scottish government facing a £4.7 billion gap between spending commitments and revenues by 2029-30.

Higher forecast inflation is adding to the Scottish government's fiscal challenges as it is for the UK.

The budget's big measures to address the cost of living were actually quite small and poorly targeted. But, inevitably, the politics are such that the Scottish government immediately committed to at least matching the UK government's support on energy costs.

It's a shame that devolution has yet to evolve to the stage where Scottish ministers have sufficient confidence to pause and consider opportunity cost.

Could the money spread very thinly through direct support to households based on council tax bands be more effectively spent to address the First Minister's key priorities, including child poverty? As far as we can tell, there was no effort to find out.

Out of all the analysis published this week, one chart sticks in the mind: the OBR's grim assessment of living standards over this Parliament.

Real household disposable income, which once upon a time could be expected to grow at around 2% per annum, is now forecast to grow at 1% over this decade.

The consequences of this persistent stagnation for our politics through and beyond the next election could be profound.

*Stephen Boyd is the director of IPPR Scotland*
