Two years after undercover footage revealed that a salmon processing facility was burying dead farmed salmon in the ground on North Uist, Scotland’s environment watchdog has only now sent a file to prosecutors.

The footage from the Green Britain Foundation caught the burial on camera at Whiteshore Cockles in Paible in May 2024.

The firm, which processes salmon mortalities rather than salmon destined for food, had told the Scottish Environment Protection Agency (SEPA) it stopped burying dead fish on January 31, 2024. The footage was filmed four months later.

But it is only this month that SEPA has confirmed that the case has gone to the Procurator Fiscal. The Crown Office says it has received the report.

Dale Vince, founder of the Green Britain Foundation, said: "SEPA says this took 'longer than would have been preferred'. We feel the same. Two years just to send a report to prosecutors. Nature can't afford a regulator that is so spectacularly ineffective."

In October 2024 a spokesperson from Whiteshore Cockles admitted: “During the summer our processing plant failed and we had to take urgent action to prevent odours by temporarily burying salmon carcasses in the previously licensed burial site.”

Whiteshore Cockles had been authorised by the Scottish Government, prior to this, to legally bury salmon at its facility while it constructed a fish waste dryer.

However, that permission had expired on January 31, 2024, after which it became illegal for Whiteshore Cockles to continue burying salmon Following the video expose of the burial, the company said that the completion of its upgraded facility meant that it would no longer bury fish.

SEPA, the Green Britain Foundation points out, had witness statements in hand by January 2025.

Its bosses met to decide on action in June 2025. Yet the file has only now reached prosecutors.

Recently, Whiteshore Cockles has applied to double its permitted processing capacity from 48 to 96 tonnes a day.

According to Salmon Business, “The company has submitted an application to the Scottish Environment Protection Agency (SEPA) to expand operations at its Balemore site, which handles dead fish from aquaculture operations.”

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/20503186.jpg?type=mds-article-620) *(Image: PA)*

Its application to SEPA includes plans for a new steam boiler, the use of recovered fish oil as fuel, and permission to accept and process ensiled salmon mortalities – dead fish preserved using acid treatment.

The proposal would expand the infrastructure needed to deal with the industry's fish waste. This application comes against a wider backdrop of mass mortalities at Scottish salmon farms.

The Procurator Fiscal will now decide whether to prosecute.
