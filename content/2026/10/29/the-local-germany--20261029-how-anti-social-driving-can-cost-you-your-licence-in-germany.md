A 23-year-old motorist in Koblenz recently had his driving licence withdrawn after failing to provide a medical and psychological assessment ordered in response to a pattern of anti-social driving (*Autoposen*).

The case shows how conduct like making excessive noise with a vehicle, accelerating aggressively and speeding can sometimes escalate from individual traffic infringements to a broader questioning as to whether someone is fit to drive.

**What counts as anti-social driving?**

Anti-social driving is a convenient description rather than a specific German traffic offence. Behaviour associated with the term can involve infringements or offences such as:

* creating unnecessary engine or exhaust noise
* needless back-and-forth driving in built-up areas that disturbs others
* excessively loud music
* speeding
* illegal street racing
* driving a vehicle with unsafe or unauthorised modifications.

The precise consequences depend on what the driver has done.

Germany’s *Bußgeldkatalog* – the national schedule of fines for traffic offences – sets a fine of €80 for unnecessary vehicle noise and €100 for needless back-and-forth driving in a built-up area that disturbs others.

Conduct involving speeding or illegal racing is dealt with under separate rules applying to those offences.

**From a fine to losing a licence**

An isolated infringement won’t automatically cost someone their driving licence. The Koblenz case was more serious because it involved a pattern of incidents.

The motorist came to the attention of the police on several occasions while driving rental cars. His reported behaviour included accelerating sharply over short distances, creating considerable engine noise, playing excessively loud music and speeding in urban areas.

He was also said to be uncooperative during a number of police stops.

In January 2026, the licensing authority required him to submit a report based on an MPU (*medizinisch-psychologische Untersuchung*), a medical and psychological assessment of fitness to drive (* Fahreignung*).

Although often associated with driving under the influence of alcohol or drug offences, an MPU may also be ordered following significant or repeated traffic violations.

When the motorist failed to submit the required report, the authority withdrew his licence in May. The Koblenz Administrative Court then rejected his application for urgent legal protection earlier this month.

An appeal against that order may still be lodged with Rhineland-Palatinate’s Higher Administrative Court.

**What is an ‘Autoposer’?**

The German term *Autoposer* describes someone who uses a car to attract attention, often through repeated city-centre cruising, aggressive acceleration or excessive noise.

Auto-posing is not a single offence in its own right. Nor is owning a powerful, expensive or modified car automatically a problem. Police action focuses on behaviour that disturbs others. But being involved in multiple driving offences, and/or having modifications that are unlawful or make a vehicle unsafe can invite attention from the authorities.

Some cities have established specialist units known as *Soko Poser* or*Soko Autoposer* to target such conduct and inspect modified vehicles.*Soko* is a common German abbreviation for a special task force (*Sonderkommission*).

A Freiburg operation in April illustrates where modifications can cross the line. Officers stopped an Audi SQ5 fitted with an illegal sound generator imitating a V8 engine. Its stationary noise level was reported as 96 decibels rather than the permitted 75.

A BMW inspected shortly afterwards reportedly had more than a dozen serious defects, including damaged, ageing tyres and an exhaust system that could bypass noise reduction.

Both vehicles had their operating permits (*Betriebserlaubnis*) revoked and were prohibited from continuing their journeys.
