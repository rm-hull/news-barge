AFTER fighting for his life during Covid, Dino Kania is unable to spend as much time as he would like at his business, Classic Car Lab in Otley, where a skilled team restore and repairing classic vehicles of all ages. To make up for that, he recently launched a podcast about cars, and many other aspects of life. We asked him about it.

Q: When did you launch your YouTube motoring podcast Under the Bonnet with Dino and what gave you the idea for it?

A: I launched Under the Bonnet with Dino a few weeks ago, and if you had told me four or five years ago that I would one day be doing a podcast and YouTube show, I would probably have laughed at you.

Cars have been a huge part of my life for decades, although even Classic Car Lab really started by accident - my wife Amanda simply said to me one day, “Why don’t we get a classic car?” Our first one was an Aston Martin DB5, and what began as a personal passion gradually developed into a business which has now been growing for more than 20 years.

But the real reason for starting the show goes much deeper than cars.

At the very beginning of the Covid pandemic, before vaccines were available, I became extremely ill and spent several weeks in intensive care at Harrogate Hospital fighting for my life.

The thing I remember most clearly from that time is not actually the fear. It is the kindness.

Every night in intensive care a nurse stayed with me, watching over me, and a doctor would regularly come in to check on me and on her. This was at a time when the doctors and nurses themselves were facing an unknown virus and putting themselves at risk while trying to keep people like me alive. That is something I will never forget.

When I eventually came home, my wife Amanda became my carer and has looked after me day and night ever since. I genuinely owe my life to her.

After I came out of hospital I met Andrew. At that stage my brain was not working in the way it had before and even speaking properly was difficult. Andrew spent time with me, helped me think again, helped me communicate and, through working with me on the Classic Car Lab website, gave my brain something to engage with and rebuild around. That sort of kindness is priceless.

During that period of relearning how to think and process things, I also began looking at AI and its practical uses. It became another tool that helped me research ideas, organise thoughts and look at problems from different angles. All of that eventually led to Under the Bonnet with Dino.

And the name is deliberately a little ambiguous. Being ‘under the bonnet’ does not necessarily mean I am going to be talking about what is underneath the bonnet of a car. I might start with a car and end up talking about an opportunity, an investment, AI, business, kindness or something completely different.

For me, it really means looking underneath the surface of something.

If I can use the things I have learned - including the mistakes I have made - to help somebody make a better decision, spot an opportunity, avoid an expensive error or simply make them laugh for half an hour, then I feel I am doing something worthwhile.

It is not easy for me to do. Because of the lasting effects of Covid, making a programme can be physically and mentally exhausting. I cannot be at Classic Car Lab every day anymore, which I find genuinely heartbreaking, so the podcast has also given me another way of remaining connected with the world I love.

Q: What does it cover and how can listeners learn from it?

A: On the surface it is a motoring programme, so naturally we talk about classic cars, modern classics, restoration, buying advice, values, bargains and cars that may represent an opportunity. But I want it to be broader than that. I have spent many years around cars and business, and over that time I have made good decisions, bad decisions and some extremely expensive mistakes. If somebody can learn from one of my mistakes without having to make it themselves, then that is useful.

I am particularly interested in value and opportunity. Why has one car suddenly become worth a fortune while another, which may be every bit as interesting, has been overlooked? What should somebody look for before they buy?

When is a bargain really a bargain, and when is it actually the beginning of a financial disaster?

AI will form part of this, as it has helped me enormously. I do not see AI as replacing experience, judgement or people. I see it as another tool that can help us ask better questions, research something properly and perhaps look at an opportunity from a different direction.

One week I may be talking about an Aston Martin, another week I could be talking about technology, an opportunity or simply something I think people might find useful. And hopefully we can have a laugh while we are doing it.

Q: How has it been received?

A: The response has amazed me. People are engaging with it: they send messages, ask questions, tell me about cars they are considering and share their own experiences. That is when it becomes a conversation rather than me simply talking into a microphone.

And because producing the show takes quite a lot out of me physically and mentally, knowing that somebody has enjoyed it or learned something from it makes the effort worthwhile.

Q: You are involved with North Yorkshire-based Mathewsons of TV’s Bangers & Cash fame. What have you been doing with them?

A: We have now been filming with Bangers & Cash: Restoring Classics for more than a year, restoring and working on cars for the programme, and it has been a fantastic experience for the whole team. We took part in Mathewsons Roadshow at the Great Yorkshire Showground - one of my favourite things to come from the event is not a car at all, it is a photograph of our team members Wade, Ellie and Eric.

Wade came to us as an apprentice and has now progressed into a junior technician mechanic role. Ellie is our apprentice painter and Eric is our apprentice technician.

When I look at those three young faces, that is the photograph that makes me smile, because they are inheriting the future.

They are learning mechanical work, restoration, paint and bodywork skills which have been passed from one generation to another, but they are learning them at a point when the whole motor industry is changing.

Our general manager, Connor, has done an amazing job. He has built a very good team around him, identified the right young people to bring into the business, developed their skills and helped them move onwards and upwards

For me, that photograph is what all of this should really be about: enjoyment, opportunity, encouragement and giving young people a chance. I do not have children of my own, so perhaps this is one of the ways I can do my little bit for the next generation. Young people are our legacy.

Q: What is happening in Classic Car Lab itself?

A: Classic Car Lab continues to grow and evolve. I cannot be there day-to-day anymore because of my health, which I find very difficult because the business has been such a large part of my life for more than 20 years.

I still go in occasionally when I am able and help from home with social media, marketing and ideas, but the day-to-day business is very much run by the team now. I am enormously proud of them. That is why their photograph means so much to me.

If Classic Car Lab can give someone their first opportunity, teach them a proper skill, build their confidence and ultimately give them a career, then that is something worth doing.

There is an enormous amount of craftsmanship within the motor industry - mechanical engineering, welding, fabrication, electrical diagnosis, bodywork and paint - and somebody has to pass that knowledge on.

Another area that has grown significantly for us, and which fits very naturally with our ‘Heritage to Modern’ philosophy, is accident damage repair. We now handle accident repairs on everything from cherished classics to new modern vehicles.

That side of the business is becoming increasingly important to us because it is another example of what Classic Car Lab is really about now: not simply restoring old cars, but looking after vehicles properly, whatever their age.

Q: What is a classic car?

A: I actually think the words ‘classic car’ are becoming less relevant.

Traditionally people often talk about the 40-year point, which is significant in the UK because of historic vehicle tax and MOT rules. But I think we are living through something much bigger than that.

For more than a century, the petrol and diesel motor car has been the dominant form of personal transport. Now we are moving towards electric vehicles, and perhaps eventually hydrogen or technologies we have not even properly developed yet.

So in a strange way, I think the petrol and diesel motor car itself is becoming a classic.

A 20-year-old petrol car may not meet somebody’s traditional definition of a classic today, but in another 20 years it could represent an era of motoring that has largely disappeared. That is why I am not particularly hung up on the label anymore.

Classic Car Lab is ‘Heritage to Modern’ because we believe the skills and the quality of the work matter whether the vehicle is five years old, 50 years old or 100 years old.
