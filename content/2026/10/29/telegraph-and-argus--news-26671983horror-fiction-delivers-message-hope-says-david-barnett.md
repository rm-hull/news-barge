YOU WOULD think we’d had our fill of horror.

The teatime news is a parade of war, death and conflict. The front pages are a window on to the worst of humanity. Doomscrolling through social media is a recipe for depression and anxiety.

And yet, despite all this, we turn to horror, whether that’s in books, in the cinemas, on TV or on stage.

The horror genre has never been more popular. Book sales are already up a whopping 30 per cent on the whole of 2025, according to book data people Nielsen.

The genre had its best ever showing at the Oscars, with films such as Sinners, Weapons and Guillermo del Toro’s Frankenstein. Bradford alone has seen such spooky on-stage delights as The Woman in Black at the Alhambra earlier this year, Ghost Light at the Bradford Playhouse this month, and Paranormal Activity coming to the city in Spring.

Perhaps at this time of year we might be forgiven for indulging our spooky side a little.

But a ghost is for life, not just Halloween, it seems. Which makes me very glad that a couple of years ago I had my first horror novel published and got in on the ground floor of a haunted lift to the 13th floor… and beyond.

A quick confession: I spent 14 years working at the Telegraph & Argus, and I would like to categorically state that my decision to write horror novels has absolutely nothing to do with that period, and that none of my colleagues provided any inspiration for the characters in my books.

The sort of horror I write is categorised as folk horror; that is, it draws upon the folklore and legends of a place to provide the scary stuff, and I’ve found that basing my novels (mostly) in the north of England is ploughing fertile ground for such things.

My first novel, Withered Hill, was published just over two years ago in September 2024, set in a strange and mysterious hidden village in the Pennine hills, where the isolated Pagan villagers worshipped a forgotten old god who lurked in the woods.

My second novel, Scuttler’s Cove, was actually set in Cornwall (I said mostly in the north) but one of the two main characters was a woman from Bradford who had married into money and found herself with a group of friends who decamped to the seaside for summer… running into some not-so friendly locals.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21633925.jpg?type=mds-article-620)David's first and fourth folk horror novels are set in the Pennines *(Image: Public)*

My third folk horror came out in March this year, and Scratch Moss was based on the mining community where I grew up, and how industrial areas have their own myths, legends and folklore… especially when the work that has defined them is taken away.

And finally, this month, my fourth book Twisted Pike was published, featuring a return to Withered Hill and drawing together the strands of all four novels into a single, cohesive horror universe.

As I said, it was good timing on my part. I’d had some success previously with rom-com novels, but the horror has proved much more successful in sales terms. Quite what that says about the book-buying public, I have no idea…

Well, actually, I do have a couple of ideas, and it’s all based around those initial thoughts about why we are embracing horror in all its forms at the current time, when you’d think we’d want to take our mind off the real-life horrors in the news and escape into something much lighter and easier to process.

Of course, not everyone is reading horror novels or watching movies such as Backrooms, Obsession, and TV shows like Widow’s Bay or Carrie. But plenty of us are, and I think that’s because horror can sometimes show us the way to get through the awfulness of life.

It might sound daft, but when we are confronted on screen or on the page by the worst thing we can imagine - whether that’s a plague of zombies, a knife-wielding killer in a hockey mask, a village full of cultists or a house haunted by unquiet spirits - we get scared, of course we do, but we also believe that we can triumph.

Films, TV, plays and books operate to certain narrative rules, and those tell us that the main characters will at least try to fight the evil they’re facing. They might not always win, but they will try to, and that’s at the heart of horror fiction.

When faced with the worst terrors of all, we have a chance to succeed. That’s what horror tells us. We can not only survive, we can thrive. We can win.

And that gives us some kind of hope for when we turn on the news or pick up the paper. Because no matter how awful what we see is, we can win.

\*Twisted Pike by David Barnett is published by Canelo.
