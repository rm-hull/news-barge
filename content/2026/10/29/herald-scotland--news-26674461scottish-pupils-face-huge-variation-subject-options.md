Pupils across Scotland are experiencing significant variation in access to courses in mainstream secondary schools, according to new research by The Herald.

Data obtained from local authorities confirms major differences in the number and type of courses available at schools, as well as the delivery methods in use.

School leaders have pointed to widespread teacher shortages and insisted that opportunities “should be shaped by young people's interests, talents and aspirations, not constrained by staffing shortages.”

A union representing high school teachers told The Herald that staff are “blackmailed” into working under conditions that are “unfair” to themselves and their pupils, while the country’s largest teaching union demanded wide-ranging reforms to the senior phase of secondary education.

Although individual schools are expected to publish some information on the various courses made available to students, no comprehensive and comparable record of provision across Scotland exists. In addition, no clear data on the types of provision in place, including the use of multi-level or multi-subject classes, has ever been collated.

As a result, it has been impossible to fully understand variations in the numbers of subjects available to pupils across the country.

To address this issue, The Herald submitted Freedom of Information (FoI) requests to all 32 local authorities in Scotland, asking them to provide detailed information on the arrangements for each secondary school in their area.

This included the subjects available at National 2, National 3, National 4, National 5, Higher, and Advanced Higher level, as well as any other qualifications, such as National Progression Awards or Foundation Apprenticeships, on offer. Only National 5, Higher, and Advanced Higher courses include traditional exams.

Schools were also asked to confirm whether classes were delivered on a single-level or multi-level basis, and whether instruction was delivered in person at school, online, or in separate institution such as another school or a local college.

Some councils, including Glasgow, Aberdeen, and East Dunbartonshire, refused to provide the requested information. Officials at Edinburgh City Council claimed that each school would have to carry out four hours of work in order to confirm their subject arrangements, and also refused to supply the data requested.

Some councils did respond but provided only some of the requested information, allowing for partial analysis of provision in these areas.

Information provided by councils who fully complied with the request reveals significant variation in courses that can be accessed by pupils in Scotland’s schools.

The findings show that National 2 provision across the country is extremely limited, with few schools offering programmes at this level. These courses are designed for pupils with significant additional support needs. Regions including East Lothian and Falkirk reported no schools with National 2 courses, but a number of schools in Dumfries and Galloway confirmed that many of the 18 subjects at this level are made available to young people.

National 3 programmes, which are equivalent to the old Standard Grade Foundation level, are more common, although provision is still inconsistent both between and within council areas. For example, while almost all schools in North Lanarkshire reported that National 3 courses are available, the number varies from less than five to more than two dozen.

Most schools for which data was provided confirmed that they offer between 20 and 30 options at National 4 and National 5 level, but the data also confirms that the majority of these classes are now organised on a multi-level basis. Often, this means that teachers must deliver both programmes in the same class at the same time, despite the demands on the two courses types – one of which includes a final exam – being markedly different.

Significant variation was also found in the provision of Higher courses, which are the main qualification used as entry requirements for university programmes. While most schools offer between 20 and 30 Highers, some schools reported significantly fewer options: a number of these serve island communities, but schools with fewer than 20 Highers were also found in areas including East Ayrshire, East Lothian, and Falkirk.

In addition, serious gaps in provision were also uncovered, with the data identifying schools that do not offer language courses or are unable to provide access to all science or social science Highers.

Responses confirmed that while multi-level teaching is less common for Higher courses than at lower levels of study, it is still prevalent in most parts of the country.

Advanced Higher courses are also less likely to be taught on a multi-level basis, but the number made available to students varies dramatically between schools.

In some locations, pupils are able to study almost all Advanced Higher courses in their own schools, but in others only a handful are available, with some young people required to travel to other schools or to learn online. Headteachers in Scotland have argued that requiring some pupils to travel to study at the highest level “can make pupils less likely to choose an Advanced Higher.”

Speaking to The Herald, one teaching union alleged that pupils with the ability to complete Advanced Highers are instead sometimes encouraged to fill up their timetable with other Highers, even when they are of little interest, “so as not to create a new class.”

The Herald’s research also shows that, in almost all instances, schools now offer more non-traditional qualifications than Advanced Highers. These programmes include courses such as National Progression Awards, Skills for Work courses, and even HNC options.

Access to these types of courses is particularly high in areas such as West Dunbartonshire, where schools are able to offer up to 90 different options, but schools in other parts of the country appear to be making very little use of these alternative qualifications.

Headteachers of Scotland’s secondary schools have insisted that changes are needed, and highlighted ongoing teacher shortages as the key barrier to progress.

Speaking exclusively to The Herald, David Barnett of School Leaders Scotland said that schools “require sufficient numbers of appropriately qualified teachers to sustain subject choice, ensure equitable access to a broad curriculum and provide high-quality learning experiences.”

He went on to explain that “staffing challenges are placing increasing pressure on curriculum delivery, raising concerns about continuity of learning, progression pathways and access to specialist subject teaching.”

In a recent survey, a majority of SLS members reported a reduction in provision for one or more subject area, as well as instances of classes being taught by non-specialist teachers. Nearly two-thirds highlighted a problem with unfilled teaching vacancies, while even more said that “the availability of supply teachers had deteriorated compared with previous years.” Almost 60% of respondents also said that some classes are now being taught by non-specialists.

Mr Barnett added:

“The findings point to a workforce challenge that is affecting schools' capacity to maintain curriculum breadth and specialist provision, with implications for equity of access and educational opportunity for young people across Scotland.

“SLS believes that all young people should have equitable access to a broad, high-quality curriculum delivered by appropriately qualified teachers. Educational choices and pathways should be shaped by young people's interests, talents and aspirations, not constrained by staffing shortages. While some variation in curriculum provision is inevitable, access to opportunities should not depend on a school's ability to recruit and retain the teachers needed to deliver them.

“Our survey indicates that staffing challenges are already affecting curriculum provision.”

Speaking on behalf of the Scottish Secondary Teachers Association (SSTA), General Secretary Seamus Searson said: “Teachers are blackmailed into taking multi course classes (N4, N5, Higher and in some instance Advance Higher in the one class) saying their subject will be dropped from the offer as the numbers are not big enough. Hence teachers don’t make a big fuss to the school or parents.

“A teacher could be teaching four different topics to four different groups of pupils at the same time. If the children do not do well the teacher takes the blame. It is an impossible situation.”

He added that every pupil should be “entitled to a full curriculum offer not dependant on available staff or headteachers preference”, and that the union remains opposed to an expansion of online learning “as it does not give a true teaching and learning experience” to pupils.

Ultimately, he said, children’s “education and life chances are not top of the list” of priorities in Scotland.

The Herald’s findings were also backed by Scotland’s largest teaching union, the EIS, with General Secretary Andrea Bradley blaming under-funding, teacher shortages, and a lack of proper action from the Scottish Government.

She confirmed: “EIS members report an increasing trend of schools declaring Highers and Advanced Highers ‘unviable’ due to staffing and timetabling constraints, with local authorities seeking to replace in-person teaching with online platforms. Councils have claimed, disingenuously, that this desperate response to financial pressure is motivated by a desire for ‘equity.’ Some have considered disrupting existing timetables to facilitate on-screen learning across schools.

“Further, EIS research reveals that staffing and timetabling constraints increasingly force teachers to deliver N4, N5 and Higher in classes simultaneously, even where course structure, content and assessment are significantly different. The additional planning and preparation time for such classes drives teacher workload, and EIS members report that the practicalities of teaching and assessing multi-level classes are driving stress and burnout. They also report that the time they can devote to the needs of individual pupils is limited and so their educational experience is diminished, with disengagement and behaviour issues common. This is not the breadth and depth of learning that young people are promised.

Ms Bradley argued that “every major report into Scottish education has identified the Senior Phase of secondary education as a priority for urgent reform”, with experts urging “a shift away from high stakes exams and tackling the distorting effects of the system’s obsession with attainment data.” She added that “little has changed” despite these findings, concluding:

“It is time for the Scottish Government to act - by implementing the Hayward recommendations in full, and by providing additional protected funding for education.”

Responding to The Herald’s findings, opposition parties also demanded action from the Scottish Government.

Meghan Gallacher MSP, the Scottish Conservatives’ education spokesperson, said that the SNP has “presided over a catastrophic decline in Scottish education”.

She added: “Every child deserves the chance to fulfil their potential, but that means putting academic achievement first and giving schools the freedom to deliver the best possible education.”

Commenting for the Scottish Liberal Democrats, Duncan Dunlop MSP said: “The SNP is fundamentally out of touch and won't make the hard choices needed to improve education. We need to make sure we have enough teachers to teach a broad range of subjects across all parts of Scotland. To improve standards in education, we need to increase pupil support assistants and secure secondary teachers.”

Speaking exclusively to The Herald, a COSLA spokesperson confirmed that teacher recruitment and retention problems have indeed had a “significant impact on the curriculum offer that schools are able to provide.”

They said that councils are working with the government on curricular reforms, and that they hoped that wider changes to public services would introduce “sustainable” provision and collective improvement in the outcomes for children and young people.

They continued: “Councils are currently experiencing recruitment pressures in both specific subjects as well as in particular geographical areas. This creates significant difficulties for local authorities as they work to deliver subject choice for learners and fill school timetables. The commitment to reduced class contact time will highlight this further.

“We are working with the Scottish Government and our trade union partners to identify solutions to these challenges. However, council services continue to struggle with rising demand and restricted funding and as part of the Public Service Reforms discussions COSLA would welcome, and expect, reform that empowers and enables local democratic decision making to be meet and reflect the needs and priorities of our children and young people.”

A Scottish Government spokesperson said:

"We cannot comment on the individual decisions taken by local authorities who have the statutory responsibility for delivering education. However, with public service reform we are keen to explore with COSLA how we can build on the significant strengths in Scotland’s education system which has seen the highest spend per pupil in the UK, the lowest class sizes and 95.7% of school leavers going into positive destinations last year.

“Decisions on curriculum delivery, including subject choice, class structure and timetabling, are a matter for individual schools and local authorities, who are expected to design flexible provision for their pupils and communities. Reform through the Curriculum Improvement Cycle will declutter Scotland's curriculum, prioritising essential knowledge and skills.

"The diversity of qualifications available in Scotland is a strength. Indeed, on Results Day we saw a thousand more entries into Advanced Higher subjects, and over 120,000 vocational and technical qualifications awarded, up 9.1% on last year.

"It is a fact that teacher numbers increased for the first time in three years between 2024 and 2025, to 53,475, and are up by more than 2,700 over the last decade."
