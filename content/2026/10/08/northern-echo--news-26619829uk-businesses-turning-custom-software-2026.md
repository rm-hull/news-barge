UK businesses are increasingly reconsidering the technology they use to manage everyday operations, as outdated systems, rising costs and growing demands for efficiency place pressure on existing software.

While off the shelf software remains a popular choice for companies of all sizes, some organisations are finding that standard platforms no longer meet their changing requirements.

From disconnected systems to time-consuming manual processes, businesses are exploring whether custom software could offer greater flexibility and more control over their operations.

John, a technology expert at Scottish software development company Pulsion Technology, believes the decision often comes down to how well existing systems support the way a business operates.

“Businesses often invest in software to solve an immediate problem, but their requirements can change significantly as they grow,” John explains.

“What worked for a company five years ago might now involve several disconnected systems, spreadsheets and manual processes. At that stage, it's worth looking at whether the technology is helping the business or creating additional work.”

## The limitations of off the shelf software

Off the shelf software provides an accessible starting point for many businesses. Accounting software, CRM platforms and project management tools allow companies to manage essential activities without investing in a dedicated development team.

These products generally offer a relatively low upfront cost, established features and ongoing vendor support.

However, problems can arise when businesses develop more complex workflows or need their systems to perform specialist tasks.

A growing business might use one platform for managing customers, another for processing orders and several spreadsheets for reporting.

Although each tool performs a particular job, employees may spend considerable time transferring data between systems.

John explains that this is where operational friction can begin to affect productivity.

“The challenge isn't necessarily that the individual software products are poor. It's that they weren't designed to work together around the specific requirements of that business.

“When employees are repeatedly entering the same information or working around limitations in their systems, it can become a sign that a different approach is needed.”

## Why bespoke software is becoming an alternative

Unlike a packaged product, bespoke software is developed around the processes, requirements and business goals of an individual organisation.

This can allow companies to create custom tools that support specific workflows rather than adapting their operations to fit existing tools.

For example, a manufacturing company might require a custom solution that connects production schedules, stock management and customer orders.

A professional services business could benefit from a system that combines client management, project tracking and reporting within a single platform.

Building custom software can also allow businesses to integrate existing systems rather than replacing every application they already use.

John says this flexibility can be particularly valuable for organisations with specialist requirements.

“Custom software isn't about building something different simply for the sake of it. It's about solving a real problem that existing products can't address effectively.

“If a business has a specific workflow that gives it a competitive advantage, the technology should ideally support that process rather than force the company to change how it works.”

## AI is influencing software decisions

The growing adoption of artificial intelligence is also changing how businesses approach software development.

Many organisations are exploring how AI could help with document processing, customer enquiries, reporting and repetitive administrative tasks.

However, introducing AI into existing business systems can require more than simply subscribing to another software service.

Companies need to consider data handling, security, integration and whether the technology can access the information required to perform its intended job.

Bespoke software can provide a way to connect AI capabilities with established business processes.

John believes businesses should concentrate on practical applications rather than adopting technology simply because it is attracting attention.

“AI presents opportunities, but businesses need to understand what they actually want it to achieve.

“There's little value in introducing another tool if it creates more complexity. The most useful applications are often those that address a specific operational challenge and integrate properly with the systems employees already use.”

## Is custom software worth the investment?

Cost remains one of the biggest considerations for companies exploring custom software.

Off the shelf SaaS products can be attractive because they require relatively little initial investment and provide access to established features through regular subscription payments.

A custom build typically involves a higher upfront cost, with businesses paying for planning, design, development, testing and implementation.

There are also ongoing costs associated with maintenance, hosting, security and future improvements.

However, businesses must consider the longer-term cost of their current tools, including overlapping subscriptions, manual administration and inefficiencies caused by disconnected systems.

John explains that the right answer depends on the organisation.

“Off the shelf software is absolutely the right choice for many businesses. There's no reason to develop a custom solution when an existing product already meets your requirements.

“But when a company is paying for several platforms, struggling with integration or spending significant time working around software limitations, it's worth exploring the alternatives.”

## What should businesses consider before making the switch?

Before beginning a software project, businesses should identify the problems they want to solve and assess whether their existing systems can be improved.

A clear understanding of project scope, essential features, security requirements and long-term support is important when evaluating a custom build.

Companies should also consider how their technology needs might change as the business grows.

John believes the starting point should always be the business problem rather than the technology itself.

“The most important question isn't whether custom software is better than an off the shelf product. It's whether the software is right for that particular business.

“Companies should understand their processes, identify where they're losing time or efficiency and then look at the most practical solution.”

As UK businesses continue to invest in digital technology throughout 2026, the choice between off the shelf tools and bespoke software is likely to remain an important consideration.

For organisations dealing with complex workflows, disconnected systems or specialist requirements, custom software could provide an opportunity to improve efficiency while gaining greater control over their technology.
