By 4am on Thursday, the sky still pitch black, 19-year-old Pvt Grayden Monroe and his mom had already arrived to the naval base in San Diego, California. As the very first military family members to get there, they huddled on a set of metal bleachers overlooking the dark water. The duo were waiting for Monroe’s 21-year-old sister to step off the USS Abraham Lincoln, the embattled aircraft carrier that’s been at sea for the better part of a year.

Monroe, a soldier stationed at Fort Campbell, was wearing his army uniform for the occasion. “I hope she’s proud of me,” he said of his sister, “and I hope she knows damn well that I’m just as proud of her.”

As the sun rose over Naval Air Station North Island, thousands of people flooded on to the base for the USS Lincoln’s long-awaited return. The atmosphere was like a de facto festival, with a live band, performers on stilts, therapy dogs, handmade signs, giant cutouts of sailors’ smiling faces. People cried, laughed, screamed with joy.

![People on deck cheer returning soldiers](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/175098a5e6fb99e87ee45d10c02b525591436f84/0_0_5378_3586/master/5378.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

When the Lincoln appeared in the middle distance around noon, rounding the corner past downtown, cheers erupted from around the bay. Sailors flanked the edges of the ship in uniform, waving to the crowd and making phone calls to loved ones. Below American-flag bunting, rust stains were visible on the sides of the carrier. The massive, city-like ship had finally reached land.

Military families and friends had longed for this moment for months.

The Lincoln first left San Diego last November, with an expected return date sometime in the spring. But as the US and Israel launched a war with Iran, the aircraft carrier was diverted to the Middle East, extending the deployment for the roughly 5,000 sailors and marines onboard for a then-unknown amount of time. The Lincoln’s deployment ended up spanning 321 days, including 264 consecutive days at sea without a port call, a record in modern naval history, the Department of Defense said.

![people kissing ](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/5115ef2a8cab73f1ef3ee4a40bbad0822f7ff5fc/0_0_5362_3575/master/5362.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

For months, sailors have reported hazardous living conditions onboard the Lincoln: shortages of food and basic supplies, a spate of suicide attempts and a general sense of deteriorating mental health and declining morale. The reports immediately set off a political firestorm, with Democratic lawmakers calling for an investigation, even as Donald Trump and the US defense secretary, Pete Hegseth, sought to downplay the situation. The US navy confirmed last month that eight service members from the Lincoln and its accompanying strike group had attempted suicide since the start of the deployment.

After the Lincoln’s return home was officially announced in August, it took the carrier almost two long months to get back to San Diego, making a brief port call in Thailand and then tracing a route east across the Pacific Ocean.

But on the naval base on Thursday, the scene wasn’t completely euphoric.

An infant in a stroller was positioned with a sign that read “I have waited 234 days to hug my daddy!” The baby’s mom, Brenda Rivas, said she was five months pregnant when her husband deployed. He missed the birth, and the first time he was able to see his daughter over a video call was six months later.

“It’s mixed emotions,” Rivas said. “I’m more sad than excited because he did miss so much.”

![Woman holds up sign at naval base](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/61f7597837e49ad121186f589b8c45e4ef239441/0_0_4896_3264/master/4896.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

And an emotional reunion on the pier wasn’t in the cards for everyone, especially for the many military families that are spread across the country.

Dawn, a mother who requested that only her first name be used because she feared retaliation from military officials, anxiously watched a live feed of the ship’s arrival from a small town in Texas, her eyes “glued to the TV” for hours. Her son is a service member on the Lincoln.

“I couldn’t sleep. Tossed and turned,” she texted early on Thursday morning. “I’m feeling so happy for my granddaughter to see her daddy!”

Imagining that the scene in San Diego would be too chaotic, Dawn is instead flying to California next week to reunite with her son and his five-year-old daughter. She’s planning to help him “get everything back in order”: make sure his house is clean, whip up some home-cooked meals.

Over the past 11 months, one thing has been keeping her son sane and tethered to a reality off the ship, she said.

“He said that all he could think about was his daughter,” she said. “That’s what got him through all this.”

## Months of dwindling rations, worsening mental health

As the sailors floated around the globe for the past year, their relatives sat at home and waited, wondering what could be happening thousands of miles away on the ship.

Dawn’s son sent her updates via WhatsApp whenever he got service. This past spring, he told her the conditions were dire.

“Like 3rd world …” he wrote in a message. “We couldn’t get soap or toothpaste.”

He sent her pictures of mold dripping down the walls, of dwindling daily rations: sectioned cafeteria trays with only a few of the compartments full. Two hard-boiled eggs and watery soap. A scoop of grits and a few limp noodles.

![people on a ship](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/d272fc71afd5a9c659f2bac8942249b8594cafa1/0_0_4896_3264/master/4896.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

The extended deployment, long working hours and supply shortages seemed to be taking their toll across the ship, especially on the younger sailors, Dawn said. Nearly half of all active-duty military members are 25 years old or younger. She could imagine her son, who’s now in his 30s, being like a father figure to some who were really struggling. The reports of some sailors attempting to jump overboard were true, her son told her.

“My son’s a tough kid, so he could take it,” she said during a phone interview last week. “But for those 19-year-old kids that go on a first deployment, it’s scary for them.”

One of the worst parts for Dawn was the unsolicited commentary, on Facebook and Instagram and in real life, saying that even if the ship’s conditions were rough, the sailors probably just couldn’t hack it.

“That’s exactly what people were saying about the people in the military: that they’re babies, and that back in 1969, or whenever they were [serving], it wasn’t like that,” she said. “I couldn’t even read the comments. I’m still upset about it.”

Those sentiments even came from Trump, who told reporters this summer that Lincoln’s deployment was “not nearly long enough”.

That type of remark changed everything about how Dawn viewed the president.

“I like President Trump. I voted for him. But I don’t really think he understands,” she said.

“Let his son be out there, just for a couple days, and see how it goes. If he really would tolerate that, if his son could really take it – I’m sure he couldn’t.”

![a man walking in between people ](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/3242ddc1724ae0c7eeb9b0d93bbb0b1928a55a75/0_0_8192_5464/master/8192.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

In a brief press conference on the naval base Thursday, Capt Daniel Keeler, the commanding officer of the Lincoln, answered several questions from journalists about the consistent reports of poor conditions on the ship.

“[Most of those issues] occurred in March and April, while we were engaged in active combat operations against a very determined foe,” he said. “Immediately once combat operations started, we were, I would say, a little bit challenged to get the normal fill of things. But I had enough food, I had all the supplies I needed to conduct combat. We never missed a mission. I also never missed a meal.”

Meanwhile, Hegseth again played down those same issues on Wednesday, saying instead that “a few folks in the media” aimed to “turn” the ship’s deployment “into some sort of symbol of dysfunction, or a symbol of low morale or a symbol of a failed mission”.

Keeler focused most of his comments this week on the success of the mission.

“For most of my sailors, this was their first time away from home, their first deployment, their first experience with combat,” he said. “I understand the anxiety that might have caused, but I can tell you this: our team of professionals on the ship led every single one of those sailors through that experience. And the great majority responded how sailors always do, with honor, courage and commitment.”

## Temporary rest, shifting political views

Now that the Lincoln has returned home after an unexpectedly grueling and high-profile deployment, sailors are left to determine what’s next.

Some of those next steps are immediate. Monroe, the 19-year-old, said his family was excited to take his sister out to dinner tonight – but then she’s back on duty tomorrow.

After the trials of the past year, a more serious takeaway for Monroe is his sense of what could happen during a deployment, and just how tough that isolation could be.

“When shit hits the fan, everything just kind of sucks. Especially when they’re out there on their own. It’s really just you and the guy next to you,” he said. “So getting stuck there and being able to push through something like that – I don’t think I could have done it, honestly.”

His sister, he added, was “probably one of my biggest role models”.

For Rivas, the wife whose husband missed their daughter’s birth, she’ll have to switch from being a single parent for a year to parenting as a team again.

![a man holding a sign](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/a60e6457464bd6ee0e1f624aad612b21a3f073b5/0_0_5500_3667/master/5500.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

“It’s gonna be different, teaching him how to take care of her,” she said. “But she’s good with people, so I’m sure she’ll adjust.”

For Dawn, as a Texan and someone who says she still wouldn’t vote for a Democrat, she walked away from the USS Lincoln experience with a very different outlook on Trump. The majority of those in her small town and in her family are lifelong Republicans, but the controversy surrounding the Lincoln has cut jaggedly across traditional party lines.

“It’s really hard for me because I just wanted [my son] to be OK. And I just, I don’t know …” she said, her voice trailing off over the phone. “I’m just not really happy with President Trump right now. At all.”

As for what her son thinks about the fraught deployment, she said, it’s probably too soon to tell how this experience will shape his life and career. But it has put some things into perspective.

“He just wants to live for his kid,” she said. “He wants to see his daughter grow up.”
