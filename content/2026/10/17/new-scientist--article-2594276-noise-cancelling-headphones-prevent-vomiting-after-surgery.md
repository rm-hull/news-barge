Nausea and vomiting are common after surgery, but wearing noise-cancelling headphones during the procedure helps prevent these unpleasant side effects. This simple intervention also supports the idea that our brain processes sounds even when we’re not consciously aware of our surroundings.

“That data is really encouraging and is actually in line with decades of research showing that anaesthesia is not simply an off switch,” says Vigi Katlowitz at the Baylor College of Medicine in Houston.

Nausea and vomiting are common side effects of anaesthetics. Patients are routinely given drugs to prevent them, but Huayue Liu at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University in Suzhou, China, and his colleagues wanted to investigate a non-pharmacological approach and presented their results at the Anesthesiology conference in San Diego.

The team recruited 192 patients who had keyhole gynaecological surgery under general anaesthesic. Half were randomly assigned noise-blocking ear defenders, which they wore from the start of anaesthesia until the end of surgery. All the patients received standard anaesthesia and anti-nausea medication.

Within 48 hours of the surgery, 35 per cent of those wearing ear defenders experienced nausea or vomiting, compared with 52 per cent of those without them. The researchers also found that symptoms were less severe among those whose hearing had been protected, defined as the patients vomiting less and it having less of an impact on their daily life.

Operating theatres aren’t generally quiet places, with Liu recording average noise levels of 66 decibels – around the level of a normal conversation – during the study.

The relationship between sound, nausea and vomiting, and anaesthesia is unclear. However, the latter affects the nervous system, and noise is thought to trigger a stress response that activates the autonomic nervous system. This helps regulate involuntary bodily functions, such as heart rate, and interacts with the brainstem pathways involved in nausea and vomiting.

From dementia to heart attacks, hearing loss has been linked to a wide range of effects across the body, and the condition is on the rise. Fortunately, we’re learning how best to safeguard this crucial sense and how we might be able to reverse the damage

However, Liu stresses that the researchers didn’t look into the mechanism behind how ear defenders prevent nausea and vomiting, so the idea of them dampening this response “remains a hypothesis”.

Nevertheless, the results tell us more about brain activity under anaesthesia. “The biological plausibility is that general anaesthesia produces unconsciousness without necessarily eliminating all neural processing of sound,” says Liu.

Liu hopes to one day conduct a larger trial at multiple locations that measures noise exposure relative to nervous system activity among patients under general anaesthetic.
