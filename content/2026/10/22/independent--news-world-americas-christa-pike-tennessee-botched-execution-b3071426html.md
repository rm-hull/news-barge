More than three weeks after surviving a botched execution attempt in Tennessee, convicted murderer Christa Pike has begun speaking in complete sentences and walking unassisted once again.

Pike's attorney told *TMZ* that medical professionals are still assessing whether the injection of lethal drugs caused lasting damage to her cognitive abilities and psychological health. In the immediate aftermath of the failed procedure, representatives said physicians at Vanderbilt University Medical Center were providing life-saving treatment, warning it was probable Pike was brain-dead and would be disconnected from life support.

However, she unexpectedly regained consciousness five days later, fully aware of the attempt on her life. Upon waking, her first words were reported as: "where am i and who are you?"

The 50-year-old’s survival after two syringes of pentobarbital failed to end her life—marking Tennessee’s second botched lethal injection this year—has thrust the state into a national legal dilemma.

A four-day Reuters/Ipsos poll completed on Monday revealed that only a third of Americans believe state authorities should attempt to execute an inmate a second time if an initial attempt fails. By contrast, 39 percent of respondents said surviving prisoners should instead serve life sentences, while 26 percent were unsure of the appropriate outcome.

While 23 U.S. states and a majority of countries worldwide have abolished capital punishment, overall domestic backing remains above half. Fifty-five percent of Americans favor the death penalty for severe crimes, a drop from 63 percent recorded in a 2017 Reuters/Ipsos poll. Roughly 31 percent oppose capital punishment, and 13 percent expressed no opinion.

As states maintaining the death penalty encounter ongoing difficulties obtaining execution drugs, some officials have considered alternative measures, including firing squads.

The legality of second execution attempts has increasingly fallen to the judiciary. The U.S. Supreme Court recently cleared the way for Alabama to set a second execution date for Kenneth Smith, who survived a 2022 execution attempt when prison personnel could not establish intravenous access. Alabama later executed Smith in 2024 utilizing nitrogen gas suffocation.

Debate over execution protocols recently escalated after Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth suggested livestreaming the scheduled firing squad execution of Nidal Hasan, the former U.S. Army major who killed 13 people during a 2009 attack at Fort Hood in Texas. The livestream was later canceled.

While 46 percent of poll respondents—including 29 percent of Democrats and 71 percent of Republicans—deemed a firing squad suitable for Hasan, 71 percent of Americans overall rejected broadcasting executions on television or online. Amid criticism from lawmakers in both parties, President Donald Trump canceled the livestream proposal, though officials still plan to execute Hasan on December 3.

The online Reuters/Ipsos survey gathered responses from 1,679U.S. adults nationwide and maintained a margin of error of 2 percentage points.
