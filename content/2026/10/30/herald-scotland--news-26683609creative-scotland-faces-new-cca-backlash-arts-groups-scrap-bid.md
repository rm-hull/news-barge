Arts companies and artists have attacked Creative Scotland for its handling of the former Centre for Contemporary Arts as they revealed they had pulled out of the running to take over the boarded-up building.

An open letter to the Scottish Government agency claims the bidding process for the Sauchiehall Street site was “too heavily stacked” against a vision of it becoming a “powerhouse creation centre and performance venue”.

The collective, which wanted to lease the building from Creative Scotland, has claimed the arts agency is favouring a sale of the site, which has been in public hands for decades.

The building has been lying empty since the sudden financial collapse of the CCA at the end of January - 12 months after it had secured £3.4m in new funding from Creative Scotland for the next three years.

The bid team included theatre and dance companies Vanishing Point, Groupwork, Shotput and Superfan, as well as artists Mamoru Iriguchi, Craig McCorquodale and Stewart Laing.

The collective envisaged the reopened building becoming an “artist-led destination venue” which would encompass rehearsal, performance and exhibition spaces for various art forms, including theatre, dance, music, visual art and film and sound installations.

Glasgow chef Rosie Healey was also lined up to take over the running of the bar-restaurant in the building.

Their group was one of 13 to submit initial plans to Creative Scotland when it invited proposals for the building in July.

However, Creative Scotland triggered a new process seeking detailed bids for the site in September, and said it would welcome “any other ideas” for the building, which was run as the Third Eye Centre from the mid-1970s until 1991 before it reopened as the CCA the following year.

It said at the time: “Proposals for ownership and operation will be preferred over proposals for operation via lease.

“Creative Scotland’s focus is on developing and supporting artists, creative practitioners, organisations and public programmes across Scotland.

“Our core remit, strategy and expertise does not include the ownership or operation of buildings or other major assets of this type.”

The arts agency has pledged to consider proposals for "a transfer of ownership at below market value" if they involve a "clear cultural use for the building with a strong public benefit" which remains intact for at least 25 years.

It has described the current bidding process as “an exciting opportunity to become the long-term custodians of a building with over 50 years of history within Glasgow and Scotland’s cultural life”.

In its initial proposals to Creative Scotland, the arts collective said: “Driven by internationalism, the work we make here will take flight to venues across Scotland and around the world, while artists from elsewhere will come to Glasgow to present major work and fertilise new ideas across the city.

“Building on the radical legacy of the Third Eye Centre, we want 350 Sauchiehall Street to become a public living room for Glasgow.

“We will build the range and frequency of public activity as the building develops; within three years, our ambition is that there will always be something on, for people of all ages.”

In its new open letter, the collective states: “We envisaged a different model of managing an arts building, with contemporary performance at its centre, honouring Scotland's legacy of experimentation while looking forward and outward.

“Through collaboration between established companies and independent artists without permanent premises, we aimed to increase artistic ambition while sharing resources and reducing administrative bloat.

“This was not simply a proposal to occupy a building. It was a proposal to help build the artistic infrastructure Scotland lacks. Under our custodianship, 350 Sauchiehall Street would have been a creation centre and artist-led destination for technically ambitious, risk-taking, contemporary performance in Glasgow.”

The collective said it had decided to withdraw from the bidding process after Creative Scotland announced it would be open to “new proposals from parties who had not applied for the first stage”.

The open letter adds: “Creative Scotland has made clear that its preference is to sell. We cannot buy the building.

“Our proposal was to combine our existing funding and resources, together with the resource of the building itself, to efficiently and effectively create and deliver the work Creative Scotland funds us to make, at the same time as leading a thriving, living arts space.

“Our collaborative management model would increase the impact of every pound Creative Scotland is already spending on our individual companies and artists and on the building itself.

“But we cannot use our existing funding to physically own and maintain the building. Indeed, we struggle to see how any organisation with the intention of keeping the building alive as a living, breathing arts space which prioritises artistic innovation, can afford to buy it.

“Creative Scotland has said it is willing to accept a proposal based on lease, rather than ownership.

“But we need more information to put such a proposal forward in good faith, and that information is not forthcoming.

“We understand Creative Scotland needs to oversee a ‘transparent’ process and that this precludes conversations with interested parties, but without truly understanding the parameters of a possible tenancy, we’ve concluded that the odds are too heavily stacked against us.”

The collective said it wanted to “engage in a dialogue with Creative Scotland, not a bidding process”.

Its letter adds: “We understand that if the aim is to sell a building, a bidding process is the appropriate avenue.

“But if the aim is to create a new piece of artistic and social infrastructure, dialogue is required between artists and funder.

“As a group of artists and organisations, we are proposing a different kind of building - innovatively managed, generative, experimental, and ambitious - and we believe that Creative Scotland wants something different too.

“But if we want a different outcome, we need a different process. Our group is, above all, a group of collaborators - that is how we came together and found unity and momentum.

“We are asking Creative Scotland to join that collaboration, and in doing so, to be ambitious, to invest in Scotland’s artistic infrastructure, and to fulfil its role not just as funder but as developer and influencer.”

A spokesperson for Creative Scotland said: "We cannot comment on or engage in discussion with any individual bid while the bidding process is ongoing, to ensure a fair and transparent process.

"The open call for expressions of interest closes today at 5pm.

"After this point, an assessment process will take place where Creative Scotland will select a preferred bidder or bidders to take forward to fully understand detailed business plans before a final decision is taken.”
