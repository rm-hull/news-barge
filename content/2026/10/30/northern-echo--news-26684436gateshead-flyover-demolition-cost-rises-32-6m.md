The expected cost of demolishing the Gateshead Flyover has now passed £32 million.

A new report has detailed how the price of removing the dilapidated A167 Gateshead Highway almost doubled from initial estimates.

The infamous concrete structure was closed in December 2024 due to safety concerns and demolition contractors recently completed work to tear it down.

Gateshead Council originally set aside an £18 million budget for the scheme in March 2025.

But the Local Democracy Reporting Service (LDRS) revealed recently that the final bill for the project was expected to top £30 million.

Latest figures due to be presented to councillors next week now estimate that the cost will come in at £32.6 million, though a final confirmed figure will not be known until spring 2027.

The council report states that contractors BAM had provided an initial estimate of £17.3 million in March 2025 to demolish the flyover in a “timely manner”, but that the price was always subject to change as the scheme developed and became more detailed.

The initial estimate is branded as “not comparable” to the current forecast due to a series of factors.

That includes the procurement of the 10-tonne steel props that were used to prevent the flyover structure from collapsing during the demolition, paying for 24-hour work periods during certain phases of the scheme, protection measures for the East Street flats, and monitoring of the underground Tyne and Wear Metro tunnels beneath the highway.

Relocation of some East Street residents, fuel price increases, running of shuttle buses during the closure of the Five Bridges roundabout, structural monitoring and surveys are also highlighted as further costs borne by the council.

While the flyover itself has now been demolished, work to clear and reinstate the area where it once stood continues and is due to be finished by December.

A Gateshead Council spokesperson said: “Demolition of the Gateshead Flyover has now been completed. The remaining works primarily involve site clearance and reinstatement, including the removal of any remaining demolition debris, repairs to areas of highway affected by the works, reinstatement of guard rails, and installation of permanent traffic signals at Park Lane roundabout.

“The final physical works are expected to be completed by December 2026. The final overall cost of the project is expected to be confirmed in Spring 2027, once all remaining works have been completed and the programme has been fully concluded.”

The council set aside an original budget of £18 million and was also granted £2.5m from the North East mayor’s office.

The Government has repeatedly been urged to commit funds towards the flyover’s removal and a subsequent regeneration of the area since the flyover’s closure, but is yet to do so.

Anneliese Hutchinson, the council’s strategic director for economy, innovation and growth, told the LDRS this summer that Civic Centre officials have identified sufficient money to cover the extra cost of the demolition but were “very keen” to keep talks going over alternative funding sources.

The flyover was suddenly shut down in December 2024 after inspections revealed concerns about its risk of collapse.

Demolition work began with the raising of the adjacent Computer House from October 2025 to March 2026, making way for BAM and Thompsons of Prudhoe to tackle the flyover.

The removal of the flyover is viewed by local leaders as a major opportunity to reshape the centre of Gateshead and attract investment that can provide a regeneration boom.

Council officers state in their report that design work for the former flyover corridor is still in development, but that consultation on a broader vision and plans for the town centre will begin in November 2026.

The document adds: “The redevelopment of the former Flyover corridor aims to achieve a more balanced and resilient network that strengthens connections to Newcastle, the river Tyne, and surrounding neighbourhoods in Gateshead, and connects the new regeneration neighbourhoods with each other and the town centre.

"Put simply, we must learn from the problems of our old outdated road system and build back a network that is fit for the future.

"It will contribute to the creation of a thriving, mixed-use destination, a growing economy, and one of the North East’s most exciting places for urban living.”
