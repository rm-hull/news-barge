When Sir Ian Wood died in July this year, obituaries and commentary tended to focus on the successes that made him one of the wealthiest individuals this country has ever known.

Scott Wright, The Herald’s deputy business editor, wrote that Wood will be remembered for “the entrepreneurial vision to diversify the family fishing business, John Wood and Son, into an oil and gas services specialist as the **North Sea** boom was in its infancy – and in subsequent decades turn it into a truly global force in the energy sector.”

At a recent memorial service, his work and character were praised by a range of international figures including Melinda French Gates, co-creator of the Giving Pledge to which Wood signed up in 2015.

Sir Ian had a huge impact in the business world, both in Scotland and around the globe, and also left a significant mark in his native north-east. But what many might not know is that he also influenced the school experiences of many thousands of pupils all across the country thanks to the work of the Wood Foundation.

The group backed projects exploring STEM teaching and ‘real world’ learning, but its longest-running and highest-profile programme is the Youth and Philanthropy Initiative (YPI), which is now incorporated into schools in every one of Scotland’s 32 council areas.

![Sir Ian Wood speaking to school pupils.](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21641822.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Sir Ian was committed to helping to developing young people across Scotland. *(Image: Fraser Band)*

Built around a student-led group project, YPI sees young people choosing a charity to support and then developing a presentation about the work that it does. Ultimately, a winning group is chosen – often at public-facing events – and their charity secures a £3000 donation to support its services.

Since being introduced in 2008, YPI has become one of the most widespread initiatives in Scottish schools, with hundreds of thousands of pupils having now taken part.

Teenagers often select local organisations with which they have some sort of personal or community connection, with data showing that the most common social issues addressed by YPI charities in the past year are mental health and wellbeing, physical health, domestic abuse, and poverty.

Winning charities have typically used the new funds to bolster existing services, but around a third have even been able to expand their offerings and help more people as a result of the contribution of a group of local school pupils.

And as for the young people themselves, they gain new knowledge, develop new skills, and sometimes discover that they are capable of things they had previously thought beyond them.

![A group of young people with Sir Ian Wood in the centre. The group is holding up a sign saying YPI 15](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21641821.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Sir Ian Wood with a group of young people celebrating 15 years of YPI. *(Image: Michal Wachucik)*

For the Wood Foundation’s CEO, Sarah Chew, the impact of YPI is a perfect reflection of Sir Ian’s legacy.

“Sir Ian the philanthropist,” she says warmly. “That's who we worked with.”

“His vision, and that genuine desire, to support and develop young people is one of the three key strands of the Wood Foundation. Obviously we have our venture for philanthropy in Africa, and that's fascinating and a really huge part of the work that the Wood Foundation does. There's also the economic, regional side that we do through our work with Opportunity Northeast and ETZ, and that's another key strand.

“And then the one that YPI speaks to is developing young people.”

Over nearly two decades of operation, the programme has seen nearly £10m in funding distributed to charities all across the country, with that money provided by the Wood Foundation. Whereas some organisations might demand detailed evaluations against key performance indicators to check that winning charities are making good use of the cash, Chew explains that the YPI is founded on trust – especially in the pupils themselves – and that the benefits of this approach are clear:

“Our investment in YPI is a way of us supporting that idea of developing young people in Scotland because while YPI is delivered in schools, for us it's about citizenship and that philanthropic opportunity. I think YPI gives young people the opportunity to find their voice and then our responsibility is to help them keep it.

“As we move forward, the next celebration of Sir Ian's legacy is continuing YPI to ensure as many young people have the opportunity to benefit from what I think is an incredible program, but also to deepen the impact that we have in schools. Developing young people ensures we have a workforce that is incredibly well-equipped to problem solve the social issues that our young people have identified as important to them.

“For nearly 20 years, we've allowed young people to connect with their community around those social issues that they identify as being important and then their goal is to make a difference, to have an impact, and some of the outcomes from what has started as a YPI project include young people volunteering their time, continuing to advocate for social issues.

But now, after so much success, the loss of its leader has led those running the foundation to consider now just the achievements of the past, but also the plans for the future.

![A woman with blonde hair and a dark top smiling to camera](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21640558.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Sarah Chew, CEO of the Wood Foundation, says that YPI is about 'investing in the next generation'. *(Image: Grant Anderson)*

“The passing of Sir Ian gives us a moment to pause and reflect and celebrate the impact that he has made possible. But equally I sit in a really privileged position within the Wood Foundation because I've an incredible board of trustees and directors who allow us to continue that work, inspired by his approach.

“We've only just begun. There has been this quiet revolution of young people being empowered, and so the idea is that 18, 19, 20 years on from something starting, we're looking to capture that next generation through our alumni work as a way of saying: what happened next?

“It's not about a quick fix. There are many, many opportunities that you can do for that quick win but I think YPI is designed to be part of a journey that a young person goes. It's now at the scale across Scotland where we are in all 32 local authorities and we do work with entire year groups, but YPI for some young people will be a bus stop on a journey. For others, it will be the destination that they build their career around.

“The goal for us is to listen to what young people need and support them on their journey. So YPI gives them an opportunity to use their skills. It actually gives them the confidence that they have those skills.

“YPI is a way of learning you can have impact. And for some young people it's an opportunity for them to feel like they have achieved something. But one of the challenges we have is YPI isn't a just about winning. Of course winning is great because the charity then receives the £3,000 grant, but I met with a charity recently who said their team didn't win but they phoned the next day and asked what more can they do. That to me is the goal.”

“When you hear about Sir Ian's love of Scotland, that's what it's for, because that's our future.

“We're investing in our next generation coming through to be all that they can be.”
