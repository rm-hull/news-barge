A survey of secondary school leaders across the country has shone a light on the scale of the staffing pressures affecting Scottish schools.

Data gathered by School Leaders Scotland (SLS) highlights the impact of severe teacher shortages, with students’ access to the curriculum being restricted as provision is cut and subjects are removed.

The research also uncovered widespread concerns that plans to cut contact time for secondary school teachers – a flagship SNP commitment – cannot be delivered safely and equitably.

Almost 200 individuals in high school leadership positions responded to the SLS survey, with information provided by staff in all 32 local authority areas.

Almost two thirds reported current vacancies in their schools, with even more stating that there are either fewer or far fewer supply teachers available compared to last year. The total reporting that more supply teachers are now available was below 5%.

Mathematics and Technical Education were the “most frequently named subjects” experiencing shortages, with around a third of respondents identifying problems in these areas. More than a fifth also confirmed vacancies for English teachers, with other problem subjects including Home Economics, Business and Administration, Computing Science, Physics, and Modern Languages.

Those taking part in the survey identified a lack of suitable applications (or in some cases simply a lack of any applications), problems caused by high teacher workload, perceptions about the difficulties facing the profession, and school location – especially rurality – as key issues affecting the ability of secondary schools to deliver a full curricular experience to young people. Competition with other sectors and employment options was also noted as a serious challenge.

More than 60% of respondents confirmed that teacher shortages have caused “reduced provision in one or more subject areas”, with a similar number reporting that their school now has classes being taught by individuals without the correct subject specialism.

Nearly half said that the number of classes available in a particular subject had had to be reduced, meaning that although a course is running, not all pupils would able to be accommodated.

More than a third also reported that classes had been combined, that whole subjects had been removed from the curriculum, and that there had been a clear reduction in choice for pupils.

The official report on the survey responses also makes clear that vacancies information does not paint a full picture of curricular gaps, because some schools have elected to remove subjects from their timetables entirely due to their inability to find suitable staffing solutions.

The report summary states: “Respondents describe curriculum decisions made before or instead of recruitment, for example, reducing the modern languages offer, dropping Drama from the curriculum, not actively recruiting for a technical subject, or changing staffing requirements. Vacancy counts alone may therefore understate the educational impact when a school is reducing its curriculum offer to fit the workforce it can obtain.”

This week, The Herald has revealed major disparities in subject provision across Scotland amidst concerns that schools are struggling to offer a full range of subjects and courses to young people. The failure to attract enough students onto teacher education programmes has also been previously reported, as has the Scottish Government’s failure to deliver promised increases in teacher numbers.

However, national data has also revealed that newly-qualified teachers have become increasingly unlikely to secure full-time, permanent contracts, despite being available to work.

In addition to quantitative data, the survey also gathered more details responses, allowing staff to provide more information about the impact of staffing shortages.

Comments included references to “large tri-level classes”, the need for “non-specialists long-term covering maths classes”, the restriction of access to some subjects for specific year groups, the removal of whole subjects from a school’s curricular provision, a “lack of ability to plan strategically”, cuts to provision in S1-3 in order to “prioritise senior phase work”, the need to outsource Advanced Higher provision to neighbouring schools.

One member of staff wrote simply: “The impact is overwhelming negative for both young people and staff.”

The SLS survey also explored the implications of the planned Reduction in Class Contact Time (RCCT) for teachers in Scotland. The SNP has pledges to increase planning and preparation time by 90 minutes per week, with implementation at secondary level planned for August 2029.

However, SLS points out that Scotland has a “a staffing system already operating with substantial pressure”, and argues that staffing shortages, combined with the complexity of delivering the change in high schools, will prevent the policy from being implemented safety and effectively.

The report notes that there is “a great deal of concern among SLS members about the deliverability of RCCT for secondary schools”.

The organisation points out that simply increasing teacher numbers would not address the issue of shortages, because “secondary staffing is organised by subject specialism, meaning that cutting contact time “can create many multiple small staffing requirements that are individually difficult to recruit to.” This problem, the report adds, is “particularly acute in small schools and one-teacher departments".

As a result, SLS warns that the government’s current policy could end up increasing rather than reducing workload by creating a “a potential feedback loop whereby staff shortages result in increased cover and leadership demands.

In conclusion, the organisation makes four specific recommendations to the Scottish Government.

It calls on officials to “establish accurate and transparent data on the workforce capacity required to delivery RCCT”. At present, despite having first promised the develop the policy in 2021, the SNP has been unable to provide figures on the number of teachers and level of funding required to implement RCCT.

SLS also urges the development of a “national subject-specific workforce plan”, and moves to “strengthen recruitment and retention, particularly in shortage subjects and locations".

Finally, the report states that the government must “protect curriculum entitlement and specialist teaching for all pupils”, noting that the “use of non-specialist teaching, curriculum reduction, cross-school collaboration and other mitigation measures should be carefully monitored to ensure they support educational quality rather than simply redistribute shortages".

Writing exclusively for The Herald, SLS General Secretary David Barnett confirmed that schools across Scotland have “faced persistent challenges in recruiting teachers in a range of subject areas”, and that the new survey data “raises important questions about the system's capacity to meet future workforce demands, particularly in light of the planned introduction of reduced class contact time (RCCT) in secondary schools from August 2029".

He continued: “Many schools have already adjusted their curriculum offer in response to ongoing recruitment difficulties. In some cases, subjects have been removed or reduced because suitably qualified teachers cannot be recruited. This also means that the reported vacancy data does not fully reflect the scale of the challenge.

“A school may report no vacancy in a subject such as Home Economics, for example, because it is no longer able to offer that subject. The result is increasing inconsistency in curricular provision across the country, with young people's access to learning opportunities too often determined by geography and local staffing circumstances rather than educational need or aspiration.

“While various approaches have been explored at both local and national levels, significant concerns remain about the ability to ensure a consistent supply of teachers in these areas. The consequence is that some young people continue to face reduced educational opportunities, potentially affecting their future pathways and life chances.”

Regarding plans to reduce teachers’ class contact time, Mr Barnett stated: “Schools already experiencing difficulties in filling vacancies are understandably concerned about the additional staffing requirements that RCCT will create. Delivering the entitlement will require additional teaching capacity, and our members report significant uncertainty about where this capacity will come from. In many schools, particularly smaller schools and those with one-teacher departments, the changes may generate small, fragmented staffing requirements that are especially difficult to recruit to. These challenges are likely to be most pronounced in rural and remote areas, where recruitment pressures are already significant.

“RCCT has the potential to deliver meaningful benefits for teachers and learners alike. However, successful implementation will depend on realistic workforce planning, appropriate investment and a clear strategy to address subject-specific teacher shortages. Without these measures, there is a risk that educational opportunities for Scotland's young people could be adversely affected. It is therefore vital that workforce and implementation challenges are addressed now, ensuring that the benefits of RCCT can be realised without compromising the breadth and quality of educational provision across Scotland.”

Scottish Greens education spokesperson Laura Moodie MSP told The Herald that the survey “confirms what many pupils, parents and teachers have been telling us for some time: teacher shortages are becoming an educational inequality issue".

She added: “When schools are forced to cut subjects or rely on non-specialist teaching, young people's choices can be limited, particularly in rural areas and communities that already face barriers to accessing opportunities. The focus should be on recruiting and retaining teachers, addressing workload pressures, and ensuring all young people have access to a broad, balanced curriculum regardless of where they live.

“We also need to recognise that staffing shortages can have a particular impact on autistic learners, pupils with learning disabilities and those requiring additional support. Inclusive education depends on time, expertise and stable staffing. When schools are struggling to fill posts, it becomes even harder to provide the tailored support many children need to thrive.”

Reform Scotland education spokesperson Amanda Lindsay MSP said: "This shocking report from school leaders confirms that pupils the length and breadth of Scotland are being deprived of a decent standard of education and the chance of a successful, prosperous adult life.

"Non-specialists are covering core subjects and entire courses are being dropped because schools simply cannot staff them.

"The SNP government’s inability to recruit and retain teachers means the educators we do have are being spread far too thinly, often across multiple schools.

"These demands are unfair and unsustainable, especially in rural areas but the biggest losers are our children, whose futures are being obliterated on the SNP’s watch.”

A Scottish Government spokesperson said: “Teacher numbers have increased by more than 2,700 over the last decade thanks to Scottish Government investment.

“We are working to encourage more students to pursue teaching careers through measures including the Teaching Bursary Scheme, which targets hard to fill teaching subjects including technical education, science and mathematics.

“Scotland already has the lowest pupil-teacher ratio, smallest class sizes and has the highest paid teaching staff of anywhere in the UK.

“Class contact time reduction in secondary schools is not due to be implemented until 2029, giving time to continue building workforce capacity.”
