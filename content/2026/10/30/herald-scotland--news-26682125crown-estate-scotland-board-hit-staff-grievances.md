The board of an important Scottish Government public body was hit by a series of grievances from staff, it can be revealed.

The development has been made public following a freedom of information (FOI) request by The Herald and show that nine grievances against the Crown Estate Scotland's governing body were made over the last three years.

In its response to the FOI request from The Herald, the Crown Estate Scotland (CES) said: "We have a record of nine complaints or grievances made against the Crown Estate Scotland board in the period in question."

CES was asked by The Herald on Thursday to detail the nature of the grievances made by staff against the board, but the organisation declined to go into detail.

A spokesperson said "significant changes" had been made, "both to the board and to overall corporate governance in the past year" which had "greatly improved the way the board operates".

The Herald also asked under freedom of information legislation if if any grievances had been made against CES's chief executive Ronan O'Hara.

Bur CES rejected this part of the request citing a freedom of information exemption relating to personal data.

It said: "Crown Estate Scotland applies section 18(1) of FOISA [Freedom of Information Scotland Act], with respect to this part of your request.

"If the information existed and were held, it would be exempt from disclosure under section 38(1)(b) of FOISA 'third-party personal data.'

"Your request concerns a single, identifiable individual. Any information falling within its scope would therefore be that individual’s personal data as defined in the Data Protection Act 2018.

"Section 18(1) is subject to a public interest test. We recognise the public interest in transparency, and in the conduct of senior members of staff.

"However, revealing whether this information exists would itself disclose personal information about the Chief Executive. We have therefore concluded that the public interest lies in upholding the exception."

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21647493.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The board of the Crown Estate Scotland has been hit by a string of staff grievances. *(Image: Crown Estate Scotland)*

The board of Crown Estate Scotland has experienced a number of changes to its membership in recent years including the departure of its former chair Euan McVicar, who was appointed chair in June 2023.

Cabinet secretary for climate action and rural affairs Gillian Martin announced the appointment of board member Michael Stewart as the new chair of the board in July this year for a three year period. The part-time appointment attracts a remuneration of £400 per day for a time commitment of 104 days per year.

Mr Stewart became a board member in November 2025 and had been an interim co-chair since April 2026 along with Ailsa Raeburn.

Established in 2017, CES manages land and property owned by the Crown and is a self-financing Statutory Public Corporation accountable to the Scottish Ministers and to the Scottish Parliament.

It is responsible for managing a diverse portfolio of land and property - called the Scottish Crown Estate - including seabed, coastline, commercial property and rural estates.

Income generated from leases, such as aquaculture, farming, and offshore wind, from the Scottish Crown Estate are used to run the organisation with profits transferred to the Scottish Consolidated Fund for public spending and capital profits are reinvested in the Estate.

It's portfolio includes Scotland's seabed, out to 12 nautical miles, about half of the foreshore, around 37,000 hectares of rural estates, salmon fishing rights, and rights to naturally-occurring gold and silver.

The organisation, which is separate from Crown Estate in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, and is a public corporation, plays a central role in managing offshore renewable energy leases, including major programmes like ScotWind, which leased portions of the seabed to energy companies for the installation of offshore wind farms.

Last month the public spending watchdog Audit Scotland said the Scottish Government must be more "transparent" over how it will use £755 million generated by the ScotWind project.

The Audit Scotland report stated: "In 2020, Crown Estate Scotland (CES) launched ScotWind. The leasing round offered buyers capped prices for the option to develop the seabed - rather than maximising immediate returns through an open auction.

"The rationale for the decision was to increase the chance of securing long-term lease payments. The CES board scrutinised the approach and it was agreed by the Scottish Government. However, no single business case for the ScotWind leasing round was produced."

The Auditor General Stephen Boyle added: “Crown Estate Scotland and the Scottish Government took a high risk and reward approach to the ScotWind leasing round.

“Whether that decision represents value for money will be determined in the coming years.

“If developers sign long-term leases before their option periods end, then annual payments will continue to benefit Scotland for the next 60 years.

“However, if they give up their options, the capped option pricing model will result in limited wider economic benefits and substantially reduced public revenues.

“What is now important is that the Scottish Government is much more transparent about their plans for how the ScotWind funds will be used across this parliamentary term and beyond.”

The Scottish Government has said the diversity of the property, rights and interests comprising the Scottish Crown Estate means that decisions on investment, use and sale of assets have the potential to deliver significant economic, social, and environmental benefits for communities and business across Scotland.

A spokesperson for Crown Estate Scotland said: “When any grievance is lodged, the matter is taken seriously and investigated thoroughly."

The spokesperson added: "Significant changes have been made, both to the board and to overall corporate governance in the past year, changes which we believe have greatly improved the way the board operates and the way it works with the rest of the Crown Estate Scotland team.

"To be clear - there have been no complaints raised against the board since November 2025, nor are there any active complaints against the board, or against individual board members.

"Crown Estate Scotland has a new Chair – Michael Stewart. He was recruited alongside four new members between November 2025 and January 2026 as part of the normal recruitment process.

"Governance has also been strengthened with a refreshed board code of conduct and enhanced training for new board members and staff. Whilst we are not complacent, we believe these – and other – changes have made a significant difference to the effective and efficient running of the organisation and its ability to deliver for Scotland."

It is understood none of the grievances were referred to the Ethical Standards Commissioner, the formal investigator of complaints against non-executive members. Two of the nine were formal grievances and these were both subsequently retracted.
