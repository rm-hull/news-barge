The Court of Session has quashed orders to release parts of the so-called Salmond files, ruling that Scotland's Information Commissioner applied the wrong legal test when judging whether disclosure could identify the women who complained about Alex Salmond.

In a judgment issued on Friday, the Inner House allowed the Scottish Government's appeals against two decision notices from Commissioner David Hamilton and sent both back to him to think again.

However, the three judges, led by the Lord President, Lord Pentland, rejected the government's argument that the Commissioner's job is merely to check whether ministers had a reasonable basis for withholding information.

The battle stems from a Freedom of Information request by student Benjamin Harrop for documents linked to the inquiry by James Hamilton into whether Nicola Sturgeon breached the ministerial code over the botched handling of harassment complaints against her predecessor.

The Scottish Government refused, arguing the information was not "held" by ministers, as Mr Hamilton was independent and the material sat in a restricted part of the government IT system accessible only to him and his team.

Mr Harrop took the case to the Scottish Information Commissioner, who in January 2023 said the information was held by the Scottish Ministers and ordered a fresh response.

The government appealed to the Court of Session, but on December 6, 2023 the Inner House rejected the appeal, confirming the Commissioner's view.

Ministers responded on January 23, 2024 by providing links to already-public material but withheld the bulk of the evidence under various FOISA exemptions.

The Commissioner accepted that some of the material fell under the exemption. He ordered the rest released.

The Scottish Government ultimately took the case to the Inner House, which found the Commissioner erred in how he reached that decision.

The ruling is the first time the court has examined section 26(c) of the Freedom of Information (Scotland) Act 2002, which exempts information if releasing it would be a contempt of court.

Unlike some other exemptions in the Act, it is absolute, so no public interest test applies.

In ordering the information to be disclosed, Mr Hamilton accepted that it would allow people to guess who the women were, but it would not prove those guesses right or wrong.

The court said that was the wrong question. The orders protecting the complainers are there to stop any real risk of them being identified, and a guess that gets stronger is part of that risk.

Lord Pentland, delivering the opinion, said the Commissioner had set the bar for contempt too high, and pointed to the 2022 appeal of Craig Murray, the blogger jailed for eight months for contempt over articles that risked identifying complainers.

![Craig Murray was jailed for contempt over articles that risked identifying the complainers](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/12849327.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Craig Murray was jailed for contempt over articles that risked identifying the complainers

The judges said the ruling in that case established that a breach does not require certainty or probability, only a real or substantial risk of identification.

The opinion said: "It is sufficient for a breach of the orders if disclosure of the information would produce a real or substantial risk of identification. There is no element of probability involved.

"If, as the Commissioner accepted, there would be a risk of assumptions being made about the complainers’ identities due to release of the information, this would serve to heighten the danger of their being identified.

"The orders would thereby be breached. It is not a prerequisite of their being breached that assumptions should be capable of being confirmed as the Commissioner reasoned.

"That approach carries with it a flavour of certainty or definitiveness which is not essential for there to be a breach. It is sufficient if disclosure of the information would create a real risk of identification of the complainers in the communities in which they lived or worked. "

It added: "Deliberate breach of any of the orders would amount to contempt of court. There can be no doubt that were the complainers’ identities to be revealed, their safety and that of their families could be endangered. "

The Commissioner's office had argued that he could not be faulted for failing to apply Murray, because ministers never cited it to him. The judges said it was "undoubtedly surprising and unfortunate" that they had not.

But they said the risk of jigsaw identification, where harmless-looking fragments are pieced together to reveal someone's identity, was clearly in play, and it would be unconscionable for the court to ignore the law.

Ministers had argued the Commissioner wrongly saw himself as the sole judge of whether disclosure would be a contempt, when only a court could decide that, and that he should merely have asked whether they had a proper basis for invoking the exemption.

The court rejected that. It said the Commissioner must reach his own independent view and owes no deference to a public authority. The opinion said: "He did not misdirect himself by misunderstanding his statutory function and what his responsibilities entailed."

The Scottish Information Commissioner's office “welcomed” the clarity offered by the court opinion: “The Commissioner will now carefully consider the court’s opinion but welcomes the clarification offered by the court in relation to the purpose, meaning and effect of the court orders referenced.

“The commissioner agrees with the court that it was ‘surprising and unfortunate’ that during the lengthy investigation undertaken by the commissioner the Scottish ministers made no reference to, nor did they at any stage seek to rely upon, the case of Murray, which is central to the court’s judgment.

“Consequently, although a thorough assessment of risk had been considered by the commissioner to prevent over-redaction by the Scottish ministers, this was not explicitly carried out within the framework of Murray.

“These decisions are now remitted back to the commissioner for his reconsideration.”

A Scottish Government spokesperson said: “The Scottish Government welcomes the decision of the court and will continue to uphold the court orders to protect the identities of women in cases of alleged sexual crimes.”
