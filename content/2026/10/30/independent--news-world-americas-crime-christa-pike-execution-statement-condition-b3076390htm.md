Christa Pike, the Tennessee death row inmate who survived a botched execution, has broken her silence after the failed attempt to describe the physical and emotional pain she has felt in the weeks that followed.

The 50-year-old convicted murderer was administered two doses of the lethal injection drug pentobarbital at Riverbend Maximum Security Institution on September 30 before officials halted the execution. She was rushed to the hospital and is recovering after the attempt, though her future health condition and whether the state will try to execute her again remain unclear.

“I am searching for meaning in what happened to me,” Pike said in a lengthy statement released Friday to Knoxville news station WBIR.

She also described the lasting physical and emotional toll of the procedure.

“There hasn't been a moment since that night when I haven't been in pain. My body hurts. My arms hurt. My hands hurt. My throat hurts. I still struggle to speak,” Pike said. “And the pain isn't just physical. There is pain in my mind and in my soul.”

![Christa Pike survived a botched execution attempt in Tennessee on September 30 after receiving two doses of the lethal injection drug pentobarbital](https://static.the-independent.com/2026/10/11/17/2026-10-10T171428Z_268372870_RC2PTNAO1FU0_RTRMADP_3_TENNESSEE-EXECUTION.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Pike said she had made peace with her impending death before entering the execution chamber and had not considered the possibility of surviving. Days later, she woke up in a hospital, confused and in pain.

“I had not contemplated life beyond that night, and I’m deeply hurt by the incompetence that has put so many people in this terrible position,” she said.

Although Pike said she is recovering and getting stronger, she is still struggling emotionally.

“But healing is not the same as being OK,” she said.

Pike was sentenced to death in March 1996 for the 1995 murder of 19-year-old Colleen Slemmer, a fellow student at a Job Corps center in Knoxville, Tennessee. Pike was 18 when she and her boyfriend, Tadaryl Shipp, 17, lured Slemmer into a wooded area, tortured her and killed her.

“I continue to think about my victim’s family and the terrible pain and loss they have endured,” Pike wrote in her statement.

Shipp remains behind bars. He was denied parole in October and will not be eligible for another review until October 2031. He has been in custody since January 1995.

Pike was scheduled to become the first woman executed in Tennessee in more than 200 years. However, the execution was halted after Pike remained alive. Media witnesses reported hearing her make snoring sounds after the second dose, while her attorneys sought emergency medical intervention. She was eventually transported by ambulance to a hospital.

The failed execution has brought up questions about Tennessee's lethal injection procedures and the medical care Pike received. Her attorneys say staff struggled to insert the IV needed to deliver the drugs, with one lawyer claiming they stuck Pike with about 10 needles trying to find a vein.

![Pike, pictured here in 2007, was scheduled to become the first woman executed in Tennessee in more than 200 years](https://static.the-independent.com/2026/10/07/00/2026-10-06T220818Z_71220444_RC2PTNAKF4UE_RTRMADP_3_TENNESSEE-EXECUTION.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Pike was hospitalized for several days before being discharged on October 10 and returned to the Debra K. Johnson Rehabilitation Center in Nashville, where she had been held before the execution attempt. She continues to receive medical care in the prison's medical ward.

She said Friday that the isolation has been particularly difficult, especially after spending 28 years in solitary confinement before the failed execution.

"Much of what I am experiencing today is a repeat of that condition," Pike said, citing the lack of human connection as another source of pain.

Despite her circumstances, Pike said support from people around the world has helped her find strength. She thanked those who have sent prayers and messages, as well as the first responders, hospital staff and attorneys who helped her.

"To know that so many people care about me, that they see me as a human being whose life matters, is something I cannot fully put into words," she said.

Tennessee Governor Bill Lee halted the state's remaining scheduled executions for the rest of 2026 following Pike's failed execution and ordered a review of the incident. Pike's attorneys have also sought to have her death sentence commuted to life imprisonment.

Pike said she does not know why she survived or what will happen next, but is trying to focus on her recovery.

"Some days, finding hope is harder than others," she said. "For now, I am trying to heal, one day at a time."

## Read Pike’s statement in full:

“One month ago on September 30, I walked into the execution chamber at Riverbend men’s prison prepared to die. I had made my peace. I had told the people who loved me, supported me and stood by me through the years I was grateful for them. I had also told the people in the room and who were there to witness my execution that I was leaving them in loving kindness.”

“Several days later I woke up alone in a hospital confused and in pain. There hasn't been a moment since that night when I haven't been in pain. My body hurts. My arms hurt. My hands hurt. My throat hurts. I still struggle to speak. And the pain isn't just physical. There is pain in my mind and in my soul.”

“I had not contemplated life beyond that night, and I’m deeply hurt by the incompetence that has put so many people in this terrible position. I continue to think about my victim’s family and the terrible pain and loss they have endured.”

“I am recovering, and I know that I am getting stronger. But healing is not the same as being okay.”

“Since that night, I have been largely isolated in the prison’s medical ward, away from other people and the human connections that mean so much to me. That isolation brings its own kind of pain. I spent 28 years in solitary confinement and much of what I am experiencing today is a repeat of that condition.”

“I am searching for meaning in what happened to me. One thing that has given me strength is the extraordinary love and support I have received from people around the world. I am overwhelmed by the kindness and prayers of people I have known for years and complete strangers who have never met me. To know that so many people care about me, that they see me as a human being whose life matters, is something I cannot fully put into words. It is humbling, and it has touched me more deeply than I can explain.”

“I also want to thank the first responders, doctors, nurses and everyone at the hospital who cared for me. They treated me with compassion, dignity and respect. I will never forget their kindness. I also want to thank my attorneys and everyone who has fought for me, stood beside me and refused to give up on me.”

“I don't know why I survived that night. I don't know what comes next. Some days, finding hope is harder than others. For now, I am trying to heal, one day at a time. And I am holding on to the love and kindness of so many people who have reminded me that I am not alone.”
