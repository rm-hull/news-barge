Andy Burnham has said he and John Swinney can "find a way of working together for Scotland's benefit" despite their constitutional differences.

In an impromptu walkabout after meeting leaders from Scotland and Wales and representatives from Northern Ireland at the National Economic Council, the Prime Minister told reporters he had received a “positive response” from the SNP leader to his offer of more devolution.

Mr Burnham said the UK Government would look at devolving innovation funding to the regions to support reindustrialisation, and at giving Scotland and Wales more control over employment support, particularly for young people.

The Prime Minister also suggested Glasgow could get more control over its own finances, similar to new powers for English city regions announced in the Budget.

He said: “I think it’s just a good thing that the two governments that people have here, the UK Government and the Scottish Government , work together and find common ground rather than look for differences, and that’s what we’ve been trying to do today.

“Find the common ground, and there is common ground, and then work together.”

The Prime Minister said he and the First Minister would “find a way of working together for Scotland’s benefit”.

He said: “I know John. I’ve worked with John over many years. I think I first met him, goodness me, 20 years ago when I was Chief Secretary to the Treasury and then we had sometimes robust discussions, but now I’ve worked with John over many years.

“I’ve got to know him. I think we can find a way of working together for Scotland’s benefit. I do think we need to get more power down into all parts of Scotland.

“To get growth going, my experience in Greater Manchester is that it’s when you’ve got power at that level where local people can come together with businesses and start to solve things.

“That’s where things really start to change. Obviously, the Budget this week has made big strides on further devolution in England.

“We’re looking at fiscal devolution to city regions and I don’t want to see Scotland left out in that.

“This is an incredible place. I love coming here to the great city of Glasgow.

“I want it to have set up in the same way to forge its own path, be proud of its amazing culture and identity and do what it wants to do and put it more in control of its own affairs.

“That’s what I’m about and it seems that we had a lot of common ground together today.”

Mr Burnham has put devolution at the heart of his agenda since entering Downing Street this summer, pledging to “rewire the state” and hand more control to regional mayors and authorities.

As part of that, he has revived the National Economic Council, first set up by Gordon Brown during the 2008 financial crisis.

The Prime Minister came to the meeting of the council in Glasgow with six members of his Cabinet and another two ministers.

While the meeting involved Mr Swinney and Welsh First Minister Rhun ap Iorwerth, Northern Ireland was represented by officials after the political fallout from the Drumcree parade dispute saw the DUP block the Sinn Féin First Minister from attending.

As well as the “top-tier” meeting between the First Ministers and the Prime Minister, there was a bilateral meeting involving Mr Swinney, his deputy Jenny Gilruth, Mr Burnham and Minister for Intergovernmental Relations Hamish Falconer.

Speaking to reporters, Mr Swinney said there had been “a series of constructive discussions this morning about a range of issues”.

He confirmed he had raised independence with the Prime Minister and said he would keep pushing for a referendum.

“The Prime Minister and I don’t agree on the question of independence, but I reiterate my point that I intend to apply the pressure and mobilise opinion within Scotland to secure a referendum on independence during this parliamentary term.”

He said he had set out to the Prime Minister “a range of proposals that I think would help us to increase sustainable economic growth in Scotland”.

Mr Burnham had “agreed to consider those issues”, he added.

“I don’t want to go into any more detail about them today because I want to respect the intergovernmental space to allow those issues to be considered, and I think that’s the right thing to do.

“It gives us the time, gives Parliament the time and the opportunity to think through the issues that I’m raising, which I think all of them would be of benefit in strengthening the ability of the Scottish Parliament and Scottish Government to deliver higher levels of economic growth.”

Mr Swinney said they had also discussed the UK’s relationship with the EU, which he said has "significant implications" for Scotland.

Mr Burnham has promised to set out options for a closer relationship, which could include rejoining the single market, the customs union or the EU itself, around next month’s UK-EU summit.

The First Minister said the discussion had been open and there may be further dialogue.

In July, Mr Swinney said the constructive relationship the Scottish Government had had with the UK Government “haemorrhaged” after Douglas Alexander was made Scottish Secretary.

Asked whether things were now improving, he said: “I engage in these discussions in good faith, with the determination to secure the best deal I can for Scotland, and we have had a constructive discussion this morning, which enables us to do that.”

The First Minister said there was an “alignment” between Mr Burnham’s devolution proposals and his government’s bid to reform local government.

Scottish Labour leader, Michael Marra, rejected the idea.

“I don’t think the two things are the same thing. If John Swinney wants to make a process of public service reform about getting powers out of Holyrood into local regions to make the difference in terms of economic growth, then we’ll be full partners in that.”

He warned the Scottish Government against taking too long, pointing to the cost of the last overhaul of local government.

“The last time we reorganised local government, it took a decade and it cost hundreds of millions of pounds.”

Mr Marra said Scotland could not wait for a similar process and had to move at pace. He said he was prepared to work with the SNP to make that happen, but wanted “good faith actions on both sides”.
