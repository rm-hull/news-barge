NatWest has delivered another highly profitable quarter, but investors appeared less impressed as rising loan losses and the prospect of a heavier tax burden took some of the shine off the banking group’s performance.

The owner of the Royal Bank of Scotland reported £1.53 billion of profit attributable to shareholders in the third quarter, while income continued to rise and lending expanded across the business. Yet NatWest shares fell as investors focused on signs that the favourable conditions enjoyed by the UK's big banks may be getting harder to sustain.

Will Howlett, financials analyst at Quilter Cheviot, described the performance as “solid” but “fairly uneventful”, noting that NatWest had not followed upgrades earlier in the year with another improvement to its guidance.

He said quarterly profit before tax of £2.2bn was “slightly below market expectations due to weaker net interest margin and higher loan losses than expected”.

The rise in loan losses was one of the more striking features of the results. NatWest recorded a £277 million impairment charge during the quarter, almost double the £140m booked during the preceding three months.

The bank nevertheless continues to generate strong returns and grow its balance sheet. Customer assets and liabilities passed £1 trillion during the quarter, while lending and deposits increased.

Chief executive Paul Thwaite said the results represented “another strong performance”, with lending, deposits and assets under management all increasing.

“We are confident in the strengths we’ve created and in the opportunities we see ahead, as we continue to invest in our capabilities, deepen customer relationships and support growth across the UK,” he added.

But the environment in which NatWest is generating these returns is becoming more challenging.

The results follow the Budget announcement of a temporary increase in the surcharge on bank profits, a development which could be particularly significant for NatWest. From January, the surcharge applied to banks’ taxable profits above a £100m allowance will rise from 3% to 6% for 15 months, returning to 3% in April 2028.

"At present, the higher for longer interest rate environment should help NatWest, as well as the other UK banks, going forward, but there are concerns that the increased bank tax surcharge will not be as time-limited as was announced," Mr Howlett said.

"NatWest is more domestically focused than its peers and thus will be among the hardest hit, so while the new rate is not as bad as feared, NatWest hasn’t got off lightly."

Another concern is how much of the recent strength of banking profits can be maintained as economic conditions change.

Nick Sherrard, managing director of Label Sessions, said strong lending growth, rising deposits and the £1 trillion customer-assets-and-liabilities milestone showed NatWest had moved beyond simply cutting costs and simplifying its business to drive performance.

However, he argued that the tailwinds which have supported UK banks are now dissipating, meaning future growth will increasingly have to come from initiatives undertaken by NatWest itself. These include its expansion in wealth management following the acquisition of Evelyn Partners.

“Against an increasingly uncertain economic backdrop, today’s results suggest NatWest is successfully balancing operational discipline with long-term investment well," Mr Sherrard said. "But innovation stops being a strategy slide right now and needs to start being a bigger part of the earnings story – and there are indications that is beginning to happen.”
