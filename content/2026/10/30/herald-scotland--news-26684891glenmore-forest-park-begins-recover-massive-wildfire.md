A beauty spot ravaged by a wildfire that burned for days over the summer is beginning to return to life.

However, conservationists are warning that it will take decades to fully recover, and some ancient trees could still be lost.

Glenmore Forest Park was significantly damaged by the wildfire that broke out on July 15 and was declared a major incident on July 24, prompting the evacuation of nearby Nethy Bridge and Glenmore.

Months later, native trees have been spotted resprouting, vegetation is returning and wildlife has begun moving back into the area.

Forestry and Land Scotland (FLS) said monitoring of the site has revealed encouraging signs of recovery, with birch, rowan and willow regenerating from surviving stems and root systems.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21650294.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Glenmore Forest Park suffered significant fire damage *(Image: FLS)*

Grasses, bracken and blaeberry are also returning, particularly in areas where moisture remained in the soil or where the fire burned less intensely, with large sections of the affected hillside already turning green once again.

Wildlife has also reappeared. While crews were still extinguishing hotspots after the blaze, deer and capercaillie were regularly detected by drones surveying the landscape.

Foresters believe deer have been drawn back by the fresh grass growth emerging in the aftermath of the fire.

The blaze also cleared much of the forest floor vegetation, a process that is expected to trigger a new wave of tree regeneration. The first Scots pine and birch seedlings have already been discovered in the burned areas, raising hopes that a new generation of woodland can become established over the coming years.

Despite those positive signs, concerns remain about the long-term future of the forest's iconic Scots pines. Older trees that have matured over centuries may have been fatally damaged, while younger regeneration is also thought to have been heavily affected.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21650296.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The agency is examining how experience gained from Glenmore can inform future measures *(Image: FLS)*

FLS FM and Stewardship Forester Eelco de Jong said: "Broadleaved recovery is promising with lots of birch, rowan and willow shooting up from the main stems or base of burned trees.

"Where their root systems are still intact, they will grow back quickly.

"We're also seeing grasses and blaeberry coming back, and large parts of the hill have already turned green again.

"There are significant challenges ahead, particularly for our Scots pine and the areas where the organic soil layer has been badly affected.

"The concern is many of the oldest 'granny pines' could be lost, removing important seed sources and habitat for species such as capercaillie.

"Some of these veteran trees were more than 100 or 200 years old and, where losses occur, it could take centuries for trees of a similar stature to return to the landscape."

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21650297.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Birch, rowan and willow shoots are emerging from the main stems or base of burned trees *(Image: FLS)*

In some of the more densely wooded parts of the forest, the fire burned through the organic soil layer, leaving fine ash behind and potentially affecting long-term recovery.

FLS said further work will be required to understand how these areas respond to heavy rainfall and snowmelt, as well as how to manage trees left unstable after root damage.

The wildfire is also providing lessons that could help improve resilience across Scotland's forests and landholdings.

The agency is examining how experience gained from Glenmore can inform future measures such as firebreaks, peatland and wetland restoration, access management and wildfire planning across its estate.

FLS is also working with partners including the RSPB, Highlands and Islands Enterprise, Cairngorms Connect and the Cairngorms National Park Authority through a newly established Environmental Response Group.

The group is coordinating monitoring, future recovery work and scientific studies, including the use of advanced LiDAR imaging to map the forest and build a clearer picture of how the landscape responds in the months and years ahead.
