*(Image: Wild Intrigue)*

Beavers could soon return to the River Ness centuries after they became extinct in the region, after the go-ahead was given to bring up to 15 families into the area.

NatureScot has approved an application to carry out a controlled release of the animals over a three-year period.

Beavers were once native to Scotland but died out in the 16th century after being hunted to extinction.

Families of the aquatic mammals have already been released at several sites, including Knapdale in Argyll, Loch Lomond and the Cairngorms National Park.

A feral population of beavers exists on the River Tay, thought to have originated from escaped animals or those introduced unlawfully. Perth has also been said to have a group of "urban beavers".

Now, a licence has been granted to Trees for Life and the Beaver Trust to bring the animals to the River Ness in a project running until 2029.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/16404160.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Beavers during a previous release *(Image: Joshua Glavin)*

The decision is intended to expand the current range of beavers in Scotland and supports a key priority set out in Scotland's Beaver Strategy 2022-2045.

The application was assessed in line with the Scottish Code for Conservation Translocations, which considers factors including site suitability, animal welfare, and potential impacts on neighbouring landowners and local communities.

NatureScot's assessment concluded that beavers could significantly boost biodiversity in the catchment, while the landscape is considered to be at low risk of negative impacts from beaver activity.

Responses to the applicants' consultation process were largely positive, with more than 73% of respondents expressing support for the proposal.

However, concerns were raised by some land managers, fisheries interests and residents living within the catchment area.

Robbie Kernahan, NatureScot's director for wildlife management, said: "After a careful assessment, we believe that bringing beavers back to the Ness catchment will significantly improve the biodiversity of this part of Scotland."

He added: "Beavers can bring many benefits for nature and people, creating habitats such as ponds and wetlands where other species thrive, as well as moderating water flows during periods of flooding and water scarcity."

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/11420890.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Beavers fight each other in Tayside *(Image: Newsquest)*

Kernahan said concerns raised during the consultation had been taken seriously and that mitigation and monitoring plans, alongside NatureScot's existing Beaver Mitigation Scheme, should help resolve any conflicts that arise.

The closest existing beaver populations to the River Ness catchment are at Abriachan and in Strathglass, with at least one beaver known to be resident on the River Oich.

However, NatureScot said the short-term establishment of a genetically diverse population in the River Ness catchment through natural expansion from those populations is unlikely.

Any beavers released under the scheme will be trapped and relocated under licence from areas where they are having a negative impact on prime agricultural land and where mitigation measures have either failed or are not possible.

The animals will undergo appropriate health screening before being released into the catchment.
