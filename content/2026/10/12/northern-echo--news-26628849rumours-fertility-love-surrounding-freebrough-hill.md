A North Yorkshire hill shrouded in legend and mystery has long captured the imagination of passers-by.

Freebrough Hill is a distinctive North York Moors landmark near Moorsholm, surrounded by legends of King Arthur, giants, Roman signal stations and the Black Death.

Some locals believe it is the burial site of plague victims from the Black Death, while others have suggested it was constructed by ancient peoples for ceremonial purposes.

One legend even claims it was formed by a giant who threw a mound of earth across the landscape, leaving behind the Hole of Horcum near Pickering.

Residents recall childhood warnings that sheep near the summit meant good weather, while creatures behind the hill would chase children who failed to wear seatbelts.

Theories about Freebrough Hill's origins are almost as varied as the legends themselves.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/12187401.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Cllr Steve Kay, with his sword, in front of Freebrough Hill *(Image: Provided)*

Having researched the stories surrounding the landmark, East Cleveland Councillor Steve Kay believes some have been passed down for centuries.

He says the hill's unusually smooth, rounded shape has long fuelled speculation, giving it the appearance of something built by human hands rather than carved by nature.

There are even suggestions that the hill’s name is linked to Freya, the Norse goddess of love and fertility, as it was first known as Freyasberg.

Nearby Roseberry Topping was once associated with Odin, a major figure in Norse mythology - so the connection is not misplaced.

In the 18th century, John Hall Stevenson of Skelton Castle famously suggested the hill was the tomb of King Arthur.

The theory took hold because of the hill's near-perfect shape seeming more the work of people than nature.

According to Cllr Kay, some believed Britain's greatest king would be buried only beneath a monument worthy of his name, and Freebrough Hill seemed to fit the bill.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/12187400.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The 2021 Royal Mail stamp featuring Freebrough Hill *(Image: Provided)*

However, most geologists agree that Freebrough Hill is a natural formation created during the Ice Age, when glaciers stripped away softer rock around it.

Made from the same hard rock as Roseberry Topping, Freebrough Hill stands out for the same reason its more famous neighbour does - it was left behind as softer surrounding land was worn away over thousands of years.

Its geology is remarkably similar to that of Roseberry Topping, helping explain why both landmarks rise so prominently from the surrounding landscape.

The hill is formed from the same tough rock as Roseberry Topping, allowing it to withstand erosion and remain one of the most distinctive features on the North York Moors skyline.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/17274509.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Roseberry Topping *(Image: Gill Davidson/NORTHERN ECHO CAMERA CLUB)*

Local historian Marjorie Brookes once offered a more unusual theory, suggesting the Romans may have adapted the hill as a beacon station, using it to relay signals between the coast and inland settlements when Britain faced Saxon raids.

The earthworks and burial barrows scattered across the surrounding landscape also suggest the area held significance for ancient communities, regardless of how the hill itself was formed.

That blend of folklore, archaeology and geology is what continues to make Freebrough Hill such an enduring source of fascination.

Whether viewed as a geological wonder, an ancient landmark or simply a source of local legend, it continues to capture imaginations as it rises above the moors.
