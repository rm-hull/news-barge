Hate Crime Awareness Week begins on Monday, 12 October, with police, councils and community organisations across York and North Yorkshire organising events intended to raise awareness, encourage reporting and improve support for victims.

The programme includes workshops on racism, antisemitism, misogyny, extremism and transgender identity, alongside public engagement events in Harrogate and other parts of the region.

However, the campaign also raises a wider question about how hate crime is defined, recorded and communicated to the public, particularly the distinction between criminal offences, incidents involving perceived hostility and lawful speech that others may find offensive.

## Events planned across the region

The week is organised locally by the York and North Yorkshire Inclusive Communities Joint Coordinating Group. Its stated aims include raising awareness of hate crime, building confidence in services, encouraging reporting and promoting support for victims.

Training sessions will cover topics including antisemitism, racism in sport, misogyny, far-right ideology and online or social subcultures. The programme also includes a launch event for the North Yorkshire Interfaith Network at North Yorkshire Police Headquarters in Northallerton on Tuesday, 13 October.

Other activities include a partnership day at York St John University, a MENCAP community engagement event in Northallerton, and local information stalls and engagement sessions across the county.

## What counts as a hate crime?

The campaign covers hostility or prejudice linked to characteristics including race, religion, disability, sexual orientation and transgender identity. The programme also addresses misogyny, although its treatment within criminal law and policing frameworks requires careful distinction from the other categories.

Under the established approach in England and Wales, a hate crime is a criminal offence perceived by the victim or another person to be motivated by hostility or prejudice towards a protected characteristic. The perception of hostility is relevant to how an incident is identified and investigated, but it does not, by itself, establish that a crime has occurred or that an offence has been proved.

That distinction matters. An allegation, a reported incident, a police-recorded crime and a criminal conviction are not interchangeable measures. Nor does the fact that somebody finds a remark offensive automatically make it a criminal offence.

Police and other agencies have a legitimate role in identifying offences motivated by prejudice and ensuring victims can obtain help. At the same time, the public should be able to understand what the figures being reported actually represent, how incidents are categorised and what evidence is required before criminal liability can be established.

There is also a wider question about the balance between tackling genuine intimidation and discrimination and protecting freedom of expression. A society can condemn threats, assault and harassment without treating every offensive opinion, disagreement or expression of religious or political belief as criminal conduct.

Clear definitions, transparent recording practices and proportionate responses are therefore important both for protecting victims and for maintaining public confidence in the system.

## Police and councils encourage reporting

Temporary Assistant Chief Constable Fiona Willey, of North Yorkshire Police, said the week would allow partner agencies to share best practice and improve their response to hate crime.

> **Temporary Assistant Chief Constable Fiona Willey, North Yorkshire Police:**
>
> There is no place for hate crime in our communities, and we will continue to work together with our partners to eliminate it across York and North Yorkshire.

She said the aim was for victims to know how to report incidents and seek professional help, with confidence that agencies would support them and seek justice.

North Yorkshire Council executive member for corporate services, Councillor Heather Phillips, said the programme was intended to provide practical advice, training and support, while promoting understanding between communities.

Councillor Anna Perrett, executive member for safer communities at City of York Council, also encouraged residents to report incidents and said partner organisations were looking at ways to improve community cohesion and their responses.

Deputy Mayor for Policing, Fire and Crime Jo Coles said levels of hate crime and discrimination across York and North Yorkshire were low, but that incidents were often under-reported.

That assessment raises a question relevant to the public debate: how are reported levels measured, and how much of the apparent picture reflects criminal offences, allegations or other incidents recorded by participating organisations? Such distinctions are important when interpreting claims about prevalence and trends.

## Harrogate event

Residents will be able to meet representatives at an engagement event in Harrogate town centre on Wednesday, 14 October, between 1pm and 4pm.

Other events include information sessions in Selby, Skipton, Malton, Scarborough and Whitby, as well as a series of roadshows in Northallerton, Stokesley, Easingwold, Bedale and Thirsk.

## How to report a crime or seek support

Anyone who believes they have been the victim of a crime can report it to North Yorkshire Police. Non-emergency reports can be made through the North Yorkshire Police website or by calling 101. Call 999 where an emergency response is required.

Support and advice are also available through organisations including Supporting Victims, Crimestoppers, the Community Security Trust, Tell MAMA and Galop.

Encouraging people to seek help when they have experienced genuine threats, violence or discrimination is important. Equally, public confidence depends on clarity about what is being reported, the distinction between allegations and proven offences, and the protection of lawful expression.
