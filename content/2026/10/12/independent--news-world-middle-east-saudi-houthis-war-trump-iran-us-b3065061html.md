Yemen’s Houthi rebels have launched a lightning offensive across the country's Red Sea coast, seizing control of the strategic Bab el-Mandeb strait and threatening to expand the frozen US-Israeli conflict with Iran to the east.

The land grab has been backed by a deadly campaign of deep strikes inside the kingdom. Over the weekend, Houthi missile and drone attacks struck King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, killing at least 13 people — marking the deadliest attack in the kingdom in more than a decade.

By securing a position overlooking the narrow Bab el-Mandeb strait, the Iran-aligned group has opened a second pressure point, further squeezing Saudi Arabia’s oil exports already diverted from the Strait of Hormuz.

Analysts say the main aim of the offensive is to secure control over the Red Sea as Iran maintains its grip over Hormuz, allowing them to raise oil prices and making it harder for Washington to resolve the crisis.

![A pickup truck burning following what the Houthis described as an attack on Saudi-backed forces in eastern Jawf province, on 9 Sep](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/11/15/download.-2..jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Gulf states – already deliberating over Iran’s ambitions to control shipping through Hormuz – will have to choose between tolerating the status quo and engaging with Tehran to protect trade across the region, they say.

Already fighting a deeply unpopular war to the east, Donald Trump has avoided any commitment to bailing ally Saudi Arabia out with strikes against the Houthis, though experts say US intervention may be needed to break the Houthi grip on the Red Sea.

*The Independent* looks at how the Houthis are advancing, and what this means for regional stability.

### Why are the Houthis fighting with Saudi Arabia?

The Houthi rebels are a military, political and religious movement that rules over most of northwest Yemen and has disrupted global trade and energy markets with strikes on cargo ships and tankers in the Red Sea.

The group exploited the chaos of the 2011 Arab Spring to oust the internationally recognised government from Sanaa in 2014, beginning the devastating Yemeni civil war.

![Armed forces patrol as prisoners loyal to the Houthi authorities arrive in the capital Sanaa on 12 September 2026](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/13/05/2294384190...jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Fearing the Iran-aligned group could seize control of the Bab el-Mandeb strait, and therefore threaten Riyadh’s ability to export oil, Saudi Arabia intervened a year later, supported by the US, UK, France and others.

A UN-brokered ceasefire followed in 2022 and since then, both sides have refrained from major escalatory actions. Iran has not directly intervened in Yemen, but the fighting is considered part of a wider Iran-Saudi proxy conflict.

It was during the Israeli war in Gaza, from 2023 to 2025, that the Houthis started attacking ships in the Red Sea in support of Iran-aligned Hamas. The UK and US hit Houthi targets in 2025 as part of a campaign to restore freedom of navigation.

Crucially, Trump last year struck a deal to end the US strikes on the Houthis in exchange for a halt to strikes on Red Sea shipping. Taking on the group now would likely expose US forces in the region to missile attacks from Yemen.

Clashes with Saudi Arabia restarted in July as both sides enforced new blockades on maritime traffic. The Houthis struck Saudi oil tankers accused of violating their blockade.

### What are they doing now?

Despite years of conflict – and with some 14 per cent of Yemen’s population displaced – the Houthis last week oversaw an astonishing push east to the coast, granting them much greater control over traffic through the Red Sea.

The escalation has since expanded into a direct campaign against Saudi territory. Over the weekend, a barrage of Houthi missile and drone strikes hit King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, killing at least 13 people — including a US citizen — and injuring more than 300.

Additional strikes targeting Saudi energy infrastructure and municipal facilities have forced temporary airport closures and heightened regional security alerts.

![Smoke rises from King Khalid airport after a Houthi attack on Saturday](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/20/16/2026-09-19T114802Z_1980126111_RC28MNAXZ2HR_RTRMADP_3_IRAN-CRISIS-SAUDI-YEMEN.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Edmund Fitton-Brown, a former UK ambassador to Yemen, told *The Independent* that after months of attacks at sea, the Houthis “think the moment has come to call the Saudi bluff”.

“They think the Saudis have lost the stomach for the fight. They think that they showed themselves to be weak and frightened in the face of the Iranians,” said Mr Fitton-Brown, a senior fellow at Foundation for Defense of Democracies.

![Donald Trump and Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman last year](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/11/07/2026-09-11T044604Z_44533553_RC25ZHA6EWGZ_RTRMADP_3_USA-TRUMP-SAUDI-HOUTHIS.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“And the Houthis think that this is the moment where they can effectively raise their demands and get the Saudis to concede even more advantageous terms than they were previously willing to do.”

With Iran controlling the strait on one side of the Arabian peninsula, and the allied Houthis controlling the other, the wider war has handed more regional influence in the region to Tehran, with Gulf countries now forced to accept they may have to engage with Iran and give up their faith in Washington as a guarantor of security.

The war presents the US with a dilemma, too: intervene and spend huge resources on a second front in the deeply unpopular war with Iran, or watch on and undermine the relationship with another ally in the region. Sources told CNN that the US has offered intelligence and targeting support to the Saudis in lieu of military support.

### How are the Houthis breaching Saudi’s air defences?

While Saudi Arabia operates advanced air defence networks — including US-supplied Patriot and THAAD missile batteries — Houthi attacks continue to slip through.

This is because the Houthis rely on a combination of staggered salvoes and mixed arsenals to overwhelm radar capabilities. By launching slow-moving suicide drones first, followed by low-altitude cruise missiles and high-speed ballistic missiles only minutes later, the Houthis force defensive systems to engage multiple distinct threats arriving at the exact same moment.

Low-flying drones often bypass long-range radar designed primarily to track high-altitude targets. At the same time, the vast geographical footprint of the kingdom — covering major airports, military bases, and oil fields across millions of square kilometres — makes complete coverage nearly impossible.

The tactic also imposes a severe cost asymmetry on defenders. Firing multi-million-dollar interceptors to destroy cheap, assembled drones rapidly drains Saudi and allied missile stockpiles during sustained multi-wave attacks.

### What has changed on the ground?

Houthi commanders announced a broad offensive targeting critical energy infrastructure and urban centres across southern and central Saudi Arabia as their ground forces pushed west.

The capture of key coastal hubs like al-Mokha and strategic points on Perim Island effectively divides the Bab el-Mandeb strait.

Middle East analyst Fawaz Gerges described the seizure of key positions along the waterway as a structural shift in regional dynamics.

![A satellite image of King Khalid Airport, the day after Yemen's Iran-backed Houthis said they attacked the airport, in Riyadh](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/12/09/2026-10-11T120924Z_28260468_RC201OAWU6LY_RTRMADP_3_IRAN-CRISIS-SAUDI-AIRPORT.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“It changes the balance of power and gives them far greater geopolitical and geoeconomic leverage over Saudi Arabia, the Gulf, the United States and global trade,” he said.

Concurrently, attacks attributed to regional proxy networks struck Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline, severely impacting the primary overland bypass used to transport crude past the blocked Strait of Hormuz.

Sources told Reuters that rising economic losses and Washington's initial reluctance to launch direct strikes are convincing several Gulf leaderships that diplomatic outreach to Tehran may be necessary for self-preservation.

### What happens next?

Mr Fitton-Brown said the region was calling for American leadership to recognise the connectedness between the fronts and intervene, where the Saudis have been unable to uphold maritime law.

“For the Americans to ignore the Red Sea, to ignore this threat to Saudi Arabia from the Houthis, is just completely self-defeating,” he said.

![Damage in the aftermath of Houthi strikes on the Red Sea port city of Mocha, on 10 August](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/14/11/2026-09-13T170424Z_1976479503_RC2YWMA8AYNV_RTRMADP_3_IRAN-CRISIS-HOUTHIS-ADVANCE.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

He said he would expect the US to remain “flaky” on the issue for now – but not back out entirely.

“They’re not going to fall on their sword because every so often these provocations will cross a threshold where they have no choice but to respond,” he said.

The challenge would be to bring together a “hybrid” coalition, including some military participants, but also countries willing to enforce sanctions and join a diplomatic coalition to force Iran to “back down”.

“It can be done, but it requires a level of diplomatic patience,” he said.

Lindsay Newman, associate fellow with Chatham House, said that one of the major open questions was the resilience of Iran’s proxy network across the region. While Hamas and Hezbollah have been degraded, the Houthi military “are proving more intractable” despite efforts to stop them.

Crucially, she said, this was not addressed by June’s interim agreement between the US and Iran. Washington had been pushing to raise the issue of Iran’s support for regional proxies before the war broke out in February.

“[The] developments make clear that a resolution on the proxy question must be integrated into any offramp for the wider regional conflict. Otherwise these groups can and likely will resupply and remain disruptive across the region.”
