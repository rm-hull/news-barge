Ferry-dependent island communities face a deepening depopulation crisis, with the working-age population in sparsely populated parts of the Western Isles projected to fall by almost a third by 2043, while the number of children across the islands is forecast to plunge over the next decade.

The working-age population in the sparsely populated area of Na h-Eileanan Siar is projected to fall by 32% between 2018 and 2043, according to the Scottish Government-commissioned analysis seen by The Herald. The forecast applies to the sparsely populated sub-area, rather than the whole Western Isles council area, but underlines the scale of the long-term challenge facing remote communities.

The outlook for the next generation is also stark with the number of children aged under 15 in the Western Isles projected to fall by 21% between 2022 and 2032, with Orkney facing a 13% decline, according to a Scottish Government impact assessment. In Shetland, the population aged 0 to 15 is forecast to fall by almost 20% over the same decade, according to National Records of Scotland (NRS) analysis.

The Western Isles also faces an overall population fall of 3.6% between 2022 and 2032, from 26,120 to 25,190, under NRS projections which point to fewer young people entering the workforce and growing pressure on communities already struggling to retain residents, sustain businesses and maintain essential services.

Now MPs are warning that poor transport links are compounding the difficulties facing island communities, and is arguing that bridges and tunnels could transform the prospects of some places by making it easier to travel, work and do business.

The UK Parliament Scottish Affairs Committee, in a new report on fixed-link connectivity, says: “These connectivity challenges are exacerbating depopulation and demographic decline, which threatens the long-term sustainability of island life and culture.”

The forecasts come after years of population losses in some of Scotland’s island communities. Arran, Bute and the Cumbraes lost 13% of their residents between 2002 and 2022, while the Western Isles’ population fell by 5.5% between the 2011 and 2022 censuses, according to the an official island data review.

Shetland’s outer islands lost 8% of their population between 2002 and 2022, while Orkney’s outer isles recorded a 5.2% fall between 2011 and 2022. The losses contrast with growth elsewhere: Orkney’s overall population increased by 3% between 2011 and 2022, with growth concentrated on its mainland and connected islands.

The figures show how apparently stable island-wide totals can mask a more serious picture in individual communities. Scotland’s islands had just under 103,000 residents at the 2022 Census, compared with 103,700 in 2011 and 99,700 in 2001. But the Scottish Government warns that the loss of working-age residents is expected to be disproportionately high in island areas, particularly those that are already sparsely populated.

The concern is that communities losing young people and workers may find it increasingly difficult to support local businesses and services or attract new residents.

The Scottish Affairs Committee argues that fixed links could bring significant benefits to some communities, but stresses that they would be viable on only a limited number of crossings. It also warns that the prospect of bridges and tunnels must not distract from the urgent need to improve ferry services.

Committee chairman Patricia Ferguson MP, said: "Scotland's island communities deserve better than their unreliable ferry services, which place intolerable limitations on islanders going about their daily life - from accessing healthcare and education, to running businesses. Our report finds that, where viable, fixed links like bridges and tunnels could bring transformative benefits to these communities. "

MPs argue that permanent links could offer an alternative on some shorter crossings, reducing dependence on vessels and allowing people to travel without having to plan their lives around sailing timetables and cancellations.

The committee says the UK Government should be prepared to support viable fixed-link projects, working alongside the Scottish Government and island councils, which have responsibilities for transport and local infrastructure.

The Scottish Government has already committed to detailed appraisals of three potential links and says it will help councils explore financing options.

They point to evidence from the Islands Strategic Partnership suggesting that communities connected by bridges or causeways have sometimes experienced a reversal in their fortunes. Eriskay’s population fell by 60% in the 50 years before its causeway opened in 2001, but rose by almost 20% over the following two decades. On Shetland, Trondra’s population fell from 91 to 17 before a bridge was built in 1971; it has since risen to around 135.

The committee also heard that the populations of South Ronaldsay and Burray, connected by the Churchill Barriers, grew by about 10% over the past decade, while several unconnected Orkney islands experienced population decline.

The committee points to the Faroe Islands, where an extensive network of bridges and subsea tunnels has helped connect remote communities. Witnesses told MPs that the Faroese population had grown from about 34,000 in the 1960s to around 55,000 today, and argued that improved connectivity had played a role in retaining and attracting residents.

Shetland is already considering a major programme of subsea tunnels.

In the Western Isles, potential links include Harris to North Uist and Barra to South Uist. A study of a fixed link across the Sound of Harris put the estimated cost at between £109.6m and £145.1m.

But the committee says the case for permanent links must be assessed on its merits, with island communities themselves leading decisions about what they need. Such projects are unlikely to be practical for every island, and the report warns against treating them as a substitute for a functioning ferry network.

The experience of Skye also demonstrates that better access brings its own challenges. Since the Skye Bridge opened in 1995, visitor numbers have risen, but concerns have been raised about pressure on housing, roads and services, as well as the possibility that easier access encourages day trips rather than longer stays.

The committee argues that permanent links should be considered as part of a wider strategy for island sustainability, rather than as a single infrastructure solution. Better connections could make it easier for residents to commute, for employers to recruit and for businesses to move goods, but they cannot create jobs or homes by themselves.

A Scottish Government spokesman said: "The Scottish Government is committed to strengthening the long-term prosperity and resilience of all our islands and our Addressing Depopulation Action Plan – backed by £637,500 in 2026/27 – sets out how we will work with regional, local and community partners to ensure that we collectively deliver a sustainable solution to these challenges.

“We recognise the impact that ferry disruption and technical issues regrettably have on island communities and businesses. That is why we are delivering 13 new vessels to improve the long-term resilience and reliability of the CalMac fleet. We are also investing a further £3 million in 2026/27 through the Islands Resilience Fund to support businesses to cope with challenging circumstances.

“We are committed to continuing our close engagement with island councils. Through our new National Islands Plan, which has population retention and attraction as its overarching objective, we have committed to working with local authorities to explore financing models and promote investment opportunities in fixed links projects within their areas.

“The 2026-27 budget delivered in full, the asks of the relevant island councils by allocating a further £11.4 million of resource and £15.5 million capital for inter-island connectivity.”
