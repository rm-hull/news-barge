With a court hearing and press conference on Christa Pike now over, here is a look at where the case currently stands:

Christa Pike’s lawyers said she is speaking “limitedly” and is “angry and confused” following a botched execution attempt involving two doses of pentobarbital last Wednesday. She remains shackled to her hospital bed.

Pike was declared brain dead on Monday night and her team was informed that life support would be removed the next day, her lawyers said. “But on Tuesday, to the shock of everyone … Christa woke up and began talking,” they added.

The first thing Pike said was “Where am I and who are you?” according to her lawyers. They also said upon waking, Pike inquired about her team and the family of 19-year-old Colleen Slemmer, whom Pike killed alongside her then boyfriend Tadaryl Shipp in 1995.

Her lawyers said Pike has not eaten yet and is unable to move her arms, explaining that “both her arms and hands are tremendously swollen” and that she has pneumonia. “Christa has a long road to recovery,” they added.

Judge I’Ashea Myles said she is granting the motion to preserve all relevant evidence after telling the court: “This case is unique to say the least.” Myles listed of things Tennessee officials must preserve, including all drugs, IV lines and tubing that are in the state’s possession – “anything that was in the actual room” must be preserved and “that includes trash”.

Pike’s lawyers have asked that she be unshackled from her hospital bed. “Based on our understanding, medical staff have asked that the shackles be removed and have been denied that request by [the Tennessee corrections department],” they said in court, adding: “We would also amend our request and ask for that relief.”

Pike’s lawyers also told the court that the state of Tennessee had made no promises that it will not seek a future execution. “The state has made no promises that Ms Pike will not be subject to a future execution, and the evidence that we are seeking to preserve now is relevant for any future challenge that she makes, because as we pled in January, the same thing is likely to happen,” Luke Ihnen said.

Assistant attorney general John W Ayers pushed back against Pike’s lawyers’ claims that their access to Pike is being limited by the Tennessee corrections department (TDOC). He said: “TDOC does not intend to obstruct access to counsel. That’s why there’s been a process set up to request visitation to facilitate that.”

In response, Myles said that “counsel are expected to follow all visitation policies that are in place at the respective hospital.” She added: “This court will not run afoul of whatever the facility has already in place,” saying that “there is a two-hour response on whether or not visitation will be allowed”.

Explaining the steps ahead, Christa Pike’s lawyer said: “Until yesterday, Ms. Pike was unable to to speak. She was unconscious. She was not able to to authorize us to file any any sort of new lawsuits.”

“And so the motion that we filed is is just to preserve evidence. It’s it’s the very very first step of of what will be a long legal battle, we presume,” he added.

Christa Pike’s lawyers are now holding a press conference following a court hearing at the Davidson county chancery court in Nashville, Tennessee.

“Today’s hearing was about transparency, about why Christa, the courts, the media, and the public should not simply take the state’s word about what happened last Wednesday, because as we saw a little bit today, their word is shrouded in secrecy, in legal jargons, in attempts to obfuscate and obscure what really happened,” Pike’s lawyer Luke Ihnen said.

“If an incarcerated individual is allowed visitation once a day from counsel, the court does not expect TDOC to deviate from that simply because the petitioner is in a hospital facility versus a prison facility,” Myles added.

Judge grants motion to preserve evidence from execution day

The judge says she is granting the motion to preserve all relevant evidence after telling the court: “This case is unique to say the least.”

I’Ashea Myles goes through a list of things Tennessee officials must preserve. It includes all drugs, IV lines and tubing that are in the state’s possession – “anything that was in the actual room” must be preserved and “that includes trash”.

Myles adds that anything that relates to the drug or supplies used must be preserved, as well as photographs, videos, audio recording, surveillance footage and anything in the witness rooms.

She also wants all communications, whether encrypted or not, to be preserved, stressing that this includes “anything concerning deviation from protocol or delays or equipment issues”.

She also warns: “Take special care not to lose, misplace, accidentally delete any information pertinent to this litigation.”

Tennessee attorney rejects claims Pike's access to lawyers was being limited

Assistant attorney general John W Ayers also pushed back against Christa Pike’s lawyers’ claims that their access to Pike is being limited by the Tennessee corrections department (TDOC).

He said: “TDOC does not intend to obstruct access to counsel. That’s why there’s been a process set up to request visitation to facilitate that. Now, these requests that were just layered on today go above and beyond what the conditions were before.”

Ayers said that Pike “never had unfettered access to a phone”, explaining that phone calls were arranged in advance but that Pike did have “unfettered access to a tablet”.

“Again, access to counsel is being facilitated. TDOC is not taking the position that counsel be should be excluded,” he said.

Evidence collected on 30 September by prison staff following the botched execution attempt on Christa Pike include observation logs, medical waste and products, as well as EKG readouts, assistant attorney general John W Ayers said.

He added that some evidence may not be in prison officials’ possession, saying: “It’s my understanding that the IV lines were cut, and so there may be portions of those IV lines that could have been still attached to Ms Pike when she was transported to the hospital.”

He added: “[The Tennessee correction department] can’t be ordered to preserve evidence that’s in the custody of another party, and so there may be items that were originally in TDOC’s custody that got transported to the hospital on the night of September 30. Again, counsel can confer on that and establish where that evidence is, but it would be premature and prejudicial, quite frankly, to enter an order requiring preservation of that kind of evidence before counsel had a chance to confer on it and get a picture of where that evidence might be.”
