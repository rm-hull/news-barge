Oliver Cromwell's forces bombarded it, royalist defenders refused to yield and generations of custodians fought to preserve it. The history of Skipton Castle is a tale of conflict, resilience and restoration that still dominates the Craven landscape today.

For centuries, Skipton Castle has stood watch over the gateway to the Yorkshire Dales, surviving war, political upheaval and the relentless passage of time. Perched above the bustling market town, the fortress remains one of Britain's best-preserved medieval castles and a powerful reminder of Yorkshire's turbulent past.

Visitors approaching from Skipton's High Street cannot fail to notice the imposing stone walls that dominate the skyline. Yet behind the familiar silhouette lies a remarkable story of siege, survival and restoration, centred on one of the most dramatic episodes in English history.

Skipton Castle played a significant role during the English Civil War of the 1640s. Loyal to King Charles I, it became a Royalist stronghold at a time when Parliamentary forces were gaining control across much of the north of England.

The castle's most famous test came during a prolonged siege. Parliamentary troops, including forces aligned with Oliver Cromwell, attempted to force the defenders into submission. Cannon were positioned on nearby high ground, including Cock Hill, from where they bombarded the fortress.

The assault left its mark, but the attackers never achieved what many believed was inevitable. Despite sustained pressure and heavy shelling, Cromwell's forces failed to storm the castle. It became one of the last Royalist bastions in the north to resist Parliament's armies.

Eventually, events beyond Skipton's walls proved decisive. Following the crushing Royalist defeat at the Battle of Marston Moor in 1644, the defenders found themselves increasingly isolated. The garrison finally surrendered, but not before securing unusually favourable terms, a reflection of the respect earned by their determined resistance.

The end of the siege did not mean the end of the castle. Although parts of the building were damaged, including the Tudor wing, the fortress escaped the wholesale destruction suffered by many English castles during the Civil War.

A central figure in its survival was Lady Anne Clifford. One of the most remarkable women of the 17th century, she inherited extensive estates and embarked on an ambitious programme of restoration. Her work helped rescue Skipton Castle from decline and ensured that future generations could continue to experience a largely intact medieval stronghold.

Today, visitors can still explore rooms and passages that would have been familiar to nobles, servants and soldiers centuries ago. The castle offers an unusually complete picture of medieval domestic and military life, from great halls and private chambers to defensive features designed to repel attackers.

The site is also closely linked to the powerful Clifford family, who owned and developed the castle over many generations. Their influence can be seen throughout the building and forms an important part of the story of Craven and the wider Yorkshire Dales.

Among the castle's more unusual treasures is its famous ancient yew tree. Long associated with the site, it has become a symbol of continuity and endurance, echoing the castle's own extraordinary survival through centuries of change.

Conservation work continues to ensure the fortress remains in good condition. Maintaining a building of such age and historical significance is an ongoing challenge, but the efforts are vital in protecting one of Yorkshire's greatest heritage assets.

Few places capture the drama of England's past quite like Skipton Castle. It witnessed civil war, withstood bombardment and survived when many similar strongholds were reduced to ruins. Thanks to generations of custodians, particularly Lady Anne Clifford, it remains not simply a monument to history but a living link to it.

For residents and visitors alike, Skipton Castle stands as a reminder that even the most determined military campaign could not conquer everything. In the case of this Yorkshire landmark, Cromwell may have battered the walls, but the castle endured.
