Ferris turned to writing about his life in Glasgow's underworld

**A Glasgow gangland enforcer who wrote books about his underworld experiences has died at the age of 62.**

Paul Ferris is believed to have died on Tuesday night at the city's Queen Elizabeth University Hospital after a short illness.

He was cleared of the murder of crime boss Arthur Thompson's son, Arthur Thompson Jnr, in 1992, before being jailed for seven years in 1998 for gun-running.

His first book, The Ferris Conspiracy, was published in 2001.

It was later made into a 2013 film, The Wee Man, featuring Line of Duty star Martin Compston as Ferris.

![Paul Ferris leaves Frankland Prison in Co Durham in 2002 after completing a seven-year sentence for for gun-running](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/5f40/live/a61821b0-c269-11f1-b278-615cdfb74f16.jpg)

Ferris left prison in 2002 after completing a seven-year sentence

Photographer Brian Anderson, who first met Ferris for a newspaper assignment in 2002, broke the news of his death on his Naked City Facebook page.

Describing Ferris as a best-selling author and a "former notorious criminal", he wrote: "News of his death will come as a shock to many who knew him throughout the different chapters of his life."

Anderson later told BBC Scotland News: "We got on really well. I found him polite, funny and kind.

"It was a shock to everyone that I remained friends with Paul for 25 years."

Thompson, who had links to the Krays in London, was an old school Glasgow criminal known as The Godfather.

By the early 80s, Ferris had become part of his gang as an enforcer, someone who threatened and, when necessary, meted out violence. He rapidly built a fearsome reputation that extended beyond the city.

But their relationship took a remarkable twist in August 1991 when Arthur "Fat Boy" Thompson Jnr, was shot dead outside the family's Provanmill home.

It was named The Ponderosa after the ranch in the hit US Western television series, Bonanza.

Ferris was arrested as the prime suspect in Thompson Jnr's murder but two others were also incriminated.

His friends Joe "Bananas" Hanlon and Bobby Glover did not live to stand trial.

On the day of Thompson Jnr's funeral both men were found shot dead in a car on the cortege route.

The murders remain unsolved.

![Paul Ferris promotes The Wee Man at Blythswood Square Hotel in Glasgow](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/275a/live/d66b58f0-c269-11f1-b278-615cdfb74f16.jpg)

The Wee Man, a film based on his life, was released in 2013

The following year Ferris's trial became, at the time, the longest and most expensive criminal case in Scottish legal history.

More than 300 witnesses gave evidence during the 54-day proceedings, which were estimated to have cost £4m.

The prosecution case relied heavily on the evidence of Dennis Woodman, who claimed Ferris had confessed to the killing while both men were being held in Barlinnie Prison.

Defence lawyers challenged the credibility of the evidence and argued the case against Ferris was deeply flawed.

After more than 24 hours of deliberations, the jury acquitted Ferris of murder and all other charges in 1992.

No-one has ever been convicted of Thompson Jnr's killing.

![Donald Findlay KC in 2019. He is wearing a grey suit, striped shirt and a burgundy tie with a paisley pattern.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/67d8/live/79c0c170-c26f-11f1-b278-615cdfb74f16.jpg)

Donald Findlay KC represented Ferris during the murder trial

In 2020 Donald Findlay KC - who defended Ferris in court - told Hey Legal, external that the case had been the most memorable of his career.

He said: "The trial of Paul Ferris for the murder of Arthur Thompson Jr had virtually all the great characters of Glasgow in that trial one way or another.

"Young Arthur was dead, his father was a witness, Ferris was regarded by the police as the heir apparent to Arthur Thompson Sr.

"We had supergrasses, it just had everything that was going for it.

"I forget how many charges that there were but of course Ferris walked on each and every one of them."

Following his release from prison in 2002 after being jailed on gun-running charges, Ferris turned to writing.

His debut book was made into a movie which starred Martin Compston - who had found fame as a teenager in Ken Loach's Sweet Sixteen - as Ferris.

John Hannah played his arch rival, Tam "The Licensee" McGraw.

The crime boss, who died in 2007 aged 55, had been linked to crimes ranging from drug smuggling to murder for more than three decades.

The movie was shot in London due to problems securing permission from Glasgow City Council and the police to film in Ferris's home city.

![Graeme Pearson](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/9b18/live/69917340-c278-11f1-b278-615cdfb74f16.jpg)

Former Scottish Crime and Drug Enforcement Agency director general Graeme Pearson first met Ferris when the convicted gun-runner was a teenager

Ferris went on to publish two further books, Vendetta and Villains, with crime writer Reg McKay, who died in 2012.

Graeme Pearson, former director general of the Scottish Crime and Drug Enforcement Agency, first encountered the convicted gun-runner when he was a teenager.

Pearson told BBC Scotland News that Ferris went on to develop a fearsome reputation.

He said: "Certainly, in his active days, he wouldn't be a man that you would choose to go to dinner with."

The ex-Labour MSP added: "At one stage in his life he was a very dangerous character.

"He was someone who seemed to take pleasure in his reputation for violence.

"Having said that, I think that in later life he certainly seemed to outlive that reputation and would appear to have put that whole gangster profile behind him."
