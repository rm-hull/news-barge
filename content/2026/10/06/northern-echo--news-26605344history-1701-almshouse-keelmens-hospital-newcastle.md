Today’s Object of the Week is a remarkable riverside building created more than three centuries ago by working men determined to look after their own.

For more than 300 years, an imposing brick building overlooking the Tyne has reflected the changing fortunes of Newcastle and the river trade on which the city’s prosperity was built.

Keelmen’s Hospital, on City Road, was completed in 1701 as an almshouse for retired, sick or injured keelmen and their families.

It was not a hospital in the modern sense, although its residents received food, shelter and medical care.

Keelmen worked aboard flat-bottomed boats known as keels, carrying coal from the banks of the shallow Tyne to larger ships which could not travel far enough upriver.

The keelmen’s work formed part of a long-established coal-export trade: Newcastle was shipping 15,000 tons of coal a year by 1378, with keelmen recorded among the city’s trades by 1452.

Their labour powered the region’s coal trade, but the seasonal, punishing work offered little security when men grew old or fell ill.

In 1699, the keelmen established a charitable society to provide for themselves.

About 1,600 men initially agreed to pay one penny from each crew member’s wages for every tide worked.

They petitioned Newcastle’s common council for land in July 1700 and the hospital was completed the following year at a reported cost of £2,000.

The project was remarkable because it had been financed by the workers themselves rather than a wealthy benefactor.

Its design was equally distinctive.

The brick building was arranged around a central courtyard and included a prominent central frontage, a square clock tower, an octagonal lantern and shaped Dutch gables.

![Keelmen’s Hospital, City Road, Newcastle upon Tyne, historic Grade II-listed almshouse built in 1701 for retired and sick keelmen and their families](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21535300.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Keelmen’s Hospital in Newcastle, the historic almshouse built in 1701 for sick and retired keelmen and their families *(Image: Architectural Heritage Fund)*

It contained more than 50 rooms, four staircases and a club room.

The identity of its architect is not known.

Through the 18th and into the 19th century, it housed sick or retired keelmen, widows and their families.

As the Tyne’s traditional keel trade declined, the building also admitted sailors, mariners and watermen.

When the keelmen’s lease expired in 1899, the building became tenement housing.

It later became council housing but remained residential until its conditions were deemed inadequate in the 20th century.

Plans to turn it into sheltered housing in the 1970s fell through.

It was later converted into student accommodation for Newcastle Polytechnic and then Northumbria University before closing in 2009.

It has stood empty since then and is listed on Historic England’s Heritage at Risk Register.

The building has now been selected by the Architectural Heritage Fund as one of 25 standout projects marking the charity’s 50th anniversary.

In 2024, the fund awarded £36,539 towards its restoration.

Tyne and Wear Building Preservation Trust and Newcastle City Council are developing plans to turn it into 20 affordable homes.

National Lottery Heritage Fund support is also helping restore the building’s original purpose: providing homes for people who need them.
