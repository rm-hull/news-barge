I can’t remember the last time I felt as conflicted about a piece of gaming tech as I do about the GamerCard. It’s a brilliant idea, brimming with potential. But it’s *immensely* frustrating.

GamerCard should be an easy sell. A small, versatile portable gaming device that’s intended to be both an accessible handheld for impulse shoppers—designed to sit alongside gift cards at cash registers, integrated hook and all—and is advanced enough for power users to experiment with. Crucially, it’s also affordable at £99 (approximately $130), which feels like a minor miracle in 2026, when a stick of RAM alone is practically a mortgageable expense.

Yet at nearly every turn, that pick-up-and-play promise clashes with the reality of using it.

## Slender Silicon

The device is an adapted Raspberry Pi Zero 2W, powered by a quad-core ARM Cortex-A53 processor. The 4-inch IPS screen (720p, 1:1 aspect ratio display), control inputs, and speakers all live directly on the motherboard, along with a 1,600-mAh battery—expect approximately four hours of use between charges.

That’s all crammed into a 128 x 88 x 6.5-millimeter shell weighing a meager 100 grams. It’s almost impossibly slim yet still packs a comprehensive array of ports: one USB-C, two Micro-USB, a mini HDMI-out, and even a Qwiic connector along the right-hand edge, below two tiny Start and Select buttons, while the top houses a microSD card slot.
