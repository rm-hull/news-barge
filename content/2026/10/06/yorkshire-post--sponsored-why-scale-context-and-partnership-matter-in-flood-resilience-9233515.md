# Why scale, context and partnership matter in flood resilience

![Flood resilience depends on understanding how water moves through entire catchments, says Mark Henderson.](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/sponsored/0&crop=&width=990&quality=70&enable=upscale.jpg)


## Flooding is often discussed in terms of individual places. A flooded road. A flooded home. A flooded community, writes Mark Henderson, Flood Risk Manager, City of York Council.

Yet water does not recognise administrative boundaries, ownership plans or organisational structures. Rivers connect landscapes and communities, influencing decisions often made many miles apart.

As flood risk increases alongside a changing climate, one of the biggest challenges facing us is understanding how those connections influence the choices we make.

Over recent years, work across the Swale, Ure, Nidd and Upper Ouse catchments has helped deepen our understanding of how landscapes, rivers and communities interact. While every catchment is different, three themes continue to emerge: scale matters, context matters and partnership matters.

![Mark Henderson, Flood Risk Manager at City of York Council.](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/sponsored/0&width=640&quality=65.jpg)


The first lesson is that **scale matters**.

Flood risk in York is influenced by what happens across a vast upstream catchment.

Evidence from catchment-scale modelling suggests that influencing flood risk and water quality downstream requires action at significant scale and in locations identified through evidence and local knowledge.

Individual projects and local interventions remain important, but the greatest opportunities often come from understanding how multiple measures work together across an entire river system.

![Understanding how water moves across whole catchments can help inform approaches to flood resilience.](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/sponsored/0&width=640&quality=65.jpg)


This is one of the reasons why nature-based solutions have attracted increasing attention. Measures such as woodland creation, floodplain reconnection and improved land management can contribute to wider resilience objectives.

However, the evidence also reminds us that meaningful change requires long-term thinking, coordination and realistic expectations about what can be achieved and over what timescales.

Recent months have highlighted another side of the water management challenge. While flooding and drought can seem very different, both are shaped by how water moves through catchments. Understanding those connections is helping drive a broader conversation about flood resilience, water quality and river health.

The second lesson is that **context matters**.

There is often a temptation to look for solutions that can be replicated everywhere. In reality, no two places are exactly the same.

Catchments are shaped by their geology, human modifications, geography, land use, river networks and communities. What works well in one location may be less appropriate elsewhere. Local evidence and local knowledge therefore remain essential.

![From hills and farmland to rivers and communities, Yorkshire’s landscape is central to understanding future flood resilience.](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/sponsored/0&crop=&width=640&quality=65.jpg)


Understanding flood resilience is not simply about identifying which interventions work. It is about understanding where, when and under what circumstances they are most effective. Good decisions depend on recognising the differences between places as much as the similarities.

The third lesson is that **partnership matters**.

No single organisation can deliver catchment resilience on its own.

Flood risk is influenced by decisions made across farming, planning, infrastructure, environmental management and local government. Building resilience therefore depends on collaboration between organisations, land managers, communities and decision-makers.

![Yorkshire’s river catchments connect upland landscapes, farmland and waterways – ultimately influencing what happens downstream.](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/sponsored/0&width=640&quality=65.jpg)


Evidence is important, but evidence alone does not create change. Turning learning into action requires trust, coordination and a shared understanding of the challenges we face.

These lessons are not unique to York and North Yorkshire. Similar conversations are taking place across the country as organisations consider how best to respond to increasing flood risk and wider environmental pressures.

There are no simple answers, and no single solution. If we are serious about building long-term resilience, we must look beyond individual projects and consider the wider systems that connect our landscapes, rivers and communities.

Because while flooding may be experienced locally, resilience is built collectively.
