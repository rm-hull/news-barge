He’s been behind bars for almost three decades - but double jeopardy killer Billy Dunlop could soon be a free man.

William Dunlop brutally killed 22-year-old Julie in Billingham in 1989 before stuffing her body behind a bath panel, even saying he felt “euphoric” after the murder.

The now 63-year-old was tried twice for her murder but acquitted - and it was only after Julie’s mum Ann Ming overturned the double jeopardy law that he finally faced justice.

Dunlop was jailed for life with a minimum term of 15 years in 2006 but last week made his bid for freedom at a public parole hearing in London.

It was there that he told a parole board how he “cringes” at "how selfish and self centred” he was”, saying he has “done a lot of reflecting” following the loss of his son last year.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/3902014.jpg?type=mds-article-620) *(Image: CLEVELAND POLICE)*

He feels he is now ready to be released into the public - despite Julie’s son Kevin Hogg saying he would "constantly be on edge, wondering what he is doing" if Dunlop was freed.

But what would his release look like?

*As Dunlop awaits a decision, and Ann Ming and her family fear the “cold and calculated” killer will be recommended for release, here is what Dunlop would be subjected to should he finally be allowed to leave prison.*

Dunlop wouldn’t be an entirely free man if he were to walk outside of the prison walls.

He would have to spend 12 months in a community forensic specialist hostel and be subject to "special monitoring and supervision", which Dunlop said he understood at the hearing.

This would include a curfew from 8pm until 7am to “restrict his movements at night time” as well as two daily sign ins, at 11am and 3pm.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/3899449.jpg?type=mds-article-620) *(Image: (c) NORTH NEWS & PICTURES)*

He would also have a tag, which would monitor his location for 18 months and have exclusion zones, banning him from entering areas including Newcastle, Darlington, and Teesside.

He would also be subjected to room searches, would have to notify his community offender manager (COM) of any vehicles he has regular use of, and make any electronic devices available for inspection when required.

He would be polygraph tested within three months of his release, and if he was to pass, the subsequent test would be within six months.

However, If he failed the test, this would be within three months. If the result was inconclusive, it would be within one month.

He would also have to make his COM aware of any relationships.

His prison psychologist argued the plan was “robust” and recommended him for release, alongside his prison offender manager (POM), prison psychologist, and an independent psychologist.

## **‘I don’t want another family to go through what we have’**

Ann spent 17 years fighting to overturn the law before she could see Dunlop jailed - and does not believe that time will have changed who he is.

The family have now gone through four parole hearings, but the most recent was the first time Dunlop was trying for a full release.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21617973.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Ann Ming in London following killer Billy Dunlop's parole hearing *(Image: THE NORTHERN ECHO)*

The killer has twice previously been recommended to be moved to an open prison before the move was blocked by two Justice Secretaries.

Ann fears he may be recommended for release this time around, but argued Dunlop "should be in prison for life, and that goes for any murderer".

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21618003.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Julie Hogg *(Image: FAMILY PIC)*

“You have got to look at public protection," she previously said.

"After he was wrongfully acquitted, he didn’t just lie low in the community did he. He tried to kill another man and woman, he attacked another woman.

“That is what his history is and time won’t change the person to me.

“Where are they coming from to think he is alright to go in the community? Would they want him to live next door to them? I don’t think so.”

Ann and her family argued that Dunlop was "hoodwinking" the parole board by arguing he was ready for release after "keeping his head down" for 15 years.

Kevin noted how he already lied about the murder, having been tried twice and acquitted by juries.

He then notoriously went on to laugh at the law when he boasted to a prison officer that he had killed Julie while in jail for another offence.

He only formally confessed after the double jeopardy law was overturned following Ann’s tireless campaign, backed by the Echo.

Dunlop, who appeared on a video link from an undisclosed prison wearing a light blue shirt during the hearing, said he was “not interested in hurting anyone" and ready to be freed.

He said he had done a “lot of reflecting” following the sudden death of his son last year and wanted Julie’s family to "just be able to get on with their life".
