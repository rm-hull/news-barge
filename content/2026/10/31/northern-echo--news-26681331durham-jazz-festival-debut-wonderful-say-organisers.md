**Durham Jazz Festival launched its first event last weekend to brilliant reviews from the Durham community. Julia Davies spoke to Alan Patrickson, one of the three organisers, about the success of the festival and the feedback he received.**

With 25 performances taking place across the weekend, there had already been encouraging signs that Durham Jazz Festival’s debut would strike a chord.

Ticket sales were strong in the weeks leading up to the festival, with Newcastle-based jazz band Knats selling out their show more than a month in advance. When Friday night arrived, they reportedly “blew the roof” off Fabio’s bar.

![Newcastle-based jazz band Knats perform during the inaugural Durham Jazz Festival](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21646496.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Knats were among the performers appearing during the inaugural Durham Jazz Festival *(Image: JOHN LYONS)*

But even the organisers were taken aback by the response. Mr Patrickson said: “By performance day, we realised that the support had exceeded our expectations.”

He believed that “almost every show was at capacity” and that at certain points “we were turning people away”.

This was by far the most important measure of success, as their priority was to provide an “amazing experience for the audience”.

He had people coming up to him after the shows, saying the performances were “absolutely wonderful” and “we wish we’d bought more tickets”.

Durham certainly came alive over the weekend, with the streets transforming into displays of a buzzing community.

Jazz can often be an overlooked genre of music, so it might be news to some that people ranging from nine to 93 years old came out to see it.

![Newcastle-based jazz band Knats perform during the inaugural Durham Jazz Festival](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21646495.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Newcastle-based jazz band Knats perform at Durham Jazz Festival *(Image: JOHN LYONS)*

When I asked Alan what prompted the idea, he said “why Jazz? We love it”.

Their passion and real understanding of jazz music not only bled into the project, but carefully shaped the motivation.

He said “everyone’s got their own idea of what jazz is” and that “it was intentional to bring music to Durham that people might not have heard before”.

At a time when concerts and live music gigs are as high in demand as they are expensive, Mr Patrickson emphasised the importance of keeping “ticket prices as low as possible”.

Most of the performances were £10 or under.

Given their success in catering to novices as well as aficionados, discovery was an important theme of the weekend in many ways.

Particularly for me, as I was struck by how special jazz is as a live performance.

It is not only scat singing and improvised melodies which can’t be replicated – neither is the narrative of the performance ever the same twice.

Arriving in Durham on Sunday afternoon, I attended one of the final events of the weekend: the Marvin Muoneké Quartet.

Despite being held in St Chads College, few students had bought tickets for the show.

But, no doubt hearing soothing music oozing through the building, they slowly started drawing into the Quad like bees to nectar.

By the end, they were listening from the balconies, heads resting on the windowsills, just as enchanted as everyone else.

I felt this was proof of what Mr Patrickson emphasised as the value of simply “being in the room with incredible musicians”.

The performance promised storytelling, which was beautifully delivered and amplified by the intimacy of the smaller venue.

He delved into the backstory of the songs, detoured into the history of jazz, telling anecdotes about the most famous jazz musicians of the 20th century, and meandered into the importance of dancing, emphasising the joy of jazz music not just through words but through the energy of his performance.

Marvin’s expertise did not just end there, as he took on many creative roles over the course of the performance.

Throughout the performance, he morphed into a storyteller, singer, songwriter, arranger, bandleader, and expert trumpeter.

His stage presence was charismatic and incredibly engaging, which was evidenced as the crowds’ enamoured trance burst into riveting applause after every song.

![People listen from the balconies during a performance by Marvin Muoneké and his quartet at Durham Jazz Festival](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21646492.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Music draws listeners to the balconies during Marvin Muoneké’s performance at Durham Jazz Festival *(Image: MALCOLM SINCLAIR)*

Demand for next year already seems to be high.

Mr Patrickson revealed that “so many people have said to us you have to do it again in 2027”.

He suggests that it owes a lot to the community who made it possible - the volunteers who “got behind us and got so much out of helping” and the invaluable “support from sponsors and stakeholders”.

Though it is too early to be confirmed, the consensus is that the festival will be a welcome return, if not a standout, of Durham’s calendar next year.

Coming from a novice myself, it felt like an incredibly authentic exhibition of what jazz music is.

As Mr Patrickson summarised, “the challenge is to provide that good of an experience again”.
