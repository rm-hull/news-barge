Fifty-one years ago this week, readers opening the Bradford Telegraph & Argus found reports of a shocking murder in Leeds.

The newspaper's front page on October 30, 1975, carried the headline: "Mother of four murdered: police hunt 'sadistic killer'."

Wilma McCann had been found dead on playing fields off Scott Hall Road, and detectives were hunting what the headline described as a "sadistic killer".

Nobody knew her murder would mark the beginning of one of Britain's most notorious murder investigations.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/17266507.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Wilma McCann *(Image: Newsquest)*

There was no nationwide manhunt and no sense that Yorkshire was about to enter a period of fear and uncertainty that would last for years.

The T&A archives offer a fascinating snapshot of how the story was first reported, before anyone understood the scale of what was to come.

## First of thirteen murders

The murder of Wilma McCann was the first killing carried out by Peter Sutcliffe.

Sutcliffe was born in Shipley and grew up in Bingley.

Between 1975 and 1980, Sutcliffe murdered 13 women and attempted to murder seven others across Yorkshire and the North of England before his arrest in January 1981.

![Peter Sutcliffe](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/16571723.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Peter Sutcliffe *(Image: File)*

Three of Sutcliffe's victims, Patricia Atkinson, Yvonne Pearson and Barbara Leach, were killed in Bradford.

He was convicted of 13 counts of murder and seven counts of attempted murder later that year.

The investigation became one of the largest and most expensive murder inquiries in British history, with police taking more than 30,000 statements and interviewing Sutcliffe several times before his eventual arrest.

## How it was reported at the time

The T&A's archive pages capture the investigation at its very beginning, when detectives were trying to solve what appeared to be a single murder.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21652771.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Reporting of the murder from the T&A archive. Thursday, October 30, 1975. *(Image: T&A Archive)*

On October 30, 1975, the paper reported how Mrs McCann, a mother of four from Scott Hall Avenue, Leeds, had been found dead on playing fields near Scott Hall Road.

Detectives described the killer as "vicious and sadistic" and launched an urgent appeal for information.

In those first reports, the focus was entirely on the immediate tragedy and the search for a single murderer.

![Reporting of the murder from the T&A archive](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21652772.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Reporting of the murder from the T&A archive. Thursday, October 30, 1975. *(Image: T&A Archive)*

Journalists pieced together Mrs McCann's last known movements, spoke to neighbours and reported on the growing police investigation.

One article detailed how her children raised the alarm after becoming concerned when their mother failed to return home.

## Second day of coverage

The following day, October 31, attention turned to potential leads.

A prominent headline declared: "Hitch-hike clue in hunt for killer."

![Reporting of the murder from the T&A archive](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21652773.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Reporting of the murder from the T&A archive. Friday, October 31, 1975. *(Image: T&A Archive)*

The report detailed how detectives were investigating sightings of Mrs McCann attempting to flag down vehicles in the hours before her death.

Some 150 detectives were reported to have searched the area, along with nine dog handlers.

The report spoke of "vital new clues" in the search for the murderer, but the breakthrough detectives were seeking remained elusive.

More than five years would pass before Peter Sutcliffe was finally arrested.

With no indication of the wider pattern that would later emerge, the archive pages preserve a moment when the investigation's most infamous chapter had yet to be written.
