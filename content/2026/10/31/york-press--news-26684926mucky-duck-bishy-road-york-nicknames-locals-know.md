## From The Mucky Duck to Bishy Road: The York nicknames only locals understand

The Old White Swan in Goodramgate aka The Mucky Duck

EVERY city has its own language, but York residents have long had a special talent for shortening, twisting and reinventing place names.

A question posted in our popular Facebook group Why We Love York Memories has uncovered dozens of affectionate, humorous and sometimes baffling nicknames for streets, schools, parks and pubs across the city.

The discussion began when a member asked: "What nicknames have you (over)heard for places in and around York?"

The responses soon flooded in, revealing a local vocabulary that would leave many newcomers scratching their heads.

Among the best-known is undoubtedly Bishopthorpe Road's "Bishy Road", a nickname now used so widely that many people barely think of it as a nickname at all.

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21651471.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Bustling Bishopthorpe Road in York – known as Bishy Road to locals

Elsewhere, York's habit of shortening place names was on full display.

"Leemo" was suggested for Leeman Road, while Wigginton became simply "Wiggy", or "Wiggy Road" to some residents.

David Ellis recalled Malton Road being known as "Molly Road", while one contributor revealed they grew up on the streets of "LA", their abbreviation for Lindsey Avenue.

Several contributors remembered colourful names for schools.

Philip Hutchinson recalled "Cano" for Canon Lee School, while Archbishop Holgate's School was affectionately known as "Archie's".

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21651473.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Archbishop Holgate's School in York affectionately known as Archies

Others remembered "JoRo" for Joseph Rowntree School, "Parky" for Park Grove School and "Woody" for Woodthorpe Primary School.

Stewart McCartney recalled attending "Weggy" and "Nunny", nicknames for Westfield and Nunthorpe schools.

The former pupil also remembered "Weggy Park" as a nickname for West Bank Park.

Many of the names reflected local geography and long-forgotten landmarks.

Alan Raimes listed a host of old favourites including "Clifton Backies", "Poppy Road", "Taddy Road" and "The Shippo" for the Shipton Street end of Bootham Crescent.

He wrote: "In fact any name that could be shortened and sounded good ending in y."

Philip Martin Pallister remembered "Backkies" for the former back fields on Clifton Aerodrome and "Chappie" for the Chapelfields area.

Some nicknames were less flattering.

Dick Sefton noted that Walmgate is sometimes referred to as "Stabgate", while Tang Hall inspired several variations, including "Tanghalla".

Meanwhile, York's pubs have generated some of the city's most enduring alternative names.

Julie Speedie pointed out that the Three-Legged Mare in High Petergate is better known to many locals as the "Wonky Donkey", while the Old White Swan in Goodramgate has long carried the nickname "The Mucky Duck".

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21651472.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The Three-Legged Mare also known as the 'Wonky Donky'

"I still want to know why the White Swan pub is referred to as The Mucky Duck!" admitted Sedge Seymour.

Carl Manning remembered another pub nickname from years gone by, recalling that the Imperial in Kingsway North was known locally as "Stalingrad".

Some of the more mysterious names prompted almost as many questions as answers.

Kevin Holland said the pedestrian tunnel beneath the railway bridge on Leeman Road was known as "Marble Arch", while Christine Keller remembered her father referring to Leeman Road as "Winnipeg", although she admitted she had "no idea why".

![](https://www.yorkpress.co.uk/resources/images/21651474.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The 'Marble Arch' tunnel in Leeman Road in York

Dave Green shared some colourful descriptions for Acomb: "My grandmother always used to call Acomb Green Yackcom sand hole; Acomb hotel (as was) was Acomb treacle mines!"

Perhaps that is the beauty of local nicknames.

Some are obvious shortcuts, some are terms of endearment and others seem to have emerged from nowhere before becoming part of everyday speech.

Whether you grew up on Bishy Road, met friends at the Mucky Duck or travelled down Wiggy Road, these names are a reminder that York's history is not just written in its streets and buildings but also in the words residents use to describe them.

And as the lively online discussion proved, many of those nicknames are still very much alive today.
