In the heart of a historic County Durham market town stands a tree which dates back around 320 million years.

Stanhope, located in the Weardale countryside, is home to one of the North East's most unusual natural landmarks.

Standing in the grounds of St Thomas' Church, just off the town's marketplace, is a fossilised tree which offers a glimpse into the region's prehistoric past.

The ancient remains are believed to date from the Carboniferous Period, when the landscape that would eventually become northern England looked dramatically different.

Stanhope's fossil tree is among the town's most extraordinary landmarks.

Unlike the trees growing around it today, the specimen dates back to a time when the area was covered by tropical forests.

Around 320 million years ago, the land which would eventually become the North Pennines lay close to the equator.

When the ancient tree died, its remains were buried beneath sediment.

Over time, sand filled the space left by the decaying wood and eventually hardened into sandstone, creating a natural cast of the original trunk.

It was eventually brought to the town in the early 1960s, where its sections were reconstructed in the churchyard of St Thomas'.

The fossil tree stands beside St Thomas' Church, another of Stanhope's most important landmarks.

The church dates back to around 1200 and occupies a prominent position overlooking the marketplace.

Its history is closely connected to the development of Weardale and the wider County Durham countryside.

Inside are features including a Roman altar, a Saxon font and historic stained glass.

Stanhope is the largest town in Upper Weardale and has long served as a centre for the surrounding communities.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21646502.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Durham Dales Centre *(Image: SARAH CALDECOTT)*

Its historic marketplace is surrounded by independent shops, cafés and traditional buildings.

The Market Cross provides another reminder of the town's trading past.

Nearby, the Durham Dales Centre offers craft and retail businesses alongside facilities for visitors.

Stanhope Castle is another historic landmark, with parts of its walls contributing to the character of the town centre.

Despite its long history, Stanhope remains a working community rather than simply a tourist destination.

Beyond the marketplace, Stanhope is surrounded by some of County Durham's most dramatic scenery.

The town lies within the North Pennines National Landscape, an area recognised for its geological importance, wildlife and historic landscapes.
