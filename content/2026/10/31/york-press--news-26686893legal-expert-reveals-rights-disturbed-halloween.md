## Legal expert reveals your rights if trick-or-treaters disturb you on Halloween

A legal expert has shared some tips on how not to be disturbed on Halloween. *(Image: Getty)*

Halloween is an exciting time for many families who will be dressing up and trick-or-treating this weekend.

Many houses will be decorated with pumpkins and will be handing out chocolates and sweets to children.

However, some people are not a fan of this holiday and will be keeping their doors shut during the evening.

A property law expert has revealed what homeowners can do if they want to avoid being disturbed on Halloween.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21651552.jpg?type=mds-article-620)If you do not decorate your house, it indicates to people that you do not want to be disturbed. *(Image: Getty)*

## Your rights if you do not want to be disturbed on Halloween

Janet Lane, a property law expert from Rogers and Norton, has shared a few steps that homeowners can take to indicate to trick-or-treaters that they do not want to be disturbed.

She said: “There is generally an implied permission for somebody to walk up the usual route to your front door and knock, which is why a delivery driver, neighbour or other caller does not normally need express permission first.

“But homeowners can make it clear that they do not want Halloween callers.

“A locked gate, or a simple and prominent ‘no trick-or-treaters’ notice on the door, gate or window is one of the clearest ways of communicating that visitors are not invited to approach.”

Legal action or police involvement is only necessary if the situation escalates into criminal damage, harassment, or anti-social behaviour.

Throwing things at a property like eggs, flour, rocks, and silly string or damaging walls, windows, and vehicles falls under criminal damage, according to the Criminal Damage Act 1971.

The Public Order Act 1986 states that using abusive, threatening or insulting words or behaviour that causes alarm or distress is also an offence.

Lane added: “There is an important difference between a child innocently knocking on the wrong door and behaviour which becomes intimidating or destructive.”

“A single knock is one thing.

“Repeatedly banging on somebody’s door after being asked to leave, damaging their property or throwing objects at their home is something very different and should never be dismissed as simply part of Halloween.”

Janet says that using a clear sign that says ‘no trick-or-treaters please’ and not putting out pumpkins or decorations will signal to people that you do not want to be disturbed.

You should also keep gates closed and make sure any signs are visible before people reach your front door.
