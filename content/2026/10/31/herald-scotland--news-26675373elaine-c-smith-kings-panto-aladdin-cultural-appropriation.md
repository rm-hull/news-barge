Where is Elaine C Smith’s head these days? Literally, it’s perfectly placed under a wee tartan hat with a seagull on top, all part of an ensemble that wouldn’t look out of place at an SNP fundraiser in Largs, or a Fran and Anna remembrance evening.

The outfit isn’t Elaine C’s personal choice, of course (one would hope), worn today because we’re here to talk panto. This year it’s all things Aladdin, and we will get to that. But first the actor reveals that the mind of Scotland’s comedy has been well and truly a little shoogled.

To offer context, at exactly this time last year, the actor hinted she was ready to abdicate. Smith felt she had done it all, she said. All the career boxes had been ticked; TV series, panto success, stage success. In fact, 2026 would be quiet, a preamble perhaps to retirement.

But then a couple of astonishing events came about. A small-scale art house play Smith had been helping to develop, Cathy, by Eilidh Loan, turned out to be a gangbusters success, a phenomenal Fringe hit this year, with standing ovations every night and more than 1000 people turned away at the box office.

Smith starred as the eponymous character, a woman wrecked by grief since the loss of her husband. “It was a play in which the (audience) greetin’ was as loud as the laughter,” she says with an immensely proud smile of the true story of Loan’s grandmother’s battle with survival.

![Glasgow panto favourites are back at city’s Kings Theatre (Glasgow): Elaine C Smith and Johnny Mac lead the cast of a brand-new version of “Aladdin”, alongside Darren Brownlie, Hannah Jarrett-Scott, Blythe Jandoo and Gordon Cooper](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21638881.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Elaine C Smith and Johnny Mac lead the cast of a brand-new version of Aladdin, alongside Darren Brownlie, Hannah Jarrett-Scott, Blythe Jandoo and Gordon Cooper *(Image: Colin Hattersley)*

But the response the play – and Smith – received was just one delight. “It was especially great for me to go to the Traverse,” she enthuses. “Previously, I’d sat with (producer) David Pugh in the theatre (watching another show), and we were talking of possibilities for staging Cathy when I said ‘We should bring it here. This is where I want to be!’ He asked why and I said, ‘I want to be right in the f\*\*\*\*\*’ middle of it.’”

The middle of what? A speech to the audience Smith made on the last night of Cathy suggests a clue. She said, ‘I just want to thank the Traverse for taking the chance on putting working class drama on the stage, and allowing the feelings and emotions, the grief and loss of working-class people to be as valid as those of the upper classes.’ Smith has long fought for working class representation in Scottish theatres. And here was she playing a huge part in bringing a working-class play (Loan grew up in a housing estate in Erskine) into a theatre world perceived to be soaked in middle class sensibility.

“It was amazing to go back to the Traverse because it’s been 26 years (since she appeared in Kate Atkinson’s Abandonment),” says Smith, with a smile as wide as an arts community cultural gap.

The claim that the production marked Elaine C Smith’s return to the Traverse Theatre main stage after 26 years is independently supported by the theatre. [traverse.co.uk], [traverse.co.uk] During this time, the actor from Newarthill in Lanarkshire has had to work hard to convince the theatre and television industry that she’s capable of delivering so much more than a gag. She’s fought to prove that actors known for comedy can very often scale dramatic heights.

“We’ve long had that thing whereby if you are in comedy, you are deemed not to be able to do straight acting; you can’t do both. But the opposite is true. And believe me, many straight actors can’t do comedy. They couldn’t time an egg.”

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/17021537.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Elaine C Smith in Two Doors Down *(Image: BBC)*

This assessment calls to mind the second unexpected career shocker of the year, the transference of the BBC TV series Two Doors Down into an OVO Hydro 10 nights sensation. And her portrayal of Christine, a creature born medically incapable of offering a smile that isn’t both deliciously dry and sarcastic, is a case in point of the intricacies of comic delivery.

“The whole thing about her is about the delivery. And playing her is about subtlety. It’s about building the drama.”

And it’s about according an audience with a level of intelligence?

“Exactly!” she agrees. “Here’s an example from the stage play. Beth asks Christine; ‘How’s the foot?’ ‘Oh, you know me, Beth. I don’t like to complain. But I’d gladly hack it off with my breadknife if it widnae make such a mess of my good rug.’ ‘Is it still swollen up?’ ‘Swollen up? Like a f\*\*\*\*\*’ zeppelin, Beth.’ When you perform these lines, you have to do it straight. The trick is not to oversell them.”

She adds, “There’s a line in which Christine offers advice. ‘Do you know who does a good Paracetamol? Morrisons.’ Which is a line (the late) Simon Carlyle actually said to (co-writer) Gregor Sharp. When I repeated the line at the Hydro I had to stop before the punchline because the anticipatory laughter was so loud. For me, it was about having the confidence to wait. To let the audience know that I know that they know what is to come. And don’t over-deliver it. But I know lots of actresses that would have done just that.”

Comedy. Drama. Smith can offer it all. And now there isn’t a theatre in the land who wouldn’t offer her the No 1 dressing room. Particularly the King’s Glasgow at Christmas time.

And that’s why she’s in the position to, well, reinterpret this year’s story of Aladdin.

“There is a problem with Aladdin, because there is cultural appropriation in it,” she maintains. “And I’m sorry to say, as someone who likes to think of themselves as someone who is culturally right-on, that it was my daughter Hannah who drew me to the problem. She said, ‘Mum, what’s with all the Chinese wigs and costumes in the panto?’ But then I should have been more aware because I did my thesis at university on panto, and I knew that Aladdin was actually an Arabian Nights story and should have been set in the Middle East.”

Smith explains how the Chinese came to feature so heavily in the panto story.

“When the Chinese arrived in London in Victorian times, they set up laundries, and the Chinese were called twankies, which was reckoned to reflect the (phonetic) sound of their laughter). And Widow Twankey was named as such because she was the widow of a Chinese sailor.”

And the Chinese policemen?

“Well, they appeared as clowns who keep getting lost and running into each other because Londoners of the time apparently thought it hilarious that Chinese people couldn’t read road signs.”

So, we’ve had a panto predicated upon racist overtones for more than 150 years?

“Yes, and you may argue it once featured elements of social satire, but it doesn’t work today. We can’t have white people dressing up as Chinese, with Chinese make-up, the lot.”

Smith backs up her argument. “When Robin Williams did the Disney version of Aladdin, he told the real story. It didn’t have the likes of Wishy Washy.”

So, how does the new Aladdin come to Glasgow and not upset a traditionalist panto soul?

Smith offers a pleased grin. “It’s a brand-new set, and a new storyline. I am Mother Goose, and we run the theatre of pantomime, in the Land of Pantomania.”

What? Panto is generally bonkers, but having a Goose loose in amongst Aladdin, well, isn’t this surreality at a whole new level?

“Aye,” she says laughing. “And Cinderella features as well, when we have the discovery of Cinderella’s slipper in the cave.”

Smith goes on to explain. “The story tells how people aren’t coming to the panto theatre so much anymore; it has lost its grant, and they don’t know what to do. My nemesis is another actress from years ago, the Wicked Witch, and she has always wanted to run the theatre.”

Okay. That’s certainly different, although some of the original Aladdin panto concept remains. For example, it features Andy Burnham levels of hope, in the form of the Spirit of the Ring because the Spirit knows that only Aladdin can access the Cave of Wonders, where the Magic Lamp is.

Meanwhile, Aladdin is in love with Princess Jemima, and King Cole is the king of Pantomania, and he puts the age-old dramatic block on working class Aladdin going anywhere near the princess.

“It’s still about telling a good v evil story, which is why it’s still about three wishes, and dreams, and it’s tied in with an economic reality – but via the saving of a theatre.”

Smith grins. “And of course there’s a Genie, three wishes and all of that.”

Phew.

“And part of the set features a gallery in which all the panto greats are up there on the wall, such as Stanley Baxter and Jimmy Logan.”

Well, panto generally requires a little suspension of disbelief, but this year’s King’s Theatre’s cross-fertilisation of themes may require just a little more?

“Maybe, but there are daft gags, which are great.” She offers a pleased laugh. “I came up with the idea of us doing a charity single, with me singing. It’s borrowed from a Will Ferrell-Christopher Walken Saturday Night Live sketch. It’s outrageous.”

Who’s to say Smith can’t judge what a panto audience wants? Already business is ‘through the roof,’ building on from the reconfigured Little Mermaid of last year.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21300444.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Elaine C Smith in Cathy *(Image: Mihaela Bodlovic)*

And who’s to say she doesn’t deserve the reward of having quite a magnificent year. And it’s set to continue. Smith reveals that Cathy will play at the Citizens in Glasgow in the New Year. And Two Doors Down will return to television for a new series. It’s also odds-on it will return to the Hydro, given its phenomenal success.

But she maintains that despite demand for her stage and TV appearances, it’s more than likely she’ll retire for good in two years’ time.

“I’ll be 70 then,” she says. “And right now, after three months non-stop I’m exhausted.”

She pauses.

“Don’t get me wrong; I’m really enjoying my work, and I don’t do anything I don’t want to do now. But here’s the thing; I don’t want to be any more famous. I’ll be ready to stop.”

Smith offers an illustration of how hard the work can be. Maintaining focus, particularly on stage, can be immensely draining. She reveals that one night during Cathy the play had to be stopped.

“A woman had a heart attack. And she turned out to be fine, thank God, but the delay meant we had come off before the ending was played out (with the next play ready to come on.) So, I drove home, but when I got there, in my head, I was still at a point in the play where Cathy was raging. I hadn’t gone through the arc of the character.”

The role correction to Cathy is consistent with the Traverse Theatre’s cast listing, which identifies Elaine C Smith as Cathy. She offers a wry smile.

“It sounds all actory, but I hadn’t come down. And I didn’t realise any of this until Bob (her husband) asked me a totally innocuous question and I snapped back with ‘Ah f\*\*\*\* n’ don’t know!’”

Smith laughs. “I hadn’t arrived at Cathy’s resolution in the play and as a result poor Bob got it in the neck.”

But Elaine, that’s not exhaustion. That’s simply you calling up the Method, the acting style you embraced back in the days of Wildcat Theatre, honed on TV sitcom City Lights and perfected on Fat Friends: The Musical?

“Yes, darling,” she says in perfect RP. “It’s the Lee Strasberg in me.”

Personally, I don’t believe that Smith will exit Stage Left for the last time in 2028. She loves the work, the thrill of being on stage, the love of working on a new script, and helping to bring out the best in young writers such as Eilidh Loan.

Yes, she has the grandchildren now, and she loves spending more time with family. But there is as much chance of Smith staying at home full-time as there is of Elton closing his Steinway lid for ever.

“We’ll see,” she says grinning. “We’ll see.”

**Aladdin, the King’s Theatre, Glasgow, November 21 – January 3, also stars Darren Brownlie as Spirit of the Ring, Hannah Jarrett-Scott as The Wicked Witch, Blythe Jandoo as Princess Jemima, Gordon Cooper as Old King Cole, and Rushand Chambers as Genie of the Lamp.**
