More than 40 years before the infamous Salem Witch Trials shocked colonial America, one of the largest mass executions for witchcraft in history took place in the North East, seeing 16 people hanged for practising dark arts.

The 1650 Newcastle Witch Trials have attracted far less cultural attention than the better-known trials at Pendle in Lancashire and Salem in Massachusetts, but mark one of the North East's bloodiest and most interesting events.

Newcastle was governed by a strongly Puritan council in the years before the trials, so in 1649 concerned councillors arranged for a witchfinder to be brought from Scotland. Contemporary reports suggest the witchfinder was paid 20 shillings for each case he handled in Berwick.

![The North Berwick witches from a contemporary pamphlet, Newes From Scotland, 1591.](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21643504.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Scottish witch hunters were common during the 17th century, with one method used by witchfinders being pricking, which used a long needle, called a witch pricker, to determine guilt. *(Image: Wikimedia)*

A town crier urged Newcastle residents to report suspected witches or accuse their neighbours. The campaign led to 31 arrests, with the accused taken to Newcastle Town Hall.

Most were held at Newgate prison, on Newgate Street, which housed suspected witches from the city. Another accused, Jane Martin, was held in the dungeons of Newcastle Castle, which served as the county prison for Northumberland.

Records suggest 31 people were tried for witchcraft at the Quayside Guildhall in 1650. Accounts differ over whether 27 were convicted, but surviving evidence shows that 16 were hanged.

Because guilty verdicts in other British witch trials usually led to execution, historians question the claim that 27 people were convicted but only 16 were hanged.

The executed group was comprised of 15 women and one man, who were taken to the Town Moor in Newcastle on August 21, 1650, and hanged.

The executions are regarded as one of England’s largest mass killings for witchcraft, with the accused later buried in unmarked graves at St Andrew’s Church on Newgate Street in Newcastle city centre.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21643486.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The executed were buried in unmarked graves at St Andrew’s Church on Newgate Street in Newcastle city centre. *(Image: Tripadvisor)*

The exact location of the graves is unknown, although it is believed they were buried on the north side of the churchyard so they would receive less sunlight.

Many details of the evidence used against the accused have been lost, but a common method used by Scottish witchfinders at the time was known as pricking, which used a long needle, called a witch pricker, to determine guilt.

If the accused bled, they were considered innocent. If they did not bleed, they were judged guilty.

Many historical records were destroyed by wartime bombing during the Second World War, including judicial papers from the 17th century, making it difficult to establish exactly what happened during the trials.

Some records survived in nearby areas, including a 1649 petition by Newcastle residents calling for an investigation into witchcraft. Another surviving document links Jane Martin (sometimes called Janet) to those executed on the Town Moor. She was from Morpeth.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21643466.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Women being hanged for witchcraft, Newcastle, 1655. *(Image: Ralph Gardiner/Wikimedia)*

Historians say a paper in the Durham archives confirms the names of those hanged and dates the executions to August 21, 1650.

Witch trials continued across parts of Europe and the American colonies during the 17th century, despite growing doubts over how witchcraft could be proved.

Historians later concluded that torture had forced false confessions in many cases.

The last recorded legal execution for witchcraft in Britain is believed to have taken place in 1727, when Janet Horne was burned alive, 77 years after Newcastle’s mass execution.

More than 370 years on, witchcraft has become a prominent aspect of Britain’s more spooky folklore. But as the North East prepares for Halloween, the deaths of 16 people remain a grim reminder of a period in history when superstition and religious fervour could prove fatal.
