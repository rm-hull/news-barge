It was sometime around the year 850 in the forests of the Ethiopian Plateau that a goat herder named Kaldi is said to have noticed something peculiar about his herd.

Whenever they ate the bright red berries from an evergreen shrub nearby something of a transformation would take place and they became unusually energetic. Curious, Kaldi tried some of the berries himself, experiencing a sudden surge of energy with all his synapses firing in unison.

According to legend, local monks initially dismissed his discovery before accidentally roasting the beans over a fire and brewing what is widely considered to be the first cup of coffee. More than a millennium later, coffee remains one of the world's most popular beverages.

With International Coffee Day falling just as the third quarter drew to a close, it provided a fitting backdrop to a period that delivered its own jolt to financial markets, leaving investors reaching for an extra shot of resilience.

Whilst many turn to coffee for a much-needed morning lift, bond investors were left feeling the grind throughout the quarter as yields climbed across most fixed income sectors. With the war in the Middle East showing little sign of abating, oil prices remained stubbornly high, pushing past $100 per barrel again during September.

The combination of higher oil prices and resilient economic data prompted another rise in government bond yields, which move inversely to prices. The 10-year US Treasury yield rose to its highest since 2007, whilst 10-year German Bund yield climbed to its highest since 2011. French government borrowing costs also moved higher after its government presented a 2027 budget bill which could struggle to get unpopular belt-tightening measures through parliament.

Whilst the bond rout also reached domestic shores with the yields on 30 and 20 year gilts hitting levels not seen since 1998, the UK enjoyed a continuation of the “Burnham Bounce” that had manifested during the summer months.

The UK economy has certainly begun to perk up, acting as a boon for the new man in Number 10. More "robusta" data towards the end of the quarter showed that Britain’s economy grew faster than previously thought during the summer, building on its position as the fastest-growing G7 economy in the first half of 2026.

Shares of UK housebuilders also performed well during September amid news of a new scheme to help first-time buyers get on the property ladder.

Unlike several of its global counterparts – including the Federal Reserve, European Central Bank and Bank of Japan – the Bank of England opted to leave interest rates unchanged during the quarter.

Raising rates for the first time in three years, the Fed said its decision was unanimous and signalled further tightening is likely in the near future to bring inflation down more quickly. With rates now at 3.75%-4%, the decision drew criticism from President Donald Trump, who called for rates to be cut to 1% shortly after the announcement.

The decision highlighted the increasingly delicate balancing act facing policymakers as they seek to bring inflation under control without undermining economic growth. After a very strong period for AI-focused stocks, the US market initially maintained some of that momentum in July as investors focused on strong earnings and capital expenditure.

However, as the quarter progressed, investors became increasingly selective, questioning elevated valuations and significant spending commitments. This prompted a broadening of market leadership beyond large-cap technology stocks, with smaller companies and more value-orientated sectors enjoying stronger performance.

The real star performer of the quarter was commodities, in particular oil. Oil prices enjoyed a solid bounce back from the previous quarter as once again, the US and Iran reached a political impasse over the opening of the Strait of Hormuz, a major artery for the flow of oil through the Gulf states. September and October consistently saw Brent crude prices above $100 a barrel as chances of a peace deal being struck between the two sides seemed like having an espresso with the lights turned off – a shot in the dark.

In what has been a volatile quarter for markets, any good barista or investor will tell you that it's all about the blend. Failing to ensure that your portfolio is widely diversified, whilst following a robust investment and hoping for consistently strong returns is a bit like ordering the largest coffee on the menu: something of a tall order.
