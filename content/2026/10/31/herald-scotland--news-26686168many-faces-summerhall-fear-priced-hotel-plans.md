**Jamie King may have schizophrenia, but that condition no longer defines him.**

In and out of hospital in his youth, today, Mr King is an artist, spending his days painting self-portraits in a small loft high above the hustle and bustle of Summerhall.

"In the eight years I've been at Summerhall, I’ve painted a lot of self-portraits,” he says, speaking about his work and the important role that the arts venue plays in his life.

“With all the chaos in my mind, sometimes I hear voices, sometimes I get very paranoid.

“When I come into this space, it's peaceful and so creative. I can feel the creativity telepathically almost from this place. And it gives me a great, creative, calm space to be.”

Mr King is just one of more than 100 artists and small businesses who call Summerhall home.

He fears that plans to turn part of the complex into a hotel or student flats will tear apart its close-knit community.

“The support from Summerhall is invaluable,” he tells me. “Everybody kind of makes sure people are okay.

“There's a community spirit that kind of emanates when people visit Summerhall. You can't just dismiss that. You can't just take that away.”

![Option 1 - one of the proposals for the future of Summerhall.](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21557113.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Option 1 - one of the proposals for the future of Summerhall. *(Image: AMA)*

Under plans by property firm AMA, several buildings at the site could be knocked down to make room for the new development, including the 1970s-era building on Summerhall Square and the so-called ‘cranked wing’ along Hope Park Terrace.

Dr Ali Afshar, who runs AMA, has promised that arts provision will continue at the site, which housed Edinburgh University's Royal Dick Veterinary School until 2011.

However, Nicholas Bone, artistic director of theatre company Magnetic North, tells me that artists have found it difficult to trust that developers have their interests in mind.

“Being somewhere like Summerhall, where there's a whole range of artists from other art forms, is fantastic for us,” he notes as we chat in his office, surrounded by theatre posters.

“It's really important to us to be in a place where there's that opportunity to have a photographer down the stairs and there's an illustrator over there and a jewellery maker there.

“It's going to need investment to continue to thrive,” he acknowledges.

Indeed, this refrain is expressed often during my afternoon walking around Summerhall, parts of which certainly have a “rough charm”, as Mr Bone puts it.

“It feels that Summerhall doesn't require the level of investment that comes from demolishing parts of it and rebuilding.

“That's an expensive development, which then means that the developer is looking to get income that will fund that kind of level of development.

“And inevitably that's not going to be us and Carol the jeweller and Robert the illustrator and Peter the photographer downstairs.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21653343.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Nicholas Bone, artistic director of award-winning theatre company Magnetic North, has been based at Summerhall since 2017. *(Image: Peter Dibdin)*

This fear, that artists will be priced out of Summerhall once the bulldozers and jackhammers fall silent, is shared by many of the people I speak to, including animator Iain Gardner.

Mr Gardner, whose film *A Bear Named Wojtek* was included in the Academy Awards shortlist several years ago, says that developers must balance financial value with the importance of culture.

“Planners often miss the cultural value within institutions and buildings. It's very easy to kind of see all the money signs when you look at the potential to turn this into a hotel or turn it into a residential space.

“But more of that is not helping build Edinburgh's reputation in a way, I dare say, that the people who are resident in this building are.

“This is supposed to be a city of culture, it's supposed to be an international focus for culture and yet we appear to be scrambling to find places for artists to actually practice, collaborate, to grow their ideas, to grow their companies, to grow their businesses.”

Mr Gardner attended a consultation event led by AMA earlier this month, and came away unimpressed.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21653344.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Iain Gardner, director of award-winning, Oscar-shortlisted production studio Animation Garden. *(Image: Peter Dibdin)*

“I spoke to the developers and their idea of what an artist studio is falls significantly short of what is required. One of the architects I spoke to seemed to think that the best solution was to have a kind of row of glass-fronted studios for tourists still looking on. It's a patronising view of what art is.

“We're not part of Edinburgh's Disneyland.

“I challenged the architect. I said, you would not want me coming into your studio and nicking your ideas and giving them to your competitors. We're actually doing serious work here.”

Artist and designer Andy McGregor agrees.

He is one of my guides on this whistle stop tour through the warren that is Summerhall, alongside photographer Peter Dibdin and filmmaker Alice Nelson.

“AMA just kind of seems to have run fairly roughshod over it and used a sort of cut and paste version from some kind of photo library of what artist studios and artist practice is all about. And it's not that, you know.

“There's so many possibilities here to have a creative and cultural resource, a village of things that are going on that are completely unique.”

Mr McGregor, who has worked with the National Galleries and the Castle of Light exhibition, among other projects, adds: “I grew up in Edinburgh and I want to see this place stay, not as is, we recognize that change has to happen, but the ethos of it, the culture of it to remain and be part of this city.”

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21653342.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Andy McGregor, internationally renowned design consultant, artist and producer in his studio at Summerhall. *(Image: Peter Dibdin)*

Ms Nelson, currently producing her first feature at Summerhall, also compares the complex to a village.

“We've all spent so much time as neighbours that we've turned into collaborators,” she explains.

“I probably have the kind of business that could exist somewhere else. But I'd be absolutely devastated if I lost somewhere. Because it's a village. I mean, it really is a village.”

“Pete is working on my film,” she says as an example, pointing to Mr Dibdin.

“What we have here is the ability to be able to plan for the future and think about how we want to grow and make our businesses and our practices grow and develop into being internationally recognised,” the snapper tells me.

“And that's so important.”

Mr Dibdin laughs.

“I've been here at Summerhall since it started, when they closed at 5pm in the evening and there was no pub. I was able to invest in my business in this building.

“One of the important things that small businesses and artists need to grow and to add to the economy is the ability to have that vision.”

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21653341.jpg?type=mds-article-620)BAFTA winning documentary director Alice Nelson, currently producing her first feature film in her studio in Summerhall. *(Image: Peter Dibdin)*

I am ushered through one of the most unique venues in Edinburgh, the Dissection Room.

Once used to examine the specimens of dead animals, the space hosts a range of music and theatre performances, including during the Fringe each summer.

Nearby, a groundbreaking laboratory is bringing art and science together.

Dr James Howie is one of the founders of ASCUS, an open lab encouraging artists to explore science through their work.

The lab would be demolished if plans for the development are approved.

“It's a real sort of melting pot for everyone, really,” Dr Howie tells me as I walk around the lab, examining microscopes and beakers.

“Artists, scientists, designers, members of the public, researchers. Summerhall is the ideal place for us to be because it's open, accessible to everybody. It's a place where everyone can feel welcome.

“We've had an artist from India come and do some work with us,” he says.

“We have people come up from London regularly to do things like growing bacteria and to make dyes to cover record covers. And we recently had a textile that was dyed with bacterial dyes sent into space.

“This is one of the spaces that would be demolished. For us, it's just a really special space. It would be really hard to replicate.

“I'm really concerned about the plans because it doesn't seem to retain very much of the essence of Summerhall at all.”

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21653345.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Andrew Barnett and Aly Mathers at Barney's Brewery, established in 2012 on the site of the original Summerhall Brewery. *(Image: Peter Dibdin)*

His concerns are shared by Aly Mathers, who works at one of the most interesting places at Summerhall, Barney’s Beer.

The microbrewery produces beer for the nearby Royal Dick Pub, a favourite of visitors and residents.

“Most of our beer stays within Edinburgh. And in fact, a large percentage of that stays at Summerhall. The Royal Dick is our biggest customer,” says Mr Mathers

The Barney’s team is following in the footsteps of some of the earliest inhabitants of Summerhall, who set up a brewery on the site in the early 1700s.

“We're very much tied in with Summerhall,” Mr Mathers explains in a small office adjacent to the brewhouse, the smell of hops heavy in the air.

“Not only is our biggest customer here on site, but we are part of it. Our designer is on site. We use the printers and things like that.

“We've done collaborations with the bands who have played here. We did a beer with Idlewild years back. So yeah, it presents a lot of opportunities being here.”

I end my sojourn at Summerhall in the office of casting director Simone Pereira Hind, perhaps best known for her work on Elizabeth (1998), including casting Cate Blanchett in the lead role.

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21653340.jpg?type=mds-article-620) Emmy award-winning casting director Simone Pereira Hind works at Summerhall. *(Image: Peter Dibdin)*

Ms Pereira Hind, an Emmy award winner for her work in casting The Primrose Railway Children (2024), says that Summerhall is a “fantastic” location.

“It is kind of crucial to what we do. All the actors that we see here, all the producers and directors are used to coming here.

“I’d really hate to lose this place for so many reasons.”

Ms Pereira Hind talks about the importance of collaboration, about the *esprit de corps* that seems to linger in the air here.

“I could move into another space, into a corporate space, but I would loathe to do that,” she says.

“I fought hard to get in here. I nagged and nagged and nagged because once I knew about this place, I was determined to get in here.

“And especially as so many of us are freelancers, so our incomes are uncertain, having somewhere that is affordable is incredibly important,” she adds.

Mr Dibdin chips in.

“It's not cheap rent. It's affordable rent,” he says.

“There's a notion that it's sort of subsidized rent, but actually the turnover from this place could be going on £1m easily.

“And what the residents have always done is help keep this place going. We are part of the economic possibility of how this building can run.

![BAFTA winning documentary director Alice Nelson, currently in production on her first feature in her studio in Summerhall. Photograph by Peter Dibdin](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21653373.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Ms Nelson says there is space for a commercial aspect at Summerhall, if artists are properly consulted. *(Image: Peter Dibdin.)*

“It's always had a kind of commercial side hand in hand with cultural stuff going on,” Ms Nelson, the filmmaker, adds.

“Personally, I think it's a pretty good combination. I think there's definitely a future for Summerhall running along those lines.”

“We know from our many conversations with the people who make Summerhall what it is just how important it is to Edinburgh's creative community,” an AMA spokesperson told The Herald.

“Summerhall's identity goes far beyond that of an arts venue. It is a home for artists, makers, performers and creative practitioners, and we are absolutely committed to ensuring that the arts remain at the heart of its future.”

Faith is the essence of things not seen, and it is clear that these artists are not quite ready to place their hopes, and their livelihoods, in the hands of Dr Afshar and his team.

The stakes could not be higher, as Mr Bone, the theatre director, reminds me.

“If we're priced out, then the very thing that has made Summerhall a desirable place will disappear," he says.

“It's not going to be an arts quarter if all the artists get squeezed out.”

Yet, change *is* coming to Summerhall — one way or another.
