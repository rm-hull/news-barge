**Looking back at the end of the Budget week, how did John Healey affect students and young people in the UK? Julia Davies is a 21-year-old Durham University student on work experience at The Northern Echo. We asked her for her views.**

Chancellor John Healey has stressed that he was creating hope for the future.

As a recent graduate, the truth of this sentiment is vital to secure my faith in Andy Burnham’s government.

My confrontation with the current job market has become a common experience: almost one million 16 to 24 year olds in the UK are not in education or employment.

The range of options available to graduates also seems to be slimming, as entry-level jobs are falling by 14 per cent year on year.

Clearly, this is an experience young people will feel is most directly and urgently affecting their future.

However, while the Chancellor was consistently outspoken about supporting families, households, and entrepreneurs, he did not deliver actionable policies targeting all issues young people are facing.

**Cost of living**

The cost of living is a tangible issue for all people in the UK. Most notably, he announced that 20million households in council tax bands A to D will receive a £100 payment directly to their bank accounts to support energy bill payments this winter.

But while some young people will certainly benefit, students will not as they are exempt from council tax.

Healey is also suspending tariffs on 100 supermarket products including baked beans, garlic, and plant-based drinks. However, the government has said this will only save individuals £21 a year.

While bills and groceries are significant living expenses, many young people live in house-shares and are not typically financially responsible for others.

Instead, the cost of living expenses which disproportionately affect students and young people are student loan repayments and high rent prices, neither of which were directly addressed in the budget.

**Business rates**

While raising the minimum wage by 3.9 per cent was a positive and necessary change, there is always the risk that key sectors might be disincentivised from hiring young people with less experience.

However, John Healey has funded this increase by increasing the threshold for business rates for the first time in 10 years - from £12,000 to £18,000 in retail, hospitality, and leisure sectors.

Research conducted by the Federation of Small Business (FSB) this year revealed that 28% of small businesses would hire more staff members if business rate thresholds were increased.

The new threshold relieves 70,000 businesses from paying the tax altogether, which gives me hope that it will relieve money businesses would rather be spending on people.

Ensuring fair wages while simultaneously incentivising hiring is difficult, as small businesses equally struggle to grow when budgets are consumed by higher wages and national insurance.

Ultimately, this is certainly a welcome tax cut to boost employment, as it provides hope for more stability in the job market.

**Employment opportunities**

It was an easy decision for me to choose university at 18. Now at 21, I would start a masters program tomorrow if I could, but weighing up whether I would incur more debt than it is worth is a serious consideration.

For many 18 year olds, even that first decision is not so easy, and John Healey has promised to offer more options: a Young Entrepreneurs Allowance and a Local Apprenticeship Service.

The Local Apprenticeship Service will see £100 million provided to mayors across the UK, and is working on creating jobs for young people outside metropolitan hubs, to allow young people to live at home.

Moreover, the Young Entrepreneurs Allowance covers “a year of living costs for young founders”, offering security and turning self-employment into a more viable career option.

**What was missing**

It remains unclear for me how the Budget will accommodate those facing the graduate job market, looking for entry-level jobs, and tackling rising student loan repayments.

Most urgently, Plan 2 student loan repayment thresholds will remain frozen until 2030, meaning they will not rise to accommodate inflation on earnings.

Martin Lewis has described this as a “breach of natural justice”, as Rachel Reeves changed the terms of this contract retroactively.

For me, this policy change indicated that Starmer’s government was willing to target low-income graduates for tax revenue, squeezing young people’s disposable income and making it difficult for them to save.

John Healey’s ignorance of this suggests that Andy Burnham’s government may be willing to do the same.

There were several welcome changes to employment policies in the Healey bBudget. But the things he chose to exclude raises concerns around Burnham’s commitment to tackling what he himself called the “economic catastrophe and moral failure” that young people are facing.
