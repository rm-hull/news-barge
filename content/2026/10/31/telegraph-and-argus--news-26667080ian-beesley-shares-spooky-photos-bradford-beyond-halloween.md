IAN Beesley has photographed many abandoned spaces - mills, factories, mines, pubs.

As an acclaimed social documentary photographer, he has captured working life and the final years of industry across Bradford and beyond.

He was present at the moment a mill ceased production and was emptied of its workers. He documented the final days of one of Bradford’s ‘lone pubs’ left standing when the houses around them were demolished, scattering local communities elsewhere. And he has often found himself “a few steps in front of the bulldozer as dozens of mills were swept away”.

Ian’s black and white photographs of the industrial North - many of which are currently on display in his Life Goes On exhibition in Salts Mill - are powerful and poignant, and some of them have an eerie beauty, capturing a lost era.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21626557.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Goitside - one of Bradford's 'lost neighbourhoods' *(Image: Ian Beesley)*

Some of the abandoned places that Ian photographed were said to be haunted. But there was only one place where he felt truly spooked....

“I went to photograph Bents Mill in Goit Stock in November 1983,” says Ian. “The mill had been empty for a number of years and the owner, Mr Kelly, kindly gave me the keys to the mill, but suggested not to stay there when it was getting dark.

I assumed this was because there was no electricity to the mill, so no lighting.

“I went to the mill in late November on one of those gloomy lightless afternoons, I was accompanied by my border collie, Leonard. The mill is in the bottom of Goit Stock, a steep sided valley and was originally a water powered mill.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21626560.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Bents Mill at Goit Stock, where Ian had an unsettling experience

“I unlocked the big main door and began to explore the dank interior. Leonard scurried about chasing mice (or rats), enjoying the excitement of this strange interior. I climbed the well worn mill steps to one of the upper floors, Leonard running in front of me.

“It was mid afternoon, but it was already getting dark. The large room was empty, apart from a few abandoned wool bales and piles of rubbish. Leonard had vanished into the shadows - he suddenly shot back and stood trembling by my side, his eyes standing out like chapel hat pegs. His hackles stood up and he was staring into a spot in the shadows, he kept growling and was clearly very agitated.

“It was unnerving, he refused to move. I was unsettled and decided to leave.

“When I returned the keys to Mr Kelly, he asked how I had got on. I told him of my experience. He nodded and said that many employees would refuse to work in that area because of the atmosphere.”

On this Halloween weekend, Ian shares one of his photos of Bents Mill on this page, along with other spooky images he has taken over the years.

His evocative image of the old Theatre Royal, taken shortly before the building was demolished in 1990, reveals the stage where Sir Henry Irving made his last appearance, shortly before he collapsed and died.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21626558.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The Theatre Royal on Manningham Lane was said to be haunted by the ghost of actor Sir Henry Irving, who collapsed and died after appearing on stage there in October 1905

The Manningham Lane theatre was said to be haunted by the ghost of Sir Henry.

At 67-years-old, he was the most famous actor-manager of his generation, the first knight of the British theatre and the Sir Laurence Olivier of his day. He was in Bradford on what was billed his Farewell Tour. It turned out to be his farewell in more ways than one...

On Friday, October 13, 1905, having just completed five of six nightly performances of classic plays on stage in the lead role, Sir Henry was driven from the theatre to the Midland Hotel.

It had been a successful week. On Tuesday Sir Henry had been feted by the Mayor at a civic lunch in his honour. On stage he had given a series of strong performances in Tennyson’s play Becket, which had a favourable review in the Bradford Daily Telegraph.

During Friday’s performance the actor seemed unwell but managed to get through it. His business manager - Dracula author Bram Stoker - left him taking a Hansom cab the short distance to his hotel, but just before midnight Irving collapsed in the foyer of the hotel and died within minutes. A plaque at the Midland Hotel commemorates the death.

Some of Ian’s spooky photos are of historic Undercliffe Cemetery, where grand gothic gravestones on the windswept hillside setting have inspired several ghost stories.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21626562.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Gravestones through the mist in Undercliffe Cemetery *(Image: Ian Beesley)*

Some visitors have spoken of seeing mysterious figures moving among the old graves and mausoleums. A recurring local legend tells of the ghost of a woman in Victorian mourning dress - a long dark gown, bonnet and veil, seen near children’s graves.

And voices are said to be heard, from whispers and murmurs to children giggling.

In such a spooky setting, where there are thousands of graves dating back to the mid-19th century, it’s perhaps not surprising that visitors have reported strange sightings and sounds. It is all part of the eerie appeal of this magnificent site, where a story lies behind every gravestone.

\* Ian Beesley has donated his entire archive - more than 200,000 items, including photographs, negatives and notebooks - to Bradford Industrial Museum.

His exhibition, Life Goes On, featuring striking images of people at work and leisure, is in Gallery 2 at Salts Mill.
