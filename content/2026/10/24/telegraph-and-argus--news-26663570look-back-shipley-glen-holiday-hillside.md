FOR generations of Bradfordians, Shipley Glen was a sort of seaside destination without the sea.

A short daytrip from the city or the busy towns of the Aire valley brought working families to a magical landscape of steep wooded slopes, gritstone rocks, and open moorland.

The prime attraction was a remarkable collection of popular entertainments, and by the late 1800s, thousands of local people were making the journey, mostly on Sundays.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/13610027.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Crowds flocking to Shipley Glen in 1938. The beauty spot attracted hundreds of people on days off from work

The railway reached Shipley in 1847, making the Glen readily accessible to the region’s rapidly growing industrial population. By the 1870s it was becoming an established leisure spot, with the Old Glen Hotel offering popular boat swings and donkey rides. The name is said to have been coined by the Reverend Peter Scott, a Shipley clergyman, who brought his Sunday school class here for annual Whitsuntide excursions as early as the 1840s.

What visitors found was a Victorian pleasure ground built on an impressive scale. In 1879 the Voss family constructed Vulcan House, where the original tearoom was soon supplemented by donkey rides, swings, dodgems, paddling pools, a penny arcade, and ice-cream stands. Later additions included a camera obscura, cable cars, a horse-drawn carousel around the pond, and a toboggan slide.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/19394892.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Sam Wilson, who set up Shipley Glen Tramway.

One of Britain’s earliest wooden rollercoasters was among the most popular attractions. The hillside quickly became a destination in its own right for Bradford’s working population, offering wholesome entertainment and fresh countryside in a single Sunday outing.

The steep approach presented an obvious physical problem, and local publican and showman Sam Wilson tackled it directly with the Shipley Glen Tramway. Opened on May 18, 1895, the quarter-mile line climbed a daunting gradient of about one in seven, with its two wooden cars passing halfway up the slope. Originally powered by a gas engine, it was successfully electrified in 1928. The tramway became an essential part of the whole experience, carrying excited visitors up from the Saltaire side directly into the pleasure grounds above.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/13656032.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The old fun fair at Shipley Glen was a popular attraction.

Yet these attractions were only part of the appeal of Shipley Glen. Beyond lay the steep, wooded ravine descending from Baildon Moor, densely lined with ancient oak trees and broken by dramatic rocky crags around Bracken Hall Green.

Flat-topped gritstone boulders still provide natural perches for sitting and sunbathing, while well-worn footpaths lead across the open moor towards Baildon or down towards Bingley. Historic walking routes even press onward towards Ilkley.

The woods themselves are a pleasure at any time of year, but for me autumn is the most magical season to visit, when the canopy turns to glorious amber and gold, and the Glen feels at its most atmospheric. Presumably there was always an ice cream to be had. Robinsons’ van, and their delicious ices, is firmly etched into my own memories of the place in relatively recent times, along with the quiet woodland trails leading away from the crowds.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/10032726.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Shipley glen Tramway 31 April 1971..

I know the Glen particularly well because its paths formed part of our school cross-country circuit. Running it repeatedly gave me a hard-earned, intimate knowledge of its gradients, hidden rocks, and treacherous bends, as well as the stretches where the ground suddenly became tougher underfoot - in winter almost impossibly waterlogged.

Back then, what eventually became the Old Glen House pub and restaurant on Prod Lane was a derelict ruin, and no one could have foreseen its splendid rebuilding and eventual conversion.

Today, the Bracken Hall Countryside Centre provides valuable information and community activities focused on the surrounding moorland, woodland, wildlife, heritage, and archaeology. Visitors learn how the Glen was originally carved out by glacial activity, while archaeological evidence includes ancient charcoal-burning platforms hidden in the woods and prehistoric rock carvings around Bracken Hall Green.

In this modern age of leisure, Shipley Glen has quietly adapted. Where mill workers and their families once strolled in their Sunday best seeking thrills and escape, today’s hikers pass through in high-tech waterproofs with maps in plastic wallets slung around their necks. The paths that once carried vast holiday crowds now form part of a much wider network for walkers exploring Baildon Moor and the countryside around Saltaire.

One of the Glen’s great advantages, of course, is that UNESCO World Heritage Saltaire, with its model streets and much-visited Salts Mill - now home to art galleries, exhibitions, shops, and cafés - lies directly on the other side of the Coach Road.

Yet for me, the Glen is far more than a scenic corridor to pass through. It remains a true destination in itself, still providing the simple joy of escaping the city. Though most of the traditional amusements have vanished, the volunteer-run tramway endures.

At Christmas, the popular Santa Specials rekindle that old magic, carrying children up the festooned track to Santa’s grotto, the route brightly lit with seasonal decorations. As they board, you see on their faces the very same wonder and excitement that spellbound earlier generations who knew nothing of foreign holidays, theme parks, or modern digital distractions.
