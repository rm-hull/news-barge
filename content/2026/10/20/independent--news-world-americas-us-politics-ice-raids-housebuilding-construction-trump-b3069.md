Two-thirds of construction contractors believe that ICE’s raids on immigrant communities have had an adverse impact on the U.S. housing market, making it harder to find laborers and leaving newbuilds at a standstill, according to a survey.

A poll conducted by John Burns Research & Consulting cited by *The Wall Street Journal* reveals a significant rise in the number of builders forced to conclude that the Department of Homeland Security’s actions had caused them knock-on problems, compared to just 13 percent who said the same in September 2025 and 23 percent in March this year.

The *Journal* suggests that while the raids ordered by President Donald Trump’s administration only created employee shortages in isolated regions like South Texas last year, the widening of the net this summer has created a much bigger problem for the sector, which has often relied on flexible migrant labor to fill roles for roofers, painters and framers.

It gives the example of Jacksonville, Florida, where some contractors claim as much as 70 percent of their workforce has disappeared.

“We’re almost dead in the water because our workforce is gone,” said Scott Brannock, president of Jacksonville-based Tidewater Homes and the Northeast Florida Builders Association.

![Two-thirds of U.S. construction contractors say ICE raids have had an adverse impact on their industry by creating a manpower shortfall](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/19/15/19/iStock-179254141.jpg?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&auto=webp)

“We have national builders who are missing projections, losing hundreds of thousands of dollars because they have slabs sitting on the ground and no one to frame.”

He continued: “Talking with large shell contractors, large builders, I don’t think they fully anticipated or estimated the amount of immigrant labor that are on our job sites every day.”

Brannock told the *Journal* that framers’ salaries have risen to $45 an hour from $20 as a result of the sudden worker shortage, explaining that framing labor now accounts for $28,000 of the cost of building a 2,500 square foot house, whereas it would previously have cost just $10,000.

Another poll carried out last month by the National Association of Home Builders likewise found that the cost of labor had increased by 4.2 percent on average year-on-year.

Similar concerns were raised last year regarding the U.S. agriculture industry, another huge employer of migrant labor in states like California, with economists suggesting a food supply problem could arise if they were not replaced.

![The Trump administration's raids on undocumented migrants have inadvertently driven up costs and left residential construction projects at a standstill](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/19/15/17/iStock-1198656937.jpg?quality=75&width=640&crop=3%3A2%2Csmart&trim=163%2C0%2C0%2C0&auto=webp)

However, supporters of Trump’s crackdown on undocumented migrants argue that deportations will ultimately lower housing costs for consumers by reducing the amount of competition for properties and say that contractors should only be hiring U.S. citizens in any case.

A Department of Homeland Security spokesperson told *The Independent*: “Worksite enforcement remains a cornerstone of our efforts to protect public safety, national security, and economic stability while rescuing individuals who may be victims of labor trafficking or exploitation. These operations target illegal employment networks that undermine American workers, destabilize labor markets, and threaten American communities.

“Additionally, the employment of illegal aliens also incentivizes dangerous and illegal practices, including social security fraud. Many illegal aliens use stolen social security numbers and identities to unlawfully obtain wages, health benefits and employment authorization. Behind every stolen social security number uncovered these operations is a real American – mothers, fathers, students, and disabled workers – now facing devastating financial, emotional, and legal fallout.

“Those who are here legally and are not breaking other laws have nothing to fear.”

Matthew O’Brien, deputy executive director of the Federation for American Immigration Reform, meanwhile told the *Journal*: “It is an absurd contention that we should stop deporting immigration lawbreakers simply because construction companies want to employ them.”

Immigration enforcement is not the only contemporary phenomenon that has eaten into the supply of house builders, however.

The data center boom has also led to more specialized tradesmen like electricians being enticed away from the sector in pursuit of higher wages, further adding to the manpower shortfall and exacerbating the problem.
