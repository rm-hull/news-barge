![Amazon Bee and shopping cart.](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/xfeW8AjnLLGsSHKF7smosf-1920-80.jpg)

Amazon has found another way to make itself part of our everyday lives with its new Bee AI devices. The Bees can record conversations and are pitched as a way to remember important details and translate plans into actions. Naturally, those actions can include buying things from Amazon, making every casual conversation a potential shopping cart. You can pay $75 or $100 for the privilege, depending on whether you choose the Bee Frontier, which clips to a wrist strap or clothes, or the Bee Eclipse, a magnetic smartphone attachment.

There are practical applications for something that remembers conversation details you might otherwise forget. An assistant that recalls an appointment, prepares a follow-up email, or reminds you about a promise could save considerable time. But Amazon's announcement also promises personalized shopping recommendations based on what Bee learns about you, which strikes me as a depressing, if inevitable destination for AI tools.

Of course, you might argue plenty of people have invited Amazon into their homes through Alexa devices. Plus, Amazon already has a potentially huge amount of customer data from shopping histories, which lets it recommend products so well that it's a cliché joke to feel like you only have to mention a topic out loud for related items to show up in your recommendations.

That joke is an explicit feature of the new Bees. Turning spoken conversations into another source of personalized assistance is supposed to appeal to potential customers. The question is whether this is a bridge too far when it comes to allowing Amazon to eavesdrop on our lives to shape our shopping.

## Idle chat becomes a shopping list

![The Bee Frontier and the Bee Eclipse.](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/J8VDVC8o3m5WaFvzT5owPQ-1200-80.jpg)

The idea of an AI assistant remembering what you say and not letting anything important slip through the cracks is enticing for the convenience it promises. But imagine casually mentioning that your back hurts, your car is making a strange noise, or your child has developed an interest in dinosaurs. Those aren't just setups for a reminder to make a doctor's appointment, bring in your car, and buy tickets to the museum you'll get. They're also data points for potential purchasing interests that might otherwise never have reached a retailer.

Amazon hasn't said Bee will automatically turn every overheard complaint into a product recommendation, yet the shopping integration creates an obvious path to do so. But it might be too much personalization. Adding information from spoken conversations to what Amazon already knows could paint a much more intimate picture of someone's life, good and bad, regardless of Amazon's promises to keep data under the user's control.

And there are real privacy protections in the system, according to the company. Bee's AI processes audio in real time and deletes it rather than retaining recordings, while conversation transcripts are encrypted and users can delete them. The devices also include physical controls and lights that indicate when they're listening. Plus, the Amazon shopping connection is optional and opt-in.

But deleting the original audio doesn't eliminate every privacy concern when a device is specifically designed to remember and interpret what people say. The information extracted from a conversation can be just as revealing as the recording itself.

## Eavesdropping Bees

![Bee wristband](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/5rp3R5RzifMoPH5Tc6etdK-904-80.png)

The privacy problem becomes even more complicated when other people enter the conversation. Someone wearing a Bee device might be perfectly comfortable having their conversations analyzed, but their friends and colleagues haven't necessarily agreed to participate. A small recording indicator is hardly a substitute for meaningful consent, especially when people may not recognize the device or understand what it does.

We've already seen this controversy unfold with Meta's Ray-Ban smart glasses. People have reported unexpected recordings, and even filming. Bee doesn't have the same camera-related concerns, but recording conversations introduces its own problems. A friend discussing a medical diagnosis or workplace dispute in confidence might not feel so open if they know you're wearing a Bee. They haven't necessarily agreed to have an AI system connected to the world's largest online retailer convert their comments into searchable data.

There are also practical questions about where recording is appropriate. Private conversations can involve confidential information, and recording laws can be wildly different depending on location and circumstances. Anyone using Bee needs to consider the consent of the people talking around them, regardless of whether Amazon deletes the original audio.

The fact that personalized shopping is part of the product's ambitions says something revealing about how the company imagines the future of personal AI. Amazon and Meta seem convinced that the future involves devices observing more of our lives so they can anticipate what we need next. And it's easy to think of reasons to develop these capabilities. People with memory difficulties, busy professionals, and anyone struggling to manage complicated schedules could benefit from reliable reminders and searchable conversations. But making recording devices an ordinary accessory could have far-reaching consequences, not all pleasant.

For me, the shopping integration is where Amazon's vision becomes particularly unappealing. I can appreciate an assistant remembering that I promised to call someone or reminding me about an appointment. I have considerably less enthusiasm for a device that might turn a casual remark about needing new shoes into another opportunity for Amazon to suggest something I should purchase.

We've spent years teaching technology companies about our interests through searches, purchases, clicks, and browsing histories. Wearable AI assistants like the Bee could extend that relationship into our conversations with other people. I don't particularly want my conversations becoming another source of shopping recommendations, though. If it's that important, maybe just write it down.
