Germany's knowledge test for citizenship (*Einbürgerungstest*) is one of the final hurdles on the road to naturalisation.

So what do people who have already taken it wish they had known beforehand?

While readers disagreed about whether Germany's citizenship test is too easy, too difficult or just about right, many were reassuring about the process itself.

For those preparing to sit the test, the overwhelming view was that success comes down to preparation rather than luck.

The exam consists of 33 multiple-choice questions on German history, politics, society and democratic institutions, with applicants needing at least 17 correct answers to pass.

**'Don't stress - just study'**

Several respondents urged nervous applicants not to overthink the exam.

"Don't stress. Just learn the material well and be relaxed on test day. It's relatively fast if you’re prepared. I was in and out in a total of 20 mins," wrote an anonymous respondent, aged 47 and living in Berlin.

Alice, a 29-year-old Brit living in Berlin, struck a similarly reassuring tone: "Don't sweat it. Starting to learn for (the test) a few days before will be fine."

Another respondent based in Düsseldorf advised people preparing for the test to "spend a few nights studying".

**Practise the official questions**

By far the most common piece of advice was to work through the official question bank and practise regularly.

Many readers pointed out that the questions are publicly available. Steve, a British-born resident of Cologne who has since become a German citizen, recommended making full use of the available resources.

"Taking the online self-test until you consistently get 100 percent over a sustained period is probably the best advice I can give anyone...The 300+ bank of questions are made public. They're serving the info up on a plate," he said.

Jim, 42, originally from Massachusetts and now living in Berlin, agreed. "You can download an app with all the questions and just practice," he said.

For Cansu, preparation starts with familiarising yourself with the official pool of questions. "Definitely check out the question pool! There are 300 questions and 30 of them will be asked in the test. It's better to be prepared than just wing it."

**Don't underestimate the importance of German**

Although several readers described the test itself as straightforward, many argued that language skills are often the real challenge.

One American respondent living in Berlin said applicants should focus on more than simply remembering answers: "Practice and learn German, which is the real focus of the test."

Laura, 40, from California and living in Nuremberg, noted that the exam can contain vocabulary that some applicants find demanding: "The hardest part is the language, because the vocabulary is not really B1 vocabulary (which is the level that most people taking this test are expected to have)."

Rob in Berlin added that, "Reading and understanding German [is] key, as a few questions have tricky multiple-choice options."

**Understand rather than memorise**

While many respondents encouraged people to learn the official questions, some urged applicants not to stop there.

Kunle, originally from Nigeria, argued that candidates should see the test as an opportunity to learn more about Germany.

"While memorisation might help pass, I'd say one should also use the opportunity to motivate themselves into learning about where they live, work and have or want to start a family."

Abhishek, from India and living in Berlin, agreed. "Read and understand the material. It is limited, so doesn’t take much time, but it really helps to understand a bit about the country you want to naturalise in."

Sama, a 37-year-old Berliner originally from Iran and Australia, recommended exploring the wider context of the questions.

"Try doing some research around the questions that you see to understand the context. It helps to understand the German culture."

**Extra tips**

A handful of respondents shared more personal strategies.

Richard, who lives in North Rhine-Westphalia, suggested turning revision into a social activity. "Practise with a friend giving you random questions," he said.

Chris, who lives in Cologne, also recommended making learning more interactive.

"Find a game to help you, using a native German to find it and play it with you. Practice especially hard the evening before you take the test," he said.

And one anonymous respondent from Mexico highlighted a practical matter that will be familiar with anyone who has struggled finding available appointments in Germany.

"Book the registration date as soon as possible and take your time studying and understanding what the questions are asking," they suggested.

At least one more respondent offered a very similar piece of advice. "The true test is to get an appointment for the test. At least in Berlin," they said.
