Extreme speech has surged under Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu 's far-right government and can be heard at the highest levels ahead of Israel's Oct. 27 elections.

With just days before the vote, Israeli think tank OFEK shared a spreadsheet with The Associated Press listing 227 extreme comments made this year by officials, as part of an ongoing project tracking incitement. While Netanyahu has not used the rhetoric of his far-right allies, he campaigns with the slogan “right or Palestine,” which some critics have called incitement.

This is the atmosphere in Israel after years of war. In the wake of the Oct. 7, 2023, attacks, a defense minister said the military was fighting “human animals” in Gaza. Such remarks were cited by South Africa in accusing Israel of genocide before the International Court of Justice, allegations Israel vehemently rejects.

The hate speech, amplified by AI and social media, shapes everyday attitudes in Israel. One commentator who backs Netanyahu has called the prime minister’s closest challenger, Gadi Eisenkot, a “monkey,” a comment widely seen as a slur against Israel's Sephardic, or non-European, Jewish population.

Some extreme comments target Israel’s Arab citizens, who have the right to vote and hold office. But most speech is aimed at Palestinians in Gaza and the occupied West Bank, where violence by Jewish settlers has surged and few arrests are made.

Across the West Bank, riddled with checkpoints and barriers for Palestinians, the Arabic on some road signs is painted out. Settlers jeer and honk as they pass a Palestinian roadside shop in Lubban al-Sharqiya, where dozens of Israeli flags were placed last month. Owner Mouayad Oweis has been threatened if he removes them.

“Here we face attacks by settlers all the time,” he said, looking worn out. Some of the harshest speech in Israel reflects a desire to see Palestinians like him leave for good.

“It’s endless. It’s insane,” said Achiya Schatz, founder of the Israeli disinformation monitor FakeReporter, of the toxic discourse. “In Israel, it’s quite regular,” and getting worse, he said.
