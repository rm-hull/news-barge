YORKSHIRE has officially entered drought status because of low water levels in reservoirs and rivers, the Environment Agency has announced.

The region was in “prolonged dry weather” status since the end of July after a lack of spring and summer rainfall, but was upgraded to “drought” on Friday.

It follows an unusually dry September, with the ongoing lack of rainfall into the autumn months causing the flow in rivers and groundwater levels to reach very low levels in parts of Yorkshire, officials said.




The region joins all of central, southern and eastern England in drought status, while the North East and North West remain in prolonged dry weather.

Yorkshire Water said it is not planning to introduce water restrictions for customers because of extensive work to manage supplies, reduce leakage and move water around the region’s network.

Andy Shaw, the firm’s head of water production, treatment and energy, said: “Yorkshire has experienced an exceptionally hot and dry summer, and conditions have remained dry into September, with the region receiving just over half the normal long term average rainfall in September.

“The Environment Agency’s decision to move Yorkshire into drought reflects these conditions, but it does not mean water restrictions are currently needed.”

Mr Shaw said water demand remains “usually high for this time of year” so it is asking customers to continue using water wisely wherever possible.

“Small actions such as shorter showers, running full washing machine loads and checking for leaks at home can make a real difference,” he said.

Vicki Townend, area environment manager for the Environment Agency in Yorkshire, also urged people to be aware of the impacts of drought and use “a little less water” to speed up the area’s recovery.

She said Yorkshire has had the fifth driest April to September on record.

“This ongoing lack of rainfall means the flow in rivers and groundwater levels in certain parts of our area are very low and have now reached our trigger for declaring a drought,” she said.

“Even if we receive a few weeks of rain, we cannot quickly reverse the effects of the lack of rainfall during a very dry spring and summer.”

Ms Townend said the Environment Agency will continue to closely monitor the situation while implementing Yorkshire’s drought plan.

The ongoing pressure on water resources across the country comes after what has been provisionally declared the hottest summer on record.

The UK saw successive heatwaves as well as long spells of no rainfall in some areas, while autumn rain has so far been insufficient to offset many of the impacts.

More widely, the country is facing increasingly extreme weather such as heatwaves, droughts and intense rainfall because of climate change, which is driven mostly by burning fossil fuels for power, heating and transport.

The Environment Agency said more volatile conditions is leading to an increased likelihood of both drought and flooding, which can occur at the same time, especially if there is sudden intense rainfall when land is hard and dry.

“As we see more impacts from climate change, heavier rainfall and drier summers will become more frequent,” Ms Townend said.

The Government and Environment Agency said they continue to scrutinise water company actions, ensuring they are adhering to their legally-binding drought plans to conserve supplies, support communities, fix leaks quickly and reduce demand.

As part of its heightened incident response to the pressure on water resources across the country, the National Drought Group – which includes the Government, Met Office, regulators, water firms, farmers and conservation experts – is meeting weekly to co-ordinate action.
