"Normal weather” is a thing of the past, the Met Office’s chief meteorologist has warned.

Ian Lisk made the statement while sitting on a panel at the Airports UK annual conference, which set out to examine the potential impacts of extreme weather on the resilience of the UK’s infrastructure and aviation industry.

“We don’t get normal weather any more, fundamentally,” he told business leaders.

The meteorologist said weather that has typically been considered extreme was becoming more frequent and storms were also becoming more severe, which the Met Office is taking into account in testing worst-case scenarios.

He said people had seen the consequences of climate change this summer, and now were worrying about heatwaves and droughts affecting next year too.

![The Met Office said the UK was on course for its hottest summer on record this year, according to provisional data](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/08/28/09/f4758216a476e0f099aac67e8279ed5dY29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzg3OTkxNDA2-2.85620602.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

“None of this is good news,” he added.

“The heatwaves and the events of this last summer had nothing to do with El Nino.

“We don’t know yet, but we’re looking at a colder and drier winter which is bad because we need a lot of rain to make up for the existing deficit.”

The Met Office said the UK was on course for its hottest summer on record this year, according to provisional data, and that human-induced climate change was influencing temperatures.

El Nino occurs when sea-surface temperatures in parts of the tropical Pacific become warmer than average and can affect weather patterns in Europe.

![Historically, El Nino events have been associated with warmer, wetter and stormier conditions across the UK](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/09/08/12/b8d27324d2597294c7cf2ffd6c66e666Y29udGVudHNlYXJjaGFwaSwxNzg4OTUwODg5-2.85682343.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Historically, El Nino events have tended to be associated with warmer, wetter and stormier conditions across the UK and northern Europe during late autumn and early winter. But El Nino is one of several climate drivers that can influence the UK’s weather and so does not determine exactly what conditions the UK is likely to experience, the Met Office has previously said.

James Richardson, chief analyst at the Climate Change Committee, who was also on the panel, cautioned that the UK needed to do more to ensure its infrastructure was resilient to extreme weather.

“The three areas of weakness for the aviation industry are heat, flood and drought,” he said.

“We are a long way behind in terms of what is needed for adaptation as a country.

“The very hot summer we have just had has alerted people to what has been coming for a long time.

“It is going to get worse than this.”
