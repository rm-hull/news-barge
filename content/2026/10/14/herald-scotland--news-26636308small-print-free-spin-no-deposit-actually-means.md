You’ve signed up to an online casino, received 50 free spins and won £2.30. But for that money to actually end up in your account, you’ll first have to place bets totalling £23 to meet the 10x wagering requirements.

This is precisely the problem with the word ‘free’ – it says nothing about the conditions under which bonus winnings become money that can be withdrawn.

In this article, we’ll break down how no-deposit free spins actually work. The figures that determine the real value of such an offer are usually scattered across the casino’s terms and conditions page, the game’s information window and a separate promotions page. And these terms apply equally whether you’re playing in Glasgow, Aberdeen or London.

In the examples below, we’ll be using Slotozilla – a casino review and comparison platform where you can play free demo versions of slots, check the RTP of each game and compare bonus terms.

We’ll break down the five key points you should look out for before registering below.

### **Free doesn’t mean without conditions**

‘No deposit’ means just one thing: you don’t need to top up your account straight away to claim the bonus. But such an offer isn’t entirely free.

To activate free spins, you usually need to create a full account: provide your name, date of birth and home address. Next comes identity verification – for operators licensed in the UK, this is a mandatory part of the registration process. Some offers may ask you to add a payment card or other payment method, even if no money is debited from it. Separately, you may be asked to agree to receive marketing communications.

For the casino, the outcome is clear: it gains a verified adult customer with contact details, who is literally one step away from making their first deposit. Ultimately, the user receives free spins, but these will not necessarily translate into a real balance.

### **It’s not the number of spins that matters, but their value**

When offered bonus free spins, a user might easily be tempted by a large number in the advert, but it’s more important to look at the price per spin. One hundred spins at £0.01 give a nominal value of just £1. Ten spins at £1 – that’s already £10.

Therefore, the phrase ‘100 free spins’ on its own tells you almost nothing about the bonus’s actual value. It is the value of a single spin that you should look for in the Terms and Conditions before activating the offer.

### **Winnings from a bonus aren’t yet money you can withdraw**

The main condition after the free spins is the wagering requirement. Put simply, before you can withdraw your bonus winnings, you must wager them a certain number of times. In other words, a second multiplier is added to the cost per spin, and together they determine whether the bonus money will make it to your bank account.

For example, you’ve won £2.30, and the multiplier is 10x. This means you need to place bets totalling £23 before you can withdraw. And this is where the maths of the slot itself comes into play, which also depends on the casino.

Book of Dead, one of the most popular slots in bonus offers, comes in several versions: the base version, set by the developer, has an RTP of 96.21 percent, but the operator has the right to set a lower one – 94.25 per cent or below. This means you should check not only the wagering requirement but also the RTP of the version of the game you’ll be playing through. If it’s the 94.25 per cent version, then over the long term, for every £100 wagered, an average of £94.25 is returned, whilst the expected loss is £5.75.

You can find out which RTP version is used at a particular casino in the information window of the game itself. For example, on the UK page of Slotozilla, the Book of Dead demo states an RTP of 94.25 per cent.

### **The rules have changed**

The Gambling Commission’s rules apply across the UK – England, Scotland and Wales – whilst Northern Ireland is regulated separately. In the UK, strict wagering requirements have already been capped at regulator level.

The UK Gambling Commission has approved two key regulations: it has banned bonuses that combine different types of gambling, such as sports betting and casino games, and has set a maximum wagering requirement cap of 10x. Announced on 26 March 2025, they were originally due to come into force on 19 December of that year, but came into effect on 19 January 2026.

The regulator itself explained the issue with a simple example: a £10 bonus with a 50x wagering requirement meant you had to place £500 worth of bets before the funds became available for withdrawal. Under the new rules, the maximum turnover for such a bonus is £100.

Let’s return to our £2.30 win from the start of the article. Under the old hypothetical 40x wagering requirement, you would have had to wager £92, and with an RTP of 94.25%, the expected loss would have been around £5.29 – more than the win itself.

Under the current 10x cap, the wagering requirement drops to £23, and the expected loss to approximately £1.32. Of course, this does not mean a guaranteed loss of exactly that amount, but it clearly illustrates what the reform has changed.

Betting limits for online slots are also in place: £5 per spin for players aged 25 and over (from 9 April 2025) and £2 for players aged 18–24 (from 21 May).

### **The 10x cap has not removed the other conditions**

Capping the wagering requirement at 10x has solved only one part of the problem. The other bonus conditions remain, and they can affect the outcome just as much as the multiplier itself.

Take the validity period, for example. In real-world offers, this can range from a few days to around ten: if you fail to meet the conditions within this timeframe, the bonus and any associated winnings are forfeited.

There is also a maximum payout. A casino may set a cap at £20, £50, £100 or £150, so even a win of £150 does not guarantee that the full amount will be allowed to be withdrawn.

Furthermore, the offer is usually limited to one bonus per person, household or IP address. And before the first withdrawal, the casino may request documents to verify your identity, even if the account was created some time ago.

There are also rules regarding how bets contribute towards the wagering requirements. Slots usually contribute 100%, whilst table games often contribute only 10–20%. This means that the same 10x wagering requirement on roulette requires 5–10 times more turnover than on slots. Furthermore, the free spins themselves are usually tied to a specific slot, so your choice of game may also be limited.

These cards are handy for a preliminary comparison, but the casino’s own T&Cs always have the final say.

### **Five points to look out for and consider before claiming a bonus**

●     **The value of a single spin.** This is what indicates the actual size of the bonus.

●     **Wagering requirement**. How many times you need to wager your winnings: 10x, 5x or no wagering requirement at all – the upper limit is set by law, but the specific value varies for each offer.

●     **Validity period**. How long you have to meet the conditions.

●     **Maximum payout**. The maximum amount you’re allowed to withdraw.

●     **Eligible games**. Where you can use the spins and which bets count towards the wagering requirements.

It makes no sense to judge a no-deposit bonus solely by the number of free spins – it’s the set of conditions as a whole that matters. Transparency acts as an indicator here: an operator with nothing to hide will display the validity period, the maximum payout and the list of permitted games where they are visible before registration, rather than after.
