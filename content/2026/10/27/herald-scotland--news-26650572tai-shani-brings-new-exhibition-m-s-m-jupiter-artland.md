Two decades after applying to art college and being rejected so often she finally gave up, Tai Shani found herself on a stage in a vintage amusement park in Margate about to be awarded the 2019 Turner Prize on live television by then-Vogue editor, Edward Enniful.

Shani, necessarily self-taught by this point, had been shortlisted for Dark Continent: Semiramis, a sprawling, year long series of exhibitions and performance pieces inspired by a 15th century proto-feminist text which imagines a city run by and for women. It was co-commissioned by muscular art biennial Glasgow International, and exhibited at the city’s Tramway during the 2018 festival.

What happened next turned the Turner Prize award ceremony into a form of performance art in its own right: Enniful announced that, collectively, the four shortlisted artists had requested there be no overall winner and that instead the prize be shared between them. Grayson Perry made history in 2003 when he accepted the award in a dress with the words: “It’s about time a transvestite potter won the Turner Prize”.

A decade and a half on, this seemed like another of those iconic Turner Prize moments. “It really felt like the appropriate thing to do,” says Shani when we speak and I ask her why the decision was made. “We were all anti-supremacist in our own ways, in different forms and directed at different nodes of power structures. But ultimately there was a thread of anti-supremacism that ran through our work. In fact it wasn’t a thread, it was like the spine. And we felt that to have a winner was not in keeping with that.”

![](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21602956.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Turner Prize-winning artist Tai Shani *(Image: Neil Hanna)*

This autumn Scotland once again plays host to Shani’s ambitious, affecting, immersive and avowedly political art when a major new exhibition titled M.I.A.S.M.A. opens at Jupiter Artland. Co-commissioned with leading Swiss gallery Kunsthalle Bern, it’s the first such international venture for the impressively-appointed sculpture park and visual art complex near Edinburgh.

The acronym suggested by the title stands for Moral Injury in the Apocalyptic Sunsets of Modernity disAssociation. The work itself re-imagines the story of Antigone, daughter of Oedipus and heroine of a famous tragedy by Sophocles. In brief: following a civil war between her brothers in which both are killed and one hailed a hero by their usurper uncle, Antigone sets out to bury the brother branded a traitor in defiance of her uncle. Condemned to be sealed up in a cave as a result, she takes her own life – her final act of resistance, if you like.

Shani has used Sophocles’s original text to write her own version of some episodes from the story. These have been turned into a 12-part choral work by composer Aga Umja. The main exhibit in the installation, a large, crumbling wall with battlements and windows, then “sings” this piece of music as visitors pass along its length. There are also paintings, and a series of 12 sculptures representing the chorus. These are filled with objects associated with death, loss and grieving: a heart pierced by an arrow, flower petals, a deaths-head hawkmoth.

Shani first encountered the story of Antigone as a teenager, while studying French playwright Jean Anouilh’s experimental 1944 adaptation. Premiered in Paris during the Nazi occupation, it escaped the censor by being heavily veiled and deliberately ambiguous, though few who saw it could miss the parallels between Antigone and the members of the French resistance.

“I think it was one of the most experimental things I had read at that time,” Shani says of that crucial encounter with Anouilh’s play and with Antigone herself. “But also as a young rebellious person I was very taken by seeing this rebellion and this disobedience in her. It also had a structure around it. It wasn’t like she was a rebel without a cause. It had a very deep cause.”

Shani has broached the story before, in a work made in 2011. But back then she feels she missed the wider political point and didn’t fully grasp the significance of Antigone’s actions.

“I thought it was just a personal rebellion. I wasn’t yet educated enough in terms of understanding how power works to really be able to place her disobedience and her rebellion in a structure that was coherent. It was just something I was attracted to. But over the last few years I felt that, with the rise of the right and fascism and various huge shifts in terms of how we understand the world, there’s been a real understanding of how power is structured and how geopolitical power is structured.”

With M.I.A.S.M.A., she says, “I was really thinking also about the response to trans people, to the genocide in Palestine, to ICE [the controversial US immigration service]. I was thinking about this general authoritarian turn – and ultimately the story of Antigone is a story of resistance. And beyond resistance, it’s about being fundamentally committed to truth and to justice. That’s what her position is. It’s a refusal of state law because what the state is asking of her goes against the most fundamental morality, which is burying the dead, basically.”

Today, then, Antigone feels like “a very relevant character”, her story an urgent illustration of what it means to “take a position that you know is real and true, even when the state is saying the opposite.”

Also on display at Jupiter Artland is Shani’s large outdoor work The Spell Or The Dream, which has been wowing audiences since its arrival last month following its exhibition at London’s Somerset House. Sited in Jupiter Artland’s orchard, it features a reclining blue figure encased in a glass tomb set on a stepped plinth. The sleeping figure breathes softly while a specially composed soundscape plays. There’s also a radio station running alongside it, a 24 hour broadcast featuring music, archive recordings and specially curated audio in which invited participants such as Brian Eno and economist Yanis Varoufakis muse on a variety of imagined futures.

As ever with this artist’s work, there’s a lot going on and much to unpack.

But if Tai Shani’s journey into the upper echelons of the art world has been decidedly non-traditional, it’s entirely in keeping with virtually everything else in her life. She had, by any reckoning, an eventful childhood. Or, as she frames it: “A very, very unconventional upbringing, to say the least.”

Her parents were members of the 3rd Eye Group, a radical underground movement in Israeli in the late 1960s and early 1970s which often fell foul of the authorities. She describes her parents as “die-hard hippies” who ultimately chose to leave the country, meaning Shani was born in London.

When her parents were caught up in the fallout from Operation Julie, a mid-1970s police operation targetting the production of LSD in the UK, they took their infant daughter and re-located to Amsterdam, where other key former members of the 3rd Eye Group also lived. “They were on the run from police and they went to Amsterdam and they were in a cafe and they met these people who also had a baby who said: ‘We’re going to Goa.’ And they were like: ‘We’re coming too.’ Which is how Shani spent the first decade of her life living in a commune in Goa.

She “dipped in and out of school” there, she says, but aged 10 or so her parents realised she was fuctionally illiterate and decided to act.

“As much as they wanted me to have a very experimental childhood, I think they wanted me to have choices as well about what my life would be. I think they realized that not being able to read or write would be a huge impediment to that.” She chuckles wryly at that. “So we moved to Spain for a year, as a kind of test, and then we moved to another commune in Brussels, where I did all my schooling.”

It was here she encountered Antigone for the first time, though the experience overall was less than nourishing. “It made me very uneasy around authority,” she recalls. “And I have to say, when I was suddenly in this very Western context of being told what to do all the time, it drove me nuts. I really struggled.”

Nor does she feel the lack of an early years education to have been a particular impediment.

“Obviously not going to school for that long does allow your imagination to become a very fundamental part of who you are and how you see the world,” she says.

Unconventional it may have been, but that childhood helped make her the artist she is today – one whose very particular and unusual vision is a boon to those who encounter it.
