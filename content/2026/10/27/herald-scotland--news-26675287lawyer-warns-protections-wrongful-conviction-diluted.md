A leading solicitor has raised fears that there are fewer safeguards in the Scottish justice system to protect against against wrongful convictions in criminal trials.

Douglas McConnell, of Duncan and McConnell solicitors, said he had watched the justice system evolve significantly over the last 20 years and while many changes were positive, the system had become "less attentive to the safeguards designed to protect against wrongful conviction".

"Victims and vulnerable witnesses are treated with greater sensitivity than in the past. Technology has improved the way evidence is captured and presented. Few would argue against progress where it makes the justice system more trauma-aware and effective," he wrote in an article in The Scotsman on Tuesday.

"Yet many practitioners, both defence and prosecution, are increasingly concerned about a different question: whether, in our determination to modernise criminal justice, we have become less attentive to the safeguards designed to protect against wrongful conviction."

He added: "That concern is not rooted in nostalgia. Nor is it an argument against supporting vulnerable witnesses. A fair justice system must do both. Rather, it reflects an unease that a series of reforms, judicial decisions and evidential developments may have altered the balance of criminal trials.

"Historically, Scots law recognised the dangers inherent in criminal accusations. People can lie. Equally, they can be sincere but mistaken. Memory is imperfect. Perception can be flawed. The law therefore developed safeguards designed to minimise the risk of error before the state imposed criminal punishment."

Mr McConnell's intervention comes after the Dean of the Faculty of Advocates Roddy Dunlop KC argued that an historic rule ensuring evidence in a criminal trial had to be corroborated by more than one source before someone can be convicted is "dead" when it comes to people on trial for rape or serious sexual offences.

It also follows a landmark judgment last year by the UK's Supreme Court that legal provisions to protect complainers from intrusive questioning over their character and sexual history had been interpreted in a way which was "too restrictive" and was putting at risk the accused right to a fair trial, enshrined in Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights.

On corroboration, Mr McConnell said the safeguard had been "steadily diluted".

He stated: "The most important of those safeguards was corroboration. For generations, an allegation alone was not enough. Independent evidence was required before a person could be convicted of a crime.

"Technically, corroboration survives. In practice, however, many criminal practitioners would struggle to recognise it in its modern form. Through judicial developments, statutory exceptions and evidential workarounds, the protection that once distinguished Scots criminal law has been steadily diluted.

He added that there had "been a gradual movement away from the traditional emphasis on testing evidence in open court" with increasing use of recorded statements, pre-recorded evidence and other forms of prior testimony often serves important purposes.

He went on: "Recently, I was involved in a case where the complainer did not attend trial, yet the prosecution proceeded on the basis of evidence gathered earlier in the process. The defence position was straightforward: the allegation was false.

"Ordinarily, such a claim would be examined through direct questioning and cross-examination of the complainer. That opportunity was absent."

He added: "The first obligation of criminal justice is not to convict the guilty. It is to avoid convicting the innocent. Everything else follows from that principle. Safeguards exist not to frustrate justice but to ensure it."

The lawyer said he was not suggesting that miscarriages of justice were commonplace but added that "reform should never be judged solely by the problems it seeks to solve".

He added: "If securing more convictions comes at the cost of weakening protections against wrongful convictions, we have not improved the system. We have merely changed the type of injustice it risks producing.

"A system that occasionally fails to convict the guilty is regrettable. A system that convicts the innocent is intolerable.

"For much of my career, I regarded the conviction of an innocent person as an exceptionally remote possibility. Today, I am less certain than I once was."

A Scottish Government spokesperson said: “Everyone has a right to a fair trial. There are well-established rules on what evidence can be led in trials, and clear routes to challenge how these are applied.

"The need for corroboration has not been removed. The fundamental requirements of fairness in proceedings for accused persons remain unchanged and guilt must still be proved beyond reasonable doubt and by corroborated evidence.”
