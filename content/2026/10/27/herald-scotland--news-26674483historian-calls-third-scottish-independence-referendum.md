One of Scotland's leading historians has argued that any future independence vote should include a built-in confirmatory referendum to avoid repeating the "shambles" of the 2016 Brexit vote.

Writing in The Herald, Professor Colin Kidd, who is a member of the advisory council of the pro-union thinktank These Islands, says that unionists “can’t keep on saying no indefinitely to a second Scottish independence referendum”.

But the Bishop Wardlaw Professor of Modern History at the University of St Andrews says there should be a further ballot, either once independence negotiations conclude or ten years after independence, to let voters judge the result.

The call comes as the SNP ramps up the push for a new vote.

First Minister John Swinney told party members last week that the referendum campaign “has already begun”.

Meanwhile Prime Minister Andy Burnham used a speech in Motherwell to urge politicians to "move beyond the constitutional question".

Prof Kidd said Brexit negotiations had resulted in a deal that looked "very different from what politicians promised in 2016" and that ten years on, polling suggests voters "now have buyers' remorse".

"All of these factors apply equally to Scottish independence," he said.

He also warned that the economic realities would be harsh. Taking on a portion of the UK's three trillion pound national debt would be "eye-watering" for an independent Scotland.

Pointing to the market chaos caused by Liz Truss's 2022 mini-budget, he argued that global financial markets would "only tolerate divergence from fiscal norms within very narrow parameters".

"What scope for manoeuvre then for a newly independent Scotland with no credit record?

"Financial markets would impose strict discipline on this fledgling state. Dreams of Scotland's independent social democratic future would be tightly constrained — if not curtailed — by the markets.

"To avoid the same kind of buffeting that the short-lived Truss government experienced, an independent Scotland would almost certainly need to run a Thatcherite economic policy."

Prof Kidd said this is not what the SNP wants "nor is it what the Scottish people would be voting for in a second independence referendum".

"In order to deal with the problem of buyers' remorse, it would be sensible in any future deal between the UK and Scottish governments for a second referendum, to build in an insurance policy: that ten years after the referendum, there is a third referendum — binding equally on the Scottish government and on the UK — asking the Scottish people whether they would prefer to remain independent or to rejoin the UK.

"We know what the answer to a similar question would be today ten years after Brexit."

![John Swinney said he believes the case for independence has moved forward](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21566199.jpg?type=mds-article-620) *(Image: PA)*

Pointing to last week's referendum in the Canadian province of Alberta, which asked residents whether they wanted a vote on independence and received a "decisive negative", Prof Kidd suggests a similar approach in Scotland might produce a different answer.

Responding to the column, an SNP spokesperson said: "From Andy Burnham down, there is a belated acknowledgment that you cannot stand in the way of Scottish democracy, but there is no need to pontificate or overcomplicate what is a simple matter.

"As the British Prime Minister understands and has made clear it is for the Scotland people to decide our own future.

"Last week the UK Chancellor admitted Scotland should have an oil fund worth £1 trillion, recent GDP figures show Scotland outperformed the UK as a whole, Scotland has the highest levels of foreign direct investment outside of London and two global credit rating agencies have given Scotland a high investment grade matching the UK and higher than some other G7 economies.

"That's before you consider the vast resources present in our wind, water and seabed - the opportunity that lies before us is immense.

"Poll after poll shows independence is the preferred choice of the Scottish people because we have the chance to use those vast resources to build an even wealthier, fairer, Scotland back home in the EU."

Vic Currie, Reform's Shadow Minister for Constitution, Culture and External Affairs, said: "Forget a second independence referendum, Professor Colin Kidd is already pitching the third.

"A third referendum to confirm an independence deal raises an obvious question: how many votes would it take before the matter is settled?

"Andy Burnham's revisiting of the Brexit question has allowed the SNP and their supporters to get carried away by similar fantasies of overruling basic democratic principles to fit their political agenda.

"Taken to its conclusion, Kidd's argument essentially undermines representative democracy; if every detail of independence legislation warrants a vote from a sceptical public, SNP MSPs are clearly not up for the job."

Scottish Tory Deputy leader Rachael Hamilton said: “The last thing Scotland needs is more constitutional navel gazing and the political agenda being dominated by calls for further divisive referendums.”

Gordon MacIntyre-Kemp, founder of the pro-independence Believe in Scotland and chief executive of Business for Scotland, said Prof Kidd's claims were "highly academic and don't hold water in the real world".

"His confirmatory referendum sounds attractive in purely academic terms.

"In the real world it would simply give London an incentive to negotiate in bad faith and try to force terms Scottish voters cannot stomach, which it would then try to exploit to overturn their decision in the confirmatory referendum.

"A referendum that gives both governments a clear mandate to deliver independence gives both a reason to find common ground.

"Offering Westminster a second chance to defeat it changes the entire negotiation and would also make the entire campaign wholly negative and combative in nature."

Mr MacIntyre-Kemp also pushed back on Prof Kidd's debt argument.

"The UK Treasury's own independence paper says Scotland's debt share and repayment terms would be negotiated. Assets also belong in that negotiation alongside liabilities and Kidd has counted the debts while ignoring the assets that Scotland helped build. A matching share of reserves, military equipment and bases, embassies around the world, etc, would all be up for negotiation."

He added: "The silliest thing about Prof Kidd's statement is his warning of constitutional uncertainty, but then he proposes keeping Scotland on constitutional probation for another decade. His suggested confirmatory referendum would manufacture the very instability he claims to fear.

"Scotland can build a wellbeing-based economy that serves its people, communities and environment and independence gives us the power to make those better choices. A Scotland that recognises that you cannot have a strong economy without a strong society and vice versa. Once we cast off the UK's neoliberal economic straitjacket, Scotland's economy will soar."

Alastair Cameron, chair of Scotland in Union, said the proposal did not address the problems facing voters.

"Scots are fed up of constitutional wrangling, as repeated polling has shown.

"They want their politicians to focus on the here and now, concentrating on key matters like health, education and other public services.

"While proposing different constitutional scenarios makes for an interesting academic exercise, it doesn't fix the problems Scots face today.

"In a global cost-of-living crisis and with such uncertainty and instability, the last thing Scots need is to be plunged back into the divisive and toxic days of 2014."

Polling published ahead of the SNP conference suggests Scotland is still split down the middle on independence.

A Survation poll commissioned by the SNP found 52% in favour of independence, and a separate Survation poll for the Forum for Research and Energy projected a 54% to 46% Yes vote once don't-knows were removed.

A YouGov poll found 53% would vote No, while More in Common put support for remaining in the UK at 52%.

Mr Burnham is due to meet EU leaders in Brussels on 20 November to sign agreements on food and drink, youth mobility and emissions trading.

He has called for an “honest discussion” about a return to freedom of movement, despite fighting the Makerfield by-election on a promise not to “re-run” Brexit arguments.

Asked in Motherwell on 16 October whether he would rule out a second independence referendum for the duration of his premiership, Mr Burnham declined to do so, saying only that he believed “unions of countries are better”.

However, Downing Street sources then told the Telegraph that Mr Burnham had ruled out a vote for as long as he was in No 10.
