An inconclusive result would leave Israel in political limbo after the country’s first national election since the October attack in 2023. The vote served as a referendum on Netanyahu’s wartime leadership.

Exit polls released by major Israeli TV stations suggest that neither Netanyahu’s political bloc nor that of his main rival, former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, secured the 61 seats needed for a parliamentary majority.

Those polls indicated that Netanyahu's bloc won 50 seats, the opposition won 52-53 and Arab parties won 17-18. The strong showing by Arab-led parties could allow them to decide the next prime minister, but both blocs have until now refused to partner with them.

Netanyahu had been tipped for a stunning defeat in today’s legislative elections despite receiving a glowing endorsement from Donald Trump.

Before polls opened, the US president reiterated his “complete and total” support for Netanyahu, writing that he had been “strong, brave and brilliant in the face of tremendous opposition”.

## Exit polls show no clear winner in Israeli elections. What happens next?

Israel’s election has ended in a deadlock as no party is on track for majority of the Knesset, exit polls have suggested.

The stalemate comes after three years of war waged in Gaza, Lebanon and Iran, which have plunged the Middle East into turmoil.

The election is widely seen as a referendum on Benjamin Netanyahu, who is seeking another term after a combined 19 years in power.

## Arab turnout has given their parties outsized influence

Benjamin Netanyahu had warned earlier that Arab parties that could make or break the election, saying they were receiving “truly massive support," and heading to the polls in "unprecedented numbers.”

In another sign of concern, Netanyahu’s right-wing Likud party petitioned to extend voting hours past 10 p.m., but the election commission ruled against it, saying there was no justification.

Eisenkot had rejected the proposed extension and called on voters to ignore Netanyahu's “scare tactics.”

Israel's Arab-led parties could emerge as kingmakers — if other parties agree to join forces with them.

“Our vote will change the government,” said Mansour Abbas, head of the Islamist Ra’am party, while voting was still underway.

Arabs in Israel make up about 20% of the population.

Israel’s far-right national security minister, Itamar Ben-Gvir, known for taunting Palestinian detainees and saying some Palestinians in Gaza are “not even people,” warned that “it will be Mansour Abbas’ government” if Netanyahu and his allies don’t win.

## 'Don't let anyone confuse you, this is a crushing defeat'- Yair Lapid

Current opposition head Yair Lapid said in a statement: “Don’t let anyone confuse you: the Netanyahu bloc has suffered a crushing defeat. This is a total collapse and the public has told them to go home.”

## Highest turnout in decades

More than 74% of eligible voters had cast ballots by 8 pm, the highest turnout at that time in decades, and eight percentage points higher than in the last elections in 2022, according to the Central Elections Committee.

Over 7 million people are eligible to vote, and tens of thousands have reportedly arrived from overseas.

Israel’s president will be tasked with asking the party leader with the best prospect of forming a government, usually one with the most seats, to cobble together a coalition.

If none can, new elections could be held early next year.

Israel held five elections between April 2019 and November 2022 because of a similar stalemate.

## Netanyahu challenger Eisenkot says exit polls signal "lack of confidence" in Netanyahu

Gadi Eisenkot, leader of the opposition bloc’s Yashar party, said the projections indicate “a total lack of confidence in Netanyahu and the ‘October 7th government’”.

## Lack of majority could work in Netanyahu's favour

The stalemate in today’s election could work in Benjamin Netanyahu's favour, allowing the prime minister to remain in power in a caretaker capacity while parties try to forge a governing coalition.

Final results could take days, and Israel's fragmented political system leaves smaller parties potential kingmakers.

No single party has ever won 61 seats outright in the 120-seat Knesset, making coalitions the norm.
