# Andy Burnham's £13,036 DWP state pension plan to be confirmed on Wednesday

## New state pensioners are set to see their DWP state pension rise to £13,036 next April under the triple lock

New state pensioners will witness their Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) state pension climb to £13,036 next April courtesy of the triple lock - which Andy Burnham has pledged to maintain until 2030. The adjustment means anyone receiving a full basic state pension would get £192.10 per week, up from £184.90, totalling approximately £9,989 annually.

That represents a substantial £3,047 difference. Your DWP State Pension becomes available when you reach State Pension age, which is no longer determined by gender.

The government rolled out reforms in 2016 that streamlined the State Pension system. Consequently, there are now two frameworks operating - the new State Pension and the basic State Pension. Which one you'll be entitled to claim hinges on when you reach your State Pension age, reports Birmingham Live.

The new State Pension is available if you're a woman born on or after 6 April 1953 or a man born on or after 6 April 1951. The basic State Pension applies if you're a woman born before 6 April 1953 or a man born before 6 April 1951.

You have the option to postpone (defer) your State Pension. Upon claiming it later, you'll still receive it under whichever system your age qualifies you for. You are eligible for the basic State Pension if you reached State Pension age prior to 6 April 2016. This applies to women born before 6 April 1953 or men born before 6 April 1951.

To receive the full basic State Pension, you'll require 30 or more qualifying years of National Insurance (NI) contributions. Should you fall short of this, your basic Pension rate will be reduced accordingly.

The calculation works out at 1/30th for each qualifying year accrued. For instance, with 20 qualifying years of NI contributions, you would receive 20/30ths of the full weekly pension amount: £184.90 ÷ 30 x 20 = £123.26.

As a general rule, you'll need a minimum of one qualifying year of NI contributions to receive any basic State Pension whatsoever. The new State Pension is available to those reaching State Pension age on or after 6 April 2016 - namely women born on or after 6 April 1953 or men born on or after 6 April 1951.

A full new State Pension requires 35 or more qualifying years of NI contributions. Those with between 10 and 35 qualifying years will receive a proportion of the full rate, calculated at 1/35th per qualifying year.

As an illustration, 20 qualifying years would yield 20/35ths of the full rate: £241.30 ÷ 35 x 20 = £137.88. Fewer than 10 qualifying years will typically result in no State Pension entitlement.

Under the new State Pension rules, Additional State Pension is no longer available. Furthermore, claiming on a spouse or civil partner's NI contributions is not permitted, though certain exceptions exist for those who are widowed or divorced.

If you opted out of Additional State Pension while working – for instance, by contributing to specific workplace pension schemes instead – you'll receive a lower amount under the new State Pension.
