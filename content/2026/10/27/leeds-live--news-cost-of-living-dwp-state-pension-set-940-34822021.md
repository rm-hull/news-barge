# DWP state pension set for £9.40 weekly rise as triple lock boost confirmed

## The DWP is set to increase State Pension rates from April 2027 under the triple lock

State pensioners under the age of 77 are set to receive a weekly payment increase of up to £9.40 from the Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) come April next year.

This uplift stems from the annual State Pension adjustment at the beginning of the new tax year, governed by the triple lock mechanism. The triple lock is a UK Government pledge ensuring the State Pension increases annually by whichever is greatest among three benchmarks: the consumer price index (CPI) inflation rate (recorded in September of the preceding year), average earnings growth between May and July of the previous year, or a baseline of 2.5%.

While Prime Minister Andy Burnham has announced intentions to scrap the triple lock in 2030 by severing the earnings link, the framework will stay intact throughout the remainder of this Parliament. Consequently, State Pension amounts are projected to climb by 3.9% from April 2027, tracking average wage growth as the leading triple lock measure, mirroring last year's pattern.

With this 3.9% enhancement on the horizon, the new State Pension would increase from £241.30 weekly to £250.70, delivering pensioners eligible for the full sum an additional £9.40 each week during the 2027 to 2028 tax year. Over the course of a year, this translates to an annual boost of £488.80 for those receiving the full entitlement, reports the Express.

The increase applies to newer state pensioners who hit State Pension age from April 6, 2016 onwards, when the qualifying age stood between 63 and 65. It subsequently climbed to 66, meaning all new state pensioners are now younger than 77.

Meanwhile, older pensioners receiving the previous basic State Pension would see a 3.9% rise, pushing weekly rates to £192.10 from £184.90, delivering a weekly uplift of £7.20, or £374.40 extra per year for those on the full amount.

You qualify for the basic State Pension if you're a man born before April 6, 1951, or a woman born before April 6, 1953, though your payment depends on your National Insurance contributions. These calculations reflect the maximum available to individuals with a complete qualifying National Insurance record, meaning those with insufficient qualifying years will receive reduced amounts.

Ed Monk, associate director at Fidelity International, explained: "The State Pension in the current 2026/27 financial year is set at £241.30 a week, or £12,548 a year, for those claiming the full new State Pension. That follows a 4.8% rise from the year before based on the increase in wages, which was the highest of the three measures.

"And we now know the rise that will apply in the 2027-28 tax year. In April 2027 the State Pension will rise to £250.70 a week - £13,036.40 a year - following a 3.9% rise in wages that was confirmed in labour market data published this month. Consider that as recently as the 2022/23 tax year it was just £185.15 a week - meaning that by next year it will have risen by more than 35% in five years."

Under Mr Burnham's proposals, the triple lock is set to be scrapped in 2030, replaced by a suggested 'double lock' mechanism, whereby State Pension rates would increase in line with either CPI inflation or a minimum of 2.5%.

The PM stated this would produce "significant savings" by severing the link to average earnings, with the funds released earmarked to help establish a new National Care Service.

Mr Burnham maintained that the State Pension "will hold its value relative to earnings over time so that pensioners will always share in the rising prosperity of the nation".

Responding to Mr Burnham's announcement, Kate Smith, head of pensions at Aegon, commented: "For millions of people, the State Pension is the bedrock of retirement income and will continue to be so.

"Increases in earnings will still have a role to play, so pensioners are able to share in the relative prosperity and won't lose out if UK earnings significantly outperform price increases. However, it's unclear how this will work in practice... We await the detail."
