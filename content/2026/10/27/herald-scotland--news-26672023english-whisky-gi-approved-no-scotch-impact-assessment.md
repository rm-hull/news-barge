The UK Government did not carry out an impact assessment on Scotch whisky before granting protected status to English whisky.

The Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs told The Herald there was no legal basis for one, because the English Whisky application was a regulatory decision made against the legal criteria for registration, not a policy decision.

Graham Leadbitter, the MP for Moray West, Nairn and Strathspey, whose constituency contains around a third of Scotland's distilleries, said the decision was "absolutely astonishing".

The row centres on how English single malt is defined.

Scotch single malt must be mashed, fermented and distilled at one distillery. Under the English rules, a whisky can be sold as single malt provided it is distilled at a single site, with other stages allowed to take place elsewhere.

The Scotch Whisky Association (SWA) has appealed the decision to the First-tier Tribunal.

Mr Leadbitter had asked the Environment Secretary whether they had made an "impact assessment of the decision to agree to the English Whisky Guild application for a Geographical Indication for English Whisky on the Scotch Whisky sector and the Single Malt Scotch Whisky category".

![Graham Leadbitter ©House of Commons](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21633991.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Graham Leadbitter said the decision was astonishing *(Image: ©House of Commons)*

Stephen Morgan, a junior Defra minister, answered without addressing the question directly. He said: "This Government supports whisky producers across the UK. English Whisky joins Scotch, Welsh and Irish whiskies as recognised and protected products. Scotch Whisky's protected status and strong reputation are unchanged, and this Government is confident that the Scotch Whisky industry will continue to thrive."

Mr Leadbitter said the omission was extraordinary. He said: "The fact the Labour Party did not even consider the impact on Scotch Whisky is absolutely astonishing. It's no wonder the Scotch Whisky Association has lodged an appeal.

"Scotch whisky is our premier product, and it is shameful that the Labour UK Government is trying to dilute that status by giving English whisky the same legal protections.

"Our whisky trade has been targeted by the Labour Party with unique tax hikes, sky-high energy costs and now this — our vital and historic industries are just an afterthought to Westminster.

"The First Minister took the case for our industry right to the heart of the White House and we secured an end to the crippling tariffs — that's what standing up for Scotland looks like.

"This decision must be reversed and the whisky tax cut, but this whole sorry episode makes one thing very clear — through a fresh start with independence we will no longer rely on Westminster governments who could not care less about Scottish industry."

Speaking to the Scottish Affairs Committee last week, Graeme Littlejohn, director of strategy at the SWA, said Scotch had effectively "built" the category of single malt in the 1960s and 1970s.

He said the new rules would damage Scotch's reputation while giving English whisky a competitive advantage.

"The equity which is now within that category has really been derived from Scotch whisky producers putting in that effort over the last few decades," he told MPs.

"Essentially, 'single malt Scotch whisky' means that malted barley is brought into a distillery, it is mash fermented and distilled on that single site, and that's well understood by consumers around the world.

"The difference with the now approved English whisky GI, which we are currently appealing through the First-tier Tribunal, is that you wouldn't have to mash and ferment on that single site, you could only distil on that site, and it would still be called single malt English whisky.

"So the practical impact of that is that you could have wash brought in from a brewery anywhere in the country, distilled, for example, in Cornwall, and you could call it Cornish Single Malt."

Mr Littlejohn said the SWA believed the connection to a single site was "vital".

"That, for me, is the very definition of watering down the definition, and that is unfortunately where we are and why we're appealing the decision," he added.

He told the committee the SWA had backed an English whisky GI from the outset and had never wanted to end up in a legal dispute.

![A Scottish whisky giant has reported a drop in profit and revenue](https://www.heraldscotland.com/resources/images/21525248.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The SWA are appealing the decision *(Image: Getty Images)*

It raised concerns about the single malt definition throughout the process, he said, and offered a compromise during the opposition period that began in February 2025.

Under it, multi-site distilleries would have sold their product as English malt whisky rather than English single malt, with a transition of three to five years for any that wanted to bring production onto one site. The English Whisky Guild rejected that, he said.

Because the case is now in the tribunal, Mr Littlejohn said, the SWA is challenging the whole GI rather than just the single malt definition. He described that as a consequence of the appeal process and not where the association wanted to be.

He said most English producers do work on a single site and have every right to call their whisky English single malt, citing distilleries in the Cotswolds and Norfolk. His objection was to letting multi-site producers, and even products that do not comply with the GI, use the same term.

Mr Littlejohn said the UK Government had told the Commons in March 2025 that it would not water down the single malt definition. However, he said consumers could now find three different products on the shelf called single malt.
