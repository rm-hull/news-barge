The Scottish Government has been urged to set its new private jet tax 'sky high', as new analysis reveals hundreds flew in and out of the country while a wildfire burned in the Cairngorms.

A new Air Departure Tax, which will replace the Air Passenger Duty, will come in on April 1, 2027 and initially be matched to the existing UK rate.

However, from April 2028 there will be higher tax rates specifically for private jets, with the level not yet decided upon and set to be announced in the next draft budget on December 3.

Oxfam is calling for the rate to be set at a 'sky high' 10 times the current Higher Rate within Air Passenger Duty which by 2027 will range from £146.63 for domestic flights to £1,178.20 for Band C which applies to all destinations where the capital city is more than 5,500 miles from London unless specifically named in other bands.

The charity has analysed data from the flight records from the Private Jet Tracking Initiative and found 7,237 private jet flights arrived in or departed from Scotland between January and September 2026.

Between July 15 and August 15, when the wildfire was raging in the Cairngorms, 1,101 private jet flights arrived in or departed from Scotland.

Together, those flights generated an estimated 7,830 tonnes of CO₂e, with each flight on average producing far more carbon pollution than someone in the poorest half of the UK emits in an entire year.

Over the course of the year up to September, 216 aircraft accounted for over half of all private jet emissions despite being just 17% of the total private aircraft (1,246).

Many flights lasted less than an hour and many travelled fewer than 500 miles, despite rail alternatives existing for numerous routes. The top destinations and origins for private jet flights were Farnborough, Luton and London.

Katherine May, head of Oxfam Scotland, said: "While firefighters and Cairngorms communities battled wildfires on the ground, private jets were pumping out white trails of pollution across the skies above them.

"The ultra-wealthy are using private jets like airborne taxis, with Scotland’s skies treated like a private motorway. Meanwhile everyone on the ground is left to deal with the increasing threat of fires, floods and rising bills, which their excess emissions help to drive.

“Ministers should use the Scottish Budget to set Scotland's new private jet tax sky high and send a clear message: private jet passengers shouldn't be able to pollute on the cheap while the rest of us pick up the bill."

The Scottish findings are published alongside Oxfam's new global report *Disaster Divide*, released ahead of COP31, which finds that climate breakdown is increasingly being driven by extreme wealth while the consequences fall hardest on those least responsible.

The report warns that while ordinary people face destroyed homes, lost livelihoods and rising living costs as climate disasters become more frequent, the ultra-rich are often able to shield themselves from the worst impacts through private insurance, second homes and access to safer locations.

Globally, Oxfam found that the richest 1 per cent are responsible for 20 per cent of global warming since 1990, generating around 100 times more emissions per person than someone in the poorest half of humanity.

Using climate attribution modelling, Oxfam estimates that just one year of emissions from the world's richest 1 per cent will cause 1.9 million excess heat-related deaths over the next century.

More than three-quarters (78 per cent) of the deaths are expected to occur in low- and lower-middle-income countries, where communities are on the front line of climate impacts despite having contributed least to the crisis and often having the fewest resources to cope with its effects.

The report also finds that a person in the richest 0.1 per cent has contributed nearly 400 times more to today's extreme heatwaves than someone in the poorest half of humanity.

Ms May said: "The climate crisis is often talked about as if we're all in the same boat but from Scotland to Sub-Saharan Africa, people are not experiencing it equally. The people who have done least to cause the climate emergency are losing homes, livelihoods and lives to floods, droughts, heatwaves and wildfires, while others can retreat to second homes, hire private firefighters, or simply buy their way out of danger.

"The climate crisis is an inequality crisis and that's why climate justice means making the biggest polluters pay. Whether it's private jets or oil and gas giants, those profiting from pollution should contribute far more to tackling the damage it causes."
