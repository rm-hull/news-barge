Australia Post chief paid ‘mind-blowing’ $1.2m bonus

Josh Taylor

The outgoing chief executive of Australia Post, Paul Graham, was paid a $1.2m bonus on top of his $1.64m base salary in the last financial year, after the $139.4m sale of property owned by Australia Post pushed the organisation into a profit for the year.

Graham, who announced this month he will retire after five years at the helm in June next year, confirmed to Senate estimates on Tuesday night he had been paid the bonus based on two performance components, with one component related to the “property strategy we had to execute”.

He said the strategy was critical to Australia Post’s transformation:

We do have the great fortune of sitting on property that has been in the network for some time, and it was important for us to get an articulated, clear strategy … releasing those non-core properties from our portfolio and ensuring that they provided the cashflow that not only got us back into a profitable position, but enabled us to make the substantial investments we’re making as part of our transformation.

Liberal senator Sarah Henderson told Graham it was a “mind-blowing amount of money”.

Australia Post reported a profit before tax of $31.8m, but had the property sales been excluded, Australia Post would have reported a loss before tax of $107.6m, as the letter volumes fell a further 14.7% in the 2026 financial year.

Man arrested after making false claims about Bondi terror attack injuries

A man who allegedly made false claims about getting injured in the Bondi Beach terror attack has been charged.

The 46-year-old is accused of making a false statement about getting injured during the mass shooting and making fraudulent claims for compensation after the event.

NSW Police have charged the man with publishing false misleading material to obtain advantage and false representation resulting in police investigation after he was arrested on Tuesday morning.

He has been granted conditional bail and is set to appear at Parramatta local court today.

At a brief appearance late last night, the Australian federal police commissioner, Krissy Barrett, revealed the force had received 785 protective security reports regarding adverse content against federal parliamentarians in the first six months of this year.

Barrett said women politicians were three times more likely to report receiving offensive or threatening contact that was sexualised or gender-based in nature while those from culturally and linguistically diverse communities were also more likely to experience “frequent and varied offensive contact”.

Online hate is spilling into the real world, AFP warns

AFP Commissioner KrissyBarrett began her late-night appearance at the Senate estimates hearing noting “a heaviness that hangs across Australia” after the alleged attack on independent MP HelenHaines.

She said it has aligned with a global trend of violence emerging with little or no warning from individuals largely unknown to authorities.

Lone actors are being motivated by a convergence of personal grievances, mental health vulnerabilities and ideological concerns …

What helped keep us safe in recent times, such as our geography, analogue systems and far away criminals, has been impacted by fully integrated technology, social media and artificial intelligence.

Barrett told the Senate estimates hearing on Tuesday night technology has enabled an “instant pipeline of hate” delivered to people’s homes, including teenagers, and workplaces because of the ubiquity of devices.

Racism going undercounted in Australia, report finds

Data on racism is inconsistent across jurisdictions and leading to people’s experience of discrimination being severely misrepresented, a report by the Australian Human Rights Commission has found.

The recommendation to develop a national anti-racism data plan was contained in the commission’s national anti-racism framework released in 2024, but to which the Albanese government is yet to respond.

The report also recommends strengthening the monitoring and evaluation of policies and programs designed to eliminate racism.

The race discrimination commissioner, GiridharanSivaraman, said:

We hear about the devastating impacts of racism from communities every day. But many experiences go unreported or uncounted, and our systems often do not capture the full picture.

This is not about collecting more data to prove racism exists. We already know it does. It is about making inequality visible, holding institutions to account and giving communities greater agency over their data to drive real change.

Australia pays biggest bill for global diesel crisis

Australia has paid more than twice as much as any other nation in additional diesel import costs during the global fuel crisis, with a report putting the toll at $9bn over six months.

The added cost equates to $324 for every Australian, exceeding the bills incurred by Brazil, South Africa, France and the UK.

The Climate Council released the figures from its energy security scorecard on Wednesday, which also found Australia paid an extra $1.6bn for petrol imports.

The findings come days after oil prices reached a six-month high amid escalating threats in the Middle East.

No new numbers on social media ban deactivations due to investigations

The eSafety commissioner won’t release updated figures on the number of accounts held by under-16s that have been deactivated or suspended in the social media ban.

In March, the commissioner, Julie Inman Grant, announced that TikTok, YouTube, Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat were under investigation for compliance with the ban, and with new powers to compel the companies to produce documents, that investigation is under way, Inman Grant told Senate estimates last night.

Anthony Albanese has said more than 5m accounts have been deactivated across 10 platforms, but no more updated figures will be coming while the investigations are ongoing as it may prejudice the outcome of those investigations, officials told Senate estimates.

No decision has been made as to whether to take any of the platforms to court to seek fines of nearly $100m for breaches of the law.

The government has resisted providing a public breakdown of the accounts deactivated across the 10 platforms but some companies – such as Meta and Snapchat – have provided their own figures.

Liberal senator Sarah Henderson requested the eSafety commissioner release the data publicly as “the numbers don’t add up”, indicating that the committee was provided much more detail privately by eSafety on the figures that had not been made public.

eSafety commissioner and family subjected to ‘onslaught of online abuse and threats’

Josh Taylor

Australia’s eSafety commissioner, Julie Inman Grant, said she and her family had been subjected to an onslaught of online abuse and threats in the 10 years she has been in the role.

In her final appearance before Senate estimates last night, Inman Grant said it was a “sensitive and pivotal moment” for the parliament in the wake of the alleged attack on Helen Haines, and the environment was “dangerously febrile for women in senior government roles.”

When public officials are used for political point scoring, a target goes on our backs and we are subjected to an onslaught of online abuse and threats.

As many of you know, this has been the normal state of affairs for me and my family for years, yet somehow this is meant to be accepted as part of public service.

Inman Grant did not name any people who have targeted her personally, but her work in the role has seen her labelled as a “censorship commissar” by the world’s richest person, Elon Musk. US Republican congressman Jim Jordan labelled Inman Grant as a “zealot for global takedowns”.

Inman Grant said more needs to be done for the next generation of leaders to feel safe in online spaces or public-facing roles.

Social media is no longer the once envisioned digital town square – it has become more like a gladiatorial arena.

Meanwhile, platforms continue to roll back abuse and violent speech policies that let some of the most egregious offenders out of digital jail.

Shoebridge to pitch increased welfare spending, hike in tax-free threshold

Tom McIlroy

The Greens leader, David Shoebridge, will address the National Press Club in Canberra today, promising to help Australians struggling with cost-of-living and answering questions about allegations against senator Jordon Steele-John.

He will propose lifting the tax-free threshold from $18,200 to $26,000, meaning people earning a wage won’t pay tax on their first $26,000. He says that will save the average working Australian more than $1,000.

The Greens are also proposing a $1,000 annual boost for people on income support, including pensioners, carers, students, and job seekers.

No matter who you are, whether you’re working, studying, looking for work or on a pension, you’ll get more than $1,000.

Costings for the Greens from the Parliamentary Budget Office show the plan would cost about $22bn a year, with Shoebridge proposing to pay for it through higher taxes on gas exports and a market-based tax on resources companies.

Steele-John is on leave from parliament amid an investigation into allegations about his conduct towards women. He has denied the allegations.

Greens senator David Shoebridge. Photograph: Mick Tsikas/AAP

Official data released at 11:30am are expected to show inflation accelerated to 4.2% in the year to September, from 4% in the month before, as rising fuel costs amid a worsening Middle East conflict compound cost of living pressures.

Look out for the Reserve Bank’s preferred gauge of underlying inflation, which removes the most volatile prices. That measure is predicted to hold at 3.6% in the year to the September quarter, economists say, but anything higher will virtually guarantee a fifth interest rate hike next Tuesday.

The RBA may feel the need to hike regardless, as recent comments from top central bank officials suggest they are more worried about high inflation becoming entrenched in the system than the rising rate of unemployment.

Financial markets are pricing in about a 65% chance of a Melbourne Cup day rate rise.

The latest inflation report also lands in the middle of a parliamentary sitting week, and the opposition will be quick to use the figures to attack Labor for its management of the economy.
