**Today's Object of the Week is a weathered memorial in a Durham churchyard which, among its fading words, tells a heartbreaking story from thousands of miles away.**

There are countless names carved into the old gravestones surrounding St Oswald’s Church in Durham, but look closely at one memorial on the north side of the church and an intriguing place name emerges from the weathered inscription: “Goose Island, Australia”.

The words mark a tragedy which befell a Durham family on the other side of the world more than 160 years ago.

![The Bradford family memorial in St Oswald’s churchyard in Durham, which commemorates members of the family including Jane and Captain Forster Hempseed, who were lost in an Australian shipwreck in 1859.](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21628378.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The inscription commemorating Jane Hempseed, daughter of William Bradford, who was lost in a shipwreck in Australia in 1859 *(Image: ANDREW WHITE)*

The memorial begins with Jane Bradford, the “beloved wife” of William Bradford, who died on August 18, 1847, aged 49.

It also remembers their daughter Elizabeth, born in 1843, who died in October 1857, aged just 14.

But further down is the name of another daughter, Jane Hempseed, and the story takes a dramatic turn.

![The Bradford family memorial in St Oswald’s churchyard in Durham, which commemorates members of the family including Jane and Captain Forster Hempseed, who were lost in an Australian shipwreck in 1859.](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21628376.jpg?type=mds-article-620)A few carved lines on a memorial in St Oswald’s churchyard preserve the story of a Durham family's tragedy on the other side of the world *(Image: ANDREW WHITE)*

Jane was born in Durham on July 11, 1835, and married Captain Forster Hempseed.

By 1859 they were in Australia with an infant son, while Forster was master and owner of the Boundary, a 188-ton sailing vessel registered in Melbourne.

On April 29 that year, the Boundary left Newcastle, New South Wales, for Melbourne carrying 280 tons of coal.

It never arrived.

The vessel appears to have sought shelter among the Furneaux Group, between Tasmania and the Australian mainland, but at about 4am one morning in mid-May it became stranded on the Vansittart Shoals.

![The Bradford family memorial in St Oswald’s churchyard in Durham, which commemorates members of the family including Jane and Captain Forster Hempseed, who were lost in an Australian shipwreck in 1859.](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21628380.jpg?type=mds-article-620)A few carved lines on a memorial in St Oswald’s churchyard preserve the story of a Durham family's tragedy on the other side of the world *(Image: ANDREW WHITE)*

The first boat launched from the stricken ship capsized within just 15 yards.

Jane, her infant son, chief officer James Dawson and three members of the crew drowned.

Forster refused to abandon his vessel before daylight, while the remaining crew headed for Vansittart Island, where they found help.

When they returned the following morning, the Boundary had broken in two and was breaking up in the heavy seas.

Forster had disappeared.

He was believed to have been washed overboard and drowned.

Five survivors were eventually taken by a sealer’s boat to Goose Island, before the barque General Wool picked them up on May 28 and carried them to Melbourne.

![The Bradford family memorial in St Oswald’s churchyard in Durham, which commemorates members of the family including Jane and Captain Forster Hempseed, who were lost in an Australian shipwreck in 1859](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21628394.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Goose Island Lighthouse in Tasmania in 1891. Survivors of the Boundary shipwreck were taken to Goose Island following the disaster in 1859 *(Image: J W Beattie/Anson Bros/State Library of Tasmania, public domain)*

News of the catastrophe appeared in Melbourne’s Argus on June 10.

The notice identified Jane as the daughter of William Bradford of Durham and recorded the loss of Forster, Jane and their son.

The exact date of the wreck is uncertain.

Contemporary newspaper reports variously gave May 16, 17 and 18, although the Durham inscription records May 16, 1859.

William Bradford lived for another six years.

He died on December 2, 1865, aged 65, and his name completes the family inscription.

Today, those few carved lines provide an extraordinary connection between Durham and a disaster thousands of miles away: a voyage which began with a cargo of coal and ended with a captain, his young wife and their baby all lost to the sea.
