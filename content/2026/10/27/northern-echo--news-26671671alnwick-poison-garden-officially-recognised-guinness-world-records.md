A North East garden filled with deadly plants has been officially recognised as a world record holder.

Northumberland’s Alnwick Poison Garden has been officially recognised by Guinness World Records for housing the most species of plant in a poison garden, earning the title with 97 toxic and narcotic species.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21633451.jpg?type=mds-article-620) *(Image: GUINNESS WORLD RECORDS 2027)*

The record-breaking achievement was celebrated earlier this month with a visit from Guinness World Records adjudicator Will Munford, who presented the official certificate at the site.

Will Munford, official Guinness World Records adjudicator, said: "It's not every day you present a Guinness World Records certificate surrounded by plants that could potentially kill you.

"The Alnwick Poison Garden is a truly fascinating place, and recognising 97 different species of poisonous and narcotic plants makes this a particularly unusual achievement."

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21633458.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The garden is home to a collection of plants that visitors are strictly forbidden from touching, tasting or even smelling. *(Image: GUINNESS WORLD RECORDS 2027)*

The garden is located behind black iron gates marked with skull-and-crossbones warnings and contains plants that visitors are strictly forbidden from touching, tasting or smelling.

The collection is also featured in the newly released Guinness World Records 2027 book.

Some of the plants may appear harmless, but many have dark histories and deadly potential.

Among the more familiar specimens are foxgloves and deadly nightshade, while more exotic dangers include the gympie-gympie, an Australian stinging tree kept in a locked enclosure to prevent visitors from coming into contact with its stinging hairs.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21633455.jpg?type=mds-article-620)There's an extraordinary 97 toxic and narcotic species. *(Image: GUINNESS WORLD RECORDS 2027)*

The garden also includes giant hogweed, which can cause severe skin reactions, alongside many other plants with dangerous reputations.

The Poison Garden was created by Jane Percy, Duchess of Northumberland, who wanted to explore the darker side of horticulture.

The Duchess said: "Apothecary gardens tend to focus on the curative properties of plants whereas I was focusing on how they could kill.

"I wanted to include plants which had great stories of death behind them."

Opened in 2005, the Poison Garden has become one of The Alnwick Garden's best-known attractions.

Despite its deadly contents, the garden serves an important educational purpose.

Entry is only allowed via guided tours, and visitors are strictly prohibited from touching, picking, tasting or smelling any of the plants.

Some of the most dangerous species are kept securely enclosed for safety.

Lachlan Rae, head gardener at The Alnwick Garden, said: "Truthfully, all plants in the Poison Garden carry risk and we act accordingly.

"Some are more benign or carry less obvious immediate danger, but we do have some absolute shockers in the garden."

As The Alnwick Garden marks its 25th anniversary, the Poison Garden continues to challenge visitors’ understanding of the natural world, highlighting the hidden dangers behind seemingly beautiful plants.

With Halloween approaching, the garden’s eerie reputation makes it an especially fitting time to explore the darker side of botany.

Mr Munford said: "It's brilliant to see the garden featured in the new Guinness World Records 2027 book, which celebrates extraordinary record-breaking achievements from around the world.

"This is a wonderful example of how records can be found in the most unexpected places, and there's certainly no better time than Halloween to celebrate this one."
