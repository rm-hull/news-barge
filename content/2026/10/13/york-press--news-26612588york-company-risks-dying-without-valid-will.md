## The risks of dying without a valid will explained

A cherub statue with flowers on the open pages of a book *(Image: Getty Images)*

Many people put off making a will, assuming it will be expensive, complicated, doesn’t apply to them or simply feel uncomfortable thinking about it, *writes Ramsdens Solicitors*.

However, dying without a valid will, known as intestacy, can have serious and often unintended consequences for those you leave behind.

A will is the only legally recognised way to ensure your wishes are followed after your death.

Without one, your estate, regardless of size, is distributed according to strict legal rules, rather than your personal preferences.

These rules do not consider modern family structures, relationships or individual circumstances, so your assets may not go to the people you would have chosen.

For example, unmarried partners are not automatically entitled to inherit under intestacy rules, regardless of how long they have been together.

Stepchildren, close friends or carers may also receive nothing.

In some cases, assets pass to estranged relatives or former spouses, depending on how your affairs are structured.

Beyond asset distribution, intestacy can create additional stress and costs at an already difficult time.

Families are often left to navigate complex legal processes whilst grieving, and uncertainty about what the deceased would have wanted can lead to disagreements.

Disputes between relatives are not uncommon and can escalate quickly, particularly in blended families or where significant assets are involved.

These conflicts can be lengthy, expensive and emotionally draining, sometimes causing lasting damage to relationships.

There are also practical considerations.

Without a will, there is no clear appointment of executors to manage your estate, which can delay the administration process.

This can result in financial uncertainty for loved ones who may be relying on access to funds or property.

Importantly, drafting a will is not a one-off task.

Life events such as marriage, divorce, the birth of children or changes in financial circumstances can all affect whether an existing will remains valid and effective.

An outdated or incorrectly prepared will can create similar issues to having no will at all.

Taking the time to put a valid will in place, or reviewing an existing one with a specialist lawyer, helps ensure your wishes are clearly documented and legally robust.

It provides reassurance that your loved ones will be protected and can help avoid unnecessary stress, delay and expense in the future.

Ultimately, making a will is not just about distributing assets.

It is about providing clarity, security and peace of mind for the people who matter most.
