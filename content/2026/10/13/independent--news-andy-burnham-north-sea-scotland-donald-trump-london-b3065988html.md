British Prime Minister Andy Burnham is facing a key test of his environmental policies as he decides on whether oil and gas companies should be allowed to drill in the North Sea.

Burnham's government has been under heavy pressure from climate campaigners and businesses over whether to approve two oil and gas fields in waters north of Scotland, including one said to be the U.K.'s largest untapped oil resource.

Environmental groups have urged Burnham to refuse permission for the projects, known as Rosebank and Jackdaw, saying they would accelerate climate change at a time when the U.K., like the rest of Europe, faces record extreme heat linked to human actions including burning fossil fuels such as coal, oil and gas.

But opposition politicians, the oil and gas industry and U.S. President Donald Trump have piled pressure on Burnham to give the green light, arguing it would protect jobs, cut household bills and improve Britain’s energy security.

British officials have been expected to make a decision since August, when public consultations on the two projects closed.

Oil and gas projects off Scotland delayed

Rosebank is an oil field that also will produce gas. Located 80 miles (130 kilometers) north of Scotland's Shetland Islands, it is estimated to be the largest undeveloped oil and gas field in the U.K., reportedly with capacity to produce 300 million to 500 million barrels of oil within about 25 years.

Jackdaw is a gas field 150 miles east of Aberdeen on Scotland's northeast coast. Production there was expected to last under a decade. The Shell Group, one of the project's co-developers, predicts the field can produce around 6.5% of the U.K.’s gas production at its peak.

The opposition Conservative Party, which has pushed for the developments to go ahead, said the Jackdaw field could start heating 1.4 million homes in the U.K. this winter if approved.

The projects were granted permission but overruled in court

The former Conservative government previously approved production at both sites, which were scheduled to start operations later this year. But a court in Edinburgh ruled last year that the approvals were unlawful, after considering a legal challenge from Greenpeace and another nonprofit.

“The public interest in authorities acting lawfully and the private interest of members of the public in climate change outweigh the private interest of the developers,” judge Andrew Stewart ruled at the time.

Critics say drilling won't bring significant benefits

Critics have argued that the oil and gas reserves in Britain's North Sea holdings are small and would make little difference to energy bills because prices are decided by international markets. They contend that new developments would increase carbon emissions while failing to contribute to Britain's energy security.

Analysts at the Energy and Climate Intelligence Unit, a London-based nonprofit, estimated that more than 90% of the oil and gas likely to be produced from the North Sea has already been extracted.

Almost 600 climate and health experts wrote a joint letter to the government last month arguing that approving the projects would breach the U.K.'s national and international climate commitments.

Separately, critics also have raised concerns about Rosebank's links to Israeli company Delek Group, which activists say profited from Israeli settlements in the West Bank. Green Party leader Zach Polanski is among critics who have asked how the U.K. can ensure revenue from any oil and gas production did not finance the settlements.

Supporters of drilling include Trump

Supporters of further drilling say it will not make a significant difference to emissions and is vital for protecting jobs. Some also argue that domestic production will result in less carbon emissions than importing oil and gas.

The Conservatives have warned that without new production, Britain risks importing 82% of its gas by 2035, leaving households exposed to every shock in the global market.

The governing Labour Party is split on the issue. Some warned that going ahead will renege on the party's pledge in its 2024 manifesto not to issue new licenses for oil and gas exploration in the North Sea.

Others disagree. “If we do not proceed with Jackdaw and Rosebank, investment will leave the UK, jobs will disappear and expertise will be lost,” lawmaker Graeme Downie said.

The issue became more politicized after Trump waded in. The U.S. president, who criticized former Prime Minister Keir Starmer's energy policies, claimed in August that people in Aberdeen were “dancing in the streets” following reports that Burnham would approve new drilling.

In his first phone call with Trump, Burnham reportedly said the U.K. “can’t ignore” resources in the North Sea, adding he would take “a pragmatic approach” to further drilling.

Britain's government has declined to directly comment, only saying the North Sea “remains a vital national asset.”

“Any decision will take into account all relevant evidence, including environmental assessments and public representations received as part of the regulatory process,” the energy security department said.
