The prospect of lower electricity bills has returned to the political agenda after Chancellor Friedrich Merz said on Monday that the CDU wants to reduce Germany's electricity tax to the European minimum.

The measure was previously promised by the governing coalition and forms part of the CDU's efforts to rebuild trust following a series of poor election results.

For households, the latest proposal could mean modest savings on annual electricity costs. But the plan still faces political and financial hurdles, meaning there’s no guarantee consumers will see lower bills any time soon.

**What has the CDU proposed?**

Following a two-day retreat of the CDU executive committee, Merz said the party wants to lower the electricity tax for households and businesses to the minimum level permitted under EU rules.

At present, Germany's electricity tax for households stands at 2.05 cents per kilowatt-hour. According to figures cited by the Federal Ministry of Finance, the European minimum rate for households is 0.1 cents per kilowatt-hour.

The CDU argues that any financial room created by stronger-than-expected economic growth should be used to fund the measure, with Merz noting the coalition had already promised such a reduction.

Last year, the government reduced the electricity tax for sectors including manufacturing, agriculture and forestry, while households remained on the standard rate.

The headline proposal sounds significant, but the impact on most households would be relatively modest.

The electricity tax currently accounts for only around five percent of the total electricity price in Germany. As a result, even a substantial reduction in the tax would only reduce overall bills by a limited amount.

For example, WDR reports that the average electricity price in North Rhine-Westphalia is currently 34.95 cents per kilowatt-hour. If the tax were cut to the proposed level, that price could fall to around 32.63 cents per kilowatt-hour.

For a three-person household consuming 4,000 kilowatt-hours of electricity per year that would translate into annual savings of about €93. Smaller households would save less.

**Why are the savings so small?**

The main reason the savings are so small is that electricity tax is only one part of what households pay for electricity. The final price charged by suppliers reflects a range of other costs including grid fees, procurement and distribution costs, VAT and other charges.

So while a reduction in the electricity tax will provide some relief, it’s unlikely to reverse the wider price pressures many households continue to experience.

**Will it definitely happen?**

The tax cut was promised in the coalition agreement and has now been brought back into the spotlight by the CDU. But the key question is how it will be paid for.

Merz has argued that an improving economic outlook could create additional room in the federal budget. He has suggested that if economic growth becomes stronger and more sustained, the government could collect more revenue and use some of that financial leeway to fund the electricity tax reduction.

While Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil (SPD) hasn’t ruled out the idea, he has questioned whether stronger growth would generate enough money to finance a permanent cut.

In practical terms, that leaves the proposal in limbo. The CDU wants to move ahead with the measure, but the coalition would first need to identify how it will be financed and secure agreement within government.

Until then, households should view lower electricity bills as a possibility rather than a certainty.
