France has witnessed thousands of arrests, hundreds of injuries, and schools set on fire after the new academic year descended into nationwide student protests.

The turmoil marks the latest test for Emmanuel Macron’s presidency as France contends with both foreign and domestic troubles.

Although the majority of demonstrators have been peaceful, violence on the fringes is having an impact, with fireworks thrown at police and tear gas fired at high school pupils.

On Tuesday, Swedish activist Greta Thunberg joined students at a high school in Paris’ Saint-Denis suburb, as the country faced a new day of nationwide marches and strikes – on top of the blockades at schools.

Here’s everything you need to know about the movement:

## Where the protests started

Demonstrations first erupted last month in a high school in Creteil southeast of Paris over a lack of staff. From there, class strikes, blockades and demonstrations spread to other Paris suburbs and then nationwide, even in smaller towns. Hundreds of schools had to close because of damage or attacks on staff.

High schoolers are expressing broad grievances over schools that are under-resourced, with dilapidated facilities and insufficient staff, notably in lower-income neighborhoods. French media have shown videos of scampering rats, collapsed ceilings, leaks, broken furniture and other woes in dilapidated schools.

![Students march to protest against under-resourced schools in Rennes, France, Tuesday, Oct. 13, 2026](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/13/15/download.-2..jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Some students also complain that during summer heat waves, they roast in classrooms without air-conditioning, while in winter they freeze. They say school days, which often run from 8 a.m. to 6 p.m., are too exhausting and the university entrance system is complex and unfair. Some university students have also joined the protests.

There have been broader concerns in France that the country's education system is losing ground to other countries and failing in its mission to enable children from disadvantaged backgrounds to move up the social ladder. Scores in reading comprehension and mathematics have plunged, according to the latest PISA report, which evaluates students worldwide.

## Police response has drawn criticism

Riot police have liberally used tear gas and wielded their batons to disperse crowds. Police made more than 6,600 arrests nationwide as of last week, an unusually high number for a French protest movement.

A police tear gas grenade shattered a 14-year-old’s jaw and many of his teeth. A 15-year-old lost a hand in an incident involving a police stun grenade. Other adolescents have also suffered life-changing injuries blamed on police weaponry during protests.

Macron’s government has repeatedly said that it respects the right to peaceful protest, but it has also issued written orders for police to break up protests that block access to schools and to rapidly put a stop to violence and vandalism.

![A person holds a French national flag during a protest against under-resourced schools in Paris, Thursday, Oct. 8](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/13/11/APTOPIX_France_School_Protests_64_76.jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Police and school staff have been targeted with powerful fireworks and other injury-causing projectiles. The interior ministry says acid has been hurled in some cases, and that people intent on destruction and looting have grafted themselves onto the high schoolers’ movement.

## Presidential candidates are seizing the moment

Candidates campaigning to succeed Macron in a coming presidential election in April and May have seized on the demonstrations as a political issue. Macron is constitutionally barred from seeking a third consecutive term.

Far-left candidate Jean-Luc Mélenchon has positioned himself firmly on the side of the students and has decried police actions. His party invited high school activists on stage at a rally Sunday, then proposed lowering the voting age to 16 and scrapping selective admissions at universities.

![French President Emmanuel Macron](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/13/15/2026-10-10T083746Z_411271093_RC270OA5RIPH_RTRMADP_3_FRANCE-GERMANY.JPG?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

Meanwhile, far-right leader Marine Le Pen has called for broader police powers against rioters. Conservative former Prime Minister Edouard Philippe, one of Le Pen’s main challengers for the presidency, has condemned those who “break things, burn things or assault people.”

## Other European countries have seen unrest

Taking grievances to the streets is hardly unusual in the country that spawned the French Revolution of 1789. To name just a few, there were mass student protests in 1968, weeks of suburban unrest in 2005, yellow vest protests in 2018-2019, and demonstrations in 2023 against raising the retirement age.

But some other countries have also seen some student unrest.

In neighboring Belgium, some schools closed in Liege, a day after violence in the city and arrests there.

Several Italian cities also saw student protests last week, though on a smaller scale than in France and focused on a broader set of domestic grievances.
