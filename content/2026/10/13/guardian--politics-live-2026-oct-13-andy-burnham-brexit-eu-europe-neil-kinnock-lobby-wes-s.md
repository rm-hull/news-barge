MI5 chief Ken McCallum confirms Russia did interfere in Brexit referendum - but says it had little impact

Dan Sabbagh

Dan Sabbagh is the Guardian’s defence and security editor.

MI5 chief Ken McCallum said today that Moscow did attempt to interfere in the 2016 Brexit referendum with anti-EU coverage on RT and Sputnik media and via troll social media accounts. But there was a big difference between “what may have been attempted and what actually resulted”, he added.

The head of the Security Service said that he agreed strongly with what the prime minister had said on Sunday and Monday, when he raised the issue of attempted Russian interference a decade ago – though Burnham then backed off when he was challenged by the Conservatives as to whether this meant he regarded the Brexit vote as illegitimate.

McCallum did not put any new evidence into the public domain but cited already known concerns about Russian broadcasters who “came in pretty strongly around the time of the referendum” in favour of Brexit. He also said that “there was a second line of interference attempted through [internet] trolls”.

However, he argued there was no evidence to suggest the result of the Brexit vote had been swayed as a result of the effort, which is generally believed to have been marginal, and said that it would be to Russia’s benefit if the issue was talked about further.

He explained:

It is up to us how far we allow our adversaries to divide us.

I’m a little bit worried that if we all stay in this debate for another few days, the Russian trolls who didn’t appear to do all that much at various points over the years, they’ll be delighted they are front page news once again despite the comparative unimpressedness of what they do at all points along the journey.

I am a little bit anxious that we don’t play into our adversaries’ hands by making these trolls look 10ft tall.

UPDATE:McCallum said:

There is a really important difference between interference attempted and outcome swayed … It’s quite important to focus on the difference between what may have been attempted and what actually resulted.

It is a matter of public record including in select committee reports published in 2019 – the DCMS [digital, culture, media and sport] committee did a bit and the intelligence and security committee did a bit also – that in 2016, before you even get to any secret intelligence … it is openly on the record that Russia Today and Sputnik and other Russian media organisations came in pretty strongly around the referendum.

And it is also a matter of open source record that there was a certain amount of interference attempted through trolls and bots and so forth.

I agree strongly with what the prime minister said yesterday, we did see a hack and leak thing inside the 2019 general election campaign.

Streeting tells MPs committing to spending 3% of GDP on defence without plan to fund it would risk Truss-type backlash

The government could trigger a Liz Truss mini-budget-type economic incident if it committed to spend 3% of GDP on defence by 2030 without saying where the money will come from, Wes Streeting has suggested.

Streeting used the argument as he gave evidence to the Commons defence committee, where he as pressed to confirm that the government will raise defence spending to 3% of GDP by 2030.

The defence investment plan (Dip) currently sets out a path to spending 2.7% of GDP on defence by the 2027-28 financial year. John Healey resigned as defence secretary when Keir Starmer refused to commit to 3% by 2030. Healey is now chancellor, but he has not yet explained how he will raise defence spending to the 3% level he treated as a resignation issue.

Streeting told the committee he never expected Healey to set out a pathway to 3% at the budget later this month and that plan was always due in the spending review, now reportedly expected next autumn.

And he defended the chancellor’s decision not to rush. He said:

We have had very bitter recent experience of a former prime minister making unfunded commitments to spend more, tax less, and borrow more all simultaneously, and it led to enormous loss of confidence, people’s mortgages going up, and a level of fundamental loss of confidence in Britain among investors, and this country is still recovering from that.

But Streeting also said that he was “confident that we will get there”, arguing that this confidence was justified “from the choices that Labour’s already made in government through to how strongly, as a matter of principle, the chancellor felt about this”.

Pat McFadden accuses Tories of hypocrisy over pensions triple lock, saying many of them accept current version unsustainable

Pat McFadden, the work and pensions secretary, has claimed he has a file “as thick as my morning porridge” with quotes from Tories arguing against the pensions triple lock.

He made the claim in a Commons statement explaining the plan announced by Andy Burnham at Labour conference to replace the current triple lock with an adjusted version that will be less generous.

The Conservatives opposed the move when it was announced and, in the Commons, McFadden’s Tory shadow, Helen Whately, claimed this policy was a “U-turn in waiting” and that it would be abandoned by Burnham because of its unpopularity.

But McFadden told Whately that plenty of her colleagues were in favour of the Labour policy. He told her:

The shadow foreign secretary [Tom Tugendhat] has called it simply not affordable and described it as a Ponzi scheme. The shadow justice secretary [Nick Timothy] actually wrote the 2017 manifesto that pledged to replace it with a double lock.

But my favourite is Lord Gove, who told LBC that if Andy Burnham changed the triple lock, he would, and I quote, “declare that I will be campaigning at the next general election exclusively for Stella Creasy in Walthamstow”. So I look forward to seeing him at the end of the Victoria line at the next election. with a red rosette on.

McFadden said these were not isolated examples.

I have a file as thick as my morning porridge full of quotes from the Tories saying that the triple lock is unsustainable and will have to be changed. They say it in private, they say it before they join the frontbench, and they say it after they leave it.

McFadden also said that, if the Tories remained committed to restoring the triple lock in its current form, they would have to explain at the next election how they would fund that. (Labour plans to legislate before the next election to change the triple lock uprating mechanism from 2030.)

Foreign Office minister tells MPs nearly 240 Iranian people and entities now subject to sanctions over hostile acts

Stephen Doughty, the Foreign Office minister, told MPs earlier that the UK has now sanctioned nearly 240 individuals and entities in relation to threats to the country posed by Iran.

A further two Iranian officers from the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) were sanctioned yesterday, he said.

In response to an urgent question, Doughty also confirmed that 34 other foreign ministers have joined Ed Miliband, the foreign secretary, in issuing a joint statement condemning “in the strongest terms Iran’s use of violent attacks, including the acts of lethal plotting, arson, sabotage, cyber-attacks, and other hybrid activities against us and our partners”.

And Doughty said that yesterday he had summoned the Iranian ambassador to the Foreign Office to tell him, “candidly and forcibly” that Iran’s activities were unacceptable.

Doughty said:

We have sanctioned nearly 240 individuals and entities in relation to Iran whose actions threaten our people and security and will continue to keep all those tools under review and will not hesitate to take further action where appropriate.

Veterans to get more protection under significant amendments to Northern Ireland Troubles bill, ministers claim

Later in the Commons, MPs are expected to pass a motion allowing them more time to consider the Northern Ireland Troubles bill before it has to be passed. The bill was introduced in the last session of parliament, which means it is subject to the carry-over procedure.

The bill replaces legislation passed by the last Tory government which in effect gave immunity to terrorists and members of the armed forces accused of crimes during the Troubles, provided they gave evidence to a Legacy Commission trying to establish the truth about unsolved Troubles-era murders. The Conservative law was hugely unpopular in Northern Ireland, and the Labour alternative – which is similar – has also been widely criticised.

In an article in the Times today, Chris Byrant, the Northern Ireland secretary, and Wes Streeting, the defence secretary, highlight amendments being made to the bill which they argue will reduce the risk of retired soldiers being subject to vexatious investigations. Under one change, in future the attorney general would have to approve any serious, Troubles-related prosecution.

They say:

By setting up a fair, workable system to deliver as many answers as possible, we will help communities to heal so that we can finally turn the page on one of the darkest periods in our recent history.

More than a quarter of the families seeking answers from the Legacy Commission are veterans’ families. Nearly one in five are the families of police officers. No one should be in any doubt how important this is.

And describing the way the legislation has been changed, they say:

Over the last few months, we have listened to veterans, victims’ groups, including those representing service families, and the wider defence community to hear their concerns. We are responding by making sure the Troubles bill properly protects all those troops who served with honour.

Under the previous government’s scheme, terrorists would have enjoyed the same immunity as veterans – something so abhorrent it should never have been considered in the first place. And so we will not only repeal it, we will make clear – in black and white – that there is no equivalence between paramilitaries, who waged campaigns of violence, murder and destruction, and the lawful actions of our armed forces sent to stop them.

For too long, our servicemen and women have had to fear the knock at the door, the call from an unknown number or the ping of an email notification, as they bear the brunt of incidents being reinvestigated years or decades after the events in question. The bill spells out that there can be no duplication of previous investigative processes unless it is essential, and that witnesses cannot be contacted unless it has been clearly demonstrated that there is compelling new evidence. Nor will veterans be forced to traipse to Northern Ireland to attend hearings. And any decision to prosecute a serious Troubles-related crime will now require the consent of the attorney general.

Reoffending 'off the charts' because of prisoner early release scheme, Tories claim

The Tories have claimed reoffending is “off the charts” due to the prisoner early release scheme.

In an urgent question in the Commons, Nick Timothy, the shadow justice secretary, said the majority of prisoners were not being tagged before release, despite assurances from the government that the tagging system would be the “toughest in history”.

He said:

The justice secretary [Alex Norris] told the country that proper safeguards would be put in place with a presumption that all released prisoners would be tagged.

He called it the toughest tagging regime in history, but that was nonsense.

I have some advice for him and his ministers – it is better to work out how to implement your policy before it starts, not after. We know a majority of prisoners are not being tagged before release, and we know reoffending is off the charts already.

Jake Richards, a justice minister, said that Timothy should be showing more contrition because prisoners were having to be released early as a result of the Tories leaving a system where the prisons were full.

In its write-up of its latest regular, weekly poll, YouGov says:

Our latest figures show Labour on 24% of the vote, down three points on last week’s poll, while the Conservatives are up three points to 23%, their highest level since we restarted our voting intention tracker in January 2025. Reform UK take a 21% share in this week’s poll, their joint-lowest on our tracker this parliament.

Speaking to reporters today, Badenoch suggested the fall in Reform UK’s ratings showed the public had been taking a “long look” at Nigel Farage’s party and “don’t like what they see.”

Asked whether she was confident her party could continue to rise in popularity, or whether it was just a temporary, post-conference blip, Badenoch told the Press Association:

I am confident that the momentum will continue, because people are seeing the Conservative party has changed under my leadership and there’s much more to come, this is just the beginning.

We left office two years ago, it was a historic defeat for the Conservative party. We made a lot of mistakes.

Asked what she thought the poll suggested about Reform UK, she said:

I think the public have been taking a long look at Reform and they don’t like what they see. What I need to show them is that if they don’t want a Labour Government there is only one credible alternative.
