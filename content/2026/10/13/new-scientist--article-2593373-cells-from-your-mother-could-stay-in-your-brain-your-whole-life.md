Cells from your mother may have penetrated your brain when you were a fetus. These alien, or “microchimeric”, cells then seem to take up residence and perform a range of jobs, even into old age.

The discovery shows that “every human brain is likely a genetic mosaic of more than one person”, says Hina Chaudhry at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai in New York, who wasn’t involved in the research.

We already knew that we have cells from our mothers – and other family members – inside us. This is thought to be because of a small proportion of cells crossing the placenta while we’re in utero, which could influence our risk of autoimmune conditions, while also playing a role in tissue repair.

There is evidence that these cells reach the brain, where they can persist for decades. But the extent of this, and the role the cells play there, was less clear. This is because brain samples from both a mother and their child are needed for comparison, and even if you have these samples, the cells can be hard to spot, says Sami Kanaan at the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center in Seattle. “They are usually 1 in 100,000.”

Now, Kanaan and his colleagues have analysed genetic sequences in samples removed from the brains of 37 children – from newborns to 14-year-olds – as part of surgery to treat epilepsy. These were compared against genetic sequences in mouth swabs taken from their mothers.

The researchers used a technique called single-nucleus RNA sequencing to identify genetic arrangements in the samples that were from just the child or their mother. They then ran a tool they had previously developed, called Cellector, to computationally look for patterns in the sequence data. “It was just magical. It models data, and it says ‘that cell does not fit in that model, so it’s probably a foreign cell’,” says Kanaan.

The team found maternal cells in 26 of the 37 children, with each sample having 2.2 maternal cells per 100,000, on average. More of these children probably had maternal cells, says Kanaan, but the method would have missed instances in which the mother and child had identical genetic variants.

“I’m very excited about this,” says Chaudhry, “because they’ve used some very sophisticated methods to really answer the question of ‘is there microchimerism?’”

Kanaan and his colleagues identified microchimeric cells working in many cell types, including neurons (nerve cells that transmit information), microglia (the brain’s immune cells), astrocytes (which support neurons), oligodendrocytes (which create the protective sheath around neurons) and endothelial cells (which line blood vessels).

In another part of the experiment, the team used Cellector to analyse RNA sequence data from three previously published studies. These comprised individuals ranging in age from a 22-week-old fetus to people in their 80s.

This revealed that people of all ages can have microchimeric cells in their brains. However, the number declined with age. This decline may be the result of apoptosis, programmed cell death that removes damaged and old cells.

“It is crazy that we found them in gestational samples,” says Kanaan. “It’s a [widespread] human feature to have this, and it starts in early life and continues throughout the whole life, which is mind-blowing.”
