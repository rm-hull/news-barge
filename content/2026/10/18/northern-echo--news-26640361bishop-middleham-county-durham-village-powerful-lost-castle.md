A quiet County Durham village was once home to one of the region's most powerful medieval residences, although little of the castle remains visible today.

Bishop Middleham, between Durham and Sedgefield, is now a peaceful village surrounded by farmland and countryside.

But centuries ago, it was an important residence of the Bishops of Durham, who held extraordinary power across much of the North East.

Their castle stood on high ground overlooking the settlement, providing a home for some of the most influential figures in medieval northern England.

Today, the buildings have largely disappeared, leaving earthworks and traces of stone walls as reminders of the village's remarkable past.

## The lost castle of Bishop Middleham

Middleham Castle was a fortified manor house used by the Bishops of Durham during the Middle Ages.

The site occupied elevated ground to the south-east of the village, overlooking the surrounding countryside.

Unlike the great stone castles at Durham or Raby, very little of the building survives above ground.

Its remains consist mainly of banks, ditches and low sections of masonry which mark where the medieval residence once stood.

But the site's historical importance is recognised nationally.

Historic England has designated Middleham Castle as a Scheduled Monument, protecting what remains of the medieval complex.

The castle served as one of the principal residences of the Bishops of Durham from the period following the Norman Conquest until the late 14th century.

For much of the medieval period, they also exercised extensive political and administrative authority within the County Palatine of Durham.

Their powers included responsibilities which elsewhere belonged to the Crown, making them some of the most influential figures in northern England.

Bishop Middleham was one of the places from which that authority was exercised.

## Two Bishops of Durham died at Bishop Middleham

The castle's significance is underlined by the fact that two Bishops of Durham are recorded as having died at Bishop Middleham.

It was a residence closely associated with the personal and official lives of Durham's medieval bishops.

By the late 14th century, however, the castle appears to have fallen out of regular use as an episcopal residence.

The buildings and surrounding land were subsequently let out, initially to the bishop's bailiff.

Over time, the once-important complex disappeared from the landscape, leaving only the surviving earthworks.

Those remains provide a link to a period when the village played a far more prominent role in the affairs of County Durham.

## Bishop Middleham's history goes back even further

Its name comes from Old English, with Middleham generally understood to mean the middle settlement or farm.

That suggests the area was occupied during the Anglo-Saxon period, probably by the ninth or tenth centuries.

One of the earliest surviving written references dates from 1146, when Osbert, a nephew of Bishop Ranulf Flambard, gave the church at Middleham to the Prior and Convent of Durham.

The village was also recorded in the Boldon Book of 1183.

This important survey documented land and obligations within the Bishop of Durham's estates.

It recorded around 32 households at Middleham and even named individual residents, including Arkil, Ralph and William the Headborough.

These details provide an unusually personal glimpse of the people who lived in the village more than 800 years ago.

Rather than simply being a place associated with powerful bishops, Middleham was also a working agricultural community with families, farms and everyday responsibilities.

## A village with centuries of hidden history

Archaeological discoveries in the surrounding area have also raised questions about activity during the Roman period.

Finds of Roman objects, including a small statue, have prompted suggestions that a temple may once have existed nearby, although no such building has been conclusively identified.

The discoveries add another layer to a settlement whose history stretches across many centuries.

Today, Bishop Middleham is a very different place from the important medieval residence it once was.

The castle's great buildings have vanished, and its surviving remains are far less obvious than those of the North East's better-known fortresses.
