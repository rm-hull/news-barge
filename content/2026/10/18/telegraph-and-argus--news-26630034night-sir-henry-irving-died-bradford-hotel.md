Bradford has welcomed plenty of famous performers over the years, but few visits ended as dramatically as that of Sir Henry Irving.

Widely regarded as the biggest star of the Victorian stage, he came to the city in October 1905 for what was meant to be a farewell tour.

Just days later, on October 13, 1905 - 121 years ago this week - he was dead.

The 67-year-old had just finished a week of acclaimed performances at Bradford's Theatre Royal and Opera House on Manningham Lane when he fell ill after returning to the Midland Hotel.

![Sir Henry Irving](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/11594428.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Sir Henry Irving *(Image: Image submitted)*

He died from a heart attack shortly before midnight.

## Who was Sir Henry Irving?

Whilst no longer a household name, Irving was once regarded as one of Britain's first great actors.

His death, hundreds of miles from home, unexpectedly linked Bradford to one of the most significant figures in British theatre history.

Adding another twist to the story, the man by his side that night was close friend Bram Stoker, the author of Dracula.

![Bram Stoker leaving the Theatre Royal, Manningham Lane, in 1905 - the day after the death of Sir Henry Irving](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/18632823.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Bram Stoker leaving the Theatre Royal, Manningham Lane, in 1905 - the day after the death of Sir Henry Irving

Stoker had worked with Irving for around 30 years and is widely believed to have drawn inspiration from him when creating the famous vampire character.

The planned final performance of The Merchant of Venice was cancelled, while crowds gathered respectfully as Irving's body was taken from Bradford by train the following day.

He was later buried in Westminster Abbey.

## How it was reported 121 years ago

The Bradford Daily Telegraph - a predecessor of today's T&A - carried the story prominently the following day.

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21571219.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Clippings from The Bradford Daily Telegraph *(Image: T&A Archive)*

It is easy to imagine reporters and printers working late into the night to produce a fitting tribute.

What stands out now is just how dramatic the coverage was.

Much of page three was devoted to Irving's death beneath the stark headline "Sir Henry Irving Dead", alongside sub-headings including "Sudden Collapse at the Midland Hotel" and "Pathetic End of a Great Career."

The newspaper also drew attention to the final words Irving had spoken on stage just hours earlier: "Into Thy Hands, O Lord."

The paper paid tribute to the actor's visit to Bradford, writing: "How much poorer is the stage for his loss! How much poorer, too, is our humanity!"

![](https://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/resources/images/21571220.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Clippings from The Bradford Daily Telegraph *(Image: T&A Archive)*

It described Irving as a man "the poor did not know as the actor, so much as the man", praising his "large-hearted generosity" and saying he "never could and never did close his ears to the cry of distress".

Reading the reports today, it is clear Bradford's newspapers viewed the event as far more than the death of a visiting performer.

The tribute was as much about the man as the actor, praising Irving's generosity and public spirit as well as his achievements on the stage.

In 2026, a plaque at the Midland Hotel marks the spot where one of Victorian Britain's most celebrated performers took his final bow, enshrining Bradford in an extraordinary chapter of theatrical history.
