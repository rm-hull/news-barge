**A suburban housing development in south London has won the UK's most prestigious architecture award, with judges praising its sensitive design and integration into the surrounding landscape.**

Lion Green Road in Coulsdon, comprising 157 homes across five distinctive 13-sided blocks, was named winner of the 2026 Stirling Prize by the Royal Institute of British Architects (Riba).

Designed by Mary Duggan Architects and built with RUFF Architects, it combines affordable and private housing for Croydon Council.

Replacing a former car park on a challenging, sloping site that varies in height by eight metres, Riba president Chris Williamson praised the design as a "new approach to higher density development" creating "a new connection between the bustling Lion Green Road and a distinctive area of green parkland beyond".

![Mary Duggan with long hair wearing black at the award ceremony](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/a3a6/live/ee3dfe00-c8d7-11f1-874f-2bba45330ab3.jpg)

Mary Duggan said Lion Green Road was a project "about the culture of dwelling"

Duggan said the win was "extraordinary, unexpected and humbling".

"Lion Green Road began in 2016 with a very clear vision, to create genuinely non-institutional housing and to think differently about what affordable housing could offer commnities.

"We think of Lion Green Road as a project about the culture of dwelling... architectural ambition, social value and social realities can co-exist."

![Riba president Chris Williamson wearing a red ribboned medallion and standing in the foreground as an awards ceremony goes on behind him.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/e047/live/fdd85070-c8d4-11f1-95b2-9f12d414946d.jpg)

Riba president Chris Williamson said the judging process was "inspirational and emotional"

Williamson told the BBC that until he went to study architecture he lived in a council estate in the East Midlands, "and every time I go back there all the council houses have been sold off, they're in private hands.

"Which in a way can be a good thing - we're in a homeowning democracy - but the sad thing is they haven't been replaced.

"Every time I go back - without getting too emotional - I think 'where would we have lived?'

"So to me that's why this particular project was important. I think a lot of people from that sort of background live in terrible accommodaiton - temporary accommodation which isn't safe or secure.

"These houses are fantastic and they're part of a mixed scheme so it's got a fantastic feel to it.

"For me it was a very inspirational, emotional, part of the process."

Duggan, who was brought up in south-west London, said the scheme "challenged conventional suburban housing layouts centred on roads, private gardens and clearly defined front and back spaces".

Winding paths and planting weave between the blocks, connecting them and linking shared outdoor spaces.

She said she wanted it to be a public space people felt they could belong to.

"Housing needs to be progressive. People who live in the building have access to green space, allotments."

The site fronts onto Lion Green Road, with low-rise green belt housing set on higher ground to the south and traditional suburban streets to the north.

![Low rise flats built with purple and pink brinks ](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/c663/live/217f4340-c7c1-11f1-b41f-d7a6cb474575.jpg)

Nestled amongst trees on the edge of green-belt land, the development's soft purple and pink brickwork blends high-density housing into an area of natural beauty.

At a time when housing developments are often judged by their cost and number of homes they can deliver, the project was recognised for the way it responds to its surroundings.

Williamson said "the housing blocks blend into the parkland beyond, while small variations in pattern add texture and give each building its own identity."

He added: "The way it fits into the hillside - it doesn't look like a large housing scheme.

"It has community gardens, it's the sort of place i would have liked to have grown up in."

![Low rise flats built with purple and pink brinks ](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/7378/live/ae25ca30-c87f-11f1-a2e8-f71844f8002e.jpg)

The project was funded by Croydon Council as a public asset, and a change in building regulations meant it "fought against five or six circumstantial elements beyond the normal challenges".

The building was praised by the jury for its "inventive and site-specific response to a highly constrained plot".

Buildings are assessed across a range of criteria, including design vision, innovation, sustainability, functionality, and the ability to delight users and visitors.
