### Our Verdict

Asus' 20th anniversary edition of the Xbox ROG Ally X is as bougie as the $300 price bump implies. Its additions are very welcome, but that's a lot of cash for a familiar package.

#### For

* OLED
* No more stick drift
* Great thermals

#### Against

* $300 more than an already pricey handheld
* No performance uplift on the Xbox Ally X

The Asus ROG 20th anniversary lineup has been interesting. Black, gold, and indulgent feels like the tagline here, with the Azoth Extreme Edition 20 and ROG Harpe II Extreme Edition 20 containing actual gold, and a fitting price tag to boot.

For any criticisms I may have, I don't think you're paying primarily for the branding on the Asus ROG Xbox Ally X20, though. You get a new OLED screen which is bigger and brighter than the IPS panel in the standard Xbox Ally X, TMR thumbsticks, refined triggers, a new cooling system, and with that screen comes a snappy 0.2 ms response time.

Refined is a good word for the device as a whole. Coming a year later than the last model, the software is smooth and intuitive, and it's easy to swap between Xbox and Windows mode, though it still doesn't feel like a console in practice. For my money, it's the best ROG Ally on the market right now, but the memory crisis has skewed the price of all gaming gear, and the X20 is certainly not the best value as a result.

### Buy if…

**✅ You want a beefy OLED handheld:** OLED handhelds are still fairly niche, and with recent price bumps, I'd pick this over the Legion Go 2 or OneXPlayer's offerings.  
**✅ You want power on the go:** I'd suggest a significantly cheaper handheld if you are after low-intensity games and don't particularly care for big-budget ones. For your Cyberpunks and your Horizons, you will want a Z2 Extreme chip or better to play them at a decent resolution and settings.

### Don't buy if…

**❌ You're fine with IPS:** $300 more for the X20 over the X is pretty steep, especially if you aren't dying to play on an OLED.   
**❌ You live in the UK:** The pricing on the X20 is not as good value in Britain, with the superior G3-equipped MSI Claw being only £100 more (though no OLED) and the standard Xbox Ally X £400 less.

The Asus ROG Xbox Ally X20 (or should I say XBOX) is a relatively granular update on the Xbox Ally X. I noticed it immediately when putting the two devices side by side, but when it comes to performance, you're getting identical experiences. They have the same chip, the same memory, the same storage. That's the Ryzen Z2 Extreme, 24 GB LPDDR5X, and 1 TB PCIe 4.0 M.2 2280, respectively.

Given you don't get a performance bump, you've gotta really care about the details to consider the X20. Arguably its biggest upgrade is the OLED panel, and it is a beauty. The DisplayHDR True Black 1400 certification isn't just for show, as it pops, appearing both bright and ultra-vibrant. It's also got a bigger screen, which does make the device more immersive, but also larger and chunkier.

You can technically upgrade the plain old joysticks in the ROG Xbox Ally X with a TMR module with a bit of know-how, but slapping in your own OLED screen would be very difficult, and I certainly wouldn't trust myself to do such fine electronic surgery. Therefore, I do see value in an upgraded, refined, and pretty older brother of last year's device. It's also got a whole new cooling system for frostier temps.

## Performance

There was a brief period when the original Asus ROG Xbox Ally X was the highest-performing gaming handheld money could buy. That was until the launch of the much more expensive MSI Claw 8 EX AI+ CG3EM (catchy name, I know) broke onto the scene.

Despite launching a year after the original Xbox Ally X, the X20 sees no difference in power (as it has identical specs), and therefore my performance figures are more or less the same as what Dave got in his review of the standard Xbox Ally X. I got a little less in Metro and a little more in Horizon and Cyberpunk 2077, with lower 1% lows in F1 25.

I left the GPU memory budget at 8 GB, flicked the device onto its 35 W Turbo mode, and had a good time in F1 24, Horizon Zero Dawn, and Metro Exodus without any upscaling. Cyberpunk 2077 is borderline, sitting around the 40 fps mark, but I couldn't recommend Black Myth Wukong without a bit of upscaling or frame generation.

These are really respectable scores, and more than enough for handheld gaming right now. The AMD Z2 Extreme chip is still a great performer, and though it's not the top dog, it's certainly one of them. The actual top dog, the MSI Claw 8 EX AI+ CG3EM and its Intel Arc G3 Extreme processor, ekes out better performance with anywhere between a 20-40% performance uplift in our game testing, but it's also just under 40% more expensive in the US.

The X20 is very likely the most comfortable handheld I've ever, well, hand…held. It has a slight cutout between the screen and the grips, which I often found myself sliding my index finger and pinky fingers under, and the new rubberised grips not only feel great but offer good enough traction that I'm never afraid of it slipping out of my hands.

It's so clear from the X20 that Microsoft know how to make controllers, as the buttons are fittingly clacky, the thumbsticks feel great, and the bumpers and triggers are easy to press down, with a comforting click to know I was successful. Well, as successful as I can be when I'm missing headshots.

I do really miss a trackpad, though. Windows 11 mode is fine enough, but feels like a slight downgrade without an intuitive way to control the mouse. My fat fingers struggle to click on the right thing sometimes, especially if it's a fairly small box, though the larger screen helps. You can control the mouse with the left analogue stick via Armoury Crate, but it doesn't beat the smoothness of a trackpad.

Still, trackpads are fairly rare among gaming handhelds, with it being done by the Legion GO, Steam Deck, and some GPD and Ayaneo devices. And if the ROG Ally X20 got a trackpad, it would struggle to fit all its buttons on without some compromises to feel.

At 756 grams, the X20 is certainly not a light device, but it is well balanced, and I've played it for hours at a time without fatigue. That's a feat, in my book.

## Sound

The Asus ROG Xbox Ally X is a very quiet device. Not from the speakers, I should add; they can be cranked up quite high. Even at 35 W, the X20 fans never get loud enough to be annoying, but they are wonderfully tuned between 15-20 W. On just 16 volume, Cyberpunk's audio drowns out the fans on 25 W mode.

I wouldn't quite call them silent, but I found a PC across the other side of the room while idle produced a louder hum than the X20 playing Elden Ring. That's impressive, and it meant I never felt self-conscious bringing it out and about with me.

![Asus ROG Xbox Ally X20 gaming handheld](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/SkWtTVG97bmxoaKVKxnbh3-1200-80.jpg)

The X20 has two sets of speakers on the front, on the left and right-hand side of the screen, and they are decently tuned and I've never wished they could get louder. The device also has a jack at the top to plug in your headphones, and Bluetooth compatibility if you can stomach the latency most Bluetooth speakers will give you.

## Thermals

Asus says the X20 is equipped with an "evolved ROG intelligent cooling system that drives air directly to the processor via a new internal channel and high-flow fans". In practice, that does indeed see it with cool thermals. I pushed the device to its limits at its 35 W Turbo mode, benchmarking all sorts of games for hours, and never saw it go above a CPU temperature of 79 °C. That is 17 entire °C below our tests for the standard Xbox Ally X.

I did also notice that the Max CPU package power was lower at 48 W, compared to the 55 W we saw last year. This suggests some software tweaks have led to more fine-tuned power management too.

I could, of course, get the device hotter by setting a custom wattage mode where peaks are higher, but that's not how I would use this device. That extra temperature headroom does mean setting manual clocks higher would worry me less too, which is pretty sweet. At 25 W playing intensive games like Cyberpunk 2077 (and too much Tales of Seikyu), I could barely get the CPU temp to crack 68 °C. Impressive.

## Software

The Xbox mode works very well on the Asus ROG Xbox Ally X20. When you first boot it up, it moves into a stripped-back bit of software that simply allows you to open any of your games from within the Xbox UI. Not only does this give streamlined access to games and mean not having to deal with Windows 11, with its habit of booting up all your software when you turn it on, but it also means being able to skip the performance hit associated with it.

However, it's not an Xbox. You're still playing PC games on a PC device. If you boot up a Steam game from the Xbox app and haven't also booted up Steam, it will load that app first, then let you into your game. This is fine, but it's not flawless, and it's not like an Xbox. It's perhaps closer to booting into Steam Big Picture Mode. The Xbox library now pools together Steam, Epic etc, so you technically only *need* to boot up Windows when doing Windows-specific things.

## Battery Life

The X20 has the exact same size battery as the X. An 80 Wh battery is very solid in a gaming handheld, offering a decent sweet spot for capacity versus size and weight.

In PC Mark 10's gaming battery life test, I saw almost three hours at 17 W, with Windows in Balanced battery mode and 2 hours and 9 minutes on the beefy 25 W mode. It's important to note that this will effectively be how much gaming you will get done on a highly intensive game, so you can expect more than that if you're not pulling out Black Myth Wukong every time you bust out the Ally X20.

## Value

So, the X20 version of this device is $300 more than the standard X one, and in return, you get an OLED screen, better thermals, a new look, and TMR thumbsticks. It is a luxurious device in a lot of ways, and has some smart design decisions, making for tame but welcome upgrades. These make the device better overall but worse for the money for the average person.

The chip, paired with 24 GB of LPDDR5X memory, makes it a formidable device capable of handling most games you can throw at it, but the pricing does make it stand out awkwardly in the market. For $300 less, you can get the same basic performance without those extras, and for $400 more, you can get the cream of the current crop. For us Brits, the Claw 8 EX AI+ CG3EM is a better pick for just £100 more.

As much as it pains me to say it, it's getting harder to find handheld gaming devices below $1,000. Even the 'cheapest' Steam Deck (512 GB with OLED screen) fetches $789 new, and its 2023 Zen 2/RDNA 2 chip wasn't exactly a powerhouse when it launched.

Last year, when I first saw the Xbox Ally X and its $1,000 price tag, I was surprised in a bad way, and yet the memory crisis has worsened to such a state that it now seems fairly reasonable. I don't see the same happening for the X20, but I can say that I've softened on it significantly since getting time with it every single day. Whether or not those extra upgrades are worth $300 will be largely dependent on your wallet and your eyes, but I can say I can see where that extra cost has been sunk straight back into the device. And for that, I'm impressed.

Asus' 20th anniversary edition of the Xbox ROG Ally X is as bougie as the $300 price bump implies. Its additions are very welcome, but that's a lot of cash for a familiar package.
