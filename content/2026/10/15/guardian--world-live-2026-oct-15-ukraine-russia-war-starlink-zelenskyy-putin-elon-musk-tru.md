Morning opening: Musk suggests US government was behind Starlink restrictions

Billionaire tech mogul Elon Musk has suggested that the US government could be behind the decision to restrict the speed of SpaceX’s satellite-technology used by Ukraine’s military.

In a post of X, he appeared to suggest the Trump administration was behind the move, which Ukraine says restricts its ability to use key defensive weapons.

A Ukrainian serviceman of 47th brigade prepares a Starlink satellite internet systems at his positions at a front line. Photograph: Reuters

Musk said:

“Starshield systems are owned and controlled by the US government. Any limits on speed or usage are decided by the US military and ultimately POTUS.

Starlink is a commercial service owned and controlled by SpaceX. Usage of Starlink is limited to civilian or defensive activities and cannot be used for offensive military action, as doing so would be inappropriate for a commercial company.”

In further posts overnight, reacting to Zelenskyy’s comments on his relationship with Donald Trump, Musk went further and appeared to suggest Zelenskyy should leave the post, saying it’s “time to go.”

In a TV interview with Piers Morgan, Zelenskyy said he felt “betrayed” by Trump’s decisions and worried about “losing a strong partner,” playing a critical role across Ukraine’s defence.

For what it’s worth, Trump denied that his administration had cut intelligence sharing on Tuesday, but Zelenskyy said in an interview with US broadcaster NPR, aired on Wednesday, that military advisers had received threats from the US that intelligence sharing could be limited in the future.

The issue is likely to come up again today and tomorrow as EU leaders meet in Brussels for key political talks on the future.

With today’s discussions focused on the bloc’s internal matters, including the EU’s next budget, Ukraine is expected to come up on Friday when Zelenskyy joins other leaders in Brussels. But the ongoing crisis in Ukraine’s relations with the US risks overshadowing the entire meeting.

Swedish parliament rejects Andersson's PM candidacy

A quick update from Sweden, wherethe country’s parliament rejected Social Democrats leader Magdalena Andersson’s candidacy to form a single-party minority government.

Swedish Social Democratic party leader Magdalena Andersson speaks during a press conference at the Riksdag, the Swedish parliament, in Stockholm, Sweden. Photograph: Jonas Ekstromer/EPA

Only 99 lawmakers voted in favour, with 248 against. Two people were absent.

The vote, forced by the parliament’s speaker Andreas Norlén, was widely expected to fail, but is intended to put more pressure on parties as they continue discussions on who could form the next government.

Four centre-left opposition parties, which have all said they favour ‌Andersson as prime minister, won last month a majority of seats in Sweden’s parliament, ⁠the Riksdag, but ​do not ​agree on ​who should be ​part ‌of ​a ​new government.

Norlén will now have to appoint a new candidate to hold further exploratory talks on the new government.

'Sanctions must not be lifted,' Zelenskyy says, as he says Russia's attacks come 'in response to signals from US'

Oh, and we have just heard from Zelenskyy again, doubling down on his criticism of Russia and attributing its latest attacks to “signals from the US.”

Responding to a “horrific” drone attack on a supermarket in Odesa, he said:

“Russia continues its bloody terror. The store was partially destroyed, and cars were damaged. Three people were injured. All necessary emergency services are working at the scene and providing assistance to those affected. It is important that our partners help us defend against this. Residential buildings, stores, energy and other critical infrastructure are under attack virtually nonstop. Not a single hour of peace.”

Crucially, he continued:

“What is happening is Russia’s pointed response to the signals it is receiving from America. Unfortunately, there are dangerous signals about the possibility of further sanctions relief. These sanctions were imposed by the world, by America, Europe, and our partners in response to Russia’s aggression against Ukraine and its war against us. Nothing has changed. The war has not stopped. The sanctions must not be lifted. Russia must end the slaughter it itself started and has continued for 1,695 days now. More money to fund the killing will not help bring this about. Strength is what is needed, and America is stronger than Russian terror.”

US effort to get Russia-Ukraine energy ceasefire collapses

Andrew Roth

in Kyiv

The White House’s unilateral effort to negotiate an energy ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine has collapsed as Moscow sent a record swarm of drones overnight to attack infrastructure in central Ukraine and Kyiv appeared to resume strikes on Russian oil refineries – despite warnings from Donald Trump.

A man stands at the site of his parents' private house, destroyed during a Russian air strike, amid Russia's attack on Ukraine, on the outskirts of Dnipro, Ukraine. Photograph: Reuters

Zelenskyy said on Wednesday that he would resume strikes on Russian oil refineries after halting those attacks to show that Ukraine was ready for peace talks. “The block [on attacking refineries] is out. We’re done,” he told the Financial Times. He also attacked the gap in US intelligence sharing as “dangerous”.

Images from Ryazan showed flames at the refinery, which regional officials said had been targeted by 69 drones and four Flamingo cruise missiles. Debris from a drone struck by anti-aircraft fire had hit the refinery and sparked the fire, the ministry claimed.

In Ukraine, more than 360 Russian drones targeted Kirovohrad, a central region that has been hit less hard than others closer to Russia, in attacks that killed three and injured six.

“The enemy deliberately hit critical infrastructure, transport enterprises, logistics ⁠facilities and residential homes,” the regional governor, Andriy Raikovych, said in a statement.

'We need honesty from our partners about this war,' Zelenskyy says

Meanwhile, Ukraine’s Volodymyr Zelenskyy offered an update on last night’s Russian attacks on Ukraine.

“Emergency services are still providing assistance in dozens of our cities and communities. Repair crews and rescuers are working to deal with the aftermath of Russian attacks. For practically all of yesterday and throughout last night, a massive shahed attack was under way. The drones kept coming in waves, and there was not a single hour when our skies were clear of them.”

He said “more than 850” drones were sent against Ukraine, “at a time when the speed of our defence capabilities is being artificially restricted.”

He added:

“It is obvious that Russia deliberately chose to escalate its attacks after receiving sanctions relief and seeing attempts to pressure Ukraine. Russia exploits every such opportunity and must be held accountable for what it does. Strong decisions are needed to end this war. Ukraine needs steady support, above all in defending our skies.

We need HONESTY from our partners about this war: It is important that everyone in America stand not with the aggressor, but with our efforts to protect lives, end this war, and guarantee security.”

Seven people missing after fire on vessel in Black Sea off Romania

Just as the leaders sit down at the big table in Brussels, Romania has said that seven people are still missing after a fire on a vessel in Romanian territorial waters of the Black Sea.

An aerial footage shows San Marino-flagged tanker wine trader ship that caught fire in Romanian territorial waters. Photograph: Romanian Interior Ministry/Reuters

Suspected Russian drones have hit several ships off Romania and neighbouring Bulgaria since last week in a spillover from the Ukraine war, AFP reported.

Romanian authorities did not say whether a drone caused the fire on the vessel, named as the Wine Trader, which reported a blaze at around 7am, approximately 13 nautical miles southeast of the Romanian city of Sulina.

“Whether it was struck by a drone or not, an investigation is currently underway,” defence minister Radu Miruță told the Digi24 television channel.

The Romanian Naval Authority said in a statement there were 12 people on board the vessel, AFP reported.

Eight crew were initially reported as missing.

“Five of the sailors on board the Wine Trader have been rescued, a further seven people are still missing,” the Naval Authority said in an update.

EU will voice dismay and 'regret' over the US diesel deal with Russia

Jennifer Rankin

in Brussels

EU leaders will voice dismay over Donald Trump’s decision to lift sanctions on Russian diesel.

European Council summit Photograph: dts News Agency Germany Michael Kappeler Pool/Shutterstock

“The European Council regrets the United States’ decision to temporarily lift sanctions on Russian diesel,” states a draft statement seen by the Guardian to be issued by the 27 leaders when the two-day summit concludes on Friday.

Other leaders, such as Sweden’s Ulf Kristersson, were blunter in their assessment of the deal (9.36).

But EU leaders tread a fine line between their obvious dislike of Trump’s actions towards Russia and dependence on US military support.

That balancing act was epitomised by comments from Estonia’s prime minister, Kristen Michal.

He recalled that the US was one of three Nato nuclear-armed partners supporting his country and described the US as “strong allies” (the others are the UK and France).

He referenced Nato’s help when Estonia faced hybrid attacks and other threats, such as the cutting of cables, Russian fighter jet incursions and drones.

“This kind of help came from Nato and we still have Nato ships on the sea guarding our critical infrastructure. So, [based] on that I must say that Nato is working well and the US is a partner in Nato. And Trump has assured us and everybody else last time in Ankara that he is following Nato and also Article 5 [on collective defence]. So we rely on them – and also sometimes even good friends need a reminder what is right.”

Estonia’s prime minister Kristen Michal answers journalists’ questions upon his arrival for a European Union summit at the EU headquarters in Brussels. Photograph: Nicolas Tucat/AFP/Getty Images

We should put pressure on Russia, not Ukraine, EU's foreign policy chief says

EU’s foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas acknowledged that the EU and the US had “definitely different approach to this war,” as she said Europeans believe “we should squeeze Russia, not Ukraine, to surrender and end this war.”

European Union foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas speaks with the media as she arrives for the EU summit at the European Council building in Brussels. Photograph: Harry Nakos/AP

Responding to the US diesel deal with Russia, she said:

“The majority of Russia’s funding for the war comes from oil revenues. Clearly, Ukraine wants this war to stop, and that’s why … cutting off the funding for the war is the way to do it.

We see that we should squeeze … Russia, not Ukraine, to surrender to end this war.”

She added that the EU has adopted a new round of sanction listings earlier this week, as “this is the only way to put pressure on Russia.”

She also said that it’s important to send a clear message to Russia that “all these things they are interested in – [unfreezing] frozen assets, lifting the sanctions – are actually up to Europeans to decide.”

“And these things are not given for free for them,” she said.

Germany's Merz calls for stronger support for Ukraine, but warns of 'very, very difficult' budget talks

Germany’s Friedrich Merz also focused on the budget talks, saying they remain “very, very difficult” and expecting them to run well into December.

German chancellor Friedrich Merz speaks to the media as he arrives for a European Council summit in Brussels, Belgium. Photograph: Olivier Matthys/EPA

On Ukraine, he warned that “we are witnessing an increasingly ruthless wave of attacks by Russia,” and said that “we must do everything possible to support Ukraine not just through the winter, but into next year as well.”

“It remains my firm conviction that we are not merely defending Ukraine’s territorial integrity; we are defending the political order of Europe, which is being subjected to intense pressure by Russia in every respect.”

Hungary’s Péter Magyar focused on other issues in his doorstep statement, particularly on migration and the next EU budget.

Hungarian prime minister Péter Magyar arrives for a European Council summit in Brussels, Belgium. Photograph: Olivier Matthys/EPA

But he welcomed the much-awaited law reform in Ukraine that could resolve a longstanding dispute over the rights of the Hungarian minority in western Ukraine, which until now threatened to bloc Kyiv’s accession path.

Ireland's Martin urges Europe to 're-engage' with US, but also calls for 'practical' support for Ukraine with EU accession prospects

Ireland’s Micheál Martin urged Europe to “re-engage” with the US and figure out the best way forward, despite tensions over Ukraine.

“We have had moments in the past when there were challenges and difficulties in respect of this, and it was retrieved by good outreach and engagement from Europe with the US, and I have no doubt that will continue.”

Irish taoiseach Micheal Martin arrives for a European Council summit in Brussels, Belgium. Photograph: Olivier Matthys/EPA

But he said the focus should also be on what the EU can do itself, and that’s primarily to do with advancing Ukraine’s accession process to the bloc.

“I would say to my colleagues across Europe that now is the time to be ambitious over the next number of months to open all clusters in respect of Ukraine and Moldova. The people of Ukraine need that as a very strong signal, given the extraordinary pressure they are under from the Russian attacks on civilians and civilian infrastructure.”

He said “the most fundamental thing we can do in Europe now is to recommit to opening all clusters in respect of Ukraine’s application.” “That would send a powerful signal to the people of Ukraine that Europe is really progressing its pathway to accession,” he said.

The recent attacks are “appalling and horrific, and it illustrates that Putin is not interested in peace.”

“That’s why we must manifest our support for Ukraine in the practical ways … and certainly the membership agenda is one of those.”

'We very much regret this agreement on diesel,' Sweden's Kristersson says

Sweden’s Ulf Kristersson also offered his backing for Ukraine, as he criticised Donald Trump’s diesel deal with Russia.

“We very much regret this agreement on diesel. It’s obviously not a very big part, but still, it’s in the wrong direction. And every step now to help the Russians to increase their the financing of the war is a bad sign.

So we very much stick to Volodymyr Zelenskyy’s view on this.”

This 'senseless war of aggression' only continues because of Putin, Dutch PM says

EU leaders are now arriving for the European Council summit in Brussels and understandably they get asked about the latest situation in Ukraine.

Dutch prime minister Rob Jetten speaks with the media as he arrives for the EU summit at the European Council building in Brussels. Photograph: Harry Nakos/AP

Dutch PM Rob Jetten offered his strong backing for Ukraine and president Volodymyr Zelenskyy, saying “there is only one reason why this senseless war of aggression is continuing, and that’s because Putin chooses to do so.”

“He is the one that can end this war by starting negotiations for just and lasting peace. And as long as he doesn’t, the Ukrainians will have to defend themselves and defend European values, and we stand shoulder to shoulder with the Ukrainians.”

He urged other leaders “to do more financially, militarily, and non-militarily, because a lot of European countries are lagging behind in the support for Ukraine.”

“So let’s step up our game and make sure that they can win this senseless war.”

Starlink satellite restrictions highlight Europe's dependency on US technology

As Ukraine reported problems with Starlink satellite internet terminals and US data that warns of Russian ballistic missile attacks, Kyiv also faced the harsh reality that its European allies cannot offer ⁠any ready-made alternatives, Reuters reported.

A Starlink satellite internet system is set up near the frontline town of Bakhmut amid Russia's attack on Ukraine, Donetsk region. Photograph: Lisi Niesner/Reuters

European countries have voiced strong support for Ukraine and Zelenskyy. But the problems with vital data highlighted that Ukraine and Europe depend heavily ‌on US systems for both low-orbit satellite communications – provided by SpaceX via Starlink – and space-based infrared sensing that feeds into early-warning systems.

A US satellite constellation called the Space-Based Infrared System provides early missile warnings.

As for Starlink, Europe does not have a ready-made alternative that Ukrainian soldiers could attach to their drones, although European governments and companies are working on long-term options.

Shares in European satellite operator Eutelsat soared on Wednesday following news of Ukraine’s difficulties with Starshield, fuelled by ‌investor bets that European providers could benefit from growing strains between Washington and Kyiv.
