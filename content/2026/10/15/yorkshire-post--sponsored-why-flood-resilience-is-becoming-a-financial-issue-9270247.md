# Why flood resilience is becoming a financial issue

![Sally Ashby, Financial Strategy Lead, Ousewem.](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/jpim-static/image/2026/10/06/13/58/Sally-Ashby-Financial-Strategy-Lead-Ousewem.jpeg?trim=167,0,169,0&crop=&width=990&quality=70&enable=upscale)


## When people think about climate resilience, they often think about floods, droughts or extreme weather events. Increasingly, however, the impacts are being felt in boardrooms as well as communities, writes Sally Ashby, Financial Strategy Lead, Ousewem.

When people think about climate resilience, they often think about floods, droughts or extreme weather events. Increasingly, however, the impacts are being felt in boardrooms as well as communities.

For many organisations, resilience is no longer simply an environmental concern. It is becoming an increasingly important business consideration.

Businesses across York and North Yorkshire depend on functioning infrastructure, reliable water resources, resilient supply chains and healthy landscapes. When these systems come under pressure, the impacts are felt far beyond the floodplain.

For many organisations, flooding is no longer the only concern. Water quality, water availability, drought, wildfire risk, environmental regulation and climate adaptation are becoming increasingly important considerations. While these challenges may appear separate, they are often connected through the way water moves across a catchment.

This creates a different conversation about resilience.

The question is no longer simply how we respond to floods when they occur. Instead, organisations are increasingly asking how they can reduce risk, improve resilience and support the places where they operate over the long term.

Natural flood management and nature-based solutions are part of that discussion. Measures such as wetlands, floodplain restoration, woodland creation and improved land management can contribute to a range of wider objectives, from flood resilience and water quality to biodiversity and climate adaptation.

However, the opportunity is not simply about individual projects.

The bigger question is how organisations with shared interests can work together.

Water companies, infrastructure providers, land managers, local authorities and private businesses often face many of the same climate and environmental risks. Historically, those risks have been addressed separately. Increasingly, there is recognition that resilience may require more joined-up thinking.

That means understanding where risks are shared, where benefits overlap and where collaboration can create value that individual organisations cannot achieve alone.

For businesses, this is not about philanthropy. It is about resilience.

The costs of disruption, whether through flooding, drought, supply chain impacts or environmental degradation, are real and growing. Investing in resilience can often be more cost-effective than dealing with the consequences of inaction.

One of the lessons emerging from Ousewem is that resilience works best when evidence, local knowledge and partnership are brought together. The same principle applies to investment.

The challenge now is not whether resilience matters. It is how we create the conditions that allow organisations to invest in it with confidence.

Because climate and water resilience are no longer simply environmental issues. They are increasingly economic ones too.
