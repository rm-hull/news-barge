An ancient iron cauldron which has lain undisturbed beneath a North East river for hundreds of years has been successfully recovered in a major archaeological operation.

The remarkably complete three-legged vessel was lifted from the River Wear at Offerton, near Sunderland, on Sunday morning after months of planning by a team led by Durham University underwater archaeologist Gary Bankhead.

Its significance could be considerable.

![Historical caldron recovered from the bed of the River Wear in Sunderland. Photograph:Stuart Boulton/The Northern Echo](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21575474.jpg?type=mds-article-620)A triumphant Gary Bankhead returned to the shore following the successful operation to recover the cauldron from the River Wear *(Image: STUART BOULTON)*

The cauldron was first discovered last September at a site which has emerged as an important Roman industrial landscape, where more than 800 whetstones have been recovered alongside evidence that they were being produced there.

But the cauldron itself may tell a different chapter in Offerton's history.

Its three-legged form is thought to be consistent with an early medieval date, while a late Iron Age origin also remains a possibility.

A Roman date cannot yet be ruled out.

Experts now hope detailed analysis of the cauldron will finally begin to unlock its secrets.

Mr Bankhead described Sunday's recovery as a "complete success".

"It went like clockwork," he said. "It was very much like a military operation, everybody had a specific job."

![Recovery of a potentially significant ancient iron cauldron from the River Wear at Offerton, Sunderland, led by underwater archaeologist Gary Bankhead.](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21575468.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Archaeologists and divers take part in the recovery operation at Offerton. The flat-bottomed boat and pontoon was supplied free of charge by Swift Water Solutions Limited *(Image: STUART BOULTON)*

The lift, which was months in the planning, had originally been scheduled for Saturday, (October 10), but high winds forced its postponement for 24 hours.

Conditions remained challenging on Sunday, with the winds and a spring tide washing debris into the Wear and reducing underwater visibility to almost zero.

Two divers first located the site by following a previously placed line from the riverbank before attaching a yellow marker buoy to guide the rest of the team.

![Historical caldron recovered from the bed of the River Wear in Sunderland. Photograph:Stuart Boulton/The Northern Echo](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21575464.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Members of the dive team work in the River Wear during the recovery of the Offerton cauldron *(Image: STUART BOULTON)*

Divers then carefully cleared accumulated silt and debris, recorded the cauldron underwater and prepared it for what would be the most delicate part of the operation.

The vessel was manoeuvred into a recovery cage before being slowly winched from the riverbed and brought up through the centre of a moon pool on the recovery craft.

After several hours of preparation work, the cauldron emerged from the Wear at about 11.10am, to the excitement of those involved.

Mr Bankhead spoke of the challenges caused by the visibility, adding: "The fact that we were able to pull it off is a miracle really and we've got something that's incredibly rare and incredibly important."

Despite spending centuries underwater, it appears remarkably complete, retaining three legs and a suspension handle.

Much of its exterior is covered by concretion, a hard mineral and sediment crust which can build up around metal underwater and, in this case, may have helped protect archaeological evidence beneath it.

Its position in a gulley on the riverbed may also have helped protect it.

![Historical caldron recovered from the bed of the River Wear in Sunderland. Photograph:Stuart Boulton/The Northern Echo](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21575454.jpg?type=mds-article-620)The cauldron safely secured aboard the recovery vessel following the successful lift *(Image: STUART BOULTON)*

Mr Bankhead said cauldrons were valuable objects which would have represented a "really important possession" and could have been used in a "high status situation".

Complete examples are rare, making the survival of the Offerton vessel particularly intriguing.

The cauldron was transferred to the University of York on Monday, where specialists will begin the conservation process. Further scientific research will then be undertaken at the Department of Archaeology at Durham University.

Analysis could reveal how the cauldron's legs and handle were attached, evidence of repairs or manufacturing techniques and traces of how the vessel was used.

Researchers will also look for surviving residues which could potentially provide clues to what was once prepared inside it, perhaps even offering a glimpse of its final contents.

They will try to establish who might have used the cauldron and how such an important object came to rest in the Wear.

![Recovery of a potentially significant ancient iron cauldron from the River Wear at Offerton, Sunderland, led by underwater archaeologist Gary Bankhead](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21575611.jpg?type=mds-article-620)A close-up of the remarkably complete cauldron after its recovery from the River Wear *(Image: GARY BANKHEAD)*

Whatever its eventual date, the discovery adds another dimension to an already remarkable archaeological landscape.

As well as 854 Roman whetstones and evidence of their manufacture, finds at Offerton include Roman coins, stone-working tools and 11 stone anchors.

The story of the cauldron is also being recorded comprehensively, from its discovery to the recovery, research and conservation, all the way through to becoming a museum exhibition.

Its original discovery and documentation beneath the river were filmed, Sunday's recovery was captured both above and below the water, and its conservation and scientific investigation will continue to be documented.

It means that, as the concretion is gradually stripped away and the cauldron gives up its secrets, archaeologists will be able to record the journey of an extraordinary object from the bed of the Wear to its eventual interpretation and, potentially, public display.

*The recovery was supported by funding from the International Nautical Archaeology Foundation (INAF), which enabled eight locally based scuba divers to receive specialist training in underwater recording and survey skills through the Nautical Archaeology Society.*

*The team thanked Jed Richardson, operations director at Swift Water Solutions Limited, for providing a flat-bottomed boat and pontoon free of charge for the operation. The equipment played a key role in bringing the cauldron to the surface.*
