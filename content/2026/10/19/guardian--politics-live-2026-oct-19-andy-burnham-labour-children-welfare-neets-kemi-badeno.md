No 10 says US defence secretary Pete Hegseth 'wrong' to say most non-US Nato soldiers in Afghanistan didn't fight

Downing Street has delivered a rare public reprimand to a member of Donald Trump’s administration, saying Pete Hegseth, the US defense secretary, was “wrong” to suggest that most non-American Nato soldiers in Afghanistan did not do any fighting.

Hegseth made the comment in a speech at the University of Tennessee, where he recalled serving in Afghanistan himself. He said:

I was a captain in Afghanistan and we would go to the headquarters in Kabul and there would be 50 flags at the headquarters. It was a beautiful thing to see, 50 different countries contributing to the Nato alliance and the Isaf [International Security Assistance Force] mission there.

Except there were maybe five that contributed. It was actually closer to one or two that by the end were actually involved in kinetic operations. In fact the patch Isaf, we joked it meant ‘I Saw Americans Fighting’.

Asked about Hegseth’s comment at the Downing Street lobby briefing, the PM’s spokesperson said Hegseth was “wrong” in what he said about the Nato operation in Afghanistan.

The spokesperson explained:

The facts speak for themselves when it comes to the UK’s and Nato’s contribution to Afghanistan. Thousands of British and Allied families made huge sacrifices to play their significant part in that war and uphold our values, and more than 450 British personnel never returned.

Their sacrifice and that of other Nato forces was made in the service of collective security and in response to an attack on our ally. We are incredibly proud of our armed forces and their service and sacrifice will never be forgotten, and it is wrong to diminish their role.

While the UK government disagrees with much of what Donald Trump and his team say and do, there is normally a reluctance to criticise them in public. However, this does not seem to apply when the reputation of British servicemen and women has been impugned. Keir Starmer tried hard to maintain a good personal relationship with the US president, but when Trump made a similar claim about Nato allies avoiding frontline fighting in Afghanistan, Starmer condemned what he said as “insulting and frankly appalling”.

Children's commissioner says devolution should be 'clean break' chance to raise standards in services for children

In a speech this afternoon, Dame Rachel de Souza, the children’s commissioner for England, is going to argue that devolution of power to local authorities could help to address some of the problems identified in her Childhood Lottery report. (See 9.34am and 10.05am.)

She is expected to say:

Devolution represents an opportunity. It offers a clean break. The chance for a line in the sand moment. The problems I have identified are not just at a service level – though sometimes they are – they are utterly systemic. The system change that devolution represents is a unique chance to remove the extreme inequality and unfairness I have found.

New powers will mean new structures, and therefore new opportunities to think about, listen to, and deliver for children. Local areas must be provided with support from central government to raise standards for children across health, social care, housing and communities, with urgent action where it falls beyond a set level. This needs to be our response to the emergency.

Given that devolving power from Whitehall to mayors and local authorities is one of Andy Burnham’s top priorities, citing this as the solution to the problems facing children in England is at the very least de Souza being tactful; it might even ensure children’s issues are higher up the agenda in the discussion about what powers should be removed from Whitehall.

But it is also an unusual argument to make in this context because devolution is often seen as a policy that increases the chance of a ‘postcode lottery’ in service provision; a more conventional policy response would be to say that, if there is a “staggering” variety in outcomes, standards should centralised.

Elsom is referring to what could be included in ‘three-for-two’ deals.

We’re determined to set children up for happy, healthy lives, and given obesity costs the NHS £9.3bn pounds a year, it’s clear we need to take action.

Scottish Labour becomes second biggest party at Holyrood after MSP quits Reform UK to sit as independent

Reform UK has lost one of its MSPs in Scotland. Amanda Bland, a former teacher and police officer who is a list MSP for Central Scotland and Lothians West, has left the Reform UK group and will sit as an independent. She has not publicly said why.

At the May elections Reform UK and Scottish Labour came joint second in terms of seats, with both parties having 17 MSPs, behind the SNP’s 58. Bland’s move means Reform UK is now the third biggest party at Holyrood.

A Reform UK spokesperson said:

We can confirm Amanda Bland has resigned as a Reform UK member of the Scottish parliament to sit as an independent MSP.

We are proud of a talented and united team of MSPs at Holyrood who have supported each other at every step of the way.

Amanda Bland should reflect on her reasons for choosing to isolate herself from that team of capable individuals.

It is incumbent on her to resign as a regional MSP for Central Scotland and Lothians West and allow her constituents to be represented by the next Reform candidate on that list.

Jackie Baillie, the deputy leader of Scottish Labour, said:

No one will be surprised to see chaos at the heart of Reform. They have always been a group of chancers, grifters and Tories in disguise.

Despite all the big talk and promises ahead of the election, Nigel Farage’s party has been utterly useless at holding the SNP to account or getting results for the Scottish people, it’s been a series of stunts, gimmicks, photocalls and precious little else.

Scottish Labour is now the second largest party at Holyrood, and we will work to hold the SNP to account and deliver better outcomes for the people of Scotland.

Bland’s move means Malcolm Offord, Reform UK’s leader in Scotland, will lose the right to be the first opposition leader called at first minister’s questions every other week.

While Reform UK enjoyed great success at the elections in May, it has done less well retaining elected representatives. In the Welsh Senedd Sarah Cooper-Lesadddefected last month from Reform UK to Plaid Cymru, the governing party. And, according to a Best for Britain tracker, there are also 63 people elected as Reform UK councillors in May who have either resigned from office, been suspended or left the party.

Cooper claims pensions triple lock in current form still needed because its work has 'still some way to go'

On the triple lock – and these are my views – we are incredibly proud as a party of what the triple lock has achieved. It was our policy implemented in government during coalition. Cooper says claims made about growth benefits of third runway at Heathrow don't 'stack up'

Asked why the Lib Dems were opposed to a third runway at Heathrow when they professed to be in favour of growth, Cooper said she did not accept that a third runway would produce all the benefits its supporters claimed.

She explained:

On Heathrow, it is clear from looking at all of the economic analysis of airport growth over the last few years that many of the promises made by all sorts of different airport companies simply haven’t come to bear.

The promise of higher wage jobs hasn’t been substantiated. Often it’s lower paid jobs, and often not as many as they’ve claimed.

It’s often claimed there’s going to be huge amounts of growth when actually there isn’t.

We’ve seen these inflated claims from airport companies. That’s the same whether it’s Heathrow or Luton airport, which is closer to me. [Cooper is MP for St Albans.] So I just don’t believe that those claims stack up.

Cooper also said a third runway would be “really bad for the climate” and that she had not seen evidence showing how a third runway could go ahead without carbon emission targets being breached.

Lib Dems call for £14bn 'breathing space budget' with fuel bill cuts, tax cuts for hospitality, and cheaper transport

Daisy Cooper, the Lib Dem deputy leader and Treasury spokesperson, is holding a press conference now.

She says the Lib Dems are urging John Healey, the chancellor, to deliver “a £14bn breathing space budget” next week, saving households £350 on average over the next year and a half.

She says these plans could be “fully funded by windfall taxes on the big banks, a clawback on the energy network monopolies and using the higher tax receipts triggered by inflation”.

The plan has three elements.

On energy, Cooper says:

For the next year and a half, we would introduce an essential energy guarantee, cutting average annual electricity and gas bills by £100, alongside additional support for households with children and disabled people.

On hospitality, she says:

For the next year and a half, we would cut hospitality VAT from 20% to 15%, giving pubs, restaurants and cafes room to keep prices down, protect jobs and stay open. It would make a meal with the kids, coffee with a friend or a pint at the local, just a little bit more affordable.

And, on travel, she says:

For an initial three months, we would cut fuel duty by 10%, cut rail fares by 10%, introduce £1 bus fares and cut VAT on kerbside EV charging to 5%.

Explaining how the Lib Dems would fund these measures, she says:

Our essential energy guarantee would be paid for by clawing back excess support handed to monopoly energy operators, and using that money to cut household bills instead.

Our hospitality package would be funded by a one-off, time-limited windfall tax on the unexpected and unearned profits of the big banks.

And our emergency travel package would be funded through additional Treasury receipts generated through higher prices at the petrol pump triggered by Trumpflation [price rises caused by Trump’s Iran war] because when motorists pay more at the pump, the Treasury shouldn’t get a windfall.

Burnham pledges ‘big changes’ to the way the welfare system works to tackle Neets crisis

Good morning. Children and young people are at the top of the agenda today, with the publication of a hard-hitting report from Dame Rachel de Souza, the children’s commissioner for England, and a Times article by Andy Burnham. They are focusing on slightly different cohorts – Burnham is writing about young people who leave school and enter the benefits system, while the de Souza reports covers the experiences of young children in multiple aspects of their lives – but their overall message is the same: the younger generation is being let down.

De Souza has studied more than 160 datasets to examine how what happens to children as they grow up varies across England and she says the variation is “staggering”. In her report, The Childhood Lottery, she explains:

In four local authorities, the total amount spent on youth services per teenager is the princely sum of £0, £421 less than in the highest spending local authority. Some areas of the country are sending nearly 90% of their looked after children to out-of-area placements, compared to the almost 20% in the areas making least use of these – although given this means being away from family ties, and friends, that is still worryingly high.

But what stands out is not merely these differences, but how much work there is to do to deliver a happy, healthy childhood in every postcode. I have made the difficult decision to highlight the 20 places where it is hardest to grow up. I want to be clear that this is not a review of the local authority, this is an assessment based on a wide range of measures looking at the challenges faced, the full range of services available and the outcomes we see.

We cannot shy away, though, from the fact that poor education outcomes, long health waiting lists or simply feeling unsafe converge in particular places.

The report identifies Middlesbrough as the most challenging place to be a child, and Cheshire East as the least challenging place. I will publish fuller tables shortly.

The overall picture is grim too. Here is an extract from the report’s summary.

The data pulled together in the Childhood Lottery highlights the scale of challenges faced by children across all areas of the country and all areas of their lives. For example that:

-Over 1 million children (1,048,965) had active referrals to children and young people’s mental health services in 2024-25.1

-Most pupils (55%) do not achieve a good standard in their English and Maths GCSEs (grade 5 or better in both) at the end of key stage 4.

-There are around 1 million young people in the UK aged 16 to 24 not in employment, education or training (NEET)

-Approaching half (44%) of looked after children are placed outside their local authority area. Far too many care leavers (40%, plus 6% with unknown activity) are Neets [not in education, employment or training].

-12% of children said they did not feel safe in their local area.

The Burnham article is specifically about Neets. The government was expected to publish Alan Milburn’s final report with recommendations on Neets before the budget. Now it is coming next month, which means the pitch-rolling process ahead of publication is unusually protracted. Milburn has given an interview to the Guardian and, as Anna Bawden and Kiran Stacey report, he is explicit about wanting some benefit payments to be cut to fund more employment support.

In his article, Burnham accepts that many people people today feel “shut out”.

There is no shortage of people lining up to take a swing at the next generation but, if we are honest, the blame for this failure lies with politicians. At the very moment growing up became harder, the support young people relied on was stripped away. Youth services were practically abolished. Children’s mental health services were cut. Families were left to fight for support for children with special educational needs and disabilities.

The pandemic stole two years, and social media is too often taking something much deeper: their confidence and sense of belonging. Add an education system focused on the university route and a job market offering too few opportunities, and we should not be surprised that so many are shut out.

And he signals that he is going to follow Milburn’s recommendations on benefits.

Today, the welfare system provides an assessment, a payment and, after that, a goodbye. Too often there is no support, no offer of skills and no opportunity to get a job …

This will be our offer: a clear guarantee of support. But it will also be matched by an expectation that young people will take up the opportunities made available to them. This will mean big changes to the way our welfare system operates. Benefits will become the backstop, not the starting point. The system will no longer begin by establishing which benefits a young person is entitled to. It will begin with their ambitions and the help they need to achieve them.
