Jean Alexander was one of Britain’s biggest TV stars with millions of fans.

She was also Auntie Jean to Sonia Hearld who lives in Burn near Selby. This month marks the 100th anniversary of her birth in Liverpool.

Sonia has been talking about having a famous relative who loved to spend Christmas with the family in Burn.

**You can listen to the chat on this week’s Sunday Late Show on YorkMix Radio here.** (it starts 30 mins in)

Jean Alexander was born in Toxteth, Liverpool, on October 11th, 1926.

Her father worked as an electrician and the family lived in a terraced house with no indoor lavatory. She had an elder brother, Kenneth.

She aspired to become an actress from an early age, and later said that she was inspired by variety acts she saw at the Pavilion Theatre in her home city.

Jean Alexander spent five years as a library assistant in Liverpool before she began her acting career in 1949 at the Adelphi Guild Theatre in Macclesfield. She later worked in Rep in York living in a flat in what is now The Grange Hotel on Bootham.

Most of her parts were minor and she also worked as a wardrobe mistress and stage manager.

Jean first appeared in Coronation Street in 1962 in a minor role as a landlady.

Two years later, she returned to the programme as Hilda Ogden. The character, married to Stan in the series, became very popular with viewers. Of course in real life she was totally different and fans were often surprised to hear her own voice which sounded nothing like Hilda.

In 1988, Alexander made a guest appearance in the long-running BBC comedy series Last of the Summer Wine as Auntie Wainwright, the money-grabbing local junk shop owner.

She returned for a second guest appearance in 1989, and finally became a series regular in 1992, remaining until the end of the series in 2010.

She also played a few roles in the North York Moors police drama, Heartbeat,

**Here’s what Sonia had to say in her chat with David Dunning**.

“Jean was my father’s younger sister. They grew up together in Liverpool, and he went into teaching,and she went into theatre eventually and television, obviously.

“She wasn’t really on the TV very much until I was probably in my early teens. She did a few small parts in Z Cars and a children’s programme called Badger’s Bend that some very old people might remember. She played the mum in that, but mostly she was working in theatre.

“I do remember being about six or seven and going to the Scala Theatre in Southport and seeing her on stage. She also played in York too.

“She loved being in York. Had a lot of friends that she kept from being in rep there, but that was sort of before I moved over the Pennines to live this side of the Great Divide.

“(In the 1960s) It was a treat to be allowed to stay up to watch it at half seven (Coronation Street) but I don’t have very clear memories. It was strange because she was Auntie Jean, that we saw when we went to grandma’s house, getting ready to go out with her friends, and then suddenly there was this very different person on the television.

“She was very glamorous when she was going out, you know, big underskirts and lots of lipstick and perfume and things like that.

“She was a very private person, very self-contained, very determined to do what she wanted to do. She had a will of iron when she wanted to learn to do something. She just quietly got on with things, and she wasn’t one for the razzmatazz.

“The only times I’ve seen her really nervous were when she had to be herself at something like an awards do. She much preferred being behind a character.

**Sonia says that although she liked to keep herself to herself, Jean Alexander was great fun to be with.But she hated speculation in the press about her private life and any love interests.**

“She was not amused by it. It was very much a Queen Victoria style we are not amused, and it’s none of their business she would say. Doesn’t have anything to do with what I do for my job, and who’s interested anyway?

“She and my dad, when they were staying with us here in Burn, they used to just make us crack out loud laughing because they were always having a go at each other and just chuntering on about things and being really silly, and she had a wicked sense of humour.

“I think she just thought it was rather amusing because she’d sort of laugh with me and say, it’s a bit of a far cry from the back streets of Liverpool. I mean, she and my dad grew up in a fairly poor district, and you know, attended the local primary school where kids had hardly any shoes and all the rest of it. And of course, they both had to go through the war. And she just found it quite amusing that suddenly she was recognised and approached, and people knew who she was.

“I remember being in York with her once, and I think we were crossing somewhere. Near one of the Bar Walls, somebody stopped her in the middle of the road and said, “It is you! It is you, isn’t it? And she said, “Well, it won’t be if we stand here because we’re going to get run over. But she was always really really good with fans and people who approached her.

“The only time she turned people away was when I was once having a meal with her and she just said, “Look, I’ll talk to you in a minute, but my soup’s getting cold. Can I just finish it?”

“She wasn’t too keen on Hilda. She found her a bit abrasive, but she loved being Auntie Wainwright (Last of the Summer Wine) and luring people into her antique shop.”

**Sonia told us about how much Jean liked to come to Burn, near Selby.**

“She would probably come here a couple of times a year, at least. She obviously came to switch our Christmas lights on back in 2009, I think it was the first one, and then she tended to come most years round about Christmas.

“She’d go to the pub with us, and we’d go into York. Villagers found it a bit sort of strange. You’d see people walk into the Wheatsheaf Inn (Burn) and sort of do a double take and say, “Is that really? Yes, it is. It was good fun and it was good for the village. It sort of boosted the publicity that we get for this little place.

“It’s very strange to see her on the television, but know, she’s not really there. However it’s comforting in a way that she’s gone but not forgotten.”
