China should build a “fertility-friendly society” with policies supporting marriage, childbirth and childrearing, the country’s State Council has pledged.

Amid a backdrop of plummeting birth rates and marriage registrations, there are fears the world’s second-most populous nation will see huge strain placed on its social insurance system with fewer babies being born each year.

At a State Council meeting today (October 23), policymakers vowed to offer more support to families to stabilise the birth rate, which in 2025 fell for the fourth consecutive year to a record low.

The number of marriage registrations ​also ⁠dropped by about 7.5% ‌in the first half of 2026 compared with the same period last year, according to official data.

![China's State Council has pledged to redirect tax and housing policies to support its vision for a 'fertility-friendly' society](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/23/16/2254131309...jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The government should focus on optimising tax, social security and housing to encourage couples to have families, the council body said, ​according ⁠to a readout aired by state television.

The State Council's pledges are ‌a sign that policymakers are concerned about ‌the strain a shrinking and ageing population could put on the country's social insurance system and overall economic growth, which has been weighed down by weak ⁠domestic consumer demand.

"Population is the foundational condition for economic and social development," officials said in the meeting, calling for efforts so that "high-quality population development" could better support "high-quality economic and social development."

Authorities have in recent years stepped up fiscal support for boosting births, including rolling out a nationwide childcare subsidy programme and waiving or cutting ‌pre-school tuition fees.

They have also vowed to foster "positive attitudes towards ​marriage and childbearing".

![For decades, Chinese families were banned from having more than one child - and the effects are still being felt](https://static.independent.co.uk/2026/10/23/16/2254131186...jpg?quality=75&width=640&auto=webp)

The cabinet meeting also called for ‌making basic public services more ⁠equal, echoing the government's moves to reform a decades-long household ⁠registration system so that workers who migrated from rural farms to cities can ‌access more urban ​public services, including enrolling their children ‌in public schools in the city.

For decades, China’s strict one-child policy attempted to curb extreme population growth by limiting families to just one baby.

It was brought in by Deng Xiaoping in 1980 after China’s population exploded from 540 million in 1949 to around 969 million.

The policy was credited for being a key factor in China’s economic boom in the last 20th century, but was often cruelly enforced, with punishments for violators scoping from hefty fines to forcible abortions and brutal sterilisation of mothers.

It also skewed towards more boys being born than girls, with widespread sex-selective abortions and female infanticide both in rural areas and in China’s cities.

While the one-child policy officially ended in January 2016 in favour of a two-child cap - which was lifted to three children in May 2021 - in 2025, just 7.92 million babies were born, a 17% decrease from the previous year.
