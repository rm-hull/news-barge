# Bishop Burton, East Yorkshire: The most distinguished Burton village of them all?

## A hatful of villages in Yorkshire, stretching from Burton in Lonsdale, about ten miles from the Irish Sea, to Burton Pidsea, a few miles from the North Sea, have names containing Burton, Old English for fortified farmstead.

At the foot of the slopes on the eastern fringe of the Yorkshire Wolds, a couple of miles west of Beverley, perhaps the most distinguished-sounding member of the Burton family, Bishop Burton, was – before the Archbishop of York established a manor there in the Middle Ages – simply known as South Burton.

Descending into the village from the southwest, the village feels sheltered, which may have been partly behind the location being selected to establish a medieval deer park. The boundary of the park, known as The Reins, remains and has been described as “the most impressive earthwork of its kind in the East Riding”. However, most of the grounds have been repurposed by specialist land-based Bishop Burton College, where more than 1,000 students are enrolled in higher education and other courses, ranging from basic tractor driving to canine behaviour management, and boasting high-tech livestock facilities, including dedicated pig, beef, and sheep units.

The college has been deeply integrated with the community for some 70 years, with students providing a much-needed boost to the local economy as well as providing facilities for groups such as Bishop Burton United Football Club.

Despite being bordered by open rolling farmland, the 890-acre campus to the north of the village, together with the busy Hull to York road, which bisects Bishop Burton, gives the settlement a busy feel. The campus is also a venue for numerous events that draw visitors to the village from far and wide, ranging from The Postcard Traders Association’s fair to equestrian tournaments to tractor runs.

The college, marked by the red brick and half-timbered entrance lodge to the long-since demolished High Hall, stands opposite a grazing field in a village with evidence aplenty of its agricultural roots. On North End, there’s the red brick 18th-century North End Farmhouse and Old Farmhouse and a similar aged orange-bricked Callais Farmhouse at the junction of Callas and Mill Lane, as well as the Georgian Eastfield and Westfield farmhouses on The Green.

Overlooking one of Bishop Burton’s most outstanding features, the spring-fed Mere, is the site of a washdyke, where farmers washed sheep ahead of shearings, marked by a Millennium Stone near benches at the west end of School Green. The numerous mature trees in the area are key to the village’s character.

On a promontory next to the mere stands a tall sandstone ashlar Latin cross on a plinth and stepped base, which, according to Historic England, pays tribute to “the tragic impacts of world events on this community”, with a wall behind the mere displaying names of several local men killed in the First World War.

As well as a home for moorhens and ducks and bordered by rushes, sedges, iris and a low white painted fence, the idyllic village pond is visited by nesting geese, terns from the Arctic in summer and wintering cormorants. Some of the birds move between the mere and Johnson’s Pond on the equally manicured The Green, beside which stands a former blacksmith’s forge, a Grade II listed cottage featuring three 12-pane unequal sashes, oversailing eaves and end stacks, which, like many other historic properties in the village, has white paint covering bricks.

Whitewashed country pub The Altisidora, off the A1079 and beside the Mere and buildings dating from the 15th century, offers numerous dishes for those counting calories as well as its indulgent 1,822-calorie mixed grill and 979-calorie melting chocolate dome.

Other common architectural features in the former estate village include rustic porches, gabled dormers and stout chimneys, with many examples on Finkle Street, Bryan Mere, Pudding Gate and the winding Church Lane.

Over the latter route, All Saints’ Church tower has dominated since the 13th century, while it has been a site for worship since the eighth century. The church has a weeping ash outside and has a plaque commemorating 17 airmen who were killed while flying to or from Beverley Aerodrome, which was built on Bishop Burton’s parish boundary at Mount Pleasant Farm, as well as a section of Beverley Racecourse. The memorial also expresses gratitude to the vicar and women from the village who cared for airmen at the First World War base.

The church’s treasures include a small 12th-century figure, with long, hanging sleeves, carved into the nave’s south wall and an alabaster chest tomb to a member of the Gee family, which owned High Hall in the 17th century.

Lawyer Sir William Gee, the son of a Hull merchant turned banker, bought the Bishop Burton estate in 1603 after marrying one of the daughters of Matthew Hutton, Archbishop of York, and when Hutton became de facto head of the Council in the North, Gee was handed the recordership of Beverley, an archepiscopal liberty, before being appointed Recorder of Hull. In 1604 he petitioned the Council in the North on behalf of the Hull Corporation to help finance relief for people infected by plague.

Withstanding the proximity of Beverley, a deep sense of pride in the community continues in the village, which benefits from a popular general store and post office and a spacious village hall despite the parish population dropping to about 660. In 1934, architect Humphrey Packington ranked Bishop Burton amongst England’s 15 “fairest villages”.

Ninety-two years later, the community’s website states: “We believe it still is.”

Recent years have seen, following a suggestion by a child resident, part of the green by Cold Harbour View transformed into a wildflower meadow, encouraging biodiversity.
