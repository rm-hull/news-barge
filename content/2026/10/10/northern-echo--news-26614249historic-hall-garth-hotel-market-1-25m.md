For more than seven centuries, Hall Garth was a country estate at the heart of County Durham life before becoming a hotel, spa and wedding venue.

The historic hall in Coatham Mundeville, near Darlington, dates to medieval times and has housed bishops, clergymen, railway pioneers and industrialists.

Hall Garth’s story began in the late 13th century with a hunting lodge believed to have been used by Anthony Bek, Prince Bishop of Durham.

The heavily forested area offered prime hunting grounds, while its proximity to the bishop’s palace in Darlington made it a convenient retreat.

The original lodge has vanished, but traces of it are believed to survive.

A section of wall and an arch are believed to date from the period, providing a rare link to Hall Garth’s medieval past.

Most of the house standing today dates from the 15th century.

The oldest section forms the building’s western side and includes a 17th-century staircase, twisted balustrade and Roman Doric doorway.

Successive owners reshaped Hall Garth over the centuries.

Wings added in the 18th and 19th centuries brought wood panelling, decorative fireplaces and Georgian and Victorian flourishes.

The building still shows the marks of its changing fortunes.

Two first-floor windows remain blocked up, a legacy of the Window Tax, which charged homeowners for the number of windows in their properties.

A small deer house dating from the late 1700s still stands in the parkland, while nearby fields preserve medieval ridge-and-furrow remains.

Dean Beck once partly encircled the estate, running from Heighington to the River Skerne and famed for its trout.

Darlington fishmongers even advertised the trout as “Hall Garth Trout”.

Durham gentleman William Wilson was Hall Garth’s earliest recorded resident, in 1684.

The Dalton family later owned the hall before it was bought by the Rev James Robson, vicar of Great Aycliffe from 1773 to 1805.

After the Robson family, local mill owner’s widow Elizabeth Porthouse bought the estate before it passed to Henry Pascoe Smith.

Smith helped shape the Hall Garth seen today.

The Victorian railway director turned Hall Garth into a grand gentleman’s residence and landscaped its grounds.

Stone sleepers from the region’s earliest railways were built into features around the estate.

In the 1890s, Hall Garth was owned by Henry Carrick, a director of the Wear Valley Railway who was important in extending the line from Frosterley up to Stanhope.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21548302.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Railway engineer Henry Carrick at Hall Garth in the 1890s. *(Image: JONATHAN PEACOCK)*

Carrick came from Haltwhistle, Northumberland, where his father was a wine and spirit merchant, and served his apprenticeship with Robert Stephenson & Company in Newcastle.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21548301.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Elizabeth Carrick, the daughter of a mayor of Gateshead, with some of her family at Hall Garth. Lizzie married Henry Carrick in 1861 and was with him in his India days. She is the great-great-aunt of Jonathan Peacock *(Image: JONATHAN PEACOCK)*

He sold Hall Garth on to Robert Bradley Summerson, whose family had a large foundry.

![](https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/resources/images/21548303.jpg?type=mds-article-620)Hall Garth in Coatham Mundeville in Henry Carrick's day. *(Image: JONATHAN PEACOCK)*

Thomas Summerson began work aged 14 in 1824, drilling stone sleepers for the Stockton and Darlington Railway before acquiring the Albert Hill Foundry, which specialised in railway fittings.

Four generations of Summersons lived at Hall Garth for around 70 years before selling it in 1976; their foundry went into liquidation in 1972.

Hall Garth was converted into a hotel in 1977.

North England Estates has owned Hall Garth since 2012.

The business includes 56 en-suite bedrooms, a restaurant, bar, function suites, meeting rooms and parking for more than 150 vehicles.

The sale also includes the recently refurbished Revive Spa, with a gym, sauna, steam room, hot tubs and treatment rooms.

Hall Garth earns money from weddings, conferences, corporate bookings, leisure guests, spa memberships and year-round events.
