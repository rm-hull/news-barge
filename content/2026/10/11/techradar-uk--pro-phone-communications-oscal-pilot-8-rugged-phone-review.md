## Oscal Pilot 8: 30-second review

Rugged phones usually make one promise: they will survive. Everything else after that tends to be an afterthought or, on some devices, even a distraction.

The Oscal Pilot 8 tries to change that narrative somewhat, while remaining reasonably tough. It uses a new Qualcomm Dragonwing QCM6690 chip instead of the usual MediaTek SoC. It includes a 6.83-inch AMOLED screen, WiFi 7, and a 10,000mAh battery. But its best trick is fitting all that tech into a body just 17.9mm thick and weighing just 389g.

That makes for a practical, rugged design that fits in a normal pocket, and with a 108MP primary camera, it can capture detailed images and 4K video at up to 60fps.

Given these mostly positive aspects, it's reasonable to expect a high price, but the quoted launch price was just $356.99. That was a special offer, and the retail cost is higher, but it still isn’t expensive for what is a decent specification.

Is this one of the best rugged phones for those out in the field who have to choose between durability and performance? Maybe, but read my full review before parting with cash.

At the time of writing, the only retailer I could get this phone from was AliExpress, and the price was $356.99 for those in the USA, £343.39 for UK delivery, and €402,39 for European buyers.

Considering the SoC, AMOLED display and 108MP camera, that looks like a remarkably competitive price. I think there was originally going to be another SKU with 16GB of RAM and 512GB of storage, but evidently memory and storage prices put a spanner in that works.

The WP210 is much closer on spec and price, and it's an alternative if you want more storage, but it has a slightly smaller 8800 mAh battery.

Given the recent price increases we’ve seen in phones, the Pilot 8 seems like a good deal, but it would help if it were more widely available.

## Oscal Pilot 8: Design

* **Clean design**
* **No wireless charging**
* **Bright AMOLED display**

![Oscal Pilot 8 rugged smartphone](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/wEp8oGKcChBRrbAjQe3wVK-1200-80.jpg)

The Pilot 8 measures 178.7 by 85.4 by 17.9mm and weighs 389g. For a phone with a 10,000mAh battery, that is relatively slim and lightweight by rugged phone standards.

The frame is aluminium alloy with reinforced corners made of a stiff TPU polymer. The back panel is transparent acrylic, through which you can see some appropriately high-tech-looking Oscal graphics.

Given how flat most of the back is, I expected wireless charging, but alas, the Pilot 8 budget didn’t stretch to that feature.

The quoted robustness protection is IP68 and IP69K, with MIL STD 810H certification. But equally, the drop rating is only 1.2 metres, which is on the modest end of the scale for rugged phones.

Many rivals claim 1.5 metres or more, so the Pilot 8 might not be the ideal choice for those who are inherently clumsy. To be clear, I’m not saying it's fragile by any gauge, but other phones do quote longer drop resistance.

Another reason to think along those lines is the screen protection. It is CSG KK9 glass, a technology that Oscal says is up to eight times more scratch-resistant. That might be true, but it's not Gorilla Glass if you specifically want a phone with that technology.

While memory and storage prices have ballooned, displays seem to be getting cheaper by the day. That's why we’ve got a sub-$400 phone with a 6.83-inch AMOLED panel at 1280 by 2772 pixels, a screen density of 446ppi.

The panel has a 120Hz refresh rate, and touch sampling is 240Hz. And because I'm reviewing it, I must mention that it has Widevine L1, so it supports 1080p streaming from Netflix and Disney. Hooray!

![Oscal Pilot 8 rugged smartphone](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/pesZgti5t4YUDXmDypcnVK-1200-80.jpg)

The screen looks bright in use, with Oscal quoting 600 nits typical and 2000 nits peak. Those figures are far apart, and some secondary sources list 600 nits as the maximum. My testing proved inconclusive on this point, but 2000 nits does seem implausibly high.

What I can say is that even in bright sunlight, the screen on the Pilot 8 is significantly more readable than most mobile phones.

What’s good about this design is that it's chock-full of practical touches, some of which are more useful than others. One curious one I discovered in the settings menu was a function to clear dust and water from the rear speaker.

It also includes glove mode and rain touch modes for the screen. The side key can launch the torch, the underwater camera, PTT, or an SOS alert, and the power button can launch the digital assistant or the camera.

The only feature I wasn’t keen on was the tiny rubber bung that Oscal used to protect the USB-C port, which rapidly started looking like a prime candidate to be ripped off and discarded.

**Design score: 4/5**

## Oscal Pilot 8: Hardware

* **Qualcomm QCM6690**
* **256GB of UFS 2.2 storage**
* **10000 mAh battery**

The Qualcomm QCM6690 SoC is this phone's headline feature, a relatively new Chip that first appeared in late August 2025. It is a 4nm octa-core chip with an Adreno 7 series GPU, and was the world's first enterprise mobile processor with fully integrated ultra-high frequency (UHF RAIN) RFID capabilities built directly on-chip.

DevCheck reports clock speeds from 480 to 2965MHz. That matches Oscal's claim of up to 2.9GHz, although this chip might clock slightly faster on other platforms.

Based on my benchmarking, this is an upper mid-range chip, not a flagship. Oscal's flagship description is therefore a stretch.

Oscal quotes an AnTuTu score of 1,151,934. Declaring it to be 2.7 times a MediaTek Helio G100, with a CPU score of 466,080 and a GPU score of 201,028. The comparison chip is a modest target, so the gap flatters the Pilot 8. A fairer rival would be the Dimensity 8300 or even the 8400.

![Oscal Pilot 8 rugged smartphone](https://cdn.mos.cms.futurecdn.net/Q3JEVL3J3GEUs95E2DyVVK-1200-80.jpg)

In the Pilot 8, the SoC is wired to 12GB of RAM, which is enough for most smartphone purposes. I do wish Oscal wouldn’t confuse customers by emblazoning the box art with 48GB in large letters, and then in much smaller letters “12GB+36GB”. Oscal adds 36GB of virtual RAM to reach 48GB, and Virtual RAM borrows storage space and therefore is not memory. I wish phone makers wouldn't do this, but many do

Storage is 256GB of UFS 2.2 with microSD support up to 2TB. UFS 2.2 is an older standard, so expect decent rather than rapid speeds, but at around 1000Mb/s, it’s fine.

The card shares a slot with the second SIM, so you will need to choose between two physical SIMs or an onboard microSD. However, the phone supports an eSIM, so you can easily circumvent this limitation to enable dual phone numbers and extra storage.

Compared with some rugged designs I could mention, the Pilot 6 is oriented towards being a replacement for an ordinary phone. But being light enough to carry and scaled to fit in a jacket pocket means the battery is only 10,000mAh. That’s not huge, even if it's double what a standard phone might expect to get.

Charging is 55W wired, with 5W reverse charging for topping up earbuds and small gadgets. Oscal claims 43 hours of calls, 24 hours of music, 11 hours of gaming and 1080 hours of standby. The standby figure works out to be an impressive 45 days, enough for any wilderness trip gone completely wrong.

The only issue I ran into with charging was that my 100W chargers didn’t charge this phone at 55W, only about half that amount, and there is no charger in the box, at least in Europe.

Overall, the hardware has good points, with a few that could be better.

* **Hardware score: 4/5**

## Oscal Pilot 8: Cameras

* **108MP and 20MP on the rear**
* **50MP on the front**
* **Three cameras in total**

The Oscal Pilot 8 has three cameras:

**Rear cameras:** 108MP Samsung ISOCELL HM6 , 20MP Sony IMX376  
**Front camera:** 50MP Samsung ISOCELL JN1

The main camera is a 108MP Samsung ISOCELL HM6 on a 1/1.67 inch sensor with an f/1.9 lens. The front camera is a 50MP ISOCELL JN1. A 20MP Sony IMX376 night vision camera sits alongside the main sensor, backed by infrared lights.

My review sample is the standard model. So it has no thermal camera or thermal app, but it does have a night vision sensor that works well within the range of the IR illumination.

Having the JN1 sensor as the selfie camera seems overkill, but it's nice if you need the resolution for still or video captures.

This is a strong camera lineup, even without a macro or depth queue sensor, since the HM6's 6P optics offer plenty of zoom levels, even if you can’t get especially close to small subjects. Video capture on the primary sensor is also good, with up to 4K resolution and 60 fps possible – on both rear and front-facing sensors.

The only major issue I had with the camera was that the app would, on occasion, lock up, and the only way to get it working again was to reset the phone. I’m not sure if this is a bug with Doke 5.0 (built from Android 16) or some other problem, but this happened twice while I was using the Pilot 8.

And, for whatever reason, taking pictures seems to take longer than it should on a phone this powerful. It takes at least two and a half seconds before the app resets after capturing an image to be ready to take another one.

I hope Oscal issues a fix for these, because both are annoying, and before anyone suggests it, deleting the cache or storage for the photo app doesn’t resolve either.

My testing results were good, even though I did experience a few glitches.

## Oscal Pilot 8: Final verdict

If the price holds and availability improves, the Pilot 8 makes a strong case. The Qualcomm chip performs nearly as well as a Dimensity 8300 in my testing, and that’s a decent level to hit. That lifts it above the usual rugged crowd that uses cheaper SoCs. The battery should get you through a full workweek of light use, and the body is slim enough to fit in a pocket.

On the flip side, the drop rating is modest, and the brightness claims seem optimistic. And, update support is largely an unknown. Unlike some other brands, Blackview (Oscal) doesn’t maintain a public, centralised chronological archive or changelog history of its firmware updates.

Some Blackview devices, like the XPLORE series, do tend to get more updates than more budget devices. So if I bought the Pilot 8, I’d be surprised if it saw any Android version above what it has now, but it might get some tweaks and security patches. The last security update that my review hardware got was dated July 5th, 2026.

With most Chinese brands, this isn’t unexpected, but it's worth keeping in mind if you plan to keep hardware for more than a handful of years.

In general, this is a decent device with a punchy SoC, good cameras and a lovely display, and getting all those things at this price point in 2026, if you can find it, is a bonus.

It will be interesting to see if the Thermal Camera and Satellite versions of the Pilot 8 are as good as the standard model when they arrive.

## Buy it if...

**You need 4K video**   
The sensors on this phone are capable of capturing 4K at 60 fps, and that includes both the primary rear-facing sensor and the front-facing camera. Therefore, if you need 4K content to document your adventures, the Pilot 8 seems a good choice. Just be aware that it only has 256GB of storage, so you might want to also invest in a 1TB microSD card.

**You need a phone for outdoors**  
The water and dust resistance on the Pilot 8 is enough to handle limited submersion and drops. It’s also not too large to fit in a pocket, or excessively heavy. Compared with some rugged designs, this is one of the more practical options.

## Don't buy it if...

**You need extended battery life**  
While 10000 mAh of battery capacity might seem plenty for a standard phone, some rugged designs have double this amount or even more. If you are intending to use the phone to charge other devices, then the battery capacity of the Pilot 8 and its 5W reverse charging isn’t ideal.

**You want macro photography**  
While the 6P optics on the primary camera allow for plenty of zoom levels, you can’t get especially close to subjects with it. There is no specific macro mode, and photographing a flower or insect at close quarters would be a real challenge. If you do close-up photography, this is not the phone for you.
