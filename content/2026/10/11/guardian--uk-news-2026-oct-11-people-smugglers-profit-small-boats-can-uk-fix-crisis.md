The smuggler works quickly. Using a small shovel, he digs up a dinghy buried in a sand dune on a beach not far from Boulogne-sur-Mer, assembles it and affixes a motor in record time.

“I never saw anyone work like this,” says Amad, from Iraq, one of a group of about 30 asylum seekers waiting to cross the Channel. “The UK government wants to stop the smugglers, but they are smart. They have plan A, plan B and plan C, so there is always a way for them to get people across this sea. For as long as asylum seekers will pay them, there is too much money involved for them to give up.”

The wind has dropped, the waves are flat and there are no French police in sight. The conditions are perfect as the smuggler rushes everyone into the dinghy as it moves away from the shore.

Amad is one of several asylum speakers who spoke to the Guardian this week from the area of northern France known as the Jungle. They were hoping to cross the Channel to the UK in the next few days.

“This place is busier than ever,” says another asylum seeker, Omar, from Yemen. “So many refugees and so many smugglers are here. Everyone wants to cross.”

![Police officers stand guard on the beach in Petit-Fort-Philippe, Gravelines, near Calais.](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/18f1370cd81755b824ba8a382352190135446047/0_0_4738_3163/master/4738.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

Some who have already been forcibly removed from the UK are hoping to return. They were sent to France under the “one in, one out” scheme, where one small-boat arrival is forcibly returned to France in exchange for another asylum seeker brought legally to the UK.

Now, after moving approximately 1,500 people in both directions across the Channel, the scheme has been brought to an end. Beset with problems, it could not be effectively scaled up, the British government concluded.

NGOs working on the ground with asylum seekers in northern France were unsurprised by the outcome and say the end of “one in, one out” must prompt a wider discussion about how to deal with the crisis in the Channel.

The UK government has shown little sign it is contemplating a radical shift in attitude. A Home Office spokesperson pointed the Guardian to an announcement about a refugee resettlement scheme that is likely to assist only a few hundred refugees a year and is highly unlikely to stop people crossing the Channel in small boats.

The spokesperson said: “Just last week, the home secretary announced the reopening of the UK resettlement scheme for new referrals, providing genuine refugees with a safe and legal route to the UK.”

The agencies calling for a different approach hope at least to expand the parameters of the debate.

![People in a bus wrapped in foil blankets with a sign that reads ‘region Hauts-de-France’](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/33e429e4aeb0cc19757a0c1a17e9a3ec9d850e32/0_0_4533_3022/master/4533.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

According to workers in the Jungle, living conditions there are appalling. Those at Utopia 56, which witnesses police interventions on French beaches where asylum seekers try to board dinghies, say that since the UK increased its funding to France earlier this year on a payment-by-results basis, police violence against migrants has increased significantly.

Utopia 56 estimates a 40% increase in police violence from April to August 2026 compared with the same period the previous year. That coincided with an agreement for the UK to pay France £660m over three years to ramp up “stop the boats” initiatives.

Sophie McCann, of Médecins Sans Frontières UK, said: “The government’s increasingly brutal efforts to stop people trying to reach the UK by boat are failing. MSF’s medical and mental health teams in Calais and Dunkirk witness the serious physical and psychological harm caused by violence and harassment meted out by French police, largely funded by the UK, on people who have already fled war, persecution and extreme hardship.

“People will continue to risk their lives crossing the Channel in search of safety in the UK until the government provides safe and accessible routes.”

The Public and Commercial Services (PCS) union, which has many Home Office staff among its members, has worked with the refugee charity Care4Calais to draw up a blueprint to introduce a refugee visa-to-travel scheme for asylum seekers to travel legally to the UK.

The plan would still require asylum claims to be considered on merit and for those whose claims are rejected to be removed from the UK. But they say it would eliminate people smugglers from the equation and save lives. According to the UN’s International Organization for Migration (IMO), at least 225 people have died while trying to cross the Channel since 2018.

![Three women wear red blankets over their heads and shoulders](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/1899bb764c1f52e378fe79364a6d98e7f5047541/0_0_5273_3516/master/5273.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

The scheme would be unlikely to halt irregular arrivals entirely, but Care4Calais says it could dent the smugglers’ business in a way that other initiatives have failed to do.

Charlotte Khan, of Care4Calais, said: “Consecutive governments have focused on border security measures that have only made Channel crossings more dangerous, resulting in a shocking number of lives lost, while completely ignoring the only solution that will work – safe routes.”

The PCS general secretary, Fran Heathcote, called for safe routes to be significantly scaled up. “Our members deal with the human impact of the asylum system on a daily basis and their voices must be heard. The only way to truly undermine criminal smuggling gangs is to remove the demand through a Ukrainian-style visa system, allowing people fleeing war and persecution to travel safely and legally to claim asylum.”

The Labour government has arguably made more efforts than any other to try to prevent asylum seekers from reaching the UK in small boats.

Previously, people travelled less visibly, in the backs of lorries or on planes, and so attracted less public attention. Images of black dinghies overcrowded with people in orange lifejackets, monetised for social media clicks by far-right agitators, have generated mounting hostility from some who say they are evidence that the government has lost control of its borders.

![An RNLI boat at night with people in hi-vis on board](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/9c78c5e7e660c2e75b23cee03dafb311a2dae7cf/0_0_8192_5464/master/8192.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

But desperate people continue to clamber into dinghies. In the six days between 2 and 7 October, 1,739 people arrived in 23 boats. Along with the now terminated “one in, one out” pilot, which government trumpeted as “groundbreaking” when it announced it in July 2025, the Home Office and the National Crime Agency make regular announcements about smugglers being intercepted and dinghies seized “upstream”.

There has been a reduction of more than 40% in small boat arrivals so far this year compared with the same period last year, probably due to a combination of UK government initiatives, windy weather and a drop in irregular migration to Europe overall. According to data published by the IMO, there has been a 39% reduction this year in these arrivals to Europe – from 98,040 during the same period last year to approximately 60,000.

Despite the reduction in arrivals, far-right agitators wearing masks and black paramilitary clothing continue to protest on the streets and try to block small boat arrivals.

![People protest against small boats arriving in Gosport near Portsmouth.](https://i.guim.co.uk/img/media/0c6fac16dc9d4e4b7a8fb3cff52642b0aacec9e6/0_0_3500_2358/master/3500.jpg?width=445&dpr=1&s=none&crop=none)

None of the policy roadblocks appear to have dented the resolve of asylum seekers who want to reach the UK.

Sohrab, from Afghanistan, has been sleeping on the streets of Paris and is making his way to the Jungle. “I want to live and I will do anything to save my life from death,” he says. “I have already spent a bad day in jail in France after police caught me trying to get into a lorry heading for the UK. The smugglers are very violent, but there is no other way for me to reach the UK apart than to go to the Jungle.”

Despite the failure of “one in, one out” to deter small boat crossings, Emily Graham, of the Future Governance Forum, believes it is the way forward.

“The ‘one-in, one-out’ pilot proved cooperation based on responsibility-sharing is right in principle and has the potential to deliver results,” she said. “To reduce the number of people arriving by small boat, the government should build on the previous pilot and seek a new ‘routes for returns’ deal with European partners.”

Amad has another idea. “We would much prefer to give our money to the UK government if they allowed us to come here legally, rather than to the smugglers,” he says. “No asylum seeker would pay smugglers who beat and exploit them, or risk our lives on a dinghy, if there was another way.”
