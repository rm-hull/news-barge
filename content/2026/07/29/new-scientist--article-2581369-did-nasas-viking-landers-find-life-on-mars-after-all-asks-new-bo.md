**Meet the NeighborsSteven A. Benner, Allen Lane**

Fifty years ago last month, a life-detection test developed by Gil Levin, a NASA investigator and an engineer schooled in sensing dangerous microbial colonies in sewage, landed on Mars. It was on board Viking 1, the first of two life-seeking landers.

Amazingly, both of the landers succeeded straight away. Levin’s test, alongside two others, quickly gave what planetary scientists call strong “life affirmative” results.

This should have been a triumph for Levin and NASA’s Viking team. Unfortunately, a consensus developed within the Mars-exploration community, making it fearful of anything so bold as to claim the discovery of alien life so soon. A negative interpretation of the results prevailed, which pulled the rug from under Viking’s finding that, on the balance of evidence (and within the experiment’s definition of life), there is life on Mars.

Worse, this propagated down the decades to influence Mars-exploration policy even today, despite the fact that findings by the Curiosity rover (which landed in 2012) should have corrected it. The result: since Viking, space agencies have run timid missions in search of signs of ancient life, past habitability and water, rather than bold missions to find existing life.

This is the core argument of *Meet The Neighbors: Life on Mars and how to find it*, a captivating tale of misunderstood (if not actually gamed) planetary biochemistry, and its impact. Its author is Steven Benner, an astrobiologist, organic biochemist and synthetic biologist, whose team at Harvard University was the first to synthesise a working, protein-encoding artificial gene.

The error influenced Mars exploration policy even today, despite findings by the Curiosity rover


Benner reveals what the Viking tests found, and why they were so vulnerable to dismissal by naysayers, by undertaking a fresh analysis of the raw data and the research published on the results at the time. Then, pleasingly, he demolishes those arguments.

It all seemed so cut and dried at first, with Robert Jastrow, then director of NASA’s Goddard Institute for Space Studies, quoted in *The Atlantic* magazine as saying: “Short of seeing something wiggling on the end of a pin, the case for life on Mars is now as complete as the Viking experiments could make it.”

Benner goes into detail on the three life-detection experiments, including the controls run to rule out chemical, rather than biological, action. Levin’s “labelled release” experiment fed radiocarbon-tagged nutrients like formic acid, glycine and lactic acid to a sample of Martian soil, which then released radioactive carbon dioxide, “indicating the presence of respiring microbial life”, he writes.

A second test found that the carbon from radiolabelled carbon monoxide and CO2 could be “fixed” into the molecules of the organic materials in the Martian soil. This was deemed analogous to how plants on Earth fix CO2 in photosynthesis and thus “evidence of an active Martian biosphere”. In the third test, water was added to Martian soil, producing a significant amount of oxygen and an “exchange of CO2 with the soil”, supporting the existence of bioactive soil on Mars.

But all three “life-positive” results fell after the gas chromatography-mass spectrometer (GC-MS) on the landers found no evidence of any organic content in the Martian soil where Viking 1 touched down, at Chryse Planitia, or on soil where Viking 2 landed, at Utopia Planitia, 6460 kilometres away. As a result, an unknown oxidising surface chemical, rather than a microbial life form, was put forward as the explanation and accepted by the Mars-exploration community as the source of life-positive results.

It was all tosh, says Benner. First, it turned out the GC-MS was nowhere near sensitive enough to pick up organics. NASA’s Curiosity rover discovered the Martian surface is full of organics – notably perchlorates – so the GC-MS results could be discarded. Mars, says Benner, is alive again, a bold claim well supported by this absorbing, convincing book.

Space agencies should now harness the economics of reusable rocketry to mount searches for existing life on Mars, says Benner. He argues they should focus on microbes that might have RNA-based building blocks – a theory he has honed with his research on artificial genes and the fact that ribose has been found in samples from the asteroid Bennu.

Viking’s resurrection would have pleased the late Levin and his late NASA collaborator Patricia Straat for another reason. Both founded a pressure group that still campaigns for samples to be analysed off-Earth to maintain planetary biosecurity. As China plans to deliver Mars samples to Earth in 2031, *Meet the Neighbors* brings that issue into sharp relief.
