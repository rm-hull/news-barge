Chidren are an impressionable lot. In our youth, we are like sponges, picking up habits and neural pathways that will resonate for the rest of our lives. It is no wonder, then, that countries including the UK, Portugal and Mexico have enacted policies to restrict the advertising of unhealthy food to children, in a bid to tackle soaring childhood obesity levels. But if this makes you think that adults are off the hook, then think again.

As we explore, eating appropriately for your age matters no matter how old you are. If you are middle-aged, you may have noticed the warning signs: a horrible hangover after drinking an amount of alcohol that would have left you unaffected decades earlier, or getting increasingly jittery after drinking coffee. These are indicators of more global changes occurring in the body, making us less and less able to tolerate a subpar diet. Breaking bad diet habits – a glass of wine after the kids have gone to bed, or a sneaky croissant from a coffee shop because you skipped breakfast – as we age is just as important as instilling healthy eating in children.

What’s more, once we reach the age of 60, it becomes ever easier to fall into a “nutrient gap” – when a slowdown in our metabolic rate means that we eat less, but age-related physiological changes mean that we actually need more vitamins and minerals from every bite of food.

That’s the bad news. Recognising these changes in our metabolism and responding accordingly – you know the drill: eating more vegetables, legumes, whole grains and fish, and less ultra-processed food high in sugar, salt and saturated fat – can help set you up for a healthy old age.

But don’t wait until you are already in your latter decades to take action. A number of studies show that, if we tune up our diet in middle age, this can have a big impact on our health and wellbeing years down the line, while a younger biological age also helps you reap the benefits of a good diet. Ditch the junk and your older self will thank you.
