**In Germany there is grief, shock and, increasingly, anger about the attack at Berlin Pride on Saturday night which left one Polish woman dead and injured at least 29 others.**

The main suspect, Abdul Ballout, a 21-year-old German citizen with Lebanese heritage, was shot and killed by police on Sunday.

Germany's Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt said Ballout "had previously drawn attention to himself through a high volume of criminal offences, radicalisation and affiliation with the Islamist scene."

As details emerge about his criminal convictions, many are asking why Ballout was not in prison.

![The 21-year old suspect identified by police as Abdul B. in a public call for his arrest, after a vehicle rammed down the crowd near Berlin's Pride CSD celebrations on Saturday evening, killing one person and wounding several, in this undated handout photo released by Polizei Berlin on July 26, 2026.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/976/cpsprodpb/488a/live/abd66eb0-88d3-11f1-b2ab-0dd01740f9f6.jpg)

Abdul Ballout was shot and killed in a police operation on Sunday afternoon

Prosecutors say that in 2025, Ballout decided to try to join Islamic State group's armed struggle in Syria, travelling via Turkey to Lebanon. In Lebanon, they say, he met several people he believed were members of IS.

He was arrested in Lebanon in July 2025 and sentenced to three months in prison for incitement to religious and sectarian conflict.

Then in November 2025 he returned to Germany, where he was arrested at Berlin Brandenburg Airport and held in pre-trial detention.

In May this year, a Berlin court sentenced Ballout to a juvenile sentence of one year and ten months, for "preparing a serious act of violence endangering the state".

The sentence was suspended while prosecutors appealed against the verdict, calling for a longer sentence.

Ballout was released and placed under the supervision of a probation officer.

Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt told RTL that the decision to impose a suspended sentence was "certainly incomprehensible... given the level of danger involved in this case".

He said the authorities were investigating how that happened.

Berlin's Governing Mayor, Kai Wegner from the centre-right Christian Democrat party (CDU), said he was dumbstruck at the way the Berlin judiciary had handled Ballout's case.

![A map shows the location of Spandau in the west of Berlin where the suspect was shot dead, with a label saying so. A second map shows the Tiergarten to the left of the Brandenburg Gate, and a label showing the Ahurnsteig street](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/ace/standard/1600/cpsprodpb/9436/live/3db92d90-8924-11f1-b2ab-0dd01740f9f6.png)

"He was sentenced... under juvenile criminal law," he told RBB TV. "And then, unfortunately, the court handed down a suspended sentence. I don't want to pass judgement on that – that's the independence of the judiciary – but I'm really at a loss for words."

The opposition far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) party described the attack as a total failure of security policy.

"If an Islamist threat known to the police was able to carry out such an act, a thorough investigation must be carried out to establish why he was still able to move about freely in the first place," said Martin Hess, the AfD's home affairs spokesperson.

According to Der Spiegel, Ballout told the Berlin court this spring that he had distanced himself from extremist ideology and violence.

Thomas Mücke, an extremism expert at the Violence Prevention Network VPN, told ARD television that he had met the suspect.

He said Ballout had given social workers "a very composed, very cautious, very controlled and very friendly impression".

"And those are precisely the criteria where we realise we can't really get to know the person," he continued.

Some have called for Germany's laws against terrorism to be made stricter.

Peter Neumann at King's College London told ZDF that Germany's criminal law on terrorism is "too lenient," and sentences too short by European standards.

He also called for a rethink of Germany's juvenile justice system, which he said prioritises education rather than the protection of the public.

The home affairs spokesperson for the centre-left Social Democrats (SPD), Sebastian Fiedler, told Deutschlandfunk radio that criminal law was not the problem, but the way it was applied.

"We need to look more closely at why [Ballout] still at large. Another key question is: 'What actually happened after his release from prison? Who was he still in contact with?' Although he was under surveillance, there was clearly a gap in the system."

Germany's Chancellor Friedrich Merz said it was too early to say what political consequences will be drawn from the attack, but promised there would be action: "We will have to respond with prudence, but also with determination."

He said if individuals classified as threats could not be detained, their freedom of movement should be restricted "as much as possible."

"I cannot understand how I can track my mobile phone from anywhere in the world... yet it is supposedly impossible, or technically unfeasible, for security authorities in Germany to determine the whereabouts of individuals classified as security threats to that same extent."

He said he had asked the interior ministry to review the law regarding monitoring jurisdiction.
