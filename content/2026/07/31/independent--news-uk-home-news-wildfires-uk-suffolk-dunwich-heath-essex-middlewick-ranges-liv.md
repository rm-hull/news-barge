Firefighters are tackling a blaze at a nature reserve in North Essex as crews continue to battle the “unpredictable” wildfire in Suffolk.

Around 30 crew responded to a fire on Middlewick Ranges nature reserve in Colchester, Essex, urging people to avoid the area. It is the latest wildfire as England and Wales experience tinderbox conditions and swathes of the country remain in drought.

Multiple fire brigades remain on site for a third day fighting a blaze in Suffolk after it spread to cover an area the size of 210 football pitches on Thursday. Firefighters identified and mapped trigger points on Thursday evening.

The Sizewell B nuclear power plant a few miles away is on alert as flames and smoke tear through Dunwich Heath, where at least 90 firefighters are battling the blaze.

Nuclear chiefs at EDF, who are understood to be monitoring the situation, said there was currently no risk to the power station.

Authorities have declared a major incident and residents in about 200 coastal properties and caravans were forced to leave. Local wildlife habitats, protected as a Site of Special Scientific Interest, have been left devastated by the flames.

Prime minister Andy Burnham promised that firefighters in Suffolk would have all the resources they needed. Green Party leader Zack Polanski called for Mr Burnham to hold an emergency Cobra meeting.

## What we know about the Colchester fire

Around 45 firefighters are at the scene of a fire in Middlewick Ranges, a well-known beauty spot in Colchester.

Firefighter have asked people to avoid the area. “We are using a lot of the surrounding roads to park fire engines and access water hydrants. Please keep these areas clear.”

Middlewick Ranges is classed as an outstandingly significant site for biodiversity, not only for Colchester, but for Essex and the wider SE region, according to the Wildlife Trust.

At 76 hectares it is one of Colchester’s largest local wildlife sites and, on its own, represents nearly 4 per cent of the Council’s complete local wildlife site acreage.

## Analysis: Why the wildfire in Suffolk is particularly challenge

*Stuti Mishra, Asia climate correspondent, reports:*

Following multiple heatwaves and hot dry conditions, the wildfire in Suffolk is particularly challenging. Dr Zakary Campbell-Lochrie, Lecturer in Fire Science, University of Edinburgh, explains that as vegetation remains dry, it can present a greater danger of spread when fires do occur.

“This is a challenging wildfire situation reflected in the Fire & Rescue Service’s declaration of a major incident. This wildfire is occurring at the same time as drought conditions have officially been declared in large parts of England including Suffolk.

“The hot, dry weather conditions, including the multiple heatwave events across much of the UK in recent weeks which lead to strong drying conditions for vegetation which can subsequently be highly susceptible to ignition and can present a much greater danger when fires do occur.

“As we’ve been seeing already the current weather conditions (e.g. wind direction and speed) will have a significant impact on the progression and impact of the fire and effectiveness of firefighting efforts. The likelihood and impact of wildfires in the UK varies not just with the weather but also varies greatly across different landscapes and vegetation types.

“The Suffolk wildfires are occurring in a heathland area, which represents a very important ecosystem type in the UK, and wildfires can have a serious impact on biodiversity and carbon storage in these areas, as well as potentially representing a significant risk to neighbouring communities and infrastructure.”

## Suffolk coast landscape left 'unrecognisable' by blaze, says National Trust

The wildfire which tore through a reserve along the Suffolk coast has left the landscape “unrecognisable”, the National Trust has said.

It is still too early to determine the true impact of the blaze, including to RSPB Minsmere, where the fire started, the conservation charity added.

“We have been left heartbroken by the total devastation we are now seeing due to the fire at Dunwich Heath, which is one of the UK’s most important coastal heathland landscapes,” they said.

“Although it’s still too early to say what the true impact has been to both RSPB Minsmere, where the fire started, and Dunwich Heath, it’s becoming clear that the fire has torn through the entire National Trust reserve and the landscape that we know and love is now unrecognisable.

“We believe that very little heathland vegetation is left and many wildlife species will have been impacted, now and long into the future. Thankfully, the iconic Coastguard Cottages have escaped untouched.

“I want to thank the Suffolk Fire & Rescue Service, as well as the incredible effort from our team, the RSPB, Natural England, local farmers and the groundworks crew at Sizewell C, who have been working tirelessly to assist with cutting firebreaks and deploying equipment to dig trenches and supply water bowsers.

“We have also assisted the fire service by bringing in air support. We believe these efforts will have ultimately saved many homes and holiday accommodation. The local community has also been incredibly supportive, banding together to support holiday-makers who were evacuated.

“The hope now is that the fire can be contained soon.”

## Inside the mitigation procedures in Sizewell B as power station remains alert

*Stuti Mishra, Asia climate correspondent, reports:*

After reports yesterday that Sizewell B was on alert over wildfires in Suffolk, Joan Cordiner FREng, professor of process engineering at University of Sheffield has revealed the process likely ongoing in the power station.

“The main thing for Sizewell B is vigilance and being prepared to enact the training and procedures for just this sort of situation.

“They will have triggers set off when they have to act on prevention and mitigative measures, when and if things escalate. Given the potential wind changes and speeds and burnable materials close to the site - they have to decide what action is needed and at what points.

“The risk management plan for the site will have identified the potential for a fire to approach the site from different situations. They will have in place preventions- that will include fire breaks in forests and ground cover/ bushes etc. They also will include firefighting systems and of course the training of their personnel.

“These procedures will have been practised and done in collaboration with the local fire brigades, local police and emergency services.

“The company will be in constant communication with these groups, their headquarters and appropriate parts of the UK government and the emergency services in their emergency plan.

“Typically, these emergency procedures will send non-essential staff home. Ensure all combustible materials are removed from the site particularly around the perimeter areas. In addition, they will decide if they need to start shutting down systems safety in good time.

“It would be typical for the emergency services to be wetting down areas at a safe distance and putting in place their plan to for extra fire break systems.

“All of this will be done in coordination with an incident command system set up for this type of event which will have the right key people directing the response.

“The key here is having done the risk assessments, building in mitigations and preventions, developing an emergency plan, they need to enact the plan that they will have practised. This is critical to ensure good and clear decision making and response.”

## Wildlife habitats in Dunwich Heath could take 25 years to recover - report

Habitats devastated by the wildfire in Dunwich Heath could take up to 25 years to recover, a wildlife expert has reportedly said.

Adam Rowlands, from the RSPB, said the blaze had been "hugely consequential" for the wildlife.

"Most of them have finished breeding so they'll be able to escape the fire, and last night we could see nightjars flying above the fireball up on the heath," he told the BBC.

"But the problem is it will look like a scene of devastation afterwards. All the vegetation will go and the majority of those birds will lose the places where they nest.

"Some of that habitat will take five, 10, 25 years to come back to what it looked like before the fire broke out yesterday afternoon."
