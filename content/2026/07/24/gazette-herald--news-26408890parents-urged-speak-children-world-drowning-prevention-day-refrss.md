PARENTS have been urged to speak with their children about water safety ahead of World Drowning Prevention Day.

A new national strategy to tackle the “scourge of drowning” has been published amid a recent surge in water-related fatalities across England and the UK.

Ross MacLeod, Water Safety Manager at the Royal National Lifeboat Institution (RNLI), said: “Drowning is preventable, and it’s a significant public health challenge here in the UK”.

Speaking ahead of World Drowning Prevention Day on Saturday, July 25, he asked the public to think about keeping themselves and their loved ones safe, particularly parents of children who “may be out unsupervised and have schools broken for the summer holidays, to have that talk with them around how to keep themselves safe”.

Between 2019 and 2023, there were 2,306 water-related fatalities in England, of which 80 per cent involved men, and alcohol or drugs were reported as present in 34 per cent of accidental water-related fatalities, while almost 40 per cent of accidental water-related fatalities happened in the summer season.

Globally, almost 300,000 people die from drowning each year, and drowning remains among the ten leading causes of death among children aged 5–14 years.

  2019 2023 Data Analysis England Drowning Prevention Strategy.


The first Water Safety England (WSE) Drowning Prevention Strategy was launched on Thursday, and reminds people that “drowning is not limited to specific environments or activities involving water and can occur in a wide range of situations, including during everyday, unexpected encounters with water, where risks may be underestimated.”

As part of his work as water safety manager at the RNLI, Mr MacLeod works with bereaved families.

He told the Local Democracy Reporting Service (LDRS): “A common theme I hear from parents who’ve lost young people to drowning is they wish they’d had that conversation with them about how to stay safe near water.

“Parents talk to them about road safety, sex safety, drugs, and a lot of other issues, but for many people, there isn’t that thought to speak to their children about water safety. So we’d encourage them to do so, and really encourage their children to only go to supervised locations.”

The Drowning Prevention Strategy reported that coastal locations accounted for 35 per cent of accidental water-related fatalities, while most accidental water-related fatalities occurred at inland waters.

Members of the public have also been reminded of the impacts of warm weather intensified by climate change.

In the UK, research has found that drowning deaths increase by 7.2 per cent for every 1°C rise in temperature. The risk is also nearly five times higher on days when temperatures exceed 25°C compared with days below 10°C.

Mr MacLeod noted that “the May heatwave, where we saw an unprecedented level of drownings during that May half-term, was a clear manifestation of some of this risk that we’ve seen coming”.

Alison Hume, MP for Scarborough and Whitby, said: “With more people heading to the coast during periods of hot weather, it’s vital everyone understands the risks and stays safe around water. Our coastline is there to be enjoyed, but conditions can change quickly and taking a few simple precautions, such as swimming between the flags on beaches, can save lives.

“That’s why, as chair of the APPG for Coastal Communities, I’m proud to have fought alongside Coastguard Rescue Officers and prevented them from being reclassified as volunteers.

“Alongside the RNLI, these dedicated teams show extraordinary courage, putting themselves in harm’s way to keep us safe. We owe them our gratitude and support, which we can demonstrate by taking care of ourselves and others.”


  **Lack of coordination**

Despite “a really engaged and collaborative sector that is willing to work together and has done so for years”, the Government has been urged to consider a more coordinated approach to promoting water safety.

Currently, responsibilities sit across multiple Government departments.

Mr MacLeod said: “What we are lacking is that coordination from central Government and potentially some funding to support some really key deliverables, such as a funded database, so we understand who is losing their lives in the water to inform targeted prevention work in the future.”

He added: “We feel strongly that it would be a big step forward in the future to have a named Government department responsible for drowning prevention in the UK.”

Cllr Derek Bastiman, chairman of the LGA Coastal Special Interest Group and Chairman of the Beach and Water Safety Group, attended the launch of the Drowning Prevention Strategy and encouraged councils across the country to promote its findings.

Cllr Bastiman, who represents Scalby and the Coast on North Yorkshire Council, noted: “This is vitally important and we should make as many people as possible aware of the dangers of water.”

This week, the World Health Organisation also published a new plan to help governments and communities implement seven proven measures to reduce drowning deaths, building on the first-ever Global Strategy for Drowning Prevention.

“Every drowning is a tragedy, and most are preventable,” said Dr Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, WHO Director-General. “The seven PROTECT strategies give countries practical, evidence-based approaches to prevent these tragedies, including making water transport safer, teaching school kids to swim, and preparing for floods. Drowning prevention isn’t the job of one ministry; it belongs to everyone, and the lives it saves are the reward.”


  **Staying safe**

As heat health alerts are in effect for parts of the UK, the RNLI has a simple safety message for people: float to live.

“If people find themselves in difficulty in the water, remember float to live, which works both inland and around the coast, which is to tilt your head back with your ears submerged, relax to try and control your breathing, and then do as little as possible to keep yourself afloat. We all float differently, your legs might sink, but that’s all right,” said Mr MacLeod.

He added: “We just need to find what floating looks and feels like for you, so have a practise, and then in an emergency situation, you can float until you regain control of your breathing, or potentially you can swim to safety.

“The final one is around knowing what to do if you see your mate in trouble or see anybody in trouble in the water, which is phone, float, throw.

“That’s phoning 999, telling the person in the water to float on their back, and then throwing them something buoyant, like a piece of public rescue equipment to keep themselves afloat until help arrives.”
