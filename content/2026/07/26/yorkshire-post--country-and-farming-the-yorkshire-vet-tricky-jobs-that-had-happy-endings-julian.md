# The Yorkshire Vet: Tricky jobs that had happy endings - Julian Norton

## Yesterday, at either end of the day, I had two interesting and very satisfying cases. Some days there is a lot of routine work; vaccination boosters, repeat examinations, post operation checks and so on. But it’s never long before something unusual appears. And the fluffy dog called Rudy with red testicles was the first of such. He came in as an emergency, on the list to be seen by the next available vet, which was me.

Sure enough, he did have a significant swelling in the scrotal department. This in itself was very unusual- dogs’ testicles are usually fairly resilient to inflammation. But there was more the matter with Rudy than a just orchitis. He was weak and lethargic and not a happy boy. I suspected this might be because he had previously been diagnosed with another unusual condition; Addison’s disease. This is a disease whereby the adrenal glands do not work properly, rendering affected dogs (and people) deficient in the hormones that help correct situations stressful to the body. Rudy had been well controlled with medications, but concurrent illnesses can lead to a condition called an Addisonian Crisis, which is every bit as serious as it sounds. Swift blood tests confirmed my suspicions and Rudy was quickly on a saline drip and receiving an intravenous bolus of steroids. As the day progressed, so did Rudy. By lunchtime the scrotal swelling had subsided, and he’d been out for a small walk. By the afternoon, he was barking his head off and had enjoyed some food. By the evening, he was ready to go home. The plan now is to neuter him to try to prevent another crisis, but that’s for another day.

As Rudy was leaving the waiting room with his relieved owner, another dog and owner took his place; one was worried, the other was sneezing constantly. It was a pug who had just inhaled a grass seed. The owner had immediately spotted this and managed to pull two strands out of his nostril, but unfortunately, it was a three-pronged grass seed! One prong was still up there.

“It was his right nostril,” the worried owner explained, before adding. “He stuck his flat face into the grass and then sort of inhaled. I suppose he can generate quite a bit of suction when his face is pressed close up against something.” I hadn’t appreciated this as one of the effects of having such a short snout before, but it made sense. I took him through to the prep room to begin my investigations. The little pug did not like my attempts to poke a small ‘scope up his nostril, even though I’d recently numbed it with local anaesthetic. This was not a surprise, but occasionally foreign bodies can be removed very easily, so it’s usually worth a brief attempt before reaching for the sedative injections. But it’s also essential to have a stationary patient: the combination of a thin or small object and a delicate, long-handled set of instruments makes this sort of job fiddly. Eventually, I could see the tail end of the offending item, but that was just the first part. Next, I had to insert some long grabbers called crocodile forceps, (because of the shape at the end of the grabber looking like the jaws of a tiny crocodile). Then came the hard part: grabbing the fragile thing and pulling it out without leaving any behind. Pesky grass seeds are a common problem for dogs and vets in summertime. Their spikey and barbed nature means they tend to migrate inwards and onwards rather than falling outwards.

This afternoon, the pug was lucky. Without too much drama, I pulled the seed out with a flourish and an appreciative gasp from my nurse assistant.
