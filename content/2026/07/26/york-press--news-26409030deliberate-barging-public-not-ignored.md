*Why deliberate barging in public spaces should not be ignored, says Fiona McCulloch of York Citizens Advice*

I NEVER imagined that simply waiting to cross the road outside York Railway Station would leave me bruised.

It was an ordinary day. I was standing at the crossing, in the sun, waiting for the lights to change, when a tall, skinny man, perhaps in his late twenties, walked directly towards me. There was plenty of room to pass. Instead, he barged forcefully into me with his shoulder, knocking me off balance before continuing on without so much as a glance behind him.

At five feet three inches and in my fifties, I was no physical match. I was left with bruising, shaken confidence and something harder to describe: a lingering sense that what had once felt like an everyday public space had suddenly become less safe.

For days afterwards, I questioned myself. Was it an accident? Had I somehow been in the way? But the more I reflected, the less likely that explanation seemed. The impact felt deliberate.

Then I discovered I was far from alone.

In Japan, a disturbing phenomenon began attracting international attention around 2018. Known as "butsukari otoko", literally "bumping men" or "barging men", it describes individuals, overwhelmingly men, who intentionally collide with women in crowded public spaces. Videos filmed on railway station platforms and city streets appeared online, and countless women shared remarkably similar experiences. Some of the perpetrators seemed to target women travelling alone, particularly those who appeared physically smaller or less likely to challenge them.

While experts debate the motivations, suggested explanations include displaced anger, a desire to intimidate, feelings of powerlessness expressed through aggression, or the thrill of exerting control over a stranger without obvious consequences. Whatever the psychology, the behaviour reflects something deeply troubling: choosing someone perceived as vulnerable as the target of deliberate intimidation.

It would be comforting to believe this is solely a Japanese issue. It isn't.

Search online and you'll find women across the UK describing almost identical encounters. They speak of men deliberately shoulder-checking them in shopping centres, transport hubs and busy streets. Many, like me, initially dismissed the incident or blamed themselves. Others simply carried on because they doubted anyone would take them seriously.

That silence is part of the problem.

Because barging occupies an uncomfortable grey area. Unless serious injury occurs, it rarely becomes a police matter. Witnesses often assume it was accidental. The victim is left questioning their own judgement.

Yet the impact can be significant.

A younger, fitter person might recover physically within days. An older person could suffer a serious fall. Someone with a disability, mobility impairment or hidden health condition could face much more severe consequences. A pregnant woman, someone recovering from surgery or a person with osteoporosis could experience life-changing injuries from what might appear to others as "just a bump."

Even where physical injuries are minor, the emotional effect shouldn't be underestimated. Public spaces rely on an unwritten social contract: we respect one another's personal space, we take care not to cause unnecessary harm, and we recognise our shared responsibility for each other's safety. Deliberately breaking that contract chips away at everyone's confidence.

That is why awareness matters.

Organisations such as Citizens Advice York may not investigate assaults or enforce the law, but they play a vital role in strengthening communities and helping people find their voice.

Someone who experiences deliberate barging may not know where to turn. They may wonder whether reporting it is worthwhile, whether others have experienced the same thing or whether they are overreacting. Simply being listened to and believed can make an enormous difference.

By raising awareness, organisations like Citizens Advice York can encourage people to report incidents, signpost victims towards appropriate support and work alongside local authorities, transport providers and community safety partnerships to identify recurring problems. They can also help start conversations about respectful behaviour in public spaces before harmful conduct becomes accepted as "just one of those things."

There is another reason why this matters.

Small acts of intimidation rarely exist in isolation. Communities flourish when people feel confident leaving their homes, using public transport and walking through their city without fear. If seemingly minor acts of aggression are dismissed repeatedly, they contribute to a wider atmosphere where people, particularly women, older residents and those who feel vulnerable, begin altering their routines to avoid potential confrontation.

That isn't the kind of York any of us wants.
