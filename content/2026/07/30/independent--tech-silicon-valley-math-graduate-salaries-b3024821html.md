Top university graduates in math and computer science are landing starting pay packages worth up to a million dollars as AI companies and Wall Street trading firms fight over the same small group of specialists.

The salary jump is being driven by trading firms shifting toward machine learning and large language models. That shift puts Wall Street in direct competition with AI labs like OpenAI and Anthropic, which are all trying to hire from the same small group of experts.

“The delineation is pre-OpenAI and post-OpenAI,” Matt Stabile, founder of the recruitment firm Stabile Search, told *The Wall Street Journal*. “That’s when you saw competition really take off.”

Stabile noted that while seven-figure packages for early-career hires used to be rare anomalies, “a million dollars is something people don’t even bat an eye at anymore.”

Standard baseline packages at top trading shops typically land between $350,000 and $500,000, but recruiters say those numbers jump quickly. In some cases, portfolio managers bypass typical pay structures to secure top summer interns before they enter the job market.

![Top mathematics and computer science university graduates are landing starting pay packages worth up to a million dollars as AI companies, such as Anthropic, and Wall Street trading firms fight over them](https://static.the-independent.com/2026/07/30/18/50/GettyImages-2227254825.jpeg)

“They happen to intern for a highly successful person on a highly profitable desk who wants to take them off the market,” Kevin Cassata, founder of executive-search firm Karbon Agency, told the publication. “They’ll just say, ‘OK, here’s your return offer — it’s $700,000.’”

Cassata said he had seen entry-level offers top out at $1.5 million, comparing the current market to free-agency bidding wars in professional sports.

The financial scale of these firms allows them to rival major tech companies. In the first quarter of 2026, quantitative trading firm Jane Street generated $10.3 billion in profit, roughly double the earnings of Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley combined, despite operating with a fraction of their workforce.

To secure talent early, firms start outreach well before college graduation.

Optiver, a leading quantitative firm, sponsors chess grandmasters, runs campus events and flies students to its offices. The company currently fills 70 percent of its graduate roles through its internship programs and plans to eventually source all entry-level hiring through that channel.

![The salary jump is being driven by trading firms shifting toward machine learning and large language models. That shift puts Wall Street in direct competition with AI labs like OpenAI](https://static.the-independent.com/2026/07/30/18/42/GettyImages-2218344194.jpeg)

“Outstanding people are in more demand than they’ve ever been, and that does create competitive pressure on wages,” Charlie Whitmer, Optiver’s U.S. chief operating officer, told *The Wall Street Journal*. “As competition increases for outstanding people, prices naturally rise.” 

Students frequently cite high compensation and faster feedback as primary reasons for choosing trading over traditional research.

Andrew Lai, who completed a doctorate in computer science at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology before joining Optiver full-time, told the publication that he preferred the field because it allowed him to see the practical results of his research quickly.

Lai noted that about a third of his doctoral lab mates entered quantitative finance, while an equal share went into tech.

“Comp was a big factor,” Daniel Wu, a Stanford computer science graduate who joined a quantitative firm in 2021, told *The Wall Street Journal*. “At the time, quant firms were making the most competitive offer financially.”

However, the lucrative packages are also diverting talent away from academia.

At Oxford University’s Mathematical Institute, summer internships offering upwards of $30,000 a month frequently convince doctoral students to abandon academic career plans. Graduates entering the field typically secure packages between $300,000 and $1 million, with those specialized in large language modeling landing at the top of that range.

“Once they have a taste of what it means to work for these firms and the amount of money they could be making, the majority of them would rather go into industry,” Alvaro Cartea, an Oxford math professor and director of the Oxford-Man Institute, said. “All I’m saying is that temptation is extremely strong.”
