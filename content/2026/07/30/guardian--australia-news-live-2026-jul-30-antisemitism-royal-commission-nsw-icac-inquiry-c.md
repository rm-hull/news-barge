Neil the seal has captured hearts and global attention for his visits to Tasmania, and racked up a bill of more than $36,000 for his most-recent stay, AAP reports.

The five-year-old elephant seal hauls out twice each year to rest and moult in the island state’s south-east, where he was born. His antics, including crushing bollards, fences and lounging on quiet beachside streets, have drawn millions of views on social media.

His 15-day stay at Seven Mile beach cost the local Clarence city council a total of $31,540.53 – the majority relating to staffing and labour. There is also a repair bill for one flattened street sign, 10 knocked-over bollards and one crushed fence, costing council $1,297.70 in materials.

Council mayor Brendan Blomeley said the overall bill was relatively small:

Neil is a wild animal and the over-exposure and constant public attention he received could have led to a more dramatic outcome.

Wherever he decides to come ashore next, I hope he is given the space and time he needs.

AstraZeneca will continue to supply Zoladex, critical breast cancer and endometriosis drug, for free after removal from PBS

The drugmaker AstraZeneca will continue to provide women with breast cancer and endometriosis free access to the vital medicine Zoladex, which the company said last month would be removed from the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme.

The company said it “understand that maintaining continuity of care is essential” and that it would provide Zoladex monthly implants at “no cost” from 1 November 2026, when it comes off the PBS. Standard pharmacy dispensing fees may still apply.

AstraZeneca added in a statement:

Health care professionals have been notified of the change in process for prescribing the Zoladex monthly (3.6mg) implant for patients without suitable treatment pathways. This includes patients who are currently prescribed and patients who may need to be prescribed the implant as a treatment option after 1 November 2026.

The decision to discontinue the Zoladex monthly (3.6mg) implant from Australia’s Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme reflects the way the PBS currently works, which means that the price offered is too low, making it unsustainable to supply the medicine on the PBS.

First witness from Liberal factional group the Reformers appears before NSW Icac

The NSW Independent Commission Against Corruption’s (Icac) Operation Rosny hearings have resumed this morning, with the first witness from the hard-right Liberal faction group the Reformers, Robert Assaf, appearing before the commission.

Icac is investigating the allegation that Liberal figures including Assaf solicited or accepted illegal donations to recruit or renew members to the party – a practice known as “branch stacking”. The group wanted more conservative policies on same-sex marriage, abortion and euthanasia.

Assaf, 30, who went to an independent Catholic school with two of the Reformers, Jeremy Greenwood and Jean-Claude Perrottet, spent a year in the Catholic seminary, but later worked in federal parliament for various ministers, senators and MPs, including Tony Abbott. Assaf appears after two days of evidence from former Liberal staffer and Catholic Schools NSW employee, Dylan Whitelaw, who was associated with the group but has not been named in allegations against the Reformers.

Whitelaw’s evidence include the allegation that it was a “running joke” between him and Assaf that the latter spent most of his time at Catholic Schools NSW on factional Liberal work. A network firewall upgrade blocked emergency service calls for Optus customers in South Australia, Western Australia, the Northern Territory and parts of New South Wales, with more than 600 calls not able to connect in the 13 hours it was offline last year.

Two deaths were linked to the outage, with people unable to connect to triple zero.

The regulator announced on Thursday it had launched action in the federal court alleging Optus had breached two separate legal obligations on 1,005 occasions. Acma alleges that Optus failed to give end-users who made emergency calls access to the emergency call service, and to ensure emergency calls were carried to the relevant termination point.

The Acma chair, Nerida O’Loughlin, said:

Australians rightly expect that when they call triple zero, their call will connect. The circumstances of this outage meant that did not reliably occur, leaving people unable to connect to potentially life-saving services.

Giving access to the emergency call service is not optional, it is a fundamental legal obligation and the most important public safety responsibility telecommunications providers have.

Optus could face penalties of up to $250,000 per contravention.
