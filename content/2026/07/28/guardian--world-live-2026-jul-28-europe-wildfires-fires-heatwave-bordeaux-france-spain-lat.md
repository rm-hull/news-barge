Spain's Sánchez says authorities 'begin to see light at the end of the tunnel,' but warns about urgency of responding to climate emergency

Spanish prime minister Pedro Sánchez has just said the authorities “can begin to see the light at the end of the tunnel in the fight against wildfires.”

Spanish prime minister Pedro Sánchez arrives to give a press conference following the last cabinet meeting before the summer holidays, at La Moncloa Palace in Madrid, Spain. Photograph: Javier Soriano/AFP/Getty Images

Speaking at a press conference in Madrid, Sánchez said there were still “complex days and hours ahead” as the emergency response continued amid concerns about the impact of the upcoming fourth heatwave.

He said the authorities “will continue to mobilise all resources until the last flame is extinguished.”

He also repeated that the fires serve as a strong reminder that the government urgently needs to plan for the climate emergency, which he says is “worsening year after year.”

He urged local governments to fall behind the government’s strategy on climate, saying that “climate denial is the worst fuel against the climate emergency.”

“Denying reality and the alarms raised by scientists do not prepare us; on the contrary, it leaves us much more vulnerable and our population much more at risk.”

'Is this how the world ends?' Tourists caught up in wildfires speak to the Guardian

Joe Pinner and Jane Clinton

Tourists caught up in the wildfires spoke to us about their experiences.

Patrick, 77, was at a beach in Arcachon on the south-west coast of France on Thursday when he noticed the sky getting darker, and a “great billowing pall of black smoke” developing.

“It was the beginning of a slightly otherworldly experience,” he said.

He and his wife were forced to hitchhike 42 miles (69km) back to Bordeaux, where they were staying on holiday, after all trains were cancelled due to an electrical fault which had nothing to do with the fire.

The couple entered a bike shop and asked for a piece of cardboard, on which Patrick wrote “Bordeaux SVP. Urgence!”.

They walked about a mile out of town to a roundabout and stuck their thumbs out, hitching a ride within five minutes. That took them halfway, until they were picked up by the owner of a chain of local coffee shops who was on route to Bordeaux to collect his wife, who was stranded because of the cancelled trains.

It was only after getting back safely to Bordeaux, and watching the news unfold, that the severity of the fires hit them.

“We realised that the two people who had given us lifts lived in areas where houses were burned and tens of thousands of people were being evacuated,” he said. “I hope they are both OK.”

He described the “chilling feeling” of seeing life on the beach seemingly going on as usual whilst smoke gathered in the skies above.

“My main memory is how bizarre it was to watch people enjoying holidays on the beach while a major catastrophe was unfolding in full view, not far away. Is this how the world ends?”

4,000 people evacuated from campsites in Lacanau near Bordeaux

But it’s not all good news: up to 4,000 people have been evacuated from campsites in Lacanau near Bordeaux in south western France as part of a “precautionary” move.

A group hold a firehose and try to suppress a fire near Lacanau, as wildfires rage outside Bordeaux, France. Photograph: Baz Ratner/AP

“Due to weather conditions (heatwave) and to facilitate emergency operations, the Prefect has ordered the preventive evacuation of campsites in Lacanau, as well as group tourist residences and holiday villages,” the Lacanau town hall said on its Facebook.

It added there are currently no plans for further evacuations.

Progress in fighting fires near Madrid 'reasonably positive,' minister says

Spain’s interior minister, Fernando Grande-Marlaska, struck a similar tone, telling the broadcaster TVE this morning that progress over recent hours had been “reasonably positive”.

Just like Ayuso, he also paid tribute to the work of firefighters deployed in the region.

Situation near Madrid 'gradually improving' leaving authorities 'more optimistic', regional president says

Madrid’s regional president Isabel Díaz Ayuso said this morning that the wildfires situation is “gradually improving” and allows authorities to be a bit “more optimistic,” as she thanked the emergency services for their work.

But the central government’s delegation to Madrid cautioned that the fire was still not under control, with more progress needed today before the expected fourth heatwave hits the area.

France in 'a race against time' before fourth heatwave hits

France was also in “a race against time” to control wildfires before another heatwave this week, said Eric Brocardi, a fire service spokesperson.

Weather forecasters have said temperatures could reach 40C (104F) is some parts of the country from Tuesday.

This would complicate efforts to control the wildfire in Gironde in south-west France, which the interior minister, Laurent Nuñez, described as “extremely intense and unpredictable … generating its own winds and advancing erratically toward the Bordeaux metropolitan area”.

As firefighters continue to battle the blazes, there is also some concern about the air quality in the affected region.

A man wears a mask, while smoke rises from a wildfire in Pierroton, Gironde, France. Photograph: Florion Goga/Reuters

France’s health agencies warned that the air quality in the Nouvelle-Aquitaine region covering Gironde is “degraded” with fine particulate matter way above the limits.

Residents were told to keep doors and windows closed, cover vents with damp clothes, and turn off ventilation systems.

3.5 million filtering FFP2 masks will be delivered to the region by Wednesday.

Morning opening: 'Coming weeks will be tough,' Macron warns, as France enters new heatwave amid fires

Jakub Krupa

The return of searing temperatures, from today in France and tomorrow in Spain, will complicate efforts to fight massive wildfires raging in the Bordeaux region and a number of sites in Spain.

On a visit to a crisis centre in Bordeaux, the French president, Emmanuel Macron, warned:

“The coming weeks will be tough and we have to hold out.”

France's President Emmanuel Macron and France's interior minister Laurent Nuñez listen to SDIS 33 Fire and Rescue Service Departemental director Marc Vermeulen during a situation briefing in the crisis room at the Departmental Fire and Rescue Operational Center (CODIS) in Bordeaux. Photograph: Kenzo Tribouillard/POOL AFP/AP

Over the past week, the gigantic blaze in the region has ravaged 42,000 hectares (103,000 acres), forced 220,000 people to flee and destroyed 240 houses. Such is its size and intensity, it has created a rare pyrocumulonimbus cloud, packing lightning.

But as of Tuesday morning, it remained stable for the second night in a row, which offers hope that firefighters will be able to make further progress before the new heatwave complicates their efforts.

Macron confirmed that a forest fire in nearby Landes – which had forced the evacuation of more than 30,000 people – was now under control. But he warned it “remains virulent and we must be extremely cautious over the next few hours and the coming days”.

French president Emmanuel Macron speaks with firefighters during a visit to Bordeaux. Photograph: Kenzo Tribouillard/EPA

Spain has one crucial day to tackle the wildfires before weather conditions worsen again.

Ahead of the new heatwave, Spain’s prime minister Pedro Sánchez told his fellow citizens: “We face difficult hours, complex hours.”

About 60,000 people have been forced to flee in Spain and 220,000 in France, often with just minutes’ notice. Hundreds were also evacuated from Italy.

The fires in Spain have affected an area of 77,000 hectares (190,000 acres) – nearly the size of New York City – according to authorities.

Spain’s state meteorological agency, Aemet, officially declared that a heatwave, the fourth of the summer, would begin on Wednesday, lasting until “at least” Sunday.
